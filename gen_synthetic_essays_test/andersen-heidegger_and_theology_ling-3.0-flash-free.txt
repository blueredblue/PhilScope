# The Ontological Ground of Theology: Evaluating Nathan Andersen's Critique of Heidegger's Pre-Theological Ontology

## I. Introduction: The Question That Divides

The relationship between philosophy and theology has constituted one of the most enduring and contentious debates in Western intellectual history. From the medieval scholastic synthesis to the modern rupture between reason and revelation, thinkers have struggled to determine whether theological understanding can legitimately draw upon philosophical categories, and if so, under what conditions. In the twentieth century, Martin Heidegger's intervention into this debate introduced a new and profoundly disruptive dimension. Heidegger claimed that theology, having lost its way in centuries of ontic inquiry about God as a supreme entity, must return to the pre-theological question of Being itself. Theology, on Heidegger's account, must ground its conceptual understanding in a philosophy that remains resolutely atheistic — a philosophy that suspends all judgments about the existence or non-existence of God in order to attend to the ontological difference between Being and beings.

This claim has provoked sustained and vigorous resistance, particularly from theologians and philosophers of religion who argue that theology's irreducible dependence on faith renders it incapable of drawing upon a non-faithful ontological framework without losing its essential identity. Nathan Andersen has emerged as one of the more incisive voices in this debate, offering a sustained critique of Heidegger's assertion that theology can and should derive its conceptual understanding from pre-theological ontology. Andersen's argument centers on a fundamental tension: Heidegger's philosophy, by its own avowed commitment to atheism, systematically excludes the dimension of faith that is constitutive of theological existence, and therefore cannot provide the ontological ground that theology requires without distorting or dissolving its subject matter. This essay evaluates Andersen's critique by examining the tension between Heidegger's atheistic ontology and theology's grounding in faith, and by asking whether an explicitly non-faithful analysis of Dasein as thrown projection can adequately account for theological phenomena such as man's guilt before God and the creative existence of the Word.

## II. Heidegger's Project: Atheism as Philosophy and the Ontological Difference

To understand the force of Andersen's critique, one must first grasp the architecture of Heidegger's philosophical project and its relationship to theology. In *Being and Time* (1927), Heidegger undertakes a fundamental ontological inquiry that he claims has been forgotten by the Western metaphysical tradition. The forgotten question is the ontological difference — the difference between Being (Sein) and beings (Seiendes). Western metaphysics, from Plato onward, has treated Being as the highest genus or as the most real being, thereby collapsing the distinction between the question of what it means for anything to be and the inquiry into particular entities that exist. Heidegger's project is to restore this distinction and to ask the question of Being anew.

The method Heidegger adopts for this restoration is phenomenological analysis of Dasein — the being for whom Being is an issue. Dasein, human existence, is the locus where the question of Being can be raised and where Being manifests itself. Through a careful analysis of Dasein's structures — its being-in-the-world, its care (Sorge), its temporality, its thrownness (Geworfenheit), and its projection (Entwurf) — Heidegger aims to uncover the meaning of Being as such. This analysis is explicitly atheistic in the sense that it does not presuppose the existence of God or any divine being. It is not an anti-theistic project in the sense of arguing against God's existence; rather, it is a project that brackets the question of God entirely in order to attend to the ontological conditions that make any inquiry, including theological inquiry, possible.

This atheism is not a contingent historical position but a structural necessity of the philosophical enterprise. Heidegger argues that philosophy, in its essence, is the thinking that precedes and grounds all particular sciences, including theology. Philosophy remains atheism not because it denies God but because it refuses to take any entity — including God — as its starting point. The ontological difference demands that we ask about the conditions of intelligibility before we ask about the being of God. Theology, in Heidegger's view, has committed a category mistake by treating God as an ontic entity to be studied rather than attending to the ontological horizon that makes the very meaning of "God" possible.

Heidegger's famous declaration that philosophy remains atheism thus carries a double meaning. On one level, it expresses a methodological commitment to ontological inquiry that does not presuppose theological commitments. On another level, it asserts that the ontological framework provided by philosophy is prior to and corrective of theology's ontic preoccupations. Theology must submit its conceptual understanding to the discipline of ontology if it wishes to recover genuine theological meaning. This is the claim that Andersen challenges.

## III. Dasein as Thrown Projection and the Phenomenology of Guilt

To evaluate whether Heidegger's ontology can serve as a ground for theology, one must examine the specific ontological categories Heidegger deploys and their relationship to theological phenomena. The two most significant categories for this inquiry are Dasein's structure as thrown projection and Heidegger's analysis of guilt (Schuld).

Dasein is thrown (Geworfenheit) into a world it did not choose. It finds itself already situated in a particular historical, cultural, and linguistic context, with a body, a name, and a set of possibilities that it did not select. Yet Dasein is also projection (Entwurf) — it is always reaching forward, choosing and actualizing possibilities that define its existence. Thrownness and projection together constitute the ekstatic temporality of Dasein: Dasein is stretched between a past it did not choose and a future it must project. This dual structure is the fundamental constitution of human existence in Heidegger's analysis.

Heidegger's analysis of guilt emerges from this structure. Dasein is always already "being-guilty" (Schuldigsein) because its thrownness means it is already entangled in a situation it did not choose, and its projection means it must make choices that carry responsibility. Guilt, in Heidegger's existential sense, is not a moral failing but the ontological structure of Dasein's being-in-the-world. Dasein is guilty because it is always already implicated in the world it finds itself in, and because its very existence involves the taking up of possibilities that entail responsibility for their consequences. Heidegger writes that Dasein "is in its Being its own possibility" and that this self-positing of possibility is the root of existential guilt.

Theological tradition, by contrast, understands guilt primarily in a relational and moral register. Human guilt before God is not merely the ontological entanglement of thrown projection but a rupture in the covenantal relationship between Creator and creature. Guilt before God involves transgression of divine law, alienation from the divine, and the need for redemption and forgiveness. While Heidegger's existential guilt and theological guilt share the language of "guilt," they operate within fundamentally different frameworks. Heidegger's guilt is immanent to Dasein's structure; theological guilt is a relation between Dasein and a transcendent divine authority.

The creative existence of the Word (Logos) presents an even more formidable challenge to Heidegger's ontological framework. In the theological tradition, the Word is not merely a human projection or an existential possibility but the creative act of God through which all things come into being. The Logos is the divine utterance that precedes and grounds human language and meaning. It is, in the Johannine tradition, "in the beginning" — prior to all human existence, all thrownness, all projection. The creative Word is not an existential structure of Dasein but a divine act that constitutes the very conditions of Dasein's existence.

Heidegger's ontology, grounded in the analysis of Dasein as thrown projection, has no conceptual apparatus for articulating a creative Word that is ontologically prior to Dasein and constitutes the conditions of Dasein's existence. Dasein's projection is always a human projecting; it is always already situated and limited by the world it inherits. The creative Word, by contrast, is not situated or limited; it is the source of all situatedness and limitation. The gap between these two concepts is not merely a matter of terminology but reflects a fundamental ontological divergence that Heidegger's framework cannot bridge.

## IV. Nathan Andersen's Critique: Theology's Irreducible Ground in Faith

Nathan Andersen's critique of Heidegger's position proceeds from a fundamental objection to the claim that theology can derive its conceptual understanding from pre-theological ontology. Andersen argues that theology, beginning and ending in faith, cannot utilize Heidegger's analysis of Dasein and essential "Being-guilty" without losing its necessary relation to faithful existence. The critique operates on multiple levels, addressing both the methodological claim that philosophy provides a ground for theology and the substantive claim that ontological categories can be translated into theological categories without loss.

At the methodological level, Andersen challenges Heidegger's assertion that philosophy remains atheism and must provide an ontological corrective to theology. Andersen argues that Heidegger's atheism is not a neutral methodological stance but a substantive philosophical commitment that systematically excludes the dimension of faith from the outset. By bracketing the question of God and restricting inquiry to the ontological structures of Dasein, Heidegger's philosophy presupposes that the meaning of theological phenomena can be reduced to ontological structures without remainder. This presupposition is precisely what theology cannot accept, because faith introduces a dimension of meaning — the meaning of encounter with the divine, of revelation, of grace — that cannot be captured by ontological analysis alone.

Andersen's argument draws attention to the fact that theology does not merely study God as an entity (the ontic approach Heidegger criticizes) but participates in a mode of existence — faithful existence — that is constituted by relationship to the divine. Theology is not an external science of God but an internal practice of faith that seeks to understand its own experience of the divine. This understanding is not derived from ontological analysis but from the lived reality of faith itself. To attempt to ground theology in pre-theological ontology is to misunderstand what theology is and what it does.

At the substantive level, Andersen argues that Heidegger's ontological categories cannot be adequately translated into theological categories without distortion. The concept of "being-guilty" in Heidegger's sense — the existential entanglement of Dasein in its thrown projection — does not capture the theological reality of guilt before God. Theological guilt involves not merely ontological entanglement but a specific relationship to a divine lawgiver, a consciousness of sin against the divine, and the experience of divine judgment and mercy. These dimensions of guilt are inaccessible to an ontology that does not presuppose the existence of God and the reality of divine-human relations.

Similarly, the creative Word cannot be understood through the concept of Dasein's projection. Human projection is always a projection within a world that is already given; it is always limited by facticity and situatedness. The creative Word, by contrast, is not a projection within a world but the act by which the world itself comes into being. The Word is not a human existential structure but a divine act that constitutes the very conditions of human existence. To conflate these two concepts is not merely a category mistake but a theological distortion that erases the transcendence of God and reduces the divine Word to a human phenomenon.

Andersen's critique thus raises the question of whether Heidegger's ontology, by its very structure and commitment, is incapable of generating genuine theological understanding. If theology's subject matter is constituted by faith and by the reality of the divine, then an ontology that excludes the divine from its inquiry cannot provide the conceptual ground that theology requires. The ontological corrective that Heidegger proposes would not correct theology but would displace it, replacing theological categories with ontological ones and thereby transforming theology into a form of philosophy rather than a discourse of faith.

## V. The Failure of Ontological Reduction: Guilt Before God and the Creative Word

The tension between Heidegger's ontology and theology's subject matter becomes most acute when one examines the specific theological phenomena that Andersen identifies as resistant to ontological reduction. The first of these is man's guilt before God. The second is the creative existence of the Word. Each of these phenomena reveals a dimension of theological meaning that Heidegger's ontology cannot capture without collapsing into a non-theological framework.

Man's guilt before God is not merely an existential structure but a relational reality. In the theological tradition, guilt before God involves the breaking of a covenant, the transgression of a divine command, and the experience of estrangement from the divine source of being. This guilt is not immanent to Dasein's thrown projection but is constituted by the relationship between Dasein and God. It is a guilt that can only be understood from within the perspective of faith — the faith that recognizes God as lawgiver, judge, and redeemer. Heidegger's existential guilt, by contrast, is understood from within the perspective of Dasein's own being, without reference to a divine other. The ontological analysis of Dasein's guilt as "being-guilty" captures a structural feature of human existence but misses the relational and covenantal dimension that gives theological guilt its specific meaning.

Moreover, the theological understanding of guilt is inseparable from the theological understanding of forgiveness. Guilt before God is not a permanent ontological condition but a state that can be overcome through divine grace and reconciliation. Heidegger's "being-guilty," however, is not overcome or resolved; it is the permanent structure of Dasein's existence, the ever-present entanglement of thrownness and projection that constitutes human finitude. The theological resolution of guilt through grace and forgiveness has no analogue in Heidegger's ontology, because Heidegger's analysis does not include the dimension of divine action that transforms the human condition.

The creative existence of the Word presents an even more decisive challenge to Heidegger's ontological framework. The Word as creative is not a human existential possibility but the divine act that brings all possibilities into being. In the theological tradition, the Word is the medium of revelation, the instrument of creation, and the ground of all meaning. The Word is not projected by Dasein but is received by Dasein as a gift from God. It is the Word that makes human language and meaning possible, not the other way around. Heidegger's ontology, which analyzes human meaning-making as the projection of Dasein within the world, cannot account for a Word that precedes and grounds human meaning-making.

The creative Word also introduces a dimension of temporality that Heidegger's ontology cannot accommodate. In the theological tradition, the Word is eternal — it exists beyond the temporal horizon of Dasein's thrown projection. The creative act of the Word is not situated within Dasein's temporality but is the ground of temporality itself. Heidegger's analysis of Dasein's ekstatic temporality — its stretching between past, present, and future — presupposes a temporal horizon that is itself constituted by the Word. To attempt to derive the Word from Dasein's temporality is to reverse the theological order, making the human condition the ground of the divine act rather than the divine act the ground of the human condition.

These failures of ontological reduction suggest that Heidegger's framework, for all its sophistication and rigor, operates within limits that theology cannot transcend. The ontological difference that Heidegger articulates — the difference between Being and beings — is a difference that theology must acknowledge but that theology cannot simply adopt as its own ground. Theology's subject matter is not Being as such but the God who is and the Word who creates and redeems. To reduce theological understanding to ontological analysis is to substitute a philosophical framework for a theological one, thereby transforming theology into a discourse about Dasein's structures rather than a discourse about the divine.

## VI. Can Theology Achieve Conceptual Understanding Through Philosophy While Remaining Within Faith?

The question that emerges from this analysis is whether theology can achieve conceptual understanding through philosophy while remaining within faith. This is the question that animates the entire debate between Heidegger and his theological critics, and it is the question that Andersen's critique foregrounds. The answer requires careful examination of what "conceptual understanding" means in a theological context and whether philosophy can provide such understanding without displacing theology's grounding in faith.

One possible response to this question draws on the tradition of existential theology, particularly the work of Rudolf Bultmann, who sought to translate Heidegger's ontological analysis into a theological framework for interpreting the New Testament. Bultmann argued that Heidegger's analysis of Dasein's existential structures could serve as a hermeneutical tool for demythologizing the New Testament and uncovering its existential kerygma. In Bultmann's hands, Heidegger's ontology becomes a resource for theology, a philosophical framework that can be baptized and transformed for theological purposes.

Andersen's critique challenges this move at its foundations. The problem is not merely that Bultmann's translation distorts Heidegger's ontology but that the very act of translation requires theology to adopt a framework that is alien to its own mode of existence. Theology begins and ends in faith; it is a discourse that emerges from and returns to the experience of encounter with the divine. To adopt Heidegger's ontological categories as a framework for theological understanding is to introduce a non-faithful perspective into the heart of theological discourse, thereby destabilizing the relationship between theology and its ground in faith.

This destabilization takes a specific form. When theology adopts Heidegger's categories, it inevitably interprets its own phenomena — guilt, the Word, faith, grace — through the lens of ontological structures that do not include the divine. The result is not a theology that has been clarified by philosophy but a theology that has been transformed into something else — a form of existential philosophy that retains the vocabulary of theology while emptying it of its theological content. The guilt before God becomes existential entanglement; the creative Word becomes human projection; faith becomes a mode of Dasein's being-in-the-world. In each case, the theological phenomenon is reduced to an ontological structure, and the dimension of the divine is systematically excluded.

Yet one might object that this objection begs the question. Heidegger would argue that theology has always already been operating within an ontological framework, whether it acknowledges it or not, and that his analysis merely makes this framework explicit. Theology's traditional talk about God as a being, about sin as moral transgression, about the Word as divine utterance — all of these presuppose an ontology that theology has never critically examined. Heidegger's project is to provide theology with the ontological clarity it needs to understand its own conceptual foundations. The fact that this clarity requires theology to suspend certain claims about God is not a loss but a gain — it allows theology to recover its genuine meaning by attending to the ontological conditions that make theological discourse possible.

This Heideggerian response, however, encounters a difficulty that Andersen's critique effectively exposes. The ontological conditions that make theological discourse possible cannot themselves be understood without attending to the dimension of faith that constitutes theology's subject matter. Theology is not merely a discourse about God that happens to presuppose certain ontological conditions; it is a discourse that emerges from and is sustained by the experience of faith. The ontological conditions of theological discourse are not external to theology but internal to it — they are the conditions of possibility for faithful existence, not for ontological inquiry as such.

This suggests that the relationship between philosophy and theology is not one of foundation and superstructure, as Heidegger's model suggests, but one of mutual implication and mutual limitation. Philosophy can illuminate certain aspects of theological discourse, but it cannot provide the ground that theology requires without becoming theology itself — or without ceasing to be philosophy in its properly atheistic sense. The ontological difference that Heidegger articulates is a genuine philosophical achievement, but it does not provide a bridge between philosophy and theology because the bridge would require philosophy to cross the very atheism that defines it.

Andersen's critique thus points toward a more radical conclusion: theology cannot achieve conceptual understanding through philosophy while remaining within faith, because the very act of achieving conceptual understanding through philosophy requires a detachment from faith that theology cannot sustain. Theology's conceptual understanding must emerge from within faith, not from without it. The task of theology is not to adopt philosophical categories and translate them into theological language but to develop its own conceptual resources from the ground up, drawing upon the experience of faithful existence and the reality of divine revelation.

This conclusion does not mean that theology can ignore philosophy or that philosophy has nothing to offer theology. It means that the relationship between the two must be reconfigured in a way that respects the irreducibility of faith. Philosophy can serve as a critical partner to theology, questioning its presupposions and clarifying its concepts, but it cannot serve as its foundation. The foundation of theology is faith itself — the faith that begins and ends in the encounter with the divine, that understands guilt as sin before God and the Word as the creative utterance of the divine, and that refuses to reduce these realities to ontological structures that exclude the transcendent.

## VII. Conclusion: The Irreconcilable Tension

The evaluation of Nathan Andersen's critique of Heidegger's claim that theology must ground its conceptual understanding in pre-theological ontology reveals a tension that is, in the final analysis, irreconcilable within the terms that Heidegger himself has established. Heidegger's philosophy remains atheism not as a temporary methodological stance but as a structural necessity of ontological inquiry. It cannot accommodate the dimension of faith without ceasing to be philosophy, and it cannot provide a ground for theology without transforming theology into a form of philosophy. The ontological analysis of Dasein as thrown projection, with its concepts of guilt and projection, cannot account for theological phenomena such as guilt before God and the creative Word because these phenomena are constituted by dimensions — the divine, the transcendent, the revelatory — that Heidegger's ontology systematically excludes.

Andersen's critique succeeds in demonstrating that theology cannot simply adopt Heidegger's ontological framework without losing its essential identity. Theology's beginning and ending in faith means that its conceptual understanding must emerge from within faith, not from a pre-theological ontology that brackets faith as irrelevant. The attempt to derive theological understanding from a non-faithful ontology is not a bridge between philosophy and theology but a one-way street that leads from theology into philosophy, dissolving the theological into the ontological and erasing the difference that defines theology's subject matter.

Yet the critique also reveals a limitation in its own argumentative strategy. By insisting on the irreducibility of theology to ontology, Andersen's critique risks isolating theology from the broader intellectual tradition that has always shaped its conceptual development. Theology has never operated in a vacuum; it has always drawn upon philosophical categories, whether it acknowledges it or not, and the question is not whether theology can avoid philosophy but how it can engage philosophy without being absorbed by it. The challenge for theology, in the wake of Heidegger and his critics, is to develop a mode of conceptual understanding that is faithful to its ground in faith while remaining open to the critical resources that philosophy can offer — a mode that is neither the reduction of theology to ontology nor the isolation of theology from all philosophical engagement.

This challenge remains unresolved, and it is likely that it will remain so as long as the fundamental tension between atheistic ontology and faithful theology persists. Heidegger's philosophy and theology's commitment to faith represent two modes of thought that are structurally incompatible in their deepest commitments, and the attempt to reconcile them — whether through translation, transformation, or mutual correction — must contend with the irreducible difference between them. Nathan Andersen's critique is valuable precisely because it refuses to paper over this difference and insists on the question of whether theology can achieve conceptual understanding through philosophy while remaining within faith. The answer, on the basis of the analysis presented here, is that it cannot — not without transforming itself into something other than what it is. And that transformation, for theology, would be a loss too great to bear.
