In the realm of philosophical theology, the debate surrounding the relationship between philosophy and theology has been a longstanding and contentious one. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether theology can, or indeed must, ground its conceptual understanding in pre-theological ontology. Martin Heidegger's claim that theology requires an ontological foundation has sparked intense discussion, with Nathan Andersen offering a critique that highlights the tension between Heidegger's assertion that philosophy remains atheism and the inherent demands of theological inquiry. This essay will delve into the complexities of this debate, examining the implications of Heidegger's ontology for theological understanding and the counter-arguments that suggest theology cannot utilize philosophical analysis without compromising its essential relation to faith.

Heidegger's philosophy, as outlined in his seminal work "Being and Time," posits that human existence, or Dasein, is characterized by its fundamental ontological structure. According to Heidegger, Dasein is a thrown projection, existing in a state of being-in-the-world that is inherently temporal and finite. This ontological analysis of Dasein is meant to provide a pre-theological foundation for understanding human existence, one that is not beholden to any particular theological or metaphysical framework. Heidegger's assertion that philosophy remains atheism reflects his conviction that philosophical inquiry must eschew any appeals to divine or supernatural explanations, instead focusing on the ontological structures that underlie human existence.

However, this raises the question of whether theology can, or should, utilize Heidegger's analysis of Dasein as a foundation for its own conceptual understanding. Heidegger's ontology is explicitly non-faithful, seeking to understand human existence in terms of its inherent ontological structures rather than any external, divine framework. This would seem to preclude the possibility of theology, which begins and ends in faith, from adopting Heidegger's analysis without losing its necessary relation to faithful existence. As Andersen argues, theology cannot simply appropriate Heidegger's ontology without compromising its own inherent commitments to faith and divine revelation.

One of the primary concerns with Heidegger's ontology is its apparent inability to account for theological phenomena such as man's guilt before God. Heidegger's concept of "Being-guilty" (Schuld) refers to the fundamental ontological structure of Dasein, wherein human existence is characterized by its own finitude and temporality. However, this concept of guilt is inherently non-moral and non-theological, lacking any reference to divine judgment or moral accountability. In contrast, theological understandings of guilt typically involve a sense of moral responsibility and accountability before God, which would seem to be absent from Heidegger's ontological analysis.

Andersen's critique of Heidegger highlights this tension, arguing that theology cannot simply adopt Heidegger's analysis of Dasein without losing its essential relation to faith. If theology were to utilize Heidegger's ontology as a foundation for its conceptual understanding, it would risk reducing the theological concept of guilt to a merely ontological category, stripped of its moral and divine significance. This would fundamentally alter the nature of theological inquiry, transforming it into a purely philosophical exercise that neglects the essential role of faith and divine revelation.

Moreover, Heidegger's ontology is inherently critical of traditional metaphysics and theology, seeking to overcome the limitations of these disciplines through a more primordial and fundamental analysis of human existence. This critique of traditional theology is reflected in Heidegger's assertion that theology must provide an ontological corrective to the ontic science of philosophy, rather than the other way around. In other words, Heidegger believes that theology must be grounded in a pre-theological ontology that provides a more fundamental understanding of human existence, rather than relying on philosophical or metaphysical frameworks that are inherently secondary and derivative.

However, this raises the question of whether an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis of Dasein can truly account for theological phenomena such as the creative existence of the Word. Theological understandings of the Word typically involve a sense of divine creativity and revelation, wherein the Word is seen as a manifestation of God's own being and will. Heidegger's ontology, on the other hand, is focused on the ontological structures of human existence, rather than any divine or supernatural reality. It is unclear whether Heidegger's analysis of Dasein as a thrown projection can truly capture the theological significance of the Word, or whether this would require a fundamentally different ontological framework that is more attuned to the demands of theological inquiry.

Despite these challenges, some philosophers have argued that Heidegger's ontology can, in fact, provide a valuable foundation for theological understanding. For example, the philosopher Rudolf Bultmann sought to utilize Heidegger's analysis of Dasein as a basis for his own theological project, arguing that the ontological structures of human existence could provide a pre-theological foundation for understanding the nature of faith and divine revelation. Bultmann's work reflects a broader trend in 20th-century theology, wherein philosophers and theologians sought to engage with Heidegger's ontology as a means of rethinking the nature of theological inquiry and the relationship between philosophy and theology.

However, this approach is not without its challenges.
