The pursuit of the good life, or *eudaimonia*, stands as the central telos of human existence, a question that has echoed through the corridors of Western philosophy since antiquity. For Aristotle, the answer to this existential quest is inextricably linked to the political community. In the *Politics* and the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle posits that man is by nature a political animal (*zoon politikon*), and that the highest human association—the state, or *polis*—exists not merely for survival or economic exchange, but for the sake of the good life. However, a profound tension arises when we scrutinize the Aristotelian framework: the state is structurally necessary for providing the basic material conditions that make virtue possible, yet it is inherently incapable of fostering the "perfect friendship" that Aristotle identifies as the pinnacle of human flourishing. This tension becomes even more acute when we project Aristotle’s ideal onto the realities of modern political regimes, which are characterized by impersonality, pluralism, and bureaucratic rationalization. Consequently, an evaluation of the Aristotelian state reveals that while it provides the indispensable scaffolding for a good life, it cannot complete the individual's pursuit of happiness and virtue. Instead, smaller societal groupings—specifically the family and close peers—must supersede the state in fulfilling the profound human need for perfect friendship, thereby completing the architecture of the good life.

To understand the Aristotelian state's role in aiding citizens, one must first appreciate its structural necessity. Aristotle’s political theory is teleological; just as the acorn grows into the oak, human associations naturally evolve from the household (*oikos*) to the village (*kome*) and ultimately to the *polis*. The state is the "completion" of human association because it alone renders life worth living. Without the *polis*, the individual is reduced to a mere animal or a god, isolated from the conditions necessary for rational flourishing. The state's primary contribution to the good life is the provision of basic material conditions. Aristotle argues that virtue requires leisure (*schole*); one cannot contemplate the highest goods or practice moral excellences if one is perpetually consumed by the struggle for survival. The *polis* provides security against external threats, organizes economic production, and ensures a baseline of material stability. Furthermore, the state administers justice, creating a framework of laws that regulates behavior and punishes vice. In this sense, the Aristotelian state is a necessary condition for the good life. It constructs the external environment—the infrastructure, the legal system, the peace—that allows the individual to pursue virtue. Without the state's structural apparatus, the pursuit of *eudaimonia* remains an abstract, unattainable ideal, crushed under the weight of mere animal survival. The state, therefore, successfully aids citizens by laying the indispensable foundation upon which the edifice of the good life can be built.

However, the good life is not merely a matter of external conditions; it is an internal, relational reality. In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle devotes two full books to the nature of friendship (*philia*), distinguishing three types: friendships of utility, friendships of pleasure, and friendships of virtue. The first two are incidental and easily dissolved when the utility or pleasure ceases; they are not constitutive of the good life. Perfect friendship, however, is the friendship of virtue. It is the bond between "good men who are alike in virtue." In this highest form of friendship, individuals love one another not for what they can extract from the relationship, but for the sake of the other's inherent goodness. The defining characteristic of perfect friendship is the loving of another as "another self" based on shared essence. This phrase is not merely metaphorical; it implies a deep ontological resonance. When one loves another as another self, one recognizes that the friend's rational nature, their *essence*, is shared with one's own. The good of the friend is indistinguishable from one's own good; their joy is one's joy, and their suffering is one's suffering. Aristotle states that a friend is "another self," meaning that in the friend, one sees a second instance of one's own rational nature, united in the pursuit of the same moral and intellectual excellences. Perfect friendship demands absolute intimacy, mutual recognition of character, and a shared pursuit of the good. It requires the slow, laborious process of mutual moral formation, where each friend acts as a mirror for the other, reflecting and refining virtue. Because human beings are inherently social and rational, perfect friendship is not a luxury but a necessity for *eudaimonia*; it is the highest actualization of rational activity and the deepest source of human joy.

The requirements of perfect friendship stand in stark contrast to the nature of modern political regimes. While Aristotle’s *polis* was a relatively intimate, face-to-face community where citizens shared a common way of life and a unified conception of the good, modern states are defined by impersonality, pluralism, and bureaucracy. The modern state, particularly in its liberal democratic form, is structurally incapable of fostering perfect friendship because its very design precludes the shared essence required for such bonds.
