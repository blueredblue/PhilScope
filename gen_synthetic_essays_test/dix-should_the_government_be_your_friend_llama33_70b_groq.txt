In the realm of philosophical thought, Aristotle's conception of the state as a means to attain the good life has been a subject of enduring debate. The Aristotelian state, as outlined in his seminal work "Politics," is premised on the idea that the state exists to provide its citizens with the necessary conditions for achieving eudaimonia, or human flourishing. However, this notion is complicated by the inherent tension between the state's structural necessity for providing basic material conditions and its inability to foster "perfect friendship," a concept Aristotle deemed essential for human fulfillment. This essay will evaluate the capacity of the Aristotelian state to aid citizens in attaining the good life, examining the conflict between the requirements of perfect friendship and the impersonal nature of modern political regimes, and arguing that smaller societal groupings, such as the family and close peers, must supersede the state in completing the individual pursuit of happiness and virtue.

Aristotle's conception of the state is rooted in his teleological understanding of human nature, where the ultimate goal of human existence is to achieve eudaimonia. The state, in this context, is seen as a necessary institution for providing citizens with the basic material conditions, such as security, education, and economic stability, required for human flourishing. However, Aristotle also recognized that the state's role in promoting human flourishing extends beyond mere material provision. He argued that the state should foster a sense of community and friendship among its citizens, which is essential for achieving the good life. In his view, perfect friendship, characterized by a deep and abiding love for another person as "another self," is the highest form of human relationship, and it is this type of friendship that is necessary for achieving true happiness and virtue.

The requirements of perfect friendship, as outlined by Aristotle, are rooted in the idea of shared essence. According to Aristotle, perfect friends must share a common understanding of what it means to live a virtuous life, and they must be united in their pursuit of eudaimonia. This shared essence is what allows perfect friends to love and care for one another as they would for themselves, and it is this type of love and care that is necessary for achieving true happiness and fulfillment. However, the impersonal, pluralistic, or bureaucratic nature of modern political regimes poses a significant challenge to the cultivation of perfect friendship. In modern societies, individuals are often reduced to abstract citizens, rather than being recognized as unique individuals with their own distinct characters and virtues. This reduction of individuals to mere abstractions makes it difficult for the state to foster the type of deep and personal relationships that are necessary for perfect friendship.

Furthermore, the state's structural necessity for providing basic material conditions often leads to a focus on efficiency and bureaucratic expediency, rather than on the cultivation of personal relationships and community. In modern societies, the state is often seen as a provider of services, rather than as a facilitator of human flourishing. This focus on service provision can lead to a sense of alienation and disconnection among citizens, as they are reduced to mere recipients of state-provided goods and services, rather than being recognized as active participants in the pursuit of eudaimonia. Additionally, the pluralistic nature of modern societies, where individuals from diverse backgrounds and with different values and beliefs coexist, can make it challenging for the state to foster a sense of shared essence and common purpose among its citizens.

In light of these challenges, it is clear that the Aristotelian state faces significant difficulties in fostering perfect friendship among its citizens. The state's inability to provide the type of personal and intimate relationships that are necessary for perfect friendship means that it must rely on smaller societal groupings, such as the family and close peers, to complete the individual pursuit of happiness and virtue. The family, in particular, is well-suited to provide the type of personal and intimate relationships that are necessary for perfect friendship. Within the family, individuals are able to form deep and abiding bonds with one another, based on shared experiences, values, and virtues. The family provides a sense of belonging and connection that is essential for human flourishing, and it is within the family that individuals are able to develop the type of character and virtue that is necessary for achieving eudaimonia.

Close peers and friendships outside of the family also play a crucial role in completing the individual pursuit of happiness and virtue. Friendships based on shared interests, values, and virtues can provide individuals with a sense of purpose and belonging, and can help to foster the type of personal growth and development that is necessary for achieving eudaimonia. Furthermore, friendships and close relationships can provide individuals with a sense of support and care, which is essential for navigating the challenges and uncertainties of life. In this sense, smaller societal groupings, such as the family and close peers, are better equipped to provide the type of personal and intimate relationships that are necessary for perfect friendship, and are therefore essential for completing the individual pursuit of happiness and virtue.

It is also worth noting that the importance of smaller societal groupings in fostering perfect friendship and human flourishing is not limited to the individual level. The cultivation of perfect friendship and community at the local level can also have a profound impact on the broader social and political landscape. When individuals are able to form deep and meaningful connections with one another, they are more likely to be engaged and active participants in their communities, and are more likely to work towards the common good. This, in turn, can lead to a more vibrant and thriving civil society, where individuals are able to pursue their own unique vision of the good life, while also contributing to the well-being and flourishing of their community.

In conclusion, while the Aristotelian state is necessary for providing the basic material conditions required for human flourishing, it is ultimately unable to foster the type of perfect friendship that is necessary for achieving true happiness and virtue. The impersonal, pluralistic, or bureaucratic nature of modern political regimes poses a significant challenge to the cultivation of perfect friendship, and the state's structural necessity for providing basic material conditions often leads to a focus on efficiency and bureaucratic expediency, rather than on the cultivation of personal relationships and community. Therefore, smaller societal groupings, such as the family and close peers, must supersede the state in completing the individual pursuit of happiness and virtue. By fostering deep and meaningful connections with one another, individuals are able to develop the type of character and virtue that is necessary for achieving eudaimonia, and are able to pursue their own unique vision of the good life, while also contributing to the well-being and flourishing of their community.

Ultimately, the pursuit of happiness and virtue is a deeply personal and individualized endeavor, and it is one that requires a nuanced and multifaceted approach. While the state has a crucial role to play in providing the basic material conditions required for human flourishing, it is ultimately up to individuals and smaller societal groupings to cultivate the type of personal and intimate relationships that are necessary for perfect friendship and human flourishing. By recognizing the importance of smaller societal groupings in fostering perfect friendship and community, we can work towards creating a more vibrant and thriving civil society, where individuals are able to pursue their own unique vision of the good life, while also contributing to the well-being and flourishing of their community.

In the final analysis, the Aristotelian state provides a necessary foundation for human flourishing, but it is ultimately insufficient for achieving the good life. The cultivation of perfect friendship and community, which is necessary for achieving true happiness and virtue, requires a more nuanced and multifaceted approach, one that recognizes the importance of smaller societal groupings, such as the family and close peers, in fostering deep and meaningful connections among individuals. By acknowledging the limitations of the state in fostering perfect friendship, and by recognizing the importance of smaller societal groupings in completing the individual pursuit of happiness and virtue, we can work towards creating a more just and flourishing society, where individuals are able to pursue their own unique vision of the good life, while also contributing to the well-being and flourishing of their community.
