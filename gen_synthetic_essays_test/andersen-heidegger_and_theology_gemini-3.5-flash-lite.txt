# Ontology, Faith, and the Tragic Ambiguity of the Word: Nathan Andersen’s Critique of Heideggerian Theology

## Introduction

The intersection of philosophy and theology in the early work of Martin Heidegger represents one of the most volatile encounters in twentieth-century thought. In his 1927 lecture *Phenomenology and Theology*, Heidegger famously positioned philosophy as a foundational, radically a-theistic science that provides an indispensable ontological corrective to the ontic science of theology. For Heidegger, theology as a positive science of faith must ground its conceptual framework in an understanding of human existence—*Dasein*—prior to any dogmatic or confessional commitments. This pre-theological ontology operates as an analytical clearing, exposing the structural coordinates of human thrownness, projection, and existential guilt. 

However, this methodological subordination of theology to philosophy provokes a profound theological crisis. Does Heidegger’s formal-indicative method evacuate the very substance of faith, substituting an empty ontological skeleton for the living, divine-human drama of grace, revelation, and personal relation to God? 

This question forms the crucible of Nathan Andersen’s incisive critique of Heidegger in *Dein Theos: Heidegger, Atheism, and the God of Faith*. Andersen interrogates the viability of Heidegger’s program, particularly its demand that theology must rely upon a fundamentally non-faithful, structurally "atheistic" analysis of *Dasein*. 

This essay will evaluate Andersen’s critique by examining the tension between Heidegger’s insistence that philosophy remains atheism and the counter-argument that theology, because it begins and ends in faith, cannot utilize Heidegger’s existential analytic—specifically his existential interpretation of "Being-guilty"—without severing its tie to faithful existence. 

Furthermore, this paper will examine whether an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis of *Dasein* as thrown projection can adequately account for specifically theological phenomena, such as man’s radical guilt *coram Deo* (before God) and the creative, transformative existence of the divine Word. Ultimately, we must resolve whether theology can ever legitimately achieve its conceptual self-understanding through Heideggerian philosophy while remaining genuinely within the sphere of faith.

---

## I. Heidegger’s Methodological Framework: Philosophy as Atheism and the Ontological Corrective

To understand Andersen’s critique, one must first map the contours of Heidegger’s project in *Phenomenology and Theology* and *Being and Time*. Heidegger’s central provocation is his claim that "philosophy is essentially questioning; and perhaps foolishness." As an inquiry into the meaning of Being, philosophy must remain radically unconstrained by positive presuppositions. It does not possess a regional object like biology or mathematics; rather, it investigates the horizon within which all objects and entities are disclosed. In this sense, philosophy is constitutively "atheistic." 

As Heidegger argues, philosophy’s atheism is not a militant denial of God, but a methodological demand:

> "Philosophy does not choose this atheism as a detachable attitude; rather, philosophy as phenomenology is in itself non-theistic, and *must* be so if it is to be authentic philosophy."

The atheism of philosophy is its refusal to take anything as given by revelation, grace, or tradition. It approaches *Dasein* strictly in terms of its immanent structural possibilities—its *existentialia*.

In contrast, theology is a "positive science." Every positive science takes as its object a pre-given domain of beings—in the case of theology, this domain is "faith," or more precisely, Christianness (*Christianheit*). Theology is the ontic science of that which is revealed by faith and for faith. 

However, Heidegger asserts that because theology operates ontic-positively, it constantly runs the risk of misunderstanding its own concepts. It employs traditional philosophical categories (often derived from Greek metaphysics or scholasticism) unreflectively, thereby distorting the very reality of faith. Therefore, theology requires an *ontological corrective*. It must borrow its formal conceptual framework from philosophy—specifically from the existential analytic of *Dasein* developed in *Being and Time*—in order to understand what it means for *Dasein* to exist in the mode of faith.

Crucially, Heidegger insists that this philosophical borrowing does not compromise theology’s independence, just as biological research does not dictate the essence of life to the physician. Philosophy provides the *formal indication* of the structural layout of existence, while theology fills this structure with the concrete content of revelation. 

Yet, this division of labor immediately troubles the theologian. If philosophy defines the very formal structure of human existence (thrownness, fallenness, anxiety, being-towards-death, guilt) *without* reference to God, revelation, or grace, does it not predetermine what human existence *is* before theology even begins to speak of God? If philosophy’s existential analytic is neutral and atheistic, can it ever truly accommodate the fundamentally God-relational character of existence that defines Christian faith?

---

## II. Nathan Andersen’s Critique: The Alienation of the Word and the Void of "Atheism"

Nathan Andersen’s critique in *Dein Theos* focuses sharply on the consequences of Heidegger’s methodological separation. Andersen argues that Heidegger’s insistence on philosophy as a non-faithful, atheistic foundational science is not merely a neutral methodological boundary; it is a substantive ontological determination that crowds out the specific reality of theological phenomena.

Andersen points out that when Heidegger analyzes *Dasein* as thrown projection (*Geworfener Entwurf*), he strips the concept of "thrownness" of any transcendent giver. For Heidegger, *Dasein* is thrown into existence without a why, abandoned to its own potentiality-for-being in a universe devoid of providential design. While Heidegger acknowledges that this abandonment generates anxiety (*Angst*) and a sense of "not-being-the-basis" of one's own being, this lack of a foundation is interpreted purely formally and immanently. 

For the theologian, however, "thrownness" is never merely an abstract structural homelessness; it is creation by a loving God, and abandonment is inextricably bound up with estrangement from that Creator through sin. 

Andersen argues that by demanding that theology ground its concepts in this pre-theological, a-theistic ontology, Heidegger forces theology to translate its God-language into a secularized, existential grammar. In doing so, the theological weight of terms like *grace*, *sin*, *redemption*, and *the Word* is drained of its transcendent vitality. They become mere ciphers for structural modifications of human self-understanding. 

Andersen writes: 

> "If theology must borrow its understanding of existence from an ontology that explicitly brackets God and revelation as methodologically irrelevant, then theology ceases to speak of an objective, living God who addresses *Dasein* from beyond itself. Instead, theology becomes an anthropologized reflection on the ecstatic projection of finite human existence."

The heart of Andersen’s critique is that Heidegger’s philosophical framework cannot do justice to the *initiative* of the divine. In Christian theology, faith is not simply a modification of *Dasein’s* self-projection; it is a response to an external, alien intervention—the Word of God that breaks into human history and shatters human self-sufficiency. 

Because Heideggerian ontology views *Dasein* as fundamentally autarkic in its projection (even in its fallenness, *Dasein* projects its possibilities, and in authenticity, it resolutely retrieves them), it leaves no genuine space for a transcendent rupture that is not reducible to an internal psychological or existential event. 

Thus, Andersen contends that theology cannot adopt Heidegger’s ontological machinery without compromising its own foundation. Faith begins and ends with God; an ontology that begins and ends with *Dasein’s* immanent possibilities must inevitably miss the God of faith.

---

## III. The Crucial Test Case: "Being-Guilty" (*Schuldigsein*) versus Sin Before God (*Coram Deo*)

To evaluate the strength of Andersen’s critique, we must examine the most critical juncture in the intersection of Heideggerian ontology and Christian theology: the concept of guilt. 

In Division Two of *Being and Time*, Heidegger undertakes a radical ontological reinterpretation of guilt. Stripping the concept of its conventional moral, legal, and theological baggage (such as sin, moral failure, or transgression of divine commandments), Heidegger defines "Being-guilty" as an ontological condition constitutive of *Dasein’s* very thrownness. 

*Dasein* is guilty because it is the ground of a nullity (*Nichtigkeit*). As thrown, *Dasein* has not brought itself into being, nor is it master of its ultimate horizon. Furthermore, in projecting itself toward certain possibilities, it perpetually closes off other possibilities. This structural inability to be the absolute ground of oneself, coupled with the inevitable failure to realize all potentiality, means that *Dasein* is "indebted" or "guilty" right down to its roots. Guilt is not something *Dasein* incurs through a specific bad action; rather, *Dasein* *is* guilty because of the very structure of its finitude.

For Heidegger, resoleness (*Entschlossenheit*) in the face of this existential guilt means owning up to this nullity, stepping into one’s thrownness without illusions, and facing death authentically.

Can theology adopt this formal analysis of *Being-guilty* to illuminate the theological reality of human sinfulness before God (*coram Deo*)? 

Rudolf Bultmann famously believed it could, utilizing Heidegger’s categories to demythologize the New Testament and interpret sin not as an inherited metaphysical substance or a juridical infraction, but as *Dasein’s* self-enclosure, its desperate attempt to secure its own being through works rather than grace.

However, Andersen’s critique exposes the profound fault lines in this translation. 

1. **The Ontological vs. The Relational:** Heidegger’s *Schuldigsein* is structurally immanent. *Dasein* is guilty *to itself* (or before the nullity of its own being). In contrast, theological guilt (*reatus*) is irreducibly relational and personal. It is guilt *before God* (*coram Deo*). It involves a personal breach of a covenant, a rebellion against a personal Creator, and a violation of a divine holiness that demands reconciliation. 
2. **The Source of Nullity:** For Heidegger, the nullity at the heart of *Dasein* is tragic and fatalistic—an inescapable condition of finitude. For Christian theology, human brokenness is not merely a structural feature of creaturely finitude (since creation was declared "good"), but the result of historical, willful alienation from God. To reduce sin to ontological "nullity" is to erase the ethical and personal gravity of disobedience, transforming sin from a moral catastrophe into an unavoidable structural predicament of being human.
3. **The Resolution of Guilt:** In Heidegger, guilt is resolutely faced through authentic anticipation of death and resolute self-projection. *Dasein* redeems itself, so to speak, through its own resoluteness. In Christian theology, human guilt can never be resolved by *Dasein’s* own resoluteness or authentic self-understanding; it requires an alien righteousness (*justitia aliena*)—atonement wrought by God in Jesus Christ. 

If theology adopts Heidegger’s concept of *Schuldigsein*, it risks replacing the drama of divine forgiveness with an immanent program of authentic self-acceptance. 

Andersen argues that this is precisely the theological cost of Heidegger’s atheism: by defining guilt as a formal structure of finitude, Heidegger evacuates the concept of its theological content, leaving theology with a category that fundamentally contradicts the gospel’s message of unmerited grace.

## IV. The Word, Language, and the Limits of Thrown Projection

A parallel tension emerges when we examine the nature of language and the "Word" (*das Wort*). In later Heidegger, language is famously described as the "house of Being" (*Haus des Seins*). Language is not merely a tool used by human subjects to communicate; rather, language is the clearing in which Being discloses itself to *Dasein*. 

In his earlier work, *Dasein’s* capacity to articulate its world (*Rede*) is tied to its thrown projection.

For Christian theology, however, the Word is not merely the structural clearing of human existence; it is the living, creative, and incarnate Logos. The Word of God (*Verbum Dei*) is an external, authoritative address that calls *Dasein* out of its old existence into a radically new one. 

The theological Word creates what it signifies (*efficax*) and stands in judgment over all human self-understanding.

Here, Andersen’s critique touches upon the profound inadequacy of an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis of *Dasein* to account for the creative existence of the Word. If *Dasein* is always already engaged in thrown projection, and if language is the articulation of *Dasein’s* pre-understandings of Being, where does the radically "other" word of revelation enter?

If the theological Word is interpreted merely as an extreme intensification of *Dasein’s* authentic self-understanding—a moment where *Dasein* hears the call of conscience (*Ruf des Gewissens*) coming from "itself and yet from beyond itself"—then revelation is effectively swallowed by immanence. 

Heidegger explicitly notes in *Being and Time* that the voice of conscience is the voice of *Dasein* calling to itself across the abyss of its own uncanniness (*Unheimlichkeit*). The caller is *Dasein*, and the one called is *Dasein*. 

To translate the living Word of God into the auto-projective voice of the *Dasein’s* conscience is, from a theological perspective, a catastrophic reduction. It turns the living God into a psychological projection of the human need for authenticity. 

Andersen argues that an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis cannot account for the creative existence of the Word because it lacks the ontological categories to express *reception from the wholly other*. 

An ontology grounded in *Dasein’s* self-projection is structurally incapable of accommodating a truly transcendent initiative. Even when Heidegger turns in his later thought to the "event of appropriation" (*Ereignis*), wherein Being itself "sends" epochs of truth, this sending remains an anonymous, impersonal destiny (*Geschick*), entirely devoid of the personal grace, love, and covenantal fidelity that define the biblical God. 

Thus, theology’s attempt to use Heideggerian ontology to understand the Word results in the muting of the Word’s prophetic and saving voice.

## V. Can Theology Achieve Conceptual Understanding Through Philosophy While Remaining Within Faith?

Having traced the depths of Andersen’s critique, we must confront the ultimate methodological and theological question: Is Heidegger’s project entirely fatal to theology, or is there a legitimate, chastened way for theology to utilize philosophical ontology without betraying its own faith?

To resolve this, we must examine the dual nature of theology’s need for conceptual articulation. 

Christianity is a historical, confessional religion rooted in events (the incarnation, crucifixion, and resurrection of Jesus Christ) and mediated through texts and traditions. To speak intelligibly about these events in a cultural and intellectual milieu, theology *must* employ concepts. It cannot rely solely on raw pious expression; it must think conceptually (*fides quaerens intellectum*—faith seeking understanding).

If theology turns away from philosophy entirely, it risks sinking into an uncritical, fideistic ghetto, speaking a private language that is entirely incomprehensible to contemporary rational discourse. Historically, theology has always found its conceptual tools in the philosophy of its day—from Platonism in the early church fathers to Aristotelianism in medieval scholasticism, and Kantianism or Idealism in the nineteenth century. Why should Heideggerian existentialism be treated any differently?

The defenders of a Heideggerian-informed theology (such as Bultmann, or later theologians like Werner Schüssler and certain postmodern theologians) would argue that Andersen and Heidegger himself draw too rigid a boundary between the "atheism" of philosophy and the "faith" of theology. They contend that while philosophy as an immanent inquiry does not *confess* faith, its formal-indicative method does not actively *prejudice* against faith. 

Instead, philosophy clears the ground, exposing the formal structures of human finitude, anxiety, and temporality. It is up to the theologian to take these empty structural vessels and fill them with the concrete, historical reality of revelation.

However, Andersen’s critique serves as a vital warning against the naive assimilation of Heideggerian categories. The danger is not that philosophy is used; the danger is that philosophy smuggles in an alien *metaphysics of subjectivity* disguised as a neutral ontology. 

*Being and Time* is deeply indebted to a tradition of modern subjectivity, even as it seeks to overcome Cartesianism. Its focus on resoluteness, authenticity, and projection can easily be baptized into a secular, self-salvific humanism where *Dasein* acts as its own redeemer.

Therefore, if theology is to utilize Heideggerian philosophy, it must do so *critically* and *subversively*. It must practice what we might call a theological subversion of the ontological analytic. 

Rather than accepting Heidegger’s categories as neutral, theology must recognize that when Heidegger speaks of *thrownness*, theology must re-describe that thrownness as creation *ex nihilo* and fallenness. When Heidegger speaks of *guilt* as an immanent structural nullity, theology must insist that this nullity is precisely the place where the *alien* grace of God breaks in. When Heidegger speaks of resoluteness (*Entschlossenheit*), theology must transform it into the obedience of faith (*oboedientia fidei*), where *Dasein’s* self-projection is broken and surrendered to the cross of Christ.

In this sense, theology can achieve conceptual understanding through philosophy *only* if it treats philosophy not as a master whose dictates must be obeyed, but as an exhausted interlocutor whose formal structures must be radically transfigured by the reality of revelation. 

Theology remains within faith not by sealing itself off from philosophy, but by constantly interrogating and converting philosophical categories so that they bear witness to the God who transcends all human ontological systems.

---

## Conclusion

Nathan Andersen’s critique of Martin Heidegger in *Dein Theos* exposes the profound risks inherent in Heidegger’s methodological demand that theology ground its conceptual understanding in a pre-theological, non-faithful ontology. 

By insisting that philosophy is essentially atheistic and that its existential analytic of *Dasein* must serve as the formal foundation for theology, Heidegger creates a conceptual framework that threatens to evacuate theology of its vital substance. 

As we have seen in the analysis of *Being-guilty* and the nature of the Word, an explicitly non-faithful ontology of thrown projection struggles mightily to account for human guilt *coram Deo* and the creative, transcendent initiative of the divine Word. It tends to reduce theological realities to immanent structures of human self-understanding, replacing the drama of grace and sin with the stoic heroism of authentic resoluteness.

Yet, while Andersen’s critique successfully unmasks the hidden theological dangers of Heidegger’s existential analytic, it does not entirely invalidate the theological imperative to seek conceptual clarity through philosophical tools. 

Theology cannot retreat into unthinking fideism; it must think its faith in the context of human existence. The resolution to the tension lies not in subordinating theology to Heideggerian ontology, nor in rejecting philosophy altogether, but in a dialectical engagement where theological faith subverts, corrects, and redeems philosophical categories. 

Ultimately, theology achieves its true conceptual understanding not by accepting the atheistic starting point of philosophy, but by demonstrating that the living God of faith is the very horizon beyond all human projection—the ultimate source who both shatters and heals the structure of *Dasein*.
