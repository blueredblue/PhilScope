# Compromise Naturalism and the Indispensability Argument

## Introduction

The Quine–Putnam indispensability argument is one of the most influential attempts to defend realism about mathematics. In its familiar form, the argument begins with two claims. First, our best scientific theories are confirmed as wholes rather than sentence by sentence: empirical evidence bears on the total theoretical framework in which observation, law, and mathematics are embedded. Second, we ought to believe in the entities that our best scientific theories quantify over, provided that those entities are indispensable to the theories’ explanatory and predictive success. Since contemporary science quantifies over numbers, functions, sets, spaces, and other mathematical entities, the argument concludes that we have reason to accept those entities as real.

Penelope Maddy’s criticisms challenge both the description of scientific practice that underlies this argument and the picture of mathematical practice that is supposed to reinforce it. Scientists, she argues, do not ordinarily treat mathematics as an autonomous body of entities whose existence is confirmed in the same way as the existence of electrons or genes. Mathematical practitioners, moreover, do not generally determine their commitments by asking which objects occur in our best physical theories. They pursue mathematics according to internal standards involving fruitfulness, elegance, explanatory power, unification, and the development of mathematical structures. The indispensability argument may therefore appear to impose a philosophical ontology that neither science nor mathematics itself recognizes.

A promising response is what may be called *compromise naturalism*. This position retains the naturalist insistence that philosophical theories must be answerable to actual scientific and mathematical practice, while rejecting the assumption that realism and ontological commitment must be identified. On this view, one can be realist about mathematical truth, objectivity, and determinacy without claiming that mathematical entities possess ontological rights in exactly the same sense as physical entities. The indispensability argument then becomes less a direct inference from scientific quantification to a full-blooded ontology of mathematical objects than a defense of the legitimacy, objectivity, and non-eliminability of mathematical discourse within our total best theory.

This essay evaluates whether that compromise is viable. It argues that separating realism from ontological commitment significantly reduces the apparent conflict between confirmation holism and naturalism. It also argues, however, that the separation cannot be entirely cost-free. If “realism” is weakened into mere stability of mathematical language or the existence of determinate truth-values, it may fail to explain why mathematical indispensability has philosophical significance. Alternative forms of realism—structuralism, modal realism, and practice-based realism—can preserve determinate mathematical truth without granting mathematical entities the same ontological status as physical objects, but each must explain what makes mathematical truth more than a useful projection of scientific or linguistic practice. Finally, indispensability theory can legitimately intervene normatively in scientific and mathematical methodology, but only if it presents itself as a reflective reconstruction of practice rather than as an external tribunal that overrides practitioners’ judgments.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore moderate. Compromise naturalism does not vindicate the strongest Quinean thesis that mathematical entities are simply on a par with physical entities in our ontology. It does, however, preserve a substantial indispensability argument: the mathematical apparatus integrated into mature science deserves a realist treatment, at least with respect to objectivity, truth, and theoretical commitment, even if its ontological status is structurally or functionally rather than object-like.

## I. The Original Indispensability Argument

The indispensability argument is usually associated with two theses drawn from Quine. The first is confirmation holism. Scientific statements are not tested in isolation. A prediction depends upon a network of laws, auxiliary assumptions, measurement principles, and mathematical principles. When a prediction succeeds, confirmation accrues to the network as a whole. When it fails, there is no uniquely determined statement that must be rejected. We may revise a physical law, an auxiliary hypothesis, an initial condition, a measurement assumption, or even a mathematical idealization.

The second thesis is ontological commitment. Quine’s criterion is often summarized by the slogan that “to be is to be the value of a bound variable.” Once a theory is regimented into a logically perspicuous form, the objects over which its quantified variables range are among the entities to which the theory is committed. If a scientific theory says that there is a function satisfying a certain equation, or that there are real numbers corresponding to magnitudes, then the theory appears to quantify over mathematical entities.

Putnam’s contribution is to emphasize that mathematics is not merely a convenient language that can be replaced without loss. Mathematical statements often enter into the empirical consequences of scientific theories. If a mathematical theorem is required for a physical prediction, and if the relevant physical theory is confirmed, then—given confirmation holism—the mathematics participates in the confirmation of the theory. The argument is not that mathematics is confirmed by isolated experiments. Rather, mathematics is confirmed indirectly through its role in a network whose empirical consequences succeed.

In its strongest form, the argument has the following structure:

1. We should believe the claims of our best scientific theories.
2. We should believe the entities over which those theories quantify.
3. Mathematical entities are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
4. Therefore, we should believe in mathematical entities.

This reasoning has considerable force against simple forms of nominalism. It is not enough to say that mathematics is merely a shorthand if eliminating it would require substantial reformulation, loss of explanatory unity, or the abandonment of well-established inferential practices. Nor is it enough to point out that mathematical entities are causally inert. The indispensability argument does not claim that numbers cause physical events. It claims that they are indispensable components of our best explanations and predictions, and that explanatory indispensability is sufficient for ontological commitment.

Maddy’s criticisms target the move from these observations to realism. Scientific practice may be holist in the weak sense that scientific theories are assessed as packages, but this does not entail that every component of the package receives the same degree or kind of confirmation. Scientists frequently distinguish between an empirical law, a mathematical representation, an idealization, and a calculational device. A mathematical formalism can be indispensable to a theory’s formulation while being treated as representational rather than ontologically descriptive. Differential equations, Hilbert spaces, probability spaces, and symmetry groups may be used because they organize physical information effectively, not because scientists believe that all the entities appearing in the formalism exist independently.

Maddy also draws attention to the asymmetry between scientific and mathematical methodology. Mathematics is not ordinarily advanced by asking which entities are indispensable to physics. Mathematical theories can be pursued before, after, or independently of their applications. Their acceptance is guided by mathematical evidence and mathematical virtues, not simply by their contribution to empirical science. A philosophical account that tells mathematicians they are committed to whatever objects happen to occur in physics risks misunderstanding mathematics from the inside.

These criticisms do not immediately refute indispensability theory, but they show that the argument requires more than the bare facts of quantification and utility. It must explain what kind of confirmation mathematical components receive, what kind of commitment this confirmation supports, and how a philosophical reconstruction may properly relate to actual scientific and mathematical practice.

## II. The Alleged Conflict Between Confirmation Holism and Naturalism

The central tension can be stated as follows. Confirmation holism appears to support the indispensability argument by treating the scientific framework as a unified whole. Naturalism, however, appears to demand deference to scientific practice, and scientific practice often refuses to infer mathematical ontology from the use of mathematics. If scientists use mathematics instrumentally or structurally, then a philosopher who extracts an ontology of numbers and sets from the same theories seems to be overriding rather than respecting science.

This conflict arises only if two assumptions are made. The first is that naturalism requires philosophy simply to reproduce the explicit ontological pronouncements of scientists. The second is that confirmation can support only one undifferentiated kind of realism. Both assumptions are questionable.

Naturalism need not mean that philosophy is a passive report of scientific self-understanding. Scientists may successfully use concepts without having settled their philosophical interpretation. Physics once employed space and time in ways that generated philosophical disputes not resolved by physicists’ ordinary methodological statements. Similarly, scientists can treat mathematical structures as representational tools without thereby demonstrating that no realist interpretation of those structures is available. Naturalism requires philosophical respect for science’s successful methods and judgments; it does not require that philosophy refrain from asking what commitments those methods imply when reconstructed systematically.

At the same time, confirmation holism need not entail that every sentence in a confirmed theory is confirmed in precisely the same way. A theory may receive empirical support as a whole while its components occupy different functional positions. Some components state generalizations about physical systems; others provide mathematical structure; others specify idealizations or boundary conditions. Holism undermines the simple distinction between empirically meaningful physical claims and purely conventional mathematical claims, but it does not erase all distinctions among theoretical roles.

This point permits a more differentiated account of confirmation. Mathematics may be confirmed *as an indispensable framework* without being confirmed in the same manner as an empirical hypothesis. Its confirmation may consist in the continued success of a network of inferences that depends upon its structure, its capacity to support counterfactual reasoning, and its contribution to explanation and unification. Such confirmation is neither direct observation of mathematical objects nor mere pragmatic convenience. It is a form of theoretical confirmation appropriate to components whose role is structural and inferential.

Compromise naturalism thus rejects the inference:

> If scientific practice does not explicitly treat mathematical entities as physical objects, then mathematical realism is incompatible with naturalism.

Instead, it proposes:

> Scientific practice determines which mathematical structures earn a place in our best theories, while philosophical reflection determines what kind of realism is appropriate to that place.

The distinction is important. Naturalism constrains the *extension* of our commitments: we should not posit mathematical entities that have no role in any serious theory merely because an abstract ontology is metaphysically attractive. But it need not dictate the *mode* of commitment. The existence of a mathematical entity may be understood as the objectivity of a structure, the validity of a position in a theory, or the determinacy of a mathematical role, rather than as the existence of a causally active object in physical space-time.

The compromise therefore preserves a Quinean insight while revising its ontology. The insight is that mathematics cannot be dismissed as external decoration when it is deeply integrated into confirmed science. The revision is that integration does not automatically establish a uniform, object-based ontology for every mathematical entity quantified over by scientific theories.

## III. Separating Realism from Ontological Commitment

The philosophical usefulness of compromise naturalism depends on separating several notions that are often run together under the label “realism.” At least four should be distinguished.

First, there is *truth realism*: mathematical statements have truth-values that do not depend merely on an individual’s current decision or preference. Second, there is *objectivity*: mathematical correctness is constrained by reasons, proofs, structures, and inferential relations that are not arbitrarily invented case by case. Third, there is *semantic realism*: mathematical language genuinely refers, or purports to refer, to something. Fourth, there is *ontological realism*: the entities referred to by mathematical language exist as part of the furniture of reality.

The Quine–Putnam argument is traditionally read as supporting all four. Compromise naturalism argues that the indispensability of mathematics most directly supports the first two, provides significant support for the third, and supports the fourth only in a qualified form.

This distinction helps with the problem of causation. Mathematical entities need not cause physical events in order to be real in the relevant sense. Physical theories can be objectively true partly because their mathematical structures correctly characterize relations among physical magnitudes. The number two does not push a body, but the claim that a system has two degrees of freedom can be true or false. Mathematical entities may therefore be understood as constituents of objective structures rather than as independent causal agents.

It also helps with the problem of ontological parity. Quine’s criterion tends to suggest that anything quantified over by a regimented theory belongs to a single domain of existence. But quantification alone may underdetermine the metaphysical category of what is quantified over. “There is a function satisfying…” and “there is an electron with…” have formally similar logical shape while differing in explanatory role, evidential basis, and relation to physical reality. The formal criterion remains useful for identifying what a theory says exists according to its own commitments, but it does not by itself establish that all such commitments have the same metaphysical character.

A compromise position may therefore say that indispensable mathematical entities have *theoretical reality* or *structural reality*. They are not dispensable fictions, because the structures they instantiate are required for the articulation and validation of successful theories. Yet they need not be independent objects in a domain whose members exist exactly as physical things do. Their reality consists in the fact that certain mathematical structures are objectively required by, and constrain, our best accounts of the world.

This position faces an obvious challenge. If mathematical reality is reduced to theoretical role, then what prevents it from collapsing into instrumentalism? A merely instrumental entity is introduced because it is useful, with no commitment to the truth of claims involving it. But compromise naturalism can distinguish role-based realism from instrumentalism. The distinction lies in whether the mathematical structure is treated as replaceable shorthand or as a constraint on correct representation. If alternative formalisms are genuinely equivalent, this does not show that mathematics is fictional. It may show that the same objective structure admits multiple descriptions. If a mathematical assumption is indispensable to deriving a confirmed prediction, its legitimacy is not exhausted by convenience; it is part of the inferential conditions under which the theory succeeds.

The cost of this view is that it no longer gives a simple answer to the question “What mathematical objects exist?” It offers instead a hierarchy of commitment. Some entities may be indispensable across many theories and embedded in broad structural practices; others may be local devices with weak or merely provisional standing. This graded conception is less elegant than Quinean ontological uniformity, but it better reflects actual scientific reasoning.

## IV. Can Alternative Realisms Preserve Determinate Truth?

The most difficult issue is whether realism without full ontological commitment can sustain determinate truth-values. If numbers, sets, and functions lack robust ontological rights, what makes “7 is prime” true rather than merely accepted? What makes the Continuum Hypothesis determinate, if the relevant sets do not exist independently of our theoretical practices? The answer cannot simply be that a statement is true whenever it is useful, since usefulness varies and does not guarantee truth.

Several alternative realisms attempt to answer this challenge.

### 1. Structuralism

Structuralism holds that mathematics concerns structures and positions within structures rather than intrinsically characterized objects. The number 2 is not a mysterious object with a special inner nature; it is the second position in the natural-number structure. What matters is the relations that define the structure and the role occupied by each element.

Structuralism fits compromise naturalism well. Science often requires mathematical structures without requiring that the objects occupying their positions be independent physical substances. A physical system may instantiate a real-number structure or a vector-space structure without the numbers themselves being causal objects. Mathematical truth is then determinate because it concerns what follows from the structure and its axioms, not because individual mathematical objects possess physical locations or causal powers.

The difficulty is that structuralism must explain which structures are real and how structures are individuated. If structures exist only when instantiated in physical systems, then pure mathematics outruns science and the view becomes too restrictive. If all mathematically coherent structures exist, the structuralist may seem to have reintroduced a vast ontology under another name. If structures are merely patterns in our descriptions, the view risks conventionalism.

A moderate structuralism can avoid these extremes by distinguishing *mathematical determinacy* from *physical instantiation*. A structure can have determinate internal consequences even when it is not instantiated physically. The truth of a theorem depends on the relations fixed by the relevant structure, while the question of whether that structure is physically instantiated is separate. This gives mathematics an objectivity that is not reducible to current scientific use, while allowing science to determine which structures receive empirical importance.

### 2. Modal or If-Thenism

A second approach interprets mathematical claims as claims about what would be the case if certain structures were instantiated. On this view, “There are infinitely many primes” means, roughly, that any system satisfying the relevant structural conditions would exhibit the corresponding result. Mathematical truth is modal rather than object-based.

This interpretation is attractive to naturalists because it avoids commitment to abstract objects. It also explains why mathematics can be indispensable to science: science may need to reason about the consequences of possible structures, even if those structures are not themselves physical entities. Mathematical statements retain determinate truth-values because they express necessary relations among stipulated or possible structures.

However, the modal account requires an account of possibility. If mathematical possibility is defined in terms of consistency, the view may become circular, since consistency itself is a mathematical notion. If it is defined in terms of physical possibility, it cannot accommodate large portions of pure mathematics that have no apparent physical application. If it is grounded in logical possibility, the nominalist must explain why logical possibility supports the rich structure of mathematics without silently reintroducing abstract entities.

The modal strategy therefore preserves determinacy only by relocating the metaphysical burden. It may still be viable, but its success depends on a substantial theory of structure and possibility.

### 3. Practice-Based or Conceptual Realism

A third approach gives greater weight to mathematical practice. Mathematical truth is determined by the norms governing proof, definition, construction, and structural comparison within a mature mathematical community. These norms are not arbitrary conventions, because they are constrained by inferential coherence, fruitfulness, generality, and the possibility of correction. On this picture, mathematical objectivity is analogous to objectivity in logic or in some parts of linguistics: it is constituted by public, revisable standards rather than by access to a separate realm.

This approach directly responds to Maddy’s insistence that mathematics has its own methods. It does not make mathematical truth depend on physical science. It allows mathematical practice to generate legitimate standards of acceptance and rejection independently of empirical application. It also explains how truth-values can be determinate even when ontological commitment is contested: a statement can be correct relative to a stable and elaborated mathematical structure of rules and inferential relations.

Yet practice-based realism risks relativism. Different mathematical practices may employ different foundational principles, and some disputed propositions may remain undecided within a given framework. If truth is simply whatever a practice ultimately endorses, then the distinction between truth and idealized consensus becomes unclear. The view must therefore say that practices discover constraints rather than create them at will. Proof and construction reveal consequences that practitioners cannot freely alter, even though the choice of foundational framework may remain open.

### 4. Pluralist Realism

A fourth alternative is pluralist. It accepts that mathematics may have several legitimate modes of existence or truth, depending on context. Numbers may be understood structurally in arithmetic, sets may function as foundational codings, and categories or spaces may be treated as real organizational forms in geometry and physics. No single ontology must underlie every mathematical practice.

Pluralism fits the diversity of mathematics and avoids forcing all mathematical entities into a single category. It also aligns with scientific practice, where different formalisms may be equally legitimate because they preserve the same structure or empirical consequences. But pluralism must avoid becoming a license to accept anything that is useful. It needs standards for distinguishing genuine structural or inferential commitment from temporary idealization.

These alternatives show that determinate truth-values do not logically require a robust ontology of abstract objects. But they also reveal that “no ontological rights” can mean different things. If it means that mathematical entities are not physical, causal, or spatial objects, alternative realism is entirely plausible. If it means that there is no fact of the matter beyond human linguistic or practical decisions, then determinacy becomes difficult to explain. Compromise naturalism is most successful when it rejects the former but not the latter. Mathematical entities need not have physical ontological rights, yet mathematical structures and truths must be more than discretionary instruments.

## V. Maddy’s Appeal to Mathematical and Scientific Practice

Maddy’s criticisms are especially powerful because they challenge the authority of philosophical reconstruction. A philosopher may observe that mathematics is indispensable in physics and infer realism, but mathematicians themselves may not regard their work as confirmed by physics. Nor do physicists generally think that using a mathematical formalism commits them to every entity in its ontology. Why should philosophical indispensability theory override these judgments?

The answer must begin by accepting that practice is not monolithic. “Scientific practice” includes multiple activities: developing formal models, interpreting theories, designing experiments, calculating predictions, simplifying equations, and debating ontology. In some contexts scientists are instrumentalists about mathematical models; in others they treat mathematical structure as revealing something objective about the world. The use of complex numbers in quantum mechanics, the role of symmetry groups in particle physics, or the employment of differential geometry in general relativity can be described in several ways. No single practitioner’s attitude settles the philosophical issue.

Likewise, “mathematical practice” contains pure research, applied mathematics, foundational work, computation, and education. Mathematics is not governed by one criterion of acceptance. Maddy is right that mathematicians do not typically infer mathematical truth from physical indispensability. But this does not show that physical indispensability is philosophically irrelevant. It shows that the direction of justification differs: mathematics may justify its own theorems internally, while science determines which mathematical structures are empirically significant.

A division of labor is therefore possible. Mathematical practice answers questions such as: What follows from these axioms? Which structures are coherent? Which definitions are fruitful? Scientific practice answers questions such as: Which structures model the world? Which mathematical assumptions contribute to successful prediction? Indispensability theory links the two without reducing one to the other. It does not claim that a physical application proves a theorem in the way a mathematical proof does. It claims that the application bears on the legitimacy and seriousness of treating the mathematical framework as part of our total theory of reality.

This distinction also weakens a common objection concerning dispensability. It is often argued that any physical theory can, in principle, be nominalized by replacing quantification over mathematical entities with quantification over physical systems, inscriptions, or concrete patterns. If so, mathematics is not strictly indispensable. But “in principle” is too weak a standard for theoretical commitment. A nominalization may preserve formal derivability while destroying explanatory unity, obscuring modal structure, and making generalization impracticably complex. Scientific methodology routinely accepts idealized entities when eliminating them would undermine the theory’s intelligibility and predictive use. The relevant question is not whether a baroque paraphrase exists, but whether mathematics can be removed while preserving the theory’s explanatory and inferential virtues at an acceptable cost.

Still, Maddy’s point remains: indispensability cannot be defined solely by philosophers’ preferred standards. If scientists consistently regard a mathematical apparatus as dispensable, or if alternative formalisms perform the same work without the alleged ontological commitments, the argument for realism is weakened. Compromise naturalism should therefore treat indispensability as multidimensional. There is logical indispensability, computational indispensability, explanatory indispensability, representational indispensability, and methodological indispensability. They need not yield equal ontological weight.

## VI. The Normative Role of Indispensability Theory

A further objection is that indispensability theory improperly overrides established methodology. Scientists may use mathematics without believing in mathematical objects; mathematicians may accept axioms for internal reasons unrelated to physics. If a philosopher insists that practice is irrational unless it accepts mathematical ontology, the philosopher seems to be legislating from outside.

This objection assumes that naturalism is purely descriptive. But any serious naturalist philosophy must have a normative dimension. Science itself is not merely a list of practices; it includes standards of evidence, coherence, simplicity, explanatory power, and theoretical commitment. Philosophers may legitimately ask whether practitioners’ explicit beliefs are consistent with the inferential norms implicit in what they do. The fact that scientists do not verbally endorse an ontology of mathematical entities does not settle whether their theories function as if those entities are part of the world described.

Normative intervention is legitimate when it is immanent rather than coercive. An immanent critique begins with successful practice and asks what commitments are required to explain its success. It does not declare practice mistaken because it fails to conform to a prior metaphysical doctrine. The indispensability theorist can say: science has reason to accept its best theories; those theories rely on mathematics in ways that are not merely abbreviatory; therefore, science has reason to accept some realist interpretation of the mathematics it employs. This is a reconstruction of scientific rationality, not an external command to scientists.

The same applies to mathematical methodology. Indispensability theory should not tell mathematicians which axioms to adopt or which areas of mathematics are legitimate. It may, however, challenge a strict separation between mathematical truth and all forms of worldly constraint. If a mathematical structure repeatedly proves essential to the best explanations of physical phenomena, that fact is philosophically relevant even if it does not determine the structure’s pure mathematical status. Conversely, if mathematical practice develops rich theories with no scientific application, this does not refute realism, but it limits what physical indispensability can establish.

The normative force of indispensability theory is therefore conditional. It is strongest against a view that treats mathematics as a dispensable fiction while relying on mathematical reasoning in every serious scientific explanation. It is weaker against a sophisticated nominalism that offers a workable replacement or against structural realism that accepts the mathematics but denies object-like ontology. The argument should not claim that all mathematical objects are equally real simply because some mathematics is indispensable. Nor should it claim that practitioners are conceptually confused for failing to articulate the argument themselves.

There is also a positive reason for philosophical intervention. Scientific methodology often operates with several partially incompatible attitudes: instrumentalism toward some models, realism toward others, agnosticism about theoretical entities, and pragmatism about formal devices. Philosophy can help clarify whether these attitudes are stable when considered together. If a mathematical structure is used not merely to calculate but to support counterfactuals, identify symmetries, formulate laws, and explain why phenomena occur, then treating it as a disposable fiction may generate an asymmetry requiring justification. The philosopher’s task is to expose that asymmetry and evaluate it, not to dictate the result in advance.

## VII. The Limits of Compromise Naturalism

Despite its advantages, compromise naturalism faces several serious limitations.

First, separating realism from ontological commitment may make the indispensability argument too weak. If the conclusion is merely that mathematics is objectively useful or that mathematical statements have determinate truth-values, a nominalist may accept it without conceding anything significant. The original argument sought to establish existence, not merely objectivity. A compromise version must explain why theoretical or structural reality counts as a genuine form of existence rather than as a rhetorical substitute.

Second, the position may be unstable between nominalism and Platonism. If structures are objectively real independently of our practices, then the view begins to resemble structural Platonism. If structures are constituted by practices, the view risks conventionalism. The compromise must articulate a middle ground in which mathematical structures are constrained by reasons and relations that are not reducible to individual decisions, yet do not form an entirely separate Platonic realm. Whether such a middle ground is metaphysically coherent remains contested.

Third, the notion of indispensability is theory-relative. A mathematical entity may be indispensable to one formulation but avoidable in another. Gauge-theoretic formulations, coordinate systems, and alternative geometries illustrate how the same physical content can be represented through different mathematical resources. If ontological commitment follows indispensability too quickly, it may attach reality to artifacts of representation. A more sophisticated criterion must focus on invariant structural content rather than on every entity appearing in a particular formalism.

Fourth, confirmation holism does not eliminate underdetermination. If multiple mathematical frameworks yield the same empirical predictions, empirical success may not determine which ontology is correct. The indispensability argument can support a realist attitude toward mathematical structure while leaving open which specific structural ontology should be adopted. This is not fatal, since science itself tolerates equivalent formulations, but it shows that the argument cannot by itself settle mathematical metaphysics.

Finally, mathematical practice may contain commitments that science does not need. Large parts of pure mathematics are not currently indispensable to empirical theories. If compromise naturalism grounds realism entirely in scientific indispensability, it risks an implausibly narrow view of mathematics. The better conclusion is that science supplies an important external constraint and a powerful case for realism about some structures, while mathematical practice supplies internal reasons for extending realism beyond current application. Neither practice should be reduced to the other.

## VIII. Toward a Qualified Indispensability Argument

A revised argument can preserve the core insight without making excessive claims:

1. Mature scientific theories are confirmed through integrated networks of empirical, theoretical, and mathematical assumptions.
2. Some mathematical structures are deeply integrated into those networks, not merely as convenient abbreviations but as conditions of explanation, prediction, generalization, and counterfactual reasoning.
3. The success of such theories provides epistemic support for treating the relevant mathematical structures as objective and non-fictional.
4. Objectivity and non-fictionality do not require mathematical entities to have the same causal or physical ontology as empirical objects.
5. Therefore, we have reason to accept a qualified realism about indispensable mathematics: realism about its truth, structural content, and theoretical reality, with ontological commitment understood in a differentiated rather than uniform way.

This conclusion responds to Maddy without surrendering the argument. It agrees that mathematical and scientific practice do not support a simple inference from use to Platonist ontology. It also agrees that mathematical practice possesses relative autonomy. But it denies that these points justify treating mathematics as a merely instrumental overlay. Mathematics can be indispensable in a way that is epistemically significant even when its entities are not physical objects.

The revised view also accommodates cases of mathematical surplus. A mathematical theory may be internally legitimate without being empirically indispensable. Its truth can be grounded in structural or practice-based considerations. If later science finds an application, that application increases the theory’s worldly significance but does not create its mathematical truth. This reverses the strongest reading of the indispensability argument: science does not manufacture mathematical ontology; it reveals which mathematical structures are especially important for describing reality.

Under this approach, the phrase “mathematical entities lack ontological rights” must be interpreted carefully. They lack the kind of ontological rights granted to electrons, organisms, or planets if “rights” means independent causal existence in space-time. They do not thereby lack every claim to reality. Their claim is mediated by their structural roles, inferential necessity, and objective constraints. Numbers, sets, and functions may be real as components of formal structures that successful theories cannot responsibly abandon, even if they are not objects encountered through ordinary empirical observation.

## Conclusion

Compromise naturalism offers a viable, though qualified, defense of the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument. Its central achievement is to dissolve a false dilemma. We need not choose between a full Platonist ontology of mathematical objects and a naturalism that treats mathematics as mere fiction. By distinguishing realism from ontological commitment, we can acknowledge that mathematical truth is objective, that mathematical structures constrain correct theorizing, and that indispensable mathematics contributes to the confirmation and explanatory power of science, without claiming that numbers and sets exist in exactly the same way as physical entities.

This distinction also clarifies the relationship between confirmation holism and naturalism. Holism supports the idea that mathematics participates in the evidential success of scientific theories, but it does not require that every mathematical component receive identical confirmation or identical metaphysical interpretation. Naturalism requires attentiveness to actual scientific and mathematical practice, but it need not reduce philosophy to a record of practitioners’ explicit ontological statements. Philosophy may reconstruct the commitments implicit in successful practice, provided that it does so critically and without imposing an alien methodology.

Alternative forms of realism can sustain determinate mathematical truth-values without granting mathematical entities full physical ontological rights. Structuralism explains truth through positions and relations; modal accounts explain it through necessary consequences of possible structures; practice-based realism grounds objectivity in public and revisable norms; pluralism recognizes that different mathematical contexts may support different modes of reality. None is without difficulty. Each must avoid collapsing into either Platonism or conventionalism. But their collective success shows that determinate truth does not straightforwardly entail object-like existence.

Finally, indispensability theory may intervene normatively in scientific and mathematical methodology, but its intervention must be immanent and modest. It should not tell scientists that they have secretly believed in Platonic objects, nor tell mathematicians that applications determine pure mathematical truth. It should instead ask whether the actual explanatory and inferential roles of mathematics are compatible with treating it as mere dispensable fiction. Where mathematical structures are indispensable to the best confirmed theories, a realist attitude toward those structures is rationally warranted. Where mathematics functions as an optional formalism or where equivalent formulations undermine claims of specific ontological commitment, restraint is appropriate.

The strongest conclusion, then, is not that every quantified mathematical entity enjoys the same ontological status as every physical entity. It is that indispensable mathematics has a legitimate claim to objectivity, truth, and structural reality. Compromise naturalism preserves that claim while respecting the differentiated methodologies of science and mathematics. In doing so, it transforms the indispensability argument from a blunt inference to Platonism into a more defensible argument for a layered realism: a realism sufficiently robust to resist fictionalism, yet sufficiently flexible to accommodate the lessons of mathematical and scientific practice.
