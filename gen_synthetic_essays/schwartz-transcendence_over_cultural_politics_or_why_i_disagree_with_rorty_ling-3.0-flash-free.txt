**The Inescapable Ground: Rorty, Brandom, and the Transcendental Necessity of Givenness**

The history of Western philosophy is largely a history of attempts to secure a foundation for human knowledge and being, a foundation that ultimately points toward some form of metaphysical transcendence. From Plato’s Forms to Kant’s transcendental subject, the search for an ultimate ground has structured the philosophical enterprise. Richard Rorty, in his ambitious project of neopragmatism, sought to dismantle this edifice entirely. His program proposes the replacement of ontology with cultural politics, arguing that philosophy’s traditional obsession with the nature of being is a relic of representationalism that should be abandoned in favor of solidarity, conversation, and the avoidance of cruelty. Yet, in replacing ontology with cultural politics, does Rorty successfully avoid the necessity of metaphysical transcendence? Or does his project, when rigorously examined, merely relocate transcendence from the realm of being to the realm of social practice, thereby failing to escape the fundamental question of the givenness of entities? 

To evaluate Rorty’s success, we must turn to Robert Brandom’s Hegelian-inflected reading of Martin Heidegger, specifically the categories of *Zuhandensein*, *Vorhandensein*, and *Dasein* in *Being and Time*. Brandom’s interpretive framework suggests that all epistemic and ontological authority reduces to social practice, seemingly providing the robust, non-metaphysical foundation Rorty requires. However, a closer analysis reveals a profound tension: Rorty’s rejection of a background of all possible practices is undermined by his reliance on William James’s standard of human happiness as a structural end for social action. Furthermore, Thomas Sheehan’s interpretation of Heidegger’s *Ereignis* and the *Kehre* (the turn) demonstrates that an adequate account of the givenness of entities requires a *tertium quid*—a third category—that transcends the ontological primacy of the social. Taken together, these philosophical interventions reveal that Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics is an elegant but ultimately insufficient evasion of metaphysical transcendence.

**I. Rorty’s Ontological Abolition and the Politics of Practice**

Rorty’s central philosophical maneuver is the elimination of the "mirror of nature." In *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*, he argues that epistemology and ontology are inseparable twins of representationalism—the idea that our minds or language mirror a mind-independent reality. By rejecting representationalism, Rorty severs the link between language and the world that necessitates a metaphysical foundation. Instead of asking "How do we know reality?", philosophy should ask "How do we justify our beliefs within a community of inquirers?" This shift replaces ontology with cultural politics. Truth becomes not a correspondence to reality but "what my peers let me get away with saying" (Rorty, *Pragmatism and the Mirror of Nature*). Ontology, the study of what is, is replaced by cultural politics, the study of how we should live together.

By replacing ontology with cultural politics, Rorty aims to avoid metaphysical transcendence. There is no noumenal realm, no transcendental subject, and no God; there is only the contingent, evolving conversation of humanity. Rorty explicitly rejects the notion of a "background" of all possible practices or a fixed human nature that precedes and constrains social life. For Rorty, the vocabulary we use is contingent and historical; there is no deep structure of being that dictates the limits of our linguistic and social practices. 

However, this radical anti-foundationalism immediately encounters a structural problem. If there is no background of all possible practices, what prevents cultural politics from collapsing into arbitrariness or nihilism? Rorty’s answer is to adopt William James’s standard of human happiness—specifically, the pragmatic maxim that the value of an idea or practice is measured by its "cash value" in human experience, and the ethical imperative to avoid cruelty. Rorty uses Jamesian happiness as a structural end for social action. It is the ultimate criterion by which we evaluate vocabularies and cultural practices: they are good insofar as they enlarge human solidarity and reduce suffering. 

Herein lies the first major tension in Rorty’s project. By rejecting a background of all possible practices, Rorty insists that there is no pre-social, transcendental constraint on what we can say or do. Yet, by relying on James’s standard of human happiness, he reintroduces a structural end that functions precisely as a background constraint. Human happiness is not just one contingent goal among others; for Rorty, it is the normative horizon that makes social action meaningful. If cultural politics is to be more than a chaotic plurality of incompatible vocabularies, it must be oriented toward this structural end. In doing so, Rorty smuggles metaphysical transcendence back into his framework, not as a being or a realm of Forms, but as a transcendental necessity: the necessity of human flourishing that constrains the space of possible social practices. Rorty avoids transcendence in the traditional sense, but he fails to avoid the necessity of a structural, normative ground.

**II. Brandom, Heidegger, and the Reduction of Authority to Social Practice**

Robert Brandom provides the most sophisticated attempt to bridge Rorty’s pragmatism with Heideggerian ontology, offering a framework to assess the claim that all epistemic and ontological authority reduces to social practice. In *Tales of the Mighty Dead* and *Between Saying and Doing*, Brandom reads Heidegger’s fundamental ontology through the lens of inferential role semantics. For Brandom, Heidegger’s three modes of being—*Vorhandensein*, *Zuhandensein*, and *Dasein*—are not a metaphysical hierarchy, but a phenomenological taxonomy of how entities show up within the space of reasons.

*Vorhandensein* (presence-at-hand) refers to objects as they are encountered theoretically, detachedly, and categorically—the objects of scientific inquiry and propositional knowledge. For Brandom, *Vorhandensein* is the domain of assertions and truth-claims; it is the ontological correlate of the explicit, discursive space of reasons. *Zuhandensein* (readiness-to-hand) refers to the practical, absorbed engagement with the tool-world—the hammer that we use without thinking about it, the equipment that vanishes into the background of our activity. Brandom interprets *Zuhandensein* as the domain of practical inference and social normative practices. When we engage with tools, we are participating in a social world of purposes and roles; the tool's reliability is a function of the normative expectations of the community of users. Finally, *Dasein* (being-there) is the entity for whom its own being is an issue. Brandom reads *Dasein* as the locus of social authority, the being that makes explicit the norms and inferential roles that govern both practical and theoretical engagement.

For Brandom, this reading supports the Rortian claim that all epistemic and ontological authority reduces to social practice. Because *Zuhandensein* is fundamentally social—it is the world of shared tools, purposes, and roles—and because *Dasein* is the being that articulates and enforces norms, all claims to truth or being are ultimately grounded in the social practices of a community. The "authority" of a scientific claim (*Vorhandensein*) or a practical tool (*Zuhandensein*) is not derived from a mind-independent reality, but from the normative structure of the social space of reasons. There is no "view from nowhere"; all authority is an "entitlement" conferred within the social.

However, this reduction faces a severe limitation when confronted with the brute givenness of entities. Heidegger’s *Zuhandensein* is precisely characterized by the "breakdown" of the tool-world (*Vorhandenheit* in its unreflective mode). When the hammer breaks, it ceases to be a transparent medium of practical engagement and becomes a present-at-hand object of theoretical inquiry. This breakdown reveals that the tool-world resists our social practices. The hammer breaks *as such*, not merely because we have ceased to agree on its social function. Brandom’s reduction of authority to social practice struggles to account for this ontological resistance. Social practices can dictate how we talk about a broken hammer, but they cannot dictate that the hammer broke in the first place. The brute facticity of entities—their "givenness"—exceeds the normative authority of the social. 

Thus, while Brandom’s Heideggerian reading successfully demonstrates that social practice is the locus of epistemic authority, it fails to demonstrate that ontological authority reduces entirely to social practice. The givenness of entities imposes a limit on the social that Brandom’s normative framework cannot fully absorb.

**III. The Tension of Jamesian Happiness and the Background of Practices**

The tension identified in Brandom’s reading of Heidegger illuminates the deeper structural flaw in Rorty’s project. Rorty explicitly rejects a "background of all possible practices," arguing that such a background would be a form of metaphysical essentialism that ties the meaning of our concepts to a fixed human nature or a pre-social world. Yet, Rorty simultaneously relies on William James’s standard of human happiness as the structural end for social action. 

James’s pragmatism posits that the truth of an idea is its "cash value"—its practical consequences in human experience. For Rorty, this translates into the ethical imperative that the ultimate goal of cultural politics is the maximization of human happiness and the minimization of cruelty. This standard is not just another vocabulary among others; it is the structural end that orients and evaluates all social practices. Without such an end, Rorty’s cultural politics would lack any criterion for distinguishing between a practice that fosters solidarity and one that fosters cruelty. 

This reliance on Jamesian happiness functions as a hidden background. By making happiness a structural end, Rorty effectively reintroduces a constraint on the space of possible practices. Not every possible social arrangement is permissible; only those that promote human happiness are validated. In this sense, Rorty’s rejection of a background of all possible practices is paradoxically underwritten by the acceptance of a background of all possible *values*—specifically, the value of human happiness. 

This reveals that Rorty’s avoidance of metaphysical transcendence is somewhat illusory. He has replaced the transcendence of being (the realm of Forms, God, or the noumenal) with the transcendence of a normative horizon (the imperative of happiness). The necessity of this horizon is not contingent or historical; it is structural. It functions as a transcendental condition for the possibility of cultural politics itself. If Rorty were to abandon the standard of human happiness, cultural politics would lose its normative core and dissolve into a meaningless plurality of vocabularies. Therefore, Rorty does not successfully avoid the necessity of a transcendental; he merely displaces it from the ontological to the ethical, from the world to the human.

**IV. Sheehan, *Ereignis*, and the *Kehre*: The *Tertium Quid* of Givenness**

If Rorty’s cultural politics and Brandom’s normative reductionism both fail to adequately account for the givenness of entities, we must look elsewhere for a solution. Thomas Sheehan’s interpretation of Heidegger’s later philosophy, particularly the *Kehre* (the turn) and the concept of *Ereignis*, offers a profound challenge to the idea that the social can be the ultimate ground of being.

Sheehan, in works like *Making Sense of Heidegger*, argues that the *Kehre* is not merely a shift in Heidegger’s vocabulary, but a fundamental transformation in his understanding of Being. In the earlier Heidegger of *Being and Time*, Being is understood through the lens of Dasein—the being for whom Being is an issue. Dasein is the center of the ontological circle; Being is disclosed through Dasein’s existential structures. However, in the later Heidegger, Dasein is de-prioritized. Being is no longer the being of Dasein, but the event of appropriation (*Ereignis*) through which entities are given. 

*Ereignis* is often translated as "event" or "appropriation," but Sheehan emphasizes its meaning as the event in which Being discloses itself, and entities are given to Dasein. *Ereignis* is not a subject-object relation, nor is it a social construct. It is the "there is" (*Es gibt*)—the sheer givenness of beings that precedes and enables any social practice or normative framework. For Sheehan, Heidegger’s later thought requires a *tertium quid*—a third category that is neither the ontic (the entities, the social practices) nor the ontological (the traditional metaphysical categories of substance and attribute), but the event of disclosure itself.

This *tertium quid* is the transcendental condition for the givenness of entities. It is the event in which the world shows up as the world, and entities appear as they do. Crucially, Sheehan’s reading of *Ereignis* demonstrates that this event is not generated by social practice. Social practices, for Heidegger, are always already situated within the disclosure of Being. We do not first create a social world and then impose meaning on a brute world; rather, the world is given to us through *Ereignis*, and our social practices are our responses to that givenness. 

Therefore, an adequate account of the givenness of entities requires a *tertium quid* that transcends the ontological primacy of the social. Rorty’s cultural politics and Brandom’s normative reductionism are both attempts to explain how entities show up for us in terms of social interaction and inferential roles. But they cannot explain why there are entities at all, or why they show up as the particular kind of entities they do. The brute facticity of the hammer breaking, the sheer givenness of the world, resists the social reduction. It requires *Ereignis*—the event of disclosure—to be accounted for. 

**V. Synthesis: The Inevitability of Transcendence**

The convergence of these three philosophical threads—Rorty’s cultural politics, Brandom’s normative reductionism, and Sheehan’s *Ereignis*—leads to an unavoidable conclusion: Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics does not successfully avoid the necessity of metaphysical transcendence; it merely transforms it.

First, Rorty’s reliance on William James’s standard of human happiness reveals that his anti-foundationalism is secretly underwritten by a structural end. By making human happiness the normative horizon of cultural politics, Rorty reintroduces a transcendental necessity that constrains the space of possible social practices. He avoids the transcendence of being, but he cannot avoid the transcendence of a normative telos.

Second, Brandom’s reading of Heidegger, while brilliant, demonstrates that the reduction of all epistemic and ontological authority to social practice is insufficient. The modes of *Zuhandensein* and *Vorhandensein* are not merely social constructs; they are ways in which entities are given to Dasein. The brute resistance of the tool-world—the breakdown of the hammer—reveals a givenness that exceeds the normative authority of the social. Social practices can articulate and interpret the world, but they cannot generate the world’s brute facticity.

Third, Sheehan’s interpretation of *Ereignis* and the *Kehre* provides the definitive critique of the social reduction. The givenness of entities requires a *tertium quid*—the event of appropriation that discloses Being. This event is neither social nor ontological in the traditional sense; it is the transcendental condition of possibility for both. Without *Ereignis*, there is no world for social practices to negotiate, no tools for *Zuhandensein* to engage, and no human happiness for Jamesian pragmatism to pursue.

Rorty’s project is a masterpiece of therapeutic philosophy. He successfully dissolves the sterile, dogmatic metaphysics of the Western tradition, replacing it with the living, breathing discourse of cultural politics. He rightly identifies that philosophy’s obsession with the "nature of being" has often been a source of dogmatism and violence. However, in his zeal to escape metaphysical transcendence, Rorty mistakes the *vocabulary* of transcendence for the *reality* of transcendence. The necessity of a ground—the necessity of the givenness of entities, the necessity of a structural end for social action, the necessity of the event of disclosure—cannot be wished away by a change in vocabulary. 

Philosophy cannot escape the question of the ground of being; it can only change the vocabulary in which the question is asked. Rorty replaces the vocabulary of ontology with the vocabulary of cultural politics, but the underlying structure of the question remains. The social is not the ultimate ground; it is a secondary articulation of a primary disclosure. The *tertium quid* of *Ereignis* reminds us that before we can be social, before we can be happy, before we can even be Dasein, there must be an event of givenness—an event that transcends the ontological primacy of the social. Thus, Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics is not a successful avoidance of metaphysical transcendence, but a sophisticated translation of it into the language of solidarity, a language that ultimately remains haunted by the ghost of the ungrounded given.
