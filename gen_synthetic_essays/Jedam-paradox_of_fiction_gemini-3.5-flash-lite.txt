# The Grammar of Grief: Radford, Wittgenstein, and the Metaphysics of Fictional Emotion

## Introduction

In his seminal 1975 essay "How Can We Be Moved by the Fate of Anna Karenina?", Colin Radford posed a problem that has bedeviled philosophy of art for half a century: How can we genuinely pity, fear, or mourn for characters and states of affairs that we know, quite well, do not exist? Radford’s formulation of this paradox—popularly known as the "paradox of fiction"—relies upon what appears to be a bedrock psychological and metaphysical principle: that emotional responses are parasitic upon belief. To legitimately fear something, one must believe that thing is real and capable of inflicting harm; to grieve for someone, one must believe they once lived and have now perished. Since we do not believe Anna Karenina exists, nor that she has met a tragic end on a railway platform, our weeping in the stalls of the theater or over the paperback pages is, according to Radford, logically incoherent. We are, he concludes, moved by what we know to be illusions, engaging in a species of sentimental self-deception or irrationality.

This essay argues that Radford’s paradox is not an enduring puzzle of human psychology, but rather a symptom of a profound metaphysical and linguistic malady. Specifically, Radford’s formulation rests upon an untenable metaphysical distinction: the Cartesian bifurcation between mind-dependent fictional states of affairs and mind-independent reality, coupled with a representationalist picture of emotion that treats feelings as internal mental states requiring external objects as validation. By analyzing Radford’s presuppositions through the lens of Ludwig Wittgenstein’s later philosophy—specifically his use-theory of meaning, the framework of language-games, and his anti-representationalist critique of the mind—we can dissolve the very requirement that genuine emotional responses imply belief in the existence of their objects. 

Furthermore, I will contend that adopting Wittgensteinian quietism radically reframes our emotional expressions toward fictional entities and monsters—such as the cinematic bogeyman Michael Myers—not as irrational psychological states or cognitive errors, but as rule-governed linguistic and affective practices embedded within specific socio-cultural contexts. By abandoning the craving for a foundational metaphysical bridge between mind and fiction, we relieve the philosophical intellect of a self-imposed cramp, allowing us to see that weeping for Anna Karenina or trembling at Michael Myers is not a logical mistake, but a masterly demonstration of what it means to participate in the manifold language-games of human life.

## Part I: The Anatomy of Radford’s Paradox and Its Metaphysical Presuppositions

To evaluate Radford’s claim, we must first map its architecture. The paradox of fiction is generated by the conjunction of three seemingly plausible, yet mutually incompatible, propositions:

1. **The Cognitive Antecedent Condition:** Emotional responses (such as fear, pity, and grief) require the belief that their objects exist and possess the properties attributed to them (e.g., to fear a tiger, one must believe a tiger exists and is dangerous).
2. **The Non-Existence Condition:** We know that fictional characters (Anna Karenina, Madame Bovary) and fictional monsters (Michael Myers, Dracula) do not exist; we know that the scenarios depicted in novels and films are invented, imaginary, and non-actual.
3. **The Genuine Emotion Condition:** We genuinely experience authentic emotions—crying, accelerated heart rates, existential dread, deep sorrow—when engaging with works of fiction.

When taken together, these three propositions form an inconsistent triad. If (1) and (2) are true, then (3) must be false: our so-called "emotions" toward fiction cannot be genuine emotions, but must be something else—perhaps quasi-emotions, simulations, or mere physical sensations. Conversely, if (2) and (3) are true, then (1) must be false: genuine emotions do not require beliefs about the actual existence of their objects. 

Radford opts to bite the bullet on the inconsistency by asserting that our emotional responses to fiction are, strictly speaking, incoherent and irrational. While we *do* feel things, these feelings are logically anomalous because they violate the cognitive constraints of the emotions themselves. We are, in Radford’s words, "irrational" because we weep for someone we know is merely a collection of ink marks on paper or light projections on a screen.

What is immediately striking about Radford’s formulation—and what forms the primary target of this critique—is its reliance on a deeply entrenched metaphysical distinction: the division between *mind-independent reality* (the realm of actual, physical entities, historical facts, and causal relations) and *mind-dependent fictional states of affairs* (the realm of imagination, artifice, and make-believe). Within this Cartesian-empiricist framework, reality is primary, robust, and populated by objects that can exert physical force upon us. Fiction, by contrast, is derivative, parasitic, and ultimately "unreal." 

From this metaphysical vantage point, an emotion is conceived as a two-place relation between a subject (with an internal psychological state) and an object (situated in the mind-independent world). The emotion’s validity, rationality, and legitimacy are calibrated entirely by the ontological status of the object. If the object is real, the emotion is rational; if the object is unreal, the emotion is irrational or "confused."

This metaphysical scaffolding, however, smuggles in a crude representationalism. It assumes that language and thought act as mirrors or pictures of an external reality, and that emotions are internal engines fueled by cognitive inputs (beliefs) about that reality. If the input is false (e.g., "Anna Karenina exists and is suffering"), the emotional engine misfires. 

Yet, as we shall see, this entire diagnostic apparatus is built upon a profound misunderstanding of how language, thought, and emotion actually function. Radford’s paradox is not a discovery about the psychological quirks of human nature; it is a conceptual muddle generated by applying the empirical grammar of physical investigation to the aesthetic grammar of artistic appreciation.

## Part II: Wittgenstein’s Use-Theory of Meaning and the Dissolution of Metaphysical Bridges

To dismantle the metaphysical assumptions underwriting Radford’s paradox, we must turn to the later philosophy of Ludwig Wittgenstein, particularly as articulated in the *Philosophical Investigations*. Wittgenstein’s diagnostic method seeks not to solve philosophical problems by discovering hidden facts, but to *dissolve* them by showing that they arise from a misunderstanding of the logic of our language.

At the heart of Wittgenstein’s later philosophy is the **use-theory of meaning**, famously encapsulated in the dictum: "For a large class of cases—though not for all—in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (§43). Meaning is not a mysterious mental substance, nor is it a shadow cast by an abstract Platonic entity; rather, it is akin to the rules of a game. To understand a word, a concept, or an expression is to know how it is deployed within the concrete practices of human life.

When applied to the philosophy of mind and emotion, the use-theory of meaning delivers a devastating blow to the representationalist paradigm that governs Radford’s paradox. Radford asks: *How can an internal mental state (emotion) latch onto an object that lacks mind-independent existence?* This question assumes that emotions are inner, private episodes that must reach across a metaphysical chasm to touch an external object, and that this reaching is mediated exclusively by the vehicle of *belief*.

Wittgenstein invites us to abandon this picture of the mind. In the *Philosophical Investigations*, he repeatedly warns against the temptation to treat psychological concepts—such as thinking, hoping, expecting, and *feeling*—as names of occult inner processes that run parallel to our physical behavior. 

> "An 'inner process' stands in need of outward criteria." (§580)

What makes an emotion the emotion it is—whether it is fear, grief, or joy—is not the presence of a proprietary inner quale coupled with a propositional belief about mind-independent existence. Rather, it is its grammar, its public criteria, its expression, and its role in our lives. We learn what "fear" is not by introspecting upon an isolated neural firing or a private mental state, but by observing how human beings behave, speak, react, and interact in specific contexts.

Within this Wittgensteinian framework, the demand that *genuine emotional responses imply belief in the existence of their objects* is exposed as a dogmatic restriction imposed upon our language. It treats all discourse—whether scientific, historical, or aesthetic—as if it were governed by a single, monolithic language-game: the language-game of empirical description and factual verification.

If we assume that every meaningful assertion or emotional reaction must be cashed out in terms of empirical truth-conditions (e.g., "Does X exist in spatiotemporal reality?"), then weeping at *Anna Karenina* becomes a logical scandal. But Wittgenstein teaches us that language is not a monolith; it is an infinitely varied city of language-games. 

> "Here the term 'language-game' is meant to bring into prominence the fact that the speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life." (§23)

To read a novel, watch a play, or view a horror film is to enter into a distinct language-game—one with its own rules, its own internal logic, and its own criteria of appropriateness. To demand that the emotional expressions within this game submit to the external verification standards of the factual/scientific game is to commit a category mistake of the highest order. It is like complaining that the pieces in a game of chess are poorly designed because they cannot be used to drive nails into a wall. The error lies not in the chess pieces, nor in our emotional responses to fiction, but in the philosopher’s insistence on applying the wrong set of rules to the practice.

## Part III: Language-Games and the Grammar of Fictional Engagement

Let us examine more closely how the concept of the *language-game* reshapes our understanding of fictional engagement. When a reader weeps over the tragic fate of Anna Karenina, what game are they playing?

Radford’s cognitive precondition assumes that the grammar of "pity" or "grief" is uniform:
*   *Grammar A (Factual):* "I pity John because he lost his job." (Implies: John exists, the job loss is real, the believer holds this as a factual truth.)
*   *Grammar B (Fictional):* "I pity Anna because she threw herself under the train." (Radford assumes this must mirror Grammar A, leading to the absurdity: "Therefore, I believe Anna exists and threw herself under a train.")

A Wittgensteinian analysis rejects this forced symmetry. The grammar of our engagement with fiction is entirely different from our engagement with empirical reports. When we say "I am worried about what will happen to Elizabeth Bennet," we are not making a prediction about the future states of a mind-independent entity. We are participating in the unfolding world of Jane Austen’s *Pride and Prejudice*. 

To understand the meaning of "worried" in this context, we must look at *how* it is used. It is used to express our absorption in the narrative, our sympathetic alignment with the character's perspective, our aesthetic valuation of the work, and our imaginative responsiveness to the author's artistry. The criteria for the appropriateness of this worry are internal to the novel: Does the narrative structure warrant tension? Has the author successfully built a world that commands our imaginative investment? 

Crucially, this does not mean we are "pretending" to feel or having "quasi-emotions" (as Noël Carroll or Kendall Walton have sometimes argued). Walton, for example, suggests that our responses to fiction are not *real* fear or pity, but "make-believe" emotions (or "quasi-fear"). He argues that while our heart rate spikes when watching a horror movie, we do not call the police, flee the theater, or take defensive action; hence, our state is not genuine fear, but *quasi-fear*.

Wittgenstein’s philosophy allows us to bypass this awkward detour through "make-believe" ontology. Why must genuine fear be defined exclusively by the disposition to take flight or call the police? That is the grammar of *physical danger*, not the grammar of *cinematic or literary engagement*. To restrict the word "fear" only to situations where bodily self-preservation is at stake is to arbitrarily legislate the boundaries of human emotion based on a biological reductionism.

Human emotional life is vastly richer and more differentiated than a crude stimulus-response model of survival can capture. We can feel sorrow for the dead without believing they can read our letters; we can feel awe before a grand cathedral without believing it possesses consciousness; and we can feel profound terror or grief toward fictional characters precisely because we have mastered the complex, rule-governed practices of storytelling, empathy, and artistic appreciation that constitute our *form of life*.

## Part IV: Wittgensteinian Quietism and the Case of Michael Myers

To test the robustness of this Wittgensteinian dissolution, let us apply it to a domain where Radford’s paradox—and cognitivist objections generally—seem particularly acute: the horror genre, and specifically our emotional responses to slasher icons like Michael Myers in John Carpenter’s *Halloween*.

When an audience member watches *Halloween* and experiences sweaty palms, racing pulse, and a gripping sense of dread as Michael Myers stalks his victims through the shadowy halls of Haddonfield, what is happening? 

Under Radford’s framework, or under strict cognitivist models (such as those advanced by Jerrold Levinson or Robert Yanal), the spectator is caught in a psychological bind. The spectator *knows* that Michael Myers is a fictional character played by stuntmen (Nick Castle, Tony Moran, et al.) wearing a modified William Shatner mask. The spectator *knows* that no real teenagers are being murdered in a small Illinois town. Therefore, according to the cognitive condition, any feeling of *fear* directed at Michael Myers is irrational, because the spectator lacks the belief: "There is an actual, dangerous maniac in my immediate vicinity who is about to kill me."

Cognitivists often try to resolve this by arguing that we fear *for* the characters (Laurie Strode), or that we experience a generalized, objectless anxiety, or that we engage in a split-level cognitive state where part of our brain temporarily suspends disbelief.

A Wittgensteinian approach dismisses these intellectual gymnastics as symptoms of what Wittgenstein called "language idling." We are spinning wheels because we are bewitched by a picture: the picture that every emotion must have an object whose existential status guarantees the emotion’s logical legitimacy.

Let us apply **Wittgensteinian quietism** to the case of Michael Myers. Quietism is the methodological stance that philosophy should not attempt to construct foundational theories to solve conceptual puzzles, but should instead clear away the conceptual confusions that make the puzzles seem intractable in the first place. 

If we look at the actual *use* of emotional expressions in the context of watching a horror film, what do we see? We see a community of viewers sharing a collective aesthetic practice. We sit in a darkened theater (or living room), we submit to the pacing of the score, we anticipate the jump-scares, we gasp, we grip our armrests, and afterward, we talk about how terrifying the film was.

Is this behavior "irrational"? Only if we measure it against an inappropriate standard—namely, the standard of survival-driven self-preservation. But watching a horror film is not a survival situation; it is an *aesthetic language-game*. 

When someone says, "Michael Myers terrified me," they are not reporting a cognitive error or laboring under the delusion that a masked fictional serial killer is standing behind their couch. They are mastering and participating in a complex cultural practice. The utterance "Michael Myers terrified me" has a clear, well-understood use within our language: it communicates the effectiveness of the film’s direction, the potency of its atmospheric tension, the visceral impact of its visual grammar, and the shared conventions of the horror genre.

To demand a metaphysical accounting of how our mind-dependent feelings hook onto a mind-independent Michael Myers is to demand an explanation where none is required. As Wittgenstein famously wrote in the *Philosophical Investigations*:

> "The aspects of things that are most important for us are hidden because of their simplicity and familiarity. (One is unable to notice something—because it is always before one's eyes.)" (§129)

What is always before our eyes is that horror films are *made to be terrifying*. They are crafted artifacts designed to elicit precisely these physiological and affective responses. To possess the concept of a "horror film" is, among other things, to know how to respond to it affectively. To refuse to be frightened by a horror film—not out of stoic bravery, but out of a pedantic insistence that "the killer isn't real"—is not a sign of superior rationality; rather, it is a sign of *failing to understand the language-game of cinematic engagement*. Such a person is like someone who refuses to laugh at a joke because "it didn't really happen." They have missed the point of the practice entirely.

## Part V: The Critique of the "Cognitive Antecedent Condition"

Let us subject the cognitive antecedent condition (Proposition 1 of Radford's triad) to rigorous Wittgensteinian scrutiny. Why has philosophy clung for so long to the dogma that emotion requires belief in existence?

The historical root lies in Cartesian internalism and the bifurcation of mind and world. In this tradition, beliefs are internal propositional attitudes that represent states of affairs, and emotions are evaluative colorings added to those representations. If the representation is empty (i.e., refers to a fiction), the coloring is logically unmoored.

Wittgenstein shatters this picture in his investigations of rule-following, intentionality, and aspect-seeing. In *Philosophical Investigations* (Part II, section xi), Wittgenstein explores the phenomenon of *seeing-as* (duck-rabbit ambiguity, facial expressions, aesthetic perception). He notes that seeing an aspect does not involve two separate mental acts: (1) perceiving a neutral set of lines, and (2) inferring or believing that the lines represent an animal. Rather, the perception itself is unified.

Similarly, our emotional engagement with fiction is not a two-step process where we first intellectually grasp a proposition ("Anna Karenina died") and then subsequently evaluate its existential status ("Does this exist? Oh, no, it's fictional—abort emotion!"). 

When we read *Anna Karenina*, we do not read it as a historical chronicle; we read it *as a novel*. The framing context—the literary genre, the bookstore shelf, the opening page, the author's name—establishes the grammar of our engagement from the very outset. We enter the language-game of fiction with our existential guard appropriately calibrated. We do not need to *believe* Anna exists in order to feel pity for her, any more than we need to *believe* a mathematical proof has a physical body in order to feel aesthetic admiration for its elegance.

To insist that genuine pity requires the belief that the object exists is to commit what Gilbert Ryle called a *category mistake*. It assumes that all emotional discourse operates on a single, literal, empirical register. But human emotion is shot through with imagination, counterfactual thinking, aesthetic appreciation, and symbolic resonance. 

Consider how we feel when we contemplate historical atrocities that occurred centuries ago, or when we contemplate our own eventual mortality, or when we feel sorrow for humanity’s general plight. In many of these cases, the object of our emotion is either temporally distant, abstract, or generalized. Yet no one argues that our grief over the fall of the Roman Empire or our anxiety about climate change is logically incoherent because the Roman Empire is no longer extant or because the worst effects of climate change have not yet fully materialized. 

Why, then, should fictional characters—who occupy a permanent place in our cultural, imaginative, and emotional landscape—be subjected to a stricter and more artificial ontological hurdle? The distinction between historical absence and fictional non-existence is, from the perspective of affective grammar, a distinction without a difference in kind. Both require us to transcend the immediate spatiotemporal present through the power of human imagination and language.

## Part VI: Responding to Objections: Fictional Realism and Expressive Norms

A critic might object at this juncture: *Does not a Wittgensteinian dissolution dissolve too much? If we abandon the requirement that emotions require existential beliefs, do we not open the floodgates to complete irrationality? If I can genuinely fear Michael Myers or grieve for Anna Karenina without believing they exist, what prevents me from claiming to genuinely fear a pink elephant in my pocket or genuinely grieve for a triangular rock?*

This is a classic anxiety: the fear that without foundationalist metaphysical criteria, philosophy descends into a lawless relativism where "anything goes." 

A Wittgensteinian response cuts straight to the heart of this anxiety by appealing to the concept of **criteria** and **forms of life**. 

We cannot genuinely fear a pink elephant in our pocket or grieve for a triangular rock because *such responses do not make sense within our shared human form of life*. There is no established language-game, no cultural practice, no artistic tradition, no narrative art form that gives sense to grieving for a triangular rock. 

Language is not a private creation; it is a public, social institution governed by norms and rules. We learn the grammar of grief, fear, and pity alongside the grammar of storytelling, drama, myth, and art. The reason we can be moved by Anna Karenina or Michael Myers is that these figures are embedded within enormously rich, highly developed cultural practices—novels, films, theatrical traditions, critical discourses—that have evolved over centuries to cultivate and structure human emotional responsiveness.

A triangular rock possesses no such cultural embedding. To claim to grieve for it is not to play a different language-game; it is to fail to play *any* intelligible game. It is to utter a string of words that lacks sense (in Wittgenstein’s rigorous sense of *Sinn*). 

Thus, Wittgensteinian quietism does not lead to lawless chaos; rather, it anchors our emotional practices in the concrete, shared forms of life that make human communication and art possible in the first place. The boundaries of what we can meaningfully feel toward are set not by abstract metaphysical decrees about what exists in mind-independent reality, but by the living, breathing, evolving practices of human culture.

---

## Conclusion: Relieving the Philosophical Cramp

Colin Radford’s paradox of fiction arises because philosophy, in its quest for foundational rigor, too often severs language and emotion from their living contexts, trapping them inside an artificially constructed metaphysical framework. By insisting that genuine emotion requires a belief in the mind-independent existence of its object, Radford and his cognitivist successors impose a Procrustean bed upon human affective life, amputating our aesthetic and imaginative responses to fit an empiricist dogma.

When we view this problem through the therapeutic lens of Ludwig Wittgenstein, the paradox simply evaporates. We realize that:
1. Meaning is use, not a mirror of metaphysical reality.
2. Language-games are plural, and the aesthetic/fictional language-game cannot be legislated by the rules of empirical verification.
3. Emotional expressions toward fictional characters (such as Anna Karenina) and cinematic monsters (such as Michael Myers) are not irrational psychological aberrations or cognitive errors, but rule-governed practices embedded within our shared form of life.

Wittgensteinian quietism relieves the intellect of the cramp induced by false metaphysical dichotomies. We no longer need to invent elaborate cognitive machinery—such as quasi-emotions, make-believe states, or split-level beliefs—to justify why we weep over novels or tremble in the dark of a cinema. We weep because we are human, because we speak a language that allows us to inhabit imaginary worlds, and because art has the profound, unmatched power to move us precisely *because* it is art, not because it is a police report.

In the end, the grammar of our grief and the terror of our nightmares do not require the validation of an ontology of actual existence. They require only what they have always had: the shared, pulsing, beautifully intricate language-games of human culture, where the unreal is rendered deeply, powerfully, and undeniably real to the heart.
