# Vulnerability, Contemplation, and the Divine Life: Aristotle, Nussbaum, and the Possibility of a Christian Ethics

Martha Nussbaum’s interpretation of Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* is animated by a compelling philosophical concern: human goodness is exposed to fortune. Friendship, political participation, bodily health, family relations, and the capacity to act effectively in the world are not optional embellishments to a life of virtue. They are conditions under which specifically human flourishing becomes possible. Aristotle’s account of the good life, on Nussbaum’s reading, therefore resists any attempt to define happiness as a self-sufficient state insulated from luck, embodiment, or the contingencies of human relationships. Yet this interpretation appears to face a serious challenge in Book X of the *Ethics*, where Aristotle identifies contemplation with the highest and most complete form of happiness and describes it as a life that is “divine” in comparison with ordinary human activity. The question is whether Book X overturns the anthropocentric, pluralistic, and vulnerability-sensitive ethical vision developed in Books I–IX.

The issue is not merely one of textual consistency. It concerns two rival conceptions of ethical rationality. On one conception, associated with Nussbaum, practical reason must deliberate among plural goods whose value cannot be reduced to a single scale. General ethical principles are provisional summaries of good judgment rather than algorithmic rules. The human good is consequently an integrated but fragile plurality. On another conception, which Book X may seem to invite, ethical value is ordered hierarchically under one superior activity: contemplation of truth. Practical virtues remain necessary, but they are subordinate to a transcendent or quasi-transcendent end. If this second conception is correct, Nussbaum’s Aristotelianism may misconstrue the final structure of Aristotle’s ethics.

The best interpretation lies between these alternatives. Nussbaum correctly identifies a genuine and indispensable dimension of Aristotle’s moral philosophy: human flourishing is embodied, relational, politically situated, and vulnerable to fortune. She is also right that practical judgment cannot be replaced by mechanical application of universal principles. Nevertheless, her emphasis on an irreducibly plural and anthropocentric form of flourishing does not fully capture Aristotle’s final position. Book X does introduce a hierarchy within the goods of life, though not necessarily a supernatural hierarchy in the Christian sense. Contemplation is superior because it realizes the distinctive power of the intellect and approaches divine self-sufficiency. But it does not simply abolish the practical virtues or render vulnerability philosophically irrelevant. Aristotle’s ethics therefore provides a rich conceptual resource for Christian doctrine, especially in its account of rational order, virtue, and contemplation, but it cannot by itself provide a complete rational foundation for Christian theology. Christian doctrine must transform Aristotle’s hierarchy by introducing creation, grace, charity, incarnation, and the resurrection of the body.

## I. Nussbaum’s Aristotle: Human Goodness Under Conditions of Fragility

Nussbaum’s interpretation is most fully developed in *The Fragility of Goodness*. Its central claim is that Greek tragedy and Aristotle share a recognition that the best human life depends upon factors outside the agent’s control. The noble person requires friends, material resources, a functioning political community, bodily health, and opportunities for action. A person who possesses excellent character but is deprived of all relationships, reduced to extreme poverty, or subjected to devastating misfortune may retain moral worth, but cannot fully flourish. Eudaimonia is not merely an inner condition of rational harmony. It is a way of living and acting that must be realized in the world.

This interpretation is directed against ethical theories that seek invulnerability. Stoicism is an obvious target, since the Stoic sage is ideally independent of external goods. But Nussbaum’s argument also challenges a certain reading of Aristotle according to which happiness consists fundamentally in a stable state of intellectual or moral perfection and external goods are merely instrumental. Aristotle’s insistence that “no one would choose to live without friends” and that happiness requires a complete life indicates that the human good cannot be detached from the circumstances in which human beings act.

Nussbaum’s anthropocentrism does not mean that she reduces ethics to subjective preference or biological survival. Rather, she argues that goods are species-relative. The form of life appropriate to a human being is determined by human capacities, needs, and activities. The good of a dolphin, a god, and a human being need not be measured by one universal standard abstracted from their different forms of life. Human beings are rational and political animals, but they are also embodied and affective beings. Human flourishing therefore includes activities expressive of these various capacities: reasoning, choosing, loving, perceiving, participating in civic life, enjoying bodily health, and sustaining attachments.

This position has a direct consequence for the relation among goods. The values involved in human flourishing are not always commensurable. Friendship cannot simply be exchanged for political honor, nor justice for pleasure, nor bodily integrity for intellectual achievement. The goods form a complex plurality. Practical reasoning must often determine how to respond when valuable commitments conflict, but it cannot assume that every value can be represented as a quantity on a single scale. The ethically appropriate response may involve loss, compromise, or tragic remainder. Even the best decision may fail to preserve all that matters.

Nussbaum also emphasizes Aristotle’s account of ethical principles as provisional. In the opening of the *Ethics*, Aristotle warns that one should not seek the same degree of precision in ethics as in mathematics. Matters concerning action are variable, and the account must be “roughly sketched.” The practically wise person does not simply deduce particular actions from universal laws. Instead, practical wisdom perceives what the situation requires. General principles are useful summaries of experience, but they must be interpreted and sometimes qualified in light of the concrete case.

This emphasis allows Nussbaum to present Aristotle as a theorist of situated rationality. Practical wisdom is not irrational intuition, but neither is it the application of a fixed decision procedure. It is an educated capacity to recognize salient features of circumstances and to deliberate about what is good here and now. Such judgment is inseparable from the emotional formation of character. The virtuous person feels pleasure and pain appropriately, perceives the significance of another’s suffering, and responds to the world with an affective intelligence that cannot be reduced to abstract calculation.

Nussbaum’s interpretation is especially powerful because it makes sense of the broad architecture of Books I–IX. Books II–V discuss the moral virtues, justice, and equity. Books VI–VIII examine practical wisdom, pleasure, friendship, and the political conditions of human life. These topics seem to describe a substantial form of flourishing in which a person thinks, chooses, feels, acts, and relates well. Eudaimonia is not presented merely as a private mental state. It is an activity of soul in accordance with virtue, realized across a complete life. The moral virtues are not external instruments for a separate goal; they are constitutive dimensions of excellent human activity.

## II. The Challenge of Book X

Book X, however, appears to change the center of gravity. After discussing pleasure, Aristotle turns to the claim that happiness consists in contemplation. The contemplative life is said to be best, most continuous, most pleasant, most self-sufficient, and most loved by the gods. It is the highest activity because intellect is the highest element in us and the objects of intellect are the highest objects of knowledge. Aristotle concludes that “this activity would be complete happiness for a human being, if it is allowed a complete span of life.”

The language is striking. Contemplation is not merely one good among others. It seems to be the supreme good. Aristotle contrasts the contemplative life with the life of moral and political activity, which requires external goods, encounters difficulty, and is subject to fluctuation. The contemplative person is said to be most self-sufficient, because contemplation requires fewer external resources than political action. Aristotle even states that one should “live in accordance with the highest thing in us,” and that such a life is not too high for human beings, despite the common advice to think human thoughts as human beings.

This passage creates a serious problem for Nussbaum. If contemplation is complete happiness, then the plurality of practical goods appears to be subordinated to one activity. Friendship, justice, courage, and generosity may remain necessary for ordinary human life, but they do not constitute the final meaning of eudaimonia. They are secondary to the intellectual activity that most closely resembles the divine life. The practical virtues may be conditions of a decent life, while contemplation is the end that makes the life truly happy.

The problem becomes sharper when Aristotle describes the contemplative life as divine. The gods, he suggests, are happy and blessed through contemplation. Human beings become godlike insofar as they engage in this activity. The divine is not here a personal creator who commands or loves in the biblical sense. Aristotle’s unmoved mover is pure actuality and thought thinking itself, not a providential deity involved in history. Nonetheless, Book X undeniably presents contemplation as participation in a mode of activity superior to ordinary human concerns.

Does this mean that Aristotle abandons the vulnerable, embodied human good described earlier? There are several possible answers.

One answer is that Book X is inconsistent with the rest of the *Ethics*. On this view, Nussbaum’s account of Books I–IX captures the genuinely humanistic Aristotle, while the contemplative conclusion represents an unresolved metaphysical remnant. Aristotle may have wished to preserve both a practical conception of happiness and an intellectualist conception, but he failed to reconcile them.

A second answer is that Books I–IX describe only a secondary form of happiness, while Book X identifies the primary and final form. The moral virtues are valuable, but ultimately subordinate. This is the dominant “dominant end” interpretation. It gives priority to contemplation and treats practical virtue as necessary mainly because a contemplative person remains human and must live in society.

A third answer is that Aristotle’s final position is inclusive: contemplation is the highest component of happiness, but practical virtue and external goods remain genuine constituents of a complete human life. The contemplative life is superior without being exclusive. It orders the other goods rather than eliminating them.

The third interpretation is the most plausible, though it must be stated carefully. Aristotle does introduce a hierarchy, but not necessarily a simple replacement of practical happiness by detached intellectual activity.

## III. Is Aristotle’s Good Pluralistic or Hierarchical?

The tension between pluralism and hierarchy can be clarified by distinguishing two claims. The first is that there are many genuine human goods. The second is that these goods may be ordered according to their contribution to the complete human good. These claims are not contradictory. A plurality can be internally structured.

Nussbaum is right that Aristotle does not treat friendship, justice, bodily life, and intellectual activity as interchangeable. But it does not follow that they are equal in rank. Aristotle’s ethical vocabulary regularly distinguishes higher and lower activities. The virtues of the rational part of the soul are superior to those of the nonrational part, and within intellectual virtue, wisdom is superior to practical intelligence in its object. Book X extends this structure by claiming that contemplation is the highest activity of the highest capacity.

The danger in Nussbaum’s approach is that “incommensurability” can be interpreted too strongly. If it means that the goods cannot be reduced to one quantitative metric, it fits Aristotle well. If it means that there is no rational ordering among goods, it does not. Aristotle does not think practical judgment is an arbitrary choice among incomparable values. The good person acts for the sake of the noble, and the wise person understands the order of ends. The fact that justice and friendship are not exchangeable does not imply that they lack a place within a larger conception of human flourishing.

Aristotle’s account of the virtues is teleological. Courage is not valuable merely because it expresses a preference for bravery; it is good because it enables a person to respond nobly to fear in pursuit of worthwhile ends. Generosity concerns the proper use of wealth, but wealth itself is subordinate to the noble activities it supports. Political life is important because human beings realize their capacities through shared action and deliberation. Virtues therefore occupy a structured field of purposes.

The intellectual virtue of contemplation has special status because it is activity for its own sake. Moral and political action, by contrast, often aims at results outside itself: justice in the city, victory in battle, the welfare of friends, or the distribution of resources. Contemplation is more continuous, more self-sufficient, and more intrinsically pleasurable. In Aristotle’s terms, it comes closer to the activity of the divine.

Yet even here caution is needed. Contemplation is not an escape from all human conditions. Aristotle’s contemplative person must eat, possess health, and enjoy some degree of leisure. The activity of thought is not performed by a disembodied intellect floating free of practical circumstances. Moreover, Aristotle’s claim that the contemplative life is self-sufficient does not mean that the contemplative person requires no external goods whatsoever. It means that contemplation depends on fewer goods than political action and is less directly exposed to the instability of fortune.

Aristotle also does not suggest that moral virtue becomes morally irrelevant once contemplation is valued. The contemplative person must possess the moral virtues, at least to the extent necessary to live a human life. In Book X, Aristotle states that someone who is going to be a contemplative person must also live in accordance with the moral virtues, since he is a human being living among other human beings. Practical wisdom remains necessary for the organization of life. Contemplation cannot be an excuse for injustice, cowardice, or intemperance.

The final structure, then, is hierarchical but inclusive. Contemplation is the highest activity, while practical virtue and external goods belong to the complete conditions of human flourishing. Aristotle’s position is not that the moral life is worthless beside contemplation. It is that the moral life does not exhaust the highest possibility of human beings.

## IV. Does Book X Introduce a Transcendent Hierarchy?

The word “transcendent” must be used with care. Book X does introduce a hierarchy that reaches beyond ordinary practical life, but it does not yet establish the theological transcendence of the New Testament. Aristotle’s contemplative ideal is metaphysical and cosmological rather than personal and historical.

For Aristotle, the divine is pure actuality, and the highest divine activity is thought thinking itself. Human contemplation resembles this activity because it is an exercise of intellect directed toward truth. But the relation between human beings and the divine is not one of covenant, grace, or filial communion. Human beings do not receive salvation from God, nor do they enter into a personal relationship with a loving creator. The divine functions as the ultimate explanatory and normative horizon of reality, not as a redeemer acting within history.

Still, the hierarchy is real. It implies that human beings are not fulfilled merely by successful social participation or the harmonious satisfaction of embodied needs. The most excellent human life reaches toward an activity whose object and structure are superior to the ordinary concerns of practical existence. This is why Nussbaum’s anthropocentrism cannot simply be equated with Aristotle’s final view. Aristotle’s ethics is anthropological in its starting point, but not exclusively anthropocentric in its orientation. It defines the human good by reference to human capacities while also claiming that the highest human capacity is directed toward what is most divine.

The notion of species-relative goods is therefore compatible with a hierarchy. A human being has a human good, but the human good may consist partly in participation in an activity that exceeds ordinary human interests. Human beings are rational animals, and rationality is not merely a tool for securing social or bodily goods. It has a theoretical orientation toward truth for its own sake. The species-relative good of human beings includes an openness to what is universal and divine.

Nussbaum’s framework captures the importance of embodiment, but it may understate the way Aristotle distinguishes the human being as an animal from the human being as intellect. Aristotle does not simply ask what supports the flourishing of an organism with human needs. He asks what activity expresses the distinctive form of the human soul at its highest level. The answer is not exhausted by political action, friendship, or emotional responsiveness, even though these remain central to human life.

The result is not a transcendent hierarchy that negates the world. Aristotle does not condemn embodiment, politics, or friendship as obstacles to spiritual purity. Rather, he differentiates levels of fulfillment. Contemplation gives the highest orientation, while practical virtue makes a coherent human existence possible. The ethical life is thus analogous to an ordered whole rather than a contest among mutually exclusive goods.

## V. Principles, Practical Wisdom, and the Limits of Nussbaum’s Provisionalism

Nussbaum is equally persuasive in emphasizing that Aristotle’s principles are provisional summaries of practical experience. Aristotle repeatedly resists excessive precision in ethics. The doctrine of the mean is not a formula yielding an exact answer in every case. The right amount of anger, generosity, fear, or confidence varies according to the agent, the object, the circumstances, and the appropriate reason. Practical wisdom grasps these particulars.

This does not mean, however, that principles are merely tentative or revisable in the modern experimental sense. Aristotle’s practical principles express stable truths about human action, even when their application varies. Courage concerns the proper response to fear; justice concerns equality and desert; truthfulness concerns the proper representation of oneself and reality. The principle is not a complete rule, but it is more than a provisional psychological generalization. It is an account of the form of the virtue.

The tension between generality and particularity is built into Aristotle’s conception of practical reason. A law is necessarily general, whereas action concerns particulars. Equity corrects the law when its generality would produce injustice in a specific case. But equity does not destroy law. It reveals the law’s purpose more adequately. Similarly, practical wisdom does not abolish ethical principles; it realizes their meaning under contingent conditions.

Nussbaum’s emphasis on vulnerability strengthens this account by explaining why practical judgment must remain responsive to the world. The agent cannot make a good decision without attending to actual human needs and circumstances. Yet if incommensurability becomes absolute, practical reason loses its teleological structure. Aristotle’s wise person is not merely someone who manages tragic conflicts with sensitivity. He is someone who understands what is genuinely choiceworthy and orders action toward the good.

The contemplative ideal reinforces this point. Theoretical wisdom supplies a horizon of truth within which practical reasoning can be oriented. It does not provide a detailed action algorithm, but it explains why practical goods are good: they contribute to the flourishing of beings whose highest capacity is rational understanding. Practical wisdom organizes life; theoretical wisdom reveals the highest end toward which life is directed.

This division is not entirely stable in Aristotle. The relation between *sophia* and *phronesis* remains difficult. Practical wisdom concerns human things, while theoretical wisdom concerns eternal truths. The person who possesses theoretical wisdom may not thereby know how to act in political or interpersonal situations. Aristotle therefore cannot simply replace practical judgment with contemplation. The two forms of reason have different objects and functions.

The best conclusion is that principles are provisional at the level of application but not at the level of ultimate orientation. Ethical rules require interpretation because human affairs are contingent. Nevertheless, the hierarchy of goods and the conception of the human function provide a rational framework within which interpretation occurs. Nussbaum captures the first point better than the second.

## VI. Vulnerability and the Pursuit of the Divine Life

The deepest philosophical issue is the relation between vulnerability and divinity. Human beings are exposed to illness, death, betrayal, political instability, and the failure of their projects. Contemplation promises a form of activity less vulnerable to these disturbances. Does the pursuit of the divine life therefore represent a flight from the conditions of humanity?

Nussbaum’s objection would be powerful if contemplation required denying vulnerability. A life that treated dependence, grief, friendship, or bodily need as mere impediments would fail to respect the reality of human flourishing. But Aristotle does not quite make that move. He acknowledges that even the contemplative person requires a complete life and some external goods. More importantly, the contemplative ideal is an activity of a human intellect, not the existence of a god. Human beings can imitate divine activity only within human limits.

The apparent contradiction can therefore be resolved by distinguishing imitation from identity. To live “according to the highest thing in us” is not to cease being a vulnerable animal. It is to exercise the capacity that most fully expresses the form of our species. The human being is both animal and rational. The highest activity does not erase the lower dimensions; it governs and completes them.

This solution has a cost. It implies that not all elements of human life have equal ultimate significance. Love, political commitment, and bodily experience may be indispensable to a complete human life, but contemplation remains superior in rank. Nussbaum is right to resist treating vulnerability as a defect to be eliminated, but Aristotle is right, on his own premises, to maintain that dependence and contingency do not by themselves identify the highest good.

The relationship may also be described in terms of dependence and transcendence. Human beings need the world in order to act, but intellect can know truths that are not reducible to immediate practical advantage. Human flourishing involves both situatedness and openness. We are creatures dependent upon bodies, communities, and fortune, yet capable of understanding forms and principles that exceed particular circumstances. The contemplative ideal expresses this second dimension without necessarily cancelling the first.

The moral virtues remain essential because vulnerability creates obligations. Courage responds to danger, justice to claims among persons, generosity to material inequality, and friendship to the needs of fellow human beings. A purely contemplative existence could not substitute for these virtues in a political community. Aristotle’s account of the best life is therefore internally divided between the activity that is highest in rank and the activities necessary for a good human existence. This division is not a mere textual accident; it reflects a genuine ambiguity in the Aristotelian conception of the human being.

## VII. Aristotle and New Testament Theology

Does Aristotle’s hierarchy anticipate New Testament doctrines? There is a limited sense in which later Christian thinkers could find Aristotle congenial. The idea that the highest human fulfillment consists in participation in divine life, the distinction between lower and higher goods, and the claim that contemplation is directed toward eternal truth all provide conceptual resources for Christian theology. Medieval thinkers, especially Thomas Aquinas, would use Aristotelian categories to articulate the relation between natural happiness and supernatural beatitude.

But it would be historically and philosophically misleading to say that Aristotle anticipates the New Testament in any direct sense. The Christian doctrines of creation, sin, incarnation, grace, resurrection, and charity have no straightforward equivalent in Aristotle. His God is not a personal creator who brings the world into being freely, enters into covenant with a people, becomes incarnate, suffers, and redeems humanity. The Aristotelian divine is not a trinitarian communion of persons, and the contemplative life is not participation in divine life through Christ.

The contrast is particularly important regarding vulnerability. Aristotle generally treats vulnerability as a condition that limits flourishing. The New Testament, by contrast, gives suffering a positive theological significance through the crucifixion and resurrection of Christ. Weakness can become a site of divine power; poverty and dependence can be spiritually privileged; love may require self-emptying rather than self-sufficiency. Aristotle’s ideal remains connected to autonomy, continuity, and the exercise of one’s own capacities. Christian discipleship may require relinquishing status, accepting persecution, serving the least powerful, and trusting in grace.

Likewise, Aristotle’s friendship of virtue is based on mutual recognition of goodness and the sharing of life. Christian agape is not opposed to such friendship, but extends love toward enemies, strangers, sinners, and those unable to reciprocate. The Christian command to love one’s enemies cannot be derived from Aristotle’s account of friendship. Nor can the Christian hope of resurrection be derived from Aristotle’s understanding of contemplation. Aristotle’s ethics is fundamentally an ethics of flourishing within the order of nature; Christian doctrine introduces a history of salvation that transforms nature from within.

Yet the absence of Christian doctrines does not mean Aristotle is irrelevant to Christianity. A rational foundation for Christian ethics need not contain every theological article in advance. Aristotle can supply an account of virtues, practical judgment, moral formation, friendship, political responsibility, and the importance of rationally ordered desire. He can explain why embodied and social goods matter, why moral character cannot be reduced to rule-following, and why the pursuit of truth belongs to the good life. These insights can be incorporated into Christian theology, though they must be revised by revelation.

Aquinas offers the clearest example. He distinguishes imperfect or natural happiness, attainable through human virtue and contemplation, from perfect happiness, consisting in the vision of God and attainable only through divine grace. Aristotle’s contemplative ideal thus becomes a philosophical analogue of, but not an identity with, the beatific vision. Christian theology preserves Aristotle’s hierarchy while making the highest end supernatural. It also incorporates moral virtues into a broader structure that includes theological virtues—faith, hope, and charity.

This Thomistic synthesis confirms both sides of the argument. Nussbaum is right that Aristotle’s natural human good includes vulnerability, embodiment, and social relations. Book X is right that this natural good is ordered toward intellectual participation in what is higher. But the Christian end exceeds Aristotle’s natural framework. The beatific vision is not simply a more intense form of philosophical contemplation; it is a gift of God, involving a personal union that Aristotle’s metaphysics does not theorize.

## VIII. Can Aristotelian Ethics Rationally Ground Christian Doctrine?

The phrase “rational foundation” can mean different things. If it means a complete deduction of Christian theology from secular philosophical premises, Aristotle cannot provide such a foundation. No argument from human function, virtue, and contemplation entails the incarnation, the Trinity, the atonement, or resurrection. Those doctrines depend upon revelation and historical claims. Moreover, certain Christian values—universal charity, the preferential concern for the poor, and the redemptive significance of suffering—challenge aspects of Aristotle’s social and ethical hierarchy.

If, however, a rational foundation means a philosophical structure that makes Christian doctrine intelligible and ethically coherent, Aristotle can make a substantial contribution. His teleology supports the idea that human beings have an objective good rather than merely subjective preferences. His account of virtue explains how moral character is formed through practice and habituation. His treatment of practical wisdom clarifies why Christian moral life cannot be reduced to the mechanical application of commandments. His account of contemplation provides a philosophical grammar for the claim that the human being is oriented toward a transcendent fulfillment.

At the same time, Aristotelian ethics must be corrected from within a Christian perspective. The Aristotelian ideal of self-sufficiency must be replaced by a recognition of creaturely dependence on God. The emphasis on magnanimity and social honor must be subordinated to humility and service. The notion that happiness requires a fortunate life must be transformed by the Christian claim that beatitude can coexist with suffering and may even be revealed through it. The contemplative life must be joined to active love, not merely as a secondary necessity but as a response to the personal love of God and neighbor.

There is also an anthropological difference. Aristotle defines human beings chiefly by their capacities for rational activity, political association, and virtue. Christianity defines them additionally as creatures made in the image of God, wounded by sin, and called to communion with God. This framework does not reject reason or virtue, but it denies that human powers alone can achieve the final end. Grace is necessary not because rational ethics is false, but because the supernatural destiny of the person exceeds natural capacity.

Thus Christian doctrine can appropriate Aristotle without simply accepting his final position. Aristotle supplies a natural teleology; Christianity adds a supernatural telos. Aristotle identifies contemplation as the highest natural activity; Christianity identifies the vision of God as a gift that surpasses the natural operation of the intellect. Aristotle treats virtue as the formation of excellent human agency; Christianity places virtue within the drama of redemption and sanctification.

## IX. Final Assessment of Nussbaum’s Interpretation

Nussbaum’s interpretation should therefore be evaluated as corrective rather than complete. She successfully exposes the weakness of readings that treat Aristotle as an advocate of detached rational self-sufficiency. Books I–IX undeniably show that eudaimonia requires a life of action, friendship, political membership, bodily existence, and emotional formation. External fortune matters, and moral excellence does not protect one from all forms of loss. Nussbaum is also right that Aristotle’s practical reason is sensitive to particularity and that universal principles cannot substitute for judgment.

Her account of species-relative goods is substantially correct insofar as Aristotle’s ethics is grounded in the form of human life. Human beings flourish through activities appropriate to rational, embodied, social animals. The good is not an abstract object independent of living beings. Yet the human form itself includes an orientation toward the divine and universal. The species-relative human good is not closed within the horizon of ordinary human interests.

Her account of incommensurability is correct if understood as a rejection of reductionism. Aristotle does not reduce all goods to pleasure, preference, wealth, or a single calculable quantity. But the goods are not thereby unranked. Contemplation occupies a superior position, and the practical virtues are organized around a conception of noble activity. Aristotle’s pluralism is structured rather than egalitarian.

Finally, her account of principles as provisional summaries is correct regarding the need for flexible application. Ethics must deal with contingent and variable matters, and practical wisdom perceives the relevant particulars. But Aristotle’s principles are not merely temporary hypotheses. They express an objective teleological order, even if they require interpretation in practice. The agent’s judgment is constrained by the nature of the virtues and by the hierarchy of ends.

The apparent contradiction between the vulnerable moral life and the divine contemplative life can therefore be resolved without choosing either Nussbaum or a radically intellectualist Aristotle. Human flourishing has multiple dimensions, but these dimensions are not equal. The practical virtues express the goodness of human beings as embodied and political agents. Contemplation expresses the highest activity of their rational nature. The former is indispensable to a complete human life; the latter gives that life its ultimate orientation. Aristotle’s final position is best described as a hierarchical inclusivism.

This conclusion also clarifies the relationship between Aristotle and Christianity. Book X does not anticipate the New Testament as though Aristotle had unconsciously formulated Christian revelation. Its divine life is not the life of the Triune God, and its contemplation is not the beatific communion promised by Christ. Nevertheless, Aristotle’s hierarchical conception of human fulfillment creates a philosophical space in which Christian theology can distinguish natural and supernatural happiness. His account of virtue and practical wisdom can support Christian moral reflection, while his contemplative ideal can be reinterpreted as a natural anticipation of humanity’s orientation toward God.

Aristotelian ethics can therefore provide a rational foundation for certain dimensions of Christian doctrine, but not for Christianity as a whole. It can establish that human life is ordered toward objective fulfillment, that virtue forms the agent for that fulfillment, that social and embodied goods are morally significant, and that the intellect reaches beyond immediate utility toward truth. It cannot establish the doctrines that most decisively reshape the meaning of fulfillment: grace, incarnation, universal charity, redemption through suffering, and resurrection.

The philosophical lesson is that transcendence need not mean the denial of vulnerability. Aristotle’s highest life rises above ordinary contingency through contemplation, but it remains the activity of a vulnerable human being. Christianity radicalizes the issue by claiming that the divine does not merely attract the intellect from above; it enters human vulnerability from within. Aristotle can explain why human beings seek the divine. The New Testament claims that the divine seeks human beings. That difference marks the limit of Aristotle’s ethical foundation and the distinctive novelty of Christian doctrine.
