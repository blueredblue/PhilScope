**The Desertist Solution to Petitionary Prayer: Effort, Unearned Traits, and the Alignment of Divine Disposition**

**I. Introduction**

The puzzle of petitionary prayer has long perplexed philosophers of religion. If God is omniscient, omnipotent, and perfectly good, what function does human petition serve? On one hand, if God already knows our needs and has ordained the outcome of all events from eternity, our prayers appear to be causally inert, adding nothing to the divine calculus. On the other hand, if prayer could somehow change God’s mind or influence the divine will, it risks portraying God as mutable, ignorant, or subject to human manipulation. The traditional theological response, drawing heavily from Thomas Aquinas and Gregory the Great, posits that prayer is not about informing or coercing God, but about ordering the soul, cultivating virtue, and aligning the human will with the divine. Yet, for many contemporary philosophers, this response leaves the puzzle of pointlessness unresolved: if the outcome is fixed, what is the *point* of the prayer itself?

Scott Hill, in his influential work on the philosophy of prayer, offers a novel resolution to this conundrum: the desertist solution. Drawing on the Thomistic and Gregorian traditions, Hill argues that respectful and non-demanding petitionary prayers generate a minuscule amount of *prima facie* desert, enabling the praying agent to overall deserve the goods that God has already disposed to give from eternity. In this essay, I will evaluate Hill’s desertist solution by analyzing its central thesis, addressing the philosophical tension concerning whether effort functions as a valid desert base, confronting Rawlsian objections regarding unearned traits and circumstances, and ultimately defending a position on whether this framework successfully rescues petitionary prayer from pointlessness without relying on controversial theological or metaphysical premises.

**II. The Desertist Thesis: Respectful Petition and Prima Facie Desert**

Hill’s central thesis rests on a subtle but crucial distinction between demanding and respectful petitionary prayer. The puzzle of petitionary prayer often assumes that prayer is a transaction: the agent asks for a good, and God either grants it or withholds it based on the agent's merits or the efficacy of the request. This transactional model leads directly to the problem of pointlessness—if God has already decided the outcome, the request changes nothing.

Hill, following Aquinas and Gregory the Great, shifts the paradigm. Aquinas argued that prayer does not change God’s mind but changes the one praying, disposing the soul toward grace. Gregory the Great, meanwhile, emphasized the humility inherent in true prayer, distinguishing between the prayer of the righteous, which is confident, and the prayer of the sinner, which is supplicating. For Gregory, true petition is an act of reverent acknowledgment of divine sovereignty, not a demanding claim upon God’s obligations.

Hill synthesizes these insights into a desert-based framework. He contends that when an agent engages in respectful, non-demanding petitionary prayer, the agent performs an act of moral significance. By approaching God with humility and reverence rather than entitlement or coercion, the agent exercises a form of moral effort. This effort, Hill argues, generates a *prima facie* right, or desert, to the goods being petitioned. Because the prayer is non-demanding, this desert is minuscule; it does not constitute an absolute entitlement that coerces the divine will, but rather a slight moral claim that makes the agent's receipt of the good *deserved* rather than merely arbitrary.

The genius of this solution lies in how it reconciles divine sovereignty with human agency. God, from eternity, has already disposed to give certain goods to certain agents. In the traditional view, this raises the question of why the agent must pray at all. Hill’s desertist solution answers that the agent must pray because the act of praying generates the *prima facie* desert that makes God’s pre-existing disposition to give those goods just. Without the prayer, the agent would lack the minimal moral claim to the good; with the prayer, the agent aligns their desert with God's eternal disposition. Thus, petitionary prayer is not pointless; it is the necessary mechanism by which an agent earns the *prima facie* right to the goods God has already ordained to bestow.

**III. Effort as a Valid Desert Base**

To fully appreciate Hill’s solution, one must grapple with the philosophical tension concerning whether effort functions as a valid desert base. In Aristotelian and Thomistic ethics, effort is intimately tied to virtue and merit. To deserve a good, one must actively pursue it; virtue is not merely a state of being but an activity of the will. Hill’s framework relies on this tradition: the effort of respectful prayer is the desert base.

However, the concept of effort as a desert base is fraught with philosophical difficulty. If I sit in a chair, I have exerted no effort and deserve no reward for sitting. If I lift a weight, I have exerted effort and might deserve a reward for my strength or perseverance. But what kind of effort is the effort of prayer? Prayer, especially in its non-demanding, reverential form, is primarily an internal act of the will and intellect. It involves humility, gratitude, and submission—states of the soul rather than bodily exertion. Yet, Hill argues that even this internal effort generates desert. The mental and volitional labor of aligning one's will with God's, of cultivating humility, constitutes a genuine exercise of agency. Because it is an exercise of agency, it qualifies as an effort that can ground *prima facie* desert.

This reliance on effort as a desert base is philosophically robust in a virtue-ethical framework. It avoids the trap of making prayer a mere transaction of words; instead, it emphasizes the transformative labor of the soul. The agent who prays respectfully is actively engaging in the moral life, and this active engagement yields a moral dividend—a right to the goods petitioned. By grounding desert in effort rather than in the sheer fact of asking, Hill ensures that prayer retains its moral weight and its capacity to rescue the practice from pointlessness.

**IV. The Rawlsian Objection: Unearned Traits and Circumstances**

Despite its virtue-ethical foundations, Hill’s desertist solution faces a formidable objection from the Rawlsian tradition. John Rawls, in *A Theory of Justice*, famously critiqued the concept of desert when it is tied to unearned traits and circumstances. Rawls argued that much of what we consider "earned" or "deserved" is actually the product of brute luck—our genetic endowments, our family of origin, our social context, and the historical contingencies that shaped our development. Because we do not choose these traits and circumstances, they cannot legitimately serve as a basis for desert. To reward someone for their intelligence or their industriousness is to reward them for factors outside their control.

Applied to Hill’s framework, the Rawlsian objection strikes at the very heart of the effort-based desert base. Can the effort of respectful prayer truly be earned if the capacity to exert that effort depends on unearned traits? Consider the agent who naturally possesses a temperament of humility, who was raised in a religious household that cultivated reverence, or who possesses the cognitive capacities to engage in complex theological reflection. These traits and circumstances are unearned. They are brute facts about the agent's situation. Therefore, the "effort" of prayer is not entirely self-generated; it is enabled by a set of unearned advantages. If unearned traits cannot ground desert, then the *prima facie* desert generated by prayer is unjustified.

This objection is particularly potent because it threatens to make the desertist solution circular or arbitrary. If the desert generated by prayer is merely a function of unearned traits, then the agent's receipt of God's goods is not earned at all; it is merely dispensed to those who happen to have been blessed with the unearned capacity to pray correctly. This would render the desertist solution indistinguishable from the very arbitrary distribution it sought to avoid.

**V. Defending Hill: The Minuscule Descent and the Baseline of Rational Agency**

Hill can, however, mount a compelling defense against the Rawlsian objection by leveraging the specific nature of the desert his theory posits: the *minuscule* and *prima facie* nature of the desert generated by prayer.

The Rawlsian critique is most powerful when applied to large claims of desert. If I deserve a massive reward because of my extraordinary efforts, and those efforts were enabled by unearned traits, the injustice is glaring. But Hill's theory does not posit that prayer generates a massive desert; it generates a minuscule amount. The desert is *prima facie*, meaning it is a default right that can be overridden or contextualized by other moral considerations, most notably divine grace.

The significance of this minuteness lies in its relationship to unearned traits. To generate a minuscule desert, the agent does not require extraordinary unearned advantages. The effort of respectful, non-demanding prayer is a baseline exercise of rational agency. Every rational agent, regardless of their unearned traits or circumstances, possesses the basic capacity to acknowledge a higher power with humility and to direct their will toward the good. The effort required to utter a reverent petition or to cultivate a posture of humility is not a heroic feat that depends on rare genetic gifts or privileged upbringings; it is the minimal expression of moral agency available to all persons.

Therefore, the Rawlsian objection loses much of its force when applied to this baseline effort. The unearned traits that might enhance one's capacity for prayer—such as a naturally pious temperament or a religious education—do not significantly amplify the desert generated. The desert is not proportional to the quality or intensity of the prayer, but to the simple fact of the respectful engagement itself. Because this engagement is a universal possibility for rational agents, the desert it generates is not contingent on unearned luck; it is grounded in the universal capacity for moral agency. The minuscule desert is thus a universal baseline right, earned by the mere exercise of rational reverence, which is not an unearned trait but a fundamental feature of human personhood.

**VI. Rescuing Petitionary Prayer from Pointlessness**

The primary philosophical merit of Hill’s desertist solution is that it successfully rescues petitionary prayer from pointlessness. By providing the agent with a *prima facie* claim to the goods petitioned, prayer ceases to be a causally inert gesture or a manipulative attempt to change God's mind. Instead, prayer becomes the mechanism by which the agent's moral desert is actualized. God's eternal disposition to give goods is not arbitrary; it is matched by the agent's earned, albeit minuscule, right to receive them. Prayer is the bridge between divine foreordination and human desert.

However, the crucial question remains: does this rescue occur without relying on controversial theological or metaphysical premises? To answer this, we must examine the metaphysical scaffolding upon which Hill's solution rests. The entire framework depends on the concept of God's eternal disposition—the idea that God has, from all eternity, already determined to give certain goods to certain agents. Furthermore, it presupposes a specific metaphysics of time and divine knowledge in which God's eternal disposition can be "matched" by a temporally generated human desert.

These are highly controversial theological and metaphysical premises. The doctrine of divine eternity, the concept of middle knowledge (Molinism), or the Calvinist doctrine of predestination are all theological frameworks that undergird the idea that God has an eternal disposition that prayer aligns with. Without these premises, the desertist solution collapses. If God does not have an eternal disposition to give goods, then the *prima facie* desert generated by prayer has nothing to align with. The prayer would still be pointless, or worse, it would be generating a desert claim that God has no intention of fulfilling.

Moreover, the very concept of *prima facie* desert in the context of an omnipotent God is metaphysically fraught. In secular or general theistic moral philosophy, *prima facie* rights are typically overridden by other moral considerations within a shared human framework. But when the being who can override the *prima facie* right is the very being who is supposed to be granting the good, the concept of desert becomes entirely dependent on the divine will. The agent's desert is not an independent moral reality that God must respect; it is a conceptual tool that only has meaning because God has chosen to respect it. This makes the desertist solution heavily reliant on the theological premise that God takes human *prima facie* desert seriously—a premise that is far from universally accepted and borders on the controversial.

**VII. Conclusion**

Scott Hill’s desertist solution offers a philosophically elegant resolution to the puzzle of petitionary prayer. By synthesizing the Thomistic emphasis on the transformative power of prayer with the Gregorian focus on humble reverence, Hill argues that respectful, non-demanding petition generates a minuscule *prima facie* desert. This desert aligns the agent's moral claim with God's eternal disposition, rescuing petitionary prayer from the charge of pointlessness. The framework successfully addresses the tension regarding effort as a desert base by grounding the desert in the universal baseline of rational agency rather than in unearned, exceptional traits. The Rawlsian critique, which highlights the role of brute luck in generating effort, is largely neutralized by the *minuscule* nature of the desert; because the desert is so small, it does not require a robust defense against the unearned traits that might facilitate it.

Nevertheless, the evaluation of Hill's solution must ultimately acknowledge its reliance on controversial theological and metaphysical premises. The framework's success in rescuing petitionary prayer is entirely contingent upon the truth of the doctrine of God's eternal disposition and the metaphysics of divine-human alignment. Without these premises, the *prima facie* desert generated by prayer floats free of any divine purpose, rendering the prayer once again pointless. Thus, while Hill's desertist solution is philosophically robust within its own theological framework, it cannot claim to be a purely secular or metaphysically neutral rescue of petitionary prayer. It is, ultimately, a brilliant theological construction that requires the very metaphysical commitments it seeks to illuminate, demonstrating that the puzzle of prayer is inextricably bound to the mysteries of the divine.
