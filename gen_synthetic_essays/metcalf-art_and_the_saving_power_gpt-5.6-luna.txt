# Art, Otherness, and the Saving Power Against Technology

## Introduction: Danger and the Possibility of Saving

Martin Heidegger’s reflections on technology and art are governed by a paradox. Technology appears to be the most powerful danger confronting modern humanity because it does not merely provide instruments for human use; it determines in advance how beings can appear at all. Yet Heidegger insists, following Hölderlin, that “where the danger is, grows the saving power also.” The saving power cannot therefore mean a technological solution to technology, nor a return to a premodern condition untouched by machines. It must arise from a transformation in our relation to revealing itself. Art is important in this transformation because it opens a mode of appearing that cannot be fully reduced to availability, calculation, or productive use.

The decisive issue is not whether technology is beneficial or harmful in its ordinary empirical effects. The issue is the kind of disclosure through which technological objects and processes become intelligible. In “The Question Concerning Technology,” Heidegger calls this disclosure Enframing (*Gestell*): the gathering command that challenges beings to present themselves as resources, or standing-reserve, available for extraction, storage, distribution, and optimization. In “The Origin of the Work of Art,” by contrast, art is understood as a happening of truth, a setting-into-work of the strife between world and earth. The work does not simply represent an already constituted reality. It allows a world to emerge while simultaneously preserving the withdrawal, opacity, and resistance of earth.

This contrast also bears upon Heidegger’s critique of modern metaphysics, especially Nietzsche’s metaphysics of the will to power. Nietzsche’s interpretation of beings as will to power describes a world in which everything is measured according to the intensification, organization, and securing of power. Heidegger reads modern technology as the historical consummation of this metaphysical trajectory. In a technological age, humanity itself becomes part of the standing-reserve, compelled continually to justify its existence through productivity, adaptability, efficiency, and self-enhancement. The unending process of justification displaces human beings from their essence because it prevents them from dwelling in the openness where beings can appear as beings.

Art offers a saving power because its relation to truth is radically other than the technological relation. It does not simply give us a more humane inventory of useful objects. It confronts us with the otherness of what cannot be mastered, with concealment as an essential dimension of truth. Gerald Bruns’s interpretation of art’s otherness helps clarify why art cannot be reduced to a cultural instrument for restoring human authenticity. For Bruns, the otherness of art is not merely a message from beyond the self, nor an alternative content that can be possessed by interpretation. It is an interruption of appropriation itself. The work places the interpreter in a relation of exposure to what exceeds conceptual capture.

Yet this saving power is difficult and incomplete. To “submit” to the displacement of the work of art may restore humanity to an essential attendance on the revealing and concealing of Being, but only if submission means relinquishing mastery rather than abandoning thought. Art saves not by placing humanity securely back in its essence but by dislocating the human from the demand to secure an essence. It teaches that human beings are essentially those who attend to the event in which beings become manifest and withdraw. The work of art therefore does not overcome technology; it opens a space in which technological disclosure can be recognized as one historical mode of revealing rather than accepted as the destiny of all appearance.

## I. Technology Beyond Instrumentality

Heidegger begins “The Question Concerning Technology” by criticizing the conventional definition of technology as a means to an end and as a human activity. These definitions are not false. A hammer is indeed a means for driving nails, and human beings do indeed produce and use technological devices. But such definitions remain “correct” without being “true” in the philosophical sense. They describe technology at the level of entities and practices while failing to disclose its essence.

For Heidegger, the essence of technology is not itself technological. It is a way of revealing. He turns to the Greek concept of *technē*, which belongs to the broader field of bringing-forth, *poiesis*. The artisan, the poet, and nature itself can all be understood in relation to a bringing-forth through which something enters unconcealment. In this older sense, technology is not primarily coercive. It participates in the disclosure of what comes to presence.

Modern technology, however, differs decisively from such bringing-forth. It challenges nature rather than allowing it to emerge according to its own movement. The Rhine is no longer primarily a river in which a landscape, a history, and a human dwelling are gathered. It becomes a site for hydroelectric power. The forest is not first encountered as a living ecosystem or a place of habitation, but as timber reserve. The soil appears as a source of minerals, and human beings as labor power, consumers, data points, or “human resources.” Modern technology does not merely use beings; it orders them in advance to appear as resources.

Heidegger names this ordering Enframing. Enframing is not a machine, a policy, or a collection of industrial practices. It is the structure of disclosure that commands beings to be available. It gathers the various forms of challenging-forth into a single horizon in which everything is assessed in terms of extractability, usability, calculability, and replaceability. The modern power plant is not significant merely because it produces electricity. It belongs to a world in which the river is revealed as an energy source and in which energy itself is stored, transported, distributed, and consumed within a system of continual availability.

The danger is consequently deeper than environmental destruction or technological domination. Even if technology became ecologically sustainable, the danger could remain if beings continued to appear only as resources. The most endangered being would then be humanity itself, not because humans would be physically destroyed, but because they would lose the possibility of understanding themselves otherwise than as components within the technological order. A person becomes a worker, consumer, user, customer, profile, biological organism, or bundle of preferences. The individual is interpreted in terms of measurable functions and possible outputs.

The human being is therefore not simply the master of Enframing. Heidegger stresses that human beings are themselves challenged by it. We do not stand outside the technological ordering and consciously impose it upon the world from a position of sovereign freedom. We are claimed by it. We are compelled to think, act, and even understand ourselves through its categories. The apparent expansion of human power coincides with a loss of freedom because the horizon within which choices are made has already been determined.

This is why the technological danger cannot be defeated by rejecting machines or by choosing a simpler lifestyle alone. Such gestures may be valuable, but they do not touch the essence of the problem. One can reject a particular technology while remaining entirely within the technological mode of disclosure. Even the denunciation of technology can become technological if it treats the problem as one of management, control, or optimization. The question is whether another mode of revealing can come into relation with Enframing and interrupt its claim to exclusivity.

## II. The Will to Power and the Unending Demand for Justification

Heidegger’s interpretation of Nietzsche helps explain why modern technology tends toward totality. Nietzsche’s central concept, the will to power, is not merely a psychological desire to dominate others. It is Heidegger’s term for the metaphysical determination of beings as essentially self-enhancing power. Everything that is seeks to expand, intensify, preserve, and organize itself. Knowledge, values, institutions, and even truth become functions of the will to power.

Heidegger does not simply accuse Nietzsche of advocating crude domination. Nietzsche’s thought is significant because it reveals the completion of a metaphysical history in which beings are increasingly understood in relation to the subject’s position, representation, valuation, and control. The will to power is the will to secure and enhance the conditions of power. It therefore includes the production of order, interpretation, measurement, and stabilization. Power is not only force; it is the capacity to establish a field in which everything can be evaluated and directed.

Modern technology manifests this metaphysics in concrete form. It does not merely produce instruments that humans may use for arbitrary purposes. It organizes the world as a system of potential operations. Every being is interpreted according to what it can contribute to the expansion and maintenance of the system. The technological world is a world of continual mobilization. Nothing is permitted simply to be. Everything must demonstrate its function, yield, efficiency, or future potential.

Here the question of justification becomes crucial. In a technological order, individuals, institutions, and forms of knowledge are expected to justify themselves continually. A university must prove its economic utility. Art must demonstrate social impact, therapeutic value, marketability, or political relevance. A person must justify education through employability, leisure through productivity, and existence through achievement or measurable contribution. Even forms of resistance are required to establish their effectiveness in terms recognized by the system.

This process is unending because no achievement can finally satisfy the demand for justification. Every resource must be made more efficient; every institution must become more competitive; every self must become more productive and adaptable. The logic of the standing-reserve is therefore temporal as well as spatial. It does not merely store beings; it projects them toward further availability. The present is valuable because it can be converted into a more productive future. The future, in turn, arrives as another demand for optimization.

Such a world displaces humanity from its essence. Heidegger’s language of human essence should not be understood as referring to a fixed biological or moral property possessed by every individual. Human essence lies in a relation: human beings are those who can let beings be disclosed, those who can attend to unconcealment and concealment, those who dwell in the clearing of Being. To be human is not first to be a producer or consumer but to stand exposed to what exceeds one’s control.

The unending process of justification blocks this relation. It leaves no room for uselessness, hesitation, receptivity, or waiting. Yet these are not mere absences of activity. They can be modes of attention in which what is not yet articulated becomes perceptible. The person compelled to justify every moment cannot allow anything to appear without immediately asking what it is for. This “for what” is the fundamental question of Enframing. It converts the world into a field of purposes and human beings into agents who must continuously prove their right to occupy it.

The metaphysics of the will to power thus reaches beyond political domination. It shapes the intimate experience of selfhood. One comes to experience oneself as a project requiring constant enhancement and validation. The self becomes its own standing-reserve. It must be maintained, upgraded, marketed, and made legible. In this condition, the human being is not liberated by possessing more choices, because choice itself has been absorbed into the demand for performance.

## III. Art as the Setting-into-Work of Truth

Against this technological disclosure, Heidegger’s “The Origin of the Work of Art” offers a different account of how beings come to presence. The work of art is not defined by its function, its beauty, or its capacity to express the subjective experience of the artist. Nor is it simply an object placed in a museum. Its essence lies in its capacity to happen as truth.

Heidegger defines truth as unconcealment, *aletheia*. This does not mean that truth is total visibility or the complete presence of an object before a knowing subject. Unconcealment is possible only because something remains concealed. Truth is a tension between disclosure and withdrawal. A thing appears as what it is precisely because it is not exhausted by its appearance. Concealment is not a temporary defect that knowledge must eliminate. It belongs essentially to the event of truth.

The work of art makes this structure visible. Heidegger’s example of van Gogh’s painting of peasant shoes is not important because the painting provides an accurate representation of footwear. The painting discloses a world: the labor, earth, weather, endurance, and belonging that gather around the shoes. At the same time, the shoes are not reduced to a transparent sign system. Their worn materiality and silent opacity remain. The work does not simply tell us what the shoes mean; it allows a world to open while preserving the thing’s resistance to final interpretation.

Heidegger describes the work through the strife between world and earth. World is not a collection of objects. It is the meaningful openness in which human practices, gods, mortality, history, and possibilities of dwelling become intelligible. Earth, by contrast, names the self-secluding dimension of beings: their density, materiality, opacity, and refusal to become fully available. The earth withdraws even as it supports the world’s emergence. In a Greek temple, for example, stone, light, landscape, divinity, mortality, and communal life are gathered into a world; yet the stone is not dissolved into meaning. Its heaviness and mute resistance remain essential.

Art is the setting-into-work of this strife. The work does not overcome concealment by presenting a clear message. It preserves concealment within disclosure. Color, sound, language, stone, rhythm, and form become sites where something appears without becoming completely present. The work of art therefore opposes the technological demand that everything be rendered available. Its material is not merely a resource from which a finished product is made. Stone in architecture, paint in painting, and language in poetry are allowed to manifest their own resistance.

This does not mean that art is outside history or free from material conditions. Artworks are produced, circulated, bought, exhibited, digitized, and interpreted. They can certainly be absorbed into the standing-reserve. A painting can become an investment asset; music can become background content; literature can become data for automated analysis; museums can become instruments of tourism and economic development. Heidegger’s point is not that artworks are immune to technological appropriation. Rather, the work possesses the possibility of another disclosure, a possibility that can be obscured but not entirely identified with its technological use.

The distinction between art and technology is therefore not a simple opposition between good objects and bad objects. A work of art can be technologically produced, and a technological artifact can disclose aspects of the world. The issue concerns the governing mode of revealing. Does the work invite a relation of listening, dwelling, and astonishment, or does it immediately become a resource for consumption and exchange? Does it allow the material and the world to remain in strife, or does it convert them into a fully available message?

## IV. The Radical Otherness of Truth

The saving power of art depends upon the otherness of truth. Truth is radically other because it cannot be reduced to the subject’s act of knowing. If truth were simply a correct representation produced by a capable mind, then the technological order could claim to possess it through increasingly accurate measurement and information. Heidegger’s concept of unconcealment refuses this reduction. Truth happens to human beings before it becomes something they control.

This otherness is not an object standing over against a subject. It is more unsettling than ordinary opposition. The otherness of truth displaces the subject from its position as the origin of meaning. A work of art can confront a viewer with something that does not merely confirm an existing interpretation but alters the field within which interpretation occurs. One does not first possess a stable self and then decide what the work means. The work can make the self’s previous categories inadequate.

In this respect, art is uncanny. The uncanny is not simply the strange or exotic. It is the disturbance of the familiar by what has never been fully mastered within it. A work can make the ordinary appear strange, but this strangeness reveals that the ordinary was never as secure as it seemed. The familiar world depends upon concealed conditions that are usually overlooked. Art can expose those conditions without turning them into a new object of mastery.

Heidegger’s account of the work’s “strife” is central here. The work does not resolve the tension between world and earth. If it did, it would cease to be a happening of truth. Its power lies in sustaining the conflict between the opening of a meaningful world and the withdrawal of what resists that opening. The work thus provides an experience of limit. It shows that disclosure is inseparable from concealment and that no final system can gather everything into complete presence.

This is precisely what technological revealing attempts to deny. Enframing treats concealment as an obstacle to be overcome. Unknown territory becomes a research problem; unmeasured behavior becomes data; bodily limitation becomes an engineering challenge; ambiguity becomes noise. Art does not necessarily reject knowledge, but it resists the identification of truth with complete availability. It gives form to the fact that what matters most may be inseparable from what cannot be made fully explicit.

The work’s otherness also interrupts the demand for justification. A work does not need to justify itself by becoming useful. Its significance may lie in its ability to suspend the question of utility. This suspension is not escapism. It can disclose the violence of a world in which utility has become the universal measure. A poem’s resistance to paraphrase, a painting’s refusal to become a proposition, or a musical work’s irreducibility to a practical message can expose the poverty of a consciousness that recognizes only what it can use.

## V. Gerald Bruns and the Anarchy of Art

Gerald Bruns’s interpretation of art’s otherness helps deepen this argument. Bruns is suspicious of accounts that treat art as a vehicle for stable meanings or as an object whose significance can be fully recovered through interpretation. His writing emphasizes the anarchic, interruptive, and irreducible character of poetry and art. The work is “other” not because it communicates a hidden doctrine from a transcendent realm, but because it resists being subordinated to a sovereign order of interpretation.

For Bruns, art’s otherness is bound to its singularity. A work is not merely an instance of a general category, such as “landscape,” “political protest,” or “expression of grief.” It has a presence that cannot be exhausted by the concepts under which it is classified. This does not mean that interpretation is impossible. Rather, interpretation becomes an encounter with resistance. One interprets because the work speaks, but one continues interpreting because it never becomes fully possessed by what one says about it.

This position complements Heidegger’s account of earth. The work’s material and formal dimensions are not disposable carriers for a meaning located elsewhere. They are the very site of its otherness. Language in poetry, for example, is not merely a transparent medium that transports an idea. Its sounds, silences, rhythms, ambiguities, and histories obstruct complete paraphrase. The poem says something through the fact that its saying cannot be separated from the manner in which it occurs.

Bruns’s emphasis on anarchy is especially relevant to the technological order. Technology depends upon hierarchy, ordering, and functional integration. It asks what a thing is within a system and how it can be made to serve the system’s aims. Art can interrupt this ordering by refusing to occupy a fully determined place. Its relation to institutions remains unstable: it can be collected, canonized, commercialized, and administered, but the work’s singularity continues to exceed those operations.

The otherness of art should not, however, be romanticized as absolute independence from interpretation or history. Bruns does not require that art be inaccessible or unintelligible. The work is other precisely within a relation. It addresses us, but it does not become ours. It invites speech, but it also makes speech falter. This is close to Heidegger’s claim that the work opens a world while preserving earth. Otherness is not a separate substance; it is the event of resistance within disclosure.

Bruns also helps correct a possible danger in Heidegger. If one speaks too quickly of the “essence” of art, one may convert art into a philosophical function. Art would then be valuable because it serves Heidegger’s critique of technology, as though its purpose were to rescue philosophy from modernity. Bruns’s account warns against this instrumentalization. Art cannot be assigned the task of saving humanity without thereby being transformed into a resource for salvation. Its saving power must remain indirect, arising from its refusal to be fully subordinated to a predetermined end.

In this sense, art saves by not saving in the ordinary sense. It does not restore a lost unity or offer a program for cultural renewal. It displaces the demand for programs. It places us in contact with a mode of appearing that cannot be converted into a final justification. The work’s otherness becomes ethically and ontologically significant because it teaches receptivity to what exceeds the will to power.

## VI. The Dialectical Tension Between Standing-Reserve and Artwork

The relation between standing-reserve and artwork can be described dialectically, but not as a simple opposition in which one term eventually eliminates the other. The technological order and the artwork represent conflicting tendencies within the historical experience of beings. Standing-reserve is the tendency toward total availability; the work is the tendency toward a disclosure that preserves withdrawal. Standing-reserve seeks to dissolve resistance into function; the work makes resistance constitutive of truth.

In the standing-reserve, beings are treated as interchangeable. What matters is not the singularity of a tree but the quantity of timber, not the uniqueness of a person but the capacity of a workforce, not the irreducibility of a place but its potential for development. The value of a being is established through its position in a network of supply and demand. Even apparent uniqueness can become a market category. “Authenticity,” “creativity,” and “originality” can be packaged as desirable properties.

The work of art interrupts interchangeability. A particular work cannot be replaced without altering the event of disclosure that belongs to it. This is not merely because it is rare or valuable. Its singularity arises from the way its materials, historical situation, and formal tensions gather a world. A reproduction may preserve information about a work, but it does not necessarily reproduce the same event of presence. The technological image of the work can circulate endlessly while the work’s otherness remains unavailable to circulation as such.

Yet the work is not simply outside circulation. Its very visibility may depend upon institutions, markets, and technologies. The question is therefore not whether the artwork has escaped the standing-reserve but whether its mode of appearing can resist being exhausted by circulation. A work can be present within the technological order and still unsettle it. Its resistance is not guaranteed by its location but occurs when it demands a different kind of attention.

This attention is not passive in the sense of inert. It is an active letting-be. To attend is to allow the work to determine the pace and terms of encounter rather than immediately imposing an external purpose. The viewer must remain with ambiguity, delay judgment, and tolerate the failure of immediate comprehension. Such attention is contrary to the speed of technological consumption, where images and messages are processed for rapid recognition and disposal.

The dialectic also concerns the human subject. The standing-reserve turns humanity into a resource, while the work displaces the subject from mastery. But the work does not restore a sovereign, authentic self hidden beneath technological alienation. It reveals that humanity’s essence is not sovereignty. Human beings belong to the opening of Being precisely through their capacity to be unsettled, addressed, and transformed. The work does not give the subject back to itself; it teaches the subject to inhabit its exposure.

## VII. Can Submission to the Work Restore Humanity?

The phrase “submitting to the displacement of the work of art” requires careful interpretation. Submission can mean either passive obedience or a willing relinquishment of mastery. Only the second meaning is philosophically productive. The work displaces the human being by interrupting the position of the self-sufficient subject. It does not provide a new secure position from which the subject can dominate technology. To submit to this displacement is to accept that one’s relation to truth involves being claimed by something one did not produce and cannot fully control.

Does this restore humanity to its essential attendance on the revealing and concealing of Being? In one sense, yes. The work can return human beings to the openness in which they are capable of dwelling. It can disclose that concealment is not failure, that the world is not a warehouse, and that beings are not exhausted by their uses. It can cultivate the patience and receptivity needed for an alternative to Enframing. Through art, humanity may remember that its deepest task is not to secure everything but to attend to what gives itself and withdraws.

But the word “restore” is misleading if it suggests a return to an original condition in which humanity possessed its essence securely. Heidegger’s own account of Being resists such possession. Human essence is not a substance that can be recovered intact. It is a historical relation, always threatened and never simply completed. The work of art does not restore an already available essence; it opens the possibility of human essence as attendance.

Moreover, art cannot by itself abolish the technological order. A museum visit does not undo global systems of extraction, labor, surveillance, or commodification. Nor does aesthetic contemplation automatically produce ethical or political transformation. Art can be appropriated by propaganda, nationalism, advertising, and the culture industry. Its resistance is contingent and must be renewed in concrete practices of attention, interpretation, education, and communal life.

The saving power of art is therefore not a substitute for political action or technological critique. It is more fundamental and more modest. It changes the conditions under which action and critique become possible. If humanity sees only standing-reserve, then even political reform will tend to become another project of management. Art can disclose the limits of this vision. It can make perceptible the concealed costs and exclusions that technological calculation ignores. It can show that no system of justification is complete because every system rests upon forms of life and meaning that it cannot itself fully account for.

The work’s displacement also has a normative consequence. It calls into question the demand that all beings justify themselves in advance. A child, a landscape, a language, a memory, or an artwork need not first establish its utility in order to matter. This does not entail that anything should be exempt from responsibility. Rather, it means that responsibility cannot be reduced to calculation. One must sometimes protect what cannot yet prove its usefulness because its value belongs to a more originary relation of dwelling.

To submit to the work, then, is to become capable of a nonviolent relation to otherness. The work does not permit the other to remain entirely outside relation; it brings the other near. But it brings the other near without erasing its distance. This is the ethical significance of the strife between world and earth. A genuine relation does not require possession. It can be sustained through proximity to what remains concealed.

## Conclusion: The Saving Power as a Change in Revealing

Heidegger’s two essays articulate a decisive conflict between technological and artistic modes of disclosure. Technology, in its modern essence as Enframing, gathers beings into standing-reserve. It compels nature, institutions, and human beings to present themselves as available resources. In relation to Nietzsche’s metaphysics of the will to power, it expresses the demand that everything be secured, enhanced, and continually justified. Humanity becomes displaced from its essence because it forgets that its deepest vocation is not to master beings but to attend to the clearing in which beings can appear.

The work of art offers a counter-movement. As the setting-into-work of truth, it opens a world while preserving earth. It discloses beings without exhausting them. Its truth is radical because it includes concealment. The artwork is other not because it delivers a hidden object to the subject but because it interrupts the subject’s claim to mastery. Gerald Bruns’s account clarifies this point by emphasizing the anarchic singularity of art, its refusal to become fully subordinated to conceptual, institutional, or technological order. The work speaks, but it cannot be reduced to what is said about it.

Art’s saving power therefore consists neither in an escape from technology nor in the production of a superior cultural resource. It lies in the possibility that art can reopen the question of revealing itself. By confronting us with an otherness that cannot be converted into standing-reserve, art loosens the grip of the unending demand for justification. It lets us experience that truth is not identical with total availability and that concealment is not simply a problem awaiting solution.

Submitting to the displacement of the work can restore humanity, but only in a transformed sense. It does not return us to a stable essence or make us sovereign over technology. It restores us to the possibility of being human as attendance: as beings who receive, interpret, preserve, and inhabit the tension between what comes to light and what withdraws. This attendance is not passive. It requires vigilance against the reduction of all things to resources and courage to remain near what cannot be mastered.

The danger of technology is extreme because it threatens to make its own mode of revealing appear universal and inevitable. The saving power of art is genuine because it makes another relation possible. Yet the saving power remains a possibility, not an automatic guarantee. Art saves only when its otherness is allowed to operate—when the work is not consumed merely as content, converted into a market asset, or enlisted as proof of cultural utility. It saves when it displaces us from mastery and teaches us to dwell with the concealed ground of every disclosure. In that displacement, humanity does not recover a lost possession. It discovers, perhaps for the first time, that its essence lies in the very act of attending to what can never be finally possessed: the revealing and concealing of Being.
