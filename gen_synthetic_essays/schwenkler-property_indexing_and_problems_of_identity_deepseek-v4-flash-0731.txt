## The Indexed Self: Leibniz’s Laws, Property-Indexing, and the Unresolved Problem of Essence

### Introduction

The principles of the Indiscernibility of Identicals (hereafter, PI) and the Identity of Indiscernibles (hereafter, PII) constitute the twin pillars of Leibniz’s metaphysics of identity. PI, the uncontroversial converse, holds that if x and y are identical, then every property of x is a property of y. PII, the more contentious thesis, holds that if x and y share all their properties, then x and y are identical. Together, these laws appear to offer a rigorous, logical criterion for individuation: objects are what they are in virtue of the complete set of their properties. Yet, when we apply these laws to the dynamic and modal dimensions of existence—identity across time and identity across possible worlds—they generate a series of intractable paradoxes. A person changes posture, a statue is constituted by a lump of clay, and a possible individual named Adam seems to share all his essential properties with another possible individual named Noah. In each case, the naive application of Leibniz’s Laws threatens to collapse distinct objects into one, or to deny the possibility of change altogether.

The contemporary philosophical response to these paradoxes has been to reformulate Leibniz’s Laws through the mechanism of *property-indexing*. By relativizing properties to times and worlds—replacing the unqualified property *being seated* with the indexed property *being-seated-at-t1*, and *being wise* with *being-wise-in-w1*—philosophers have sought to preserve the formal validity of PI and PII while accommodating the evident facts of change and contingency. This essay evaluates whether this reformulation successfully resolves the dilemmas raised by Bobby’s changing posture, the swapped lumps of clay, and Roderick Chisholm’s Adam and Noah thought experiments. I will argue that property-indexing is a necessary formal device that successfully eliminates the *logical* contradictions that arise from applying unindexed Leibniz’s Laws to diachronic and trans-world identity. However, I will contend that this success is achieved only by displacing the metaphysical problem onto the question of *essential properties*. The indexing mechanism itself cannot distinguish between essential and accidental properties, and without a robust, independently justified theory of essence, property-indexing either renders identity trivially analytic or arbitrarily stipulative. Engaging with Alvin Plantinga’s account of world-indexed essential properties and Saul Kripke’s rigid-designation semantics, I will demonstrate that while indexing provides a coherent formal framework, it fails to resolve the underlying epistemological objection regarding the identification of essential properties. Consequently, property-indexing does not successfully eliminate the purported paradoxes of trans-world and diachronic identity without rendering identity trivial; rather, it transforms them into a more fundamental, unresolved metaphysical query.

### Section 1: The Paradoxes of Unindexed Identity

To understand the purported necessity of property-indexing, one must first appreciate the severity of the paradoxes it aims to solve. The first dilemma concerns diachronic identity, exemplified by the case of Bobby. Suppose Bobby sits at time t1 and stands at time t2. If we apply PI strictly, we must assert that if Bobby-at-t1 is identical to Bobby-at-t2, then every property of the former is a property of the latter. Yet Bobby-at-t1 has the property *being seated*, while Bobby-at-t2 lacks this property, possessing instead *being standing*. Since these are contradictory properties, PI appears to force the conclusion that Bobby-at-t1 is not identical to Bobby-at-t2. The alternative—that Bobby has both properties simultaneously—is a contradiction. The law of non-contradiction and the law of identity seem irreconcilable with the simple fact of change. This is not merely a linguistic quibble; it strikes at the heart of our notion of persistence. If identity is strict and absolute, how can an object survive any alteration in its properties?

The second dilemma concerns the problem of material constitution, often illustrated by the swapped lumps of clay. Consider a statue, *Statue*, and the lump of clay, *Lump*, that constitutes it at a given time. They occupy the same region, have the same mass, shape, and color. If we apply PII, we must conclude that *Statue* and *Lump* are identical, for they share all their spatio-temporal and physical properties. However, they manifestly differ in their modal properties. *Lump* can survive being squashed into a ball, whereas *Statue* cannot—if squashed, the statue ceases to exist, while the clay persists. More dramatically, consider a scenario where the lump is swapped for an identical-looking lump, or where the statue is melted and recast. The lump of clay has the property *possibly being squashed*; the statue does not. If PII holds, then since they differ in at least one property, they cannot be identical. Yet they share all their non-modal, actual properties. This forces a choice: either PII is false, or we must admit that there are two distinct objects occupying the same space at the same time, which seems to violate the principle of material occupancy. The paradox is that our common-sense ontology seems to require both the identity of the statue and the lump (they are one object) and their distinctness (they have different persistence conditions).

The third dilemma, drawn from Roderick Chisholm’s thought experiments, extends the problem to trans-world identity. Chisholm asks us to consider two possible individuals, Adam and Noah. In the actual world, Adam is wise and Noah is foolish. However, we can conceive of a possible world w1 where Adam is foolish, and a possible world w2 where Noah is wise. If we strip away all their accidental properties—their wisdom, their height, their color—and consider only their essential properties, what remains? If we assume a version of mereological essentialism, their essential properties might include their constituent parts or their haecceity (thisness). But if we abstract away from all qualitative properties, it seems that Adam and Noah might share all their essential properties. If PII holds, then they must be identical. But they are clearly distinct individuals in the actual world. Chisholm’s deeper point is that if we allow for trans-world identity, we face a dilemma: either identity is not governed by PII (and we must posit brute, primitive identity), or we must deny that distinct possible individuals can share all their properties. Chisholm himself leaned toward a form of mereological essentialism and the denial of trans-world identity for composite objects, arguing that any change in parts results in a new object. But this leads to the absurd conclusion that we are constantly becoming new individuals. The paradox is that our modal intuitions—that Adam *could have been* foolish—conflict with the strict logical dictates of PII.

### Section 2: The Mechanism of Property-Indexing

The proposed solution to these paradoxes is to reformulate the properties involved by indexing them to times and worlds. The core idea is that we do not have unqualified properties like *being seated* or *being wise*; rather, we have relational properties like *being-seated-at-t1* or *being-wise-in-w1*. This reformulation allows us to preserve the truth of PI and PII by ensuring that the objects in question share all the *relevant* indexed properties.

For the diachronic case of Bobby, the solution is straightforward. We replace the property *being seated* with *being-seated-at-t1* and *being standing* with *being-standing-at-t2*. Bobby-at-t1 has the property *being-seated-at-t1*. Bobby-at-t2 has the property *being-standing-at-t2*. Crucially, Bobby-at-t2 also has the property *being-seated-at-t1*, because it is true of Bobby-at-t2 that he was seated at t1 (assuming the past is fixed). Similarly, Bobby-at-t1 has the property *being-standing-at-t2* (in the sense that he will stand at t2). Thus, Bobby-at-t1 and Bobby-at-t2 share all their time-indexed properties. PI is satisfied: they are identical because they share all properties, where the properties are indexed to times. The apparent contradiction disappears because we are no longer asserting that Bobby is both seated and standing *simpliciter*; we are asserting that he is seated-at-t1 and standing-at-t2, which are perfectly compatible.

For the case of the statue and the lump, we index modal properties to worlds. The statue has the property *being-squashed-in-w1*? No—the statue cannot be squashed in any world where it exists as a statue. Instead, we say that *Lump* has the property *being-squashed-in-w1* (where w1 is a world where the lump is squashed), while *Statue* lacks this property because in w1, the statue does not exist (or is destroyed). More precisely, we can index the property of *surviving squashing* to worlds. *Lump* has the property *survives-squashing-in-w1*, while *Statue* has the property *does-not-survive-squashing-in-w1*. Since they differ in at least one world-indexed property, PII is satisfied—they are distinct. The paradox is resolved because we have found a property that differentiates them without positing a mysterious co-location of two distinct objects in the same space at the same time. The statue and the lump are distinct because they have different modal profiles, and those profiles are cashed out in terms of world-indexed properties.

For Chisholm’s Adam and Noah, the indexing solution works similarly. We replace the property *being wise* with *being-wise-in-w1* and *being foolish* with *being-foolish-in-w2*. Adam in the actual world has the property *being-wise-in-w1* (if w1 is the world where Adam is wise) and *being-foolish-in-w2* (if w2 is the world where Adam is foolish). Noah has *being-foolish-in-w2* and *being-wise-in-w1*. But wait—if Adam and Noah both have *being-wise-in-w1* and *being-foolish-in-w2*, do they share all their world-indexed properties? Not necessarily. We must index the properties to the individual as well. The property is not just *being-wise-in-w1* but *being-wise-in-w1-and-being-Adam*. However, this seems to beg the question. The standard indexing solution posits that the world-indexed property *being-wise-in-w1* is a property of the individual in the actual world. Adam has the property *being-wise-in-w1* (which means: Adam is wise in w1). Noah has the property *being-foolish-in-w2*. But if we consider a world w3 where Adam has all of Noah’s qualitative properties, then Adam would have *being-foolish-in-w2* as well. The indexing solution avoids the collapse by insisting that the *world-indexed* property is tied to the *individual* in the actual world. Adam has the property *being-Adam-and-wise-in-w1*, while Noah has *being-Noah-and-foolish-in-w2*. These are different properties. Thus, PII does not force them to be identical, because they do not share all their world-indexed properties—they differ in the indexical component (being Adam vs. being Noah). This, however, relies on a primitive notion of haecceity or thisness, which is a non-qualitative property. The formal mechanism of world-indexing, when combined with haecceitism, successfully blocks the collapse of distinct possible individuals.

### Section 3: The Epistemological Objection—The Problem of Essential Properties

At first glance, property-indexing appears to be a triumphant formal solution. It preserves the logical validity of Leibniz’s Laws while accommodating change and contingency. However, a deeper problem emerges: the indexing mechanism is entirely silent on *which* properties should be indexed. If we index *all* properties to times and worlds, then identity becomes trivially analytic. Consider the property *being-identical-to-Bobby*. If we index this to time, we get *being-identical-to-Bobby-at-t1*. Bobby-at-t1 has this property, and Bobby-at-t2 also has this property (since he is identical to Bobby-at-t1). But this is just a tautology. If we index *every* property, then PII reduces to the statement: if x and y share all their properties (including the property of being identical to x), then x=y. This is true, but it is vacuous—it tells us nothing about the world. It is akin to saying that everything is identical to itself.

To avoid this triviality, we must distinguish between essential and accidental properties. We must say that some properties are indexed (e.g., *being seated* is indexed to time because it is accidental) while others are not (e.g., *being a person* or *being identical to Bobby* is essential and unindexed). But this immediately raises the epistemological objection: how do we know which properties are essential? The indexing mechanism itself provides no criterion. It merely offers a formal language for expressing our pre-theoretic intuitions about essence. If we say that Bobby’s *being seated* is indexed to time, we are implicitly asserting that his posture is not essential to his identity. But why should we believe this? Why not say that *being seated* is essential, and that Bobby ceases to exist when he stands? The indexing solution cannot answer this question; it presupposes an answer.

This problem is vividly illustrated by the case of the statue and the lump. The indexing solution tells us that the statue and the lump differ in their world-indexed properties—the statue lacks *survives-squashing-in-w1* while the lump has it. But this presupposes that *surviving squashing* is an essential property of the lump and not of the statue. Why is this the case? The lump is composed of clay; the statue is also composed of clay. If we adopt a mereological essentialism, then both the statue and the lump have the same parts, and thus the same essential properties—they would be identical. If we adopt a more liberal view, we might say that the statue’s *being a statue* is essential, while the lump’s *being a lump* is essential. But this is a metaphysical stipulation, not a consequence of the logical form of indexing. The indexing mechanism is neutral between these competing metaphysical theories. It can accommodate mereological essentialism, haecceitism, or a bundle theory of properties. But it cannot decide between them.

The epistemological objection is not merely a technicality; it strikes at the heart of the project. The paradoxes of identity arose because we applied Leibniz’s Laws to unindexed properties. By indexing, we have avoided the contradiction, but we have done so by arbitrarily deciding which properties are subject to relativization. If we relativize all properties, identity is trivial. If we relativize only some, we need a theory of essence. The indexing solution thus fails to resolve the paradoxes; it merely relocates them to the question of essential properties. This is precisely the challenge posed by Chisholm’s thought experiment. Chisholm argued that if we strip away all accidental properties, Adam and Noah share all their essential properties. The indexing solution responds by adding haecceitistic properties (being Adam, being Noah) as essential. But this is a metaphysical commitment that goes beyond the formal mechanism. It is a way of saying that identity is primitive and not analyzable in terms of qualitative properties. If we accept haecceitism, then PII is false for purely qualitative properties, and the indexing solution is unnecessary. If we reject haecceitism, then the indexing solution fails to distinguish Adam from Noah.

### Section 4: Plantinga’s World-Indexed Essentialism

Alvin Plantinga offers a sophisticated attempt to ground the indexing mechanism in a robust theory of essence. Plantinga argues that essential properties are those that an object has in all possible worlds where it exists. However, he introduces a crucial distinction between *world-indexed* properties and *non-indexed* properties. For Plantinga, the property *being wise* is not essential to Socrates, because there is a possible world where Socrates is not wise. But the property *being-wise-in-the-actual-world* is essential to Socrates, because in every world where Socrates exists, it is true that he is wise in the actual world. This is a trivial truth, but it is a truth nonetheless. Plantinga’s key move is to argue that world-indexed properties are essential, while the non-indexed properties they are built from are accidental. Thus, Socrates has the essential property *being-wise-in-the-actual-world*, but the accidental property *being wise*.

This account provides a formal solution to the paradox of trans-world identity. If we ask whether Adam and Noah are distinct, Plantinga would say yes, because Adam has the essential property *being-Adam* (a haecceity) and *being-wise-in-w1*, while Noah has *being-Noah* and *being-foolish-in-w2*. These are distinct world-indexed properties. PII is preserved because Adam and Noah do not share all their world-indexed properties—they differ in their haecceities. However, Plantinga’s solution is vulnerable to the epistemological objection. How do we know that *being-Adam* is an essential property? Plantinga argues that haecceities are non-qualitative properties that are directly grasped by the intellect. But this is a highly contentious claim. Critics argue that we have no epistemic access to such properties; we cannot observe a haecceity, nor can we verify that it is essential. Plantinga’s reliance on intuition or rational insight is not a satisfactory answer to the skeptic who demands a criterion for distinguishing essential from accidental properties.
