# Friendship, Self-Love, and “Self-Referential Altruism” in Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics*

## Introduction

Aristotle’s treatment of friendship in the *Nicomachean Ethics* presents a distinctive challenge to modern theories of altruism. On the one hand, Aristotle insists that friendship is indispensable to a flourishing human life. No one, he claims, would choose to live without friends, even if supplied with every other good. On the other hand, his account appears to ground friendship in self-concern. We love our friends partly because they benefit us, partly because they are pleasant companions, and, in the best case, because their virtuous character resembles and completes our own. The good friend is described as “another self,” and the motives that make a person a good friend are repeatedly connected to the motives by which a person cares for himself.

This raises a difficult question. If concern for a friend ultimately derives from concern for oneself, can it count as genuinely altruistic? Or does the friend merely function as an extension of the agent’s own interests? Aristotle’s position seems to occupy an intermediate space between pure egoism and pure altruism. Friendship is not selfless in the sense of requiring the complete extinction of self-concern. Yet it is not selfish in the sense of treating others merely as instruments for private advantage. The best friendship involves each person willing and pursuing the other’s good for the other’s sake, while also recognizing that one’s own flourishing is inseparable from the flourishing of those with whom one shares a life.

This essay evaluates the claim that personal friendship in the *Nicomachean Ethics* constitutes “self-referential altruism.” The phrase captures an important feature of Aristotle’s position: self-concern serves as the psychological and ethical origin of concern for others, and the friend is loved as another self. Nevertheless, the phrase must be handled carefully. It can suggest that friendship remains covertly egoistic, as if concern for the friend were only a disguised concern for oneself. Aristotle’s own account is more complex. He argues that the virtuous person’s self-love is not a defect but a model for friendship: one must first possess a proper relation to oneself before one can properly love another. Yet the friend is not simply a duplicate or possession of the self. In a complete friendship, the other is loved for the other’s own sake, and the good of the other becomes part of the agent’s practical identity without being reduced to private utility.

The Aristotelian synthesis therefore successfully challenges the opposition between egoism and altruism, but only if “self-referential” means that friendship is structurally grounded in the self rather than that it is secretly directed only toward the self. Aristotle does not eliminate self-seeking elements from friendship. Instead, he distinguishes legitimate self-love from vulgar self-interest and shows how the former can generate other-directed concern. His account remains limited, however, by its emphasis on close associates, family, similarity, reciprocity, and shared life. It does not provide a fully universal account of moral concern. The result is a powerful but incomplete theory: good friendship is genuinely other-regarding, but it is other-regarding through a form of selfhood that is relational, virtuous, and extended into the lives of others.

## Friendship and the Conditions of Eudaimonia

Aristotle’s ethical theory is organized around the concept of *eudaimonia*, usually translated as happiness or flourishing. Eudaimonia is not a temporary feeling or a private state of satisfaction. It is an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life. Since human beings are rational and social animals, flourishing requires not merely internal excellence but participation in a shared human world. Friendship is therefore not an optional supplement to an otherwise complete life. It belongs to the structure of human flourishing.

In Books VIII and IX of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle presents friendship as both necessary and noble. A person needs friends in prosperity because prosperity is incomplete without someone with whom it can be shared; a person needs friends in adversity because friends provide assistance and consolation. Friendship also supports moral development. We learn how to act by acting with and around others, and a virtuous friend can serve as an object of admiration, a source of moral correction, and a partner in noble activity. Friendship enables the kind of stable shared life in which virtue can become habitual and visible.

Yet the very connection between friendship and eudaimonia creates a tension. If friendship is necessary for my flourishing, then I have a self-interested reason to maintain friendships. Does this mean that I value my friend only because the friend contributes to my own happiness? Aristotle explicitly distinguishes among kinds of friendship in response to this concern. Friendships based on utility involve loving another because that person provides some advantage. Friendships based on pleasure involve loving another because the person is enjoyable. Both forms are unstable because the relationship depends on changing benefits or pleasures. When the source of utility or pleasure disappears, the friendship tends to disappear as well.

The highest form is friendship based on virtue. In this friendship, each person loves the other because the other is good and wishes good for the other for the other’s own sake. The relationship is more stable because it is grounded in character rather than in accidental benefit or pleasure. Nevertheless, even virtuous friendship remains connected to the agents’ own flourishing. The friends enjoy each other’s company, share activities, and help one another to live well. Their relationship is not a sacrifice of eudaimonia but one of its forms.

This means that Aristotle refuses to treat personal flourishing and concern for friends as competing goals. The good person does not have to choose between being happy and caring for others. Rather, a genuinely flourishing person finds fulfillment in relationships characterized by mutual recognition and shared virtuous activity. A friend’s good is not external to the good life of the agent. But this does not automatically make concern for the friend selfish. The fact that a relationship benefits me does not prove that I value the friend only as a means. It may instead indicate that human flourishing is relational and that certain goods can be possessed only in common.

The issue turns on the distinction between instrumental and constitutive relations. If I value a friend solely because the friend provides money, status, entertainment, or emotional comfort, then the friend is being treated instrumentally. By contrast, if I value the friend’s existence, character, and well-being as part of a shared form of life, then the friend’s good is not merely a means to my own. It is a good in which I participate. Aristotle’s account of virtue friendship seeks to explain how this participation is possible without requiring a person to become indifferent to his or her own welfare.

## The Three Friendships and the Question of Altruism

Aristotle’s classification of friendship clarifies the extent to which personal friendship can be altruistic. The friendship of utility is plainly self-regarding in its motivation. Each person desires what the other can provide. Such relationships may be perfectly legitimate, but they do not exemplify friendship in its fullest sense. The friend is loved not as a person possessing an intrinsic claim on one’s concern but as a source of advantage.

The friendship of pleasure is more complicated. Here the friend is loved because the friend’s presence is enjoyable. The relationship is not necessarily exploitative. Pleasure can arise from appreciating another person’s personality, humor, intelligence, or companionship. Still, it remains dependent on the agreeable experience generated by the relationship. The friend is loved as someone whose presence gives pleasure, and therefore the relationship is vulnerable to changes in taste, circumstance, or character.

Virtue friendship combines the elements of usefulness and pleasure without reducing itself to either. Good people are useful and pleasant to one another, but they are loved primarily because they are good. Aristotle’s claim that each friend wishes good for the other “for the other’s sake” appears to establish a genuinely altruistic dimension. The good friend does not merely ask, “What can this person do for me?” Instead, the friend responds to the other as a being worthy of flourishing.

Nevertheless, Aristotle’s language does not amount to a theory of pure altruism. The virtuous friend’s concern is still shaped by the agent’s own judgments, commitments, and conception of the good. The friend is loved because the friend is perceived as good, and the agent’s recognition of goodness is itself part of the agent’s moral perspective. Furthermore, Aristotle holds that friendship requires reciprocity. One-sided benevolence may be goodwill, but it is not yet friendship. Friendship involves a shared awareness of mutual affection. Thus, even the best friendship is not a unilateral act of self-sacrifice. It is a reciprocal relationship in which each person’s good is actively embraced by the other.

This reciprocity does not necessarily undermine altruism. If altruism means acting for another’s good without reducing the other to a means, reciprocal benefit is compatible with altruistic concern. A parent may care for a child for the child’s sake even while receiving love and meaning in return. Similarly, friends may each wish the good of the other while also experiencing the relationship as beneficial to themselves. The presence of mutual gain shows that the relationship is relational, not that the concern is insincere.

The strongest objection is that Aristotle’s account may be circular. We call a friendship virtuous because each person loves the other for the other’s sake, but we identify the other as worthy of such love because the other resembles ourselves in virtue. If the friend is “another self,” perhaps the agent loves the friend only insofar as the friend mirrors the agent’s own values. The friend may then function as a projection of the self rather than as an independent center of value.

Aristotle’s theory partly accepts this concern. Friendship is not neutral toward every possible person. It is attracted to goodness, similarity, shared activity, and mutual recognition. A person does not become a close friend simply by being a human being. Personal friendship is selective. But selectivity does not entail selfishness. It may reflect the fact that deeper forms of care require knowledge, trust, shared history, and the possibility of reciprocal action. The limitation is real, but it is not equivalent to the reduction of the friend to a mere instrument.

## Self-Love as the Origin of Friendship

The most important foundation for Aristotle’s theory appears in Book IX, where he discusses self-love. He asks whether a person ought to love himself more than anyone else. The question is difficult because “self-love” often signifies greed, vanity, and an excessive desire to secure the largest share of wealth, honor, or pleasure. In this ordinary sense, self-love is morally defective. The self-lover is one who seeks advantage for himself at the expense of others.

Aristotle distinguishes this vulgar form of self-love from the self-love of the virtuous person. The virtuous person seeks the greatest share not of external goods but of noble action. Such a person is willing to surrender money, honor, or even life itself when doing so is required by virtue. In a conflict between his own material advantage and the good of a friend or city, he may give up external goods because he values noble action more highly. Paradoxically, Aristotle suggests that the truly virtuous person is the greatest self-lover, because he secures for himself the greatest good: virtuous activity.

This argument is central to the possibility of self-referential altruism. Aristotle does not claim that the virtuous person ceases to care about himself. Instead, he redefines the self that is properly loved. The self is not merely the bearer of appetites for possession, pleasure, and status. It is a rational and moral agent whose true good consists in excellent activity. To love oneself properly is to desire one’s own genuine flourishing, and genuine flourishing includes acting nobly toward others.

Self-concern therefore serves as the origin of friendship in two ways. First, a person who knows what it means to wish himself well can extend a structurally similar attitude toward another. Aristotle argues that the characteristics of friendship toward others are first found in one’s relation to oneself. A person wishes good for himself, desires his own existence, enjoys his own company, and seeks consistency among the elements of his life. These patterns are then reproduced in friendship. The friend is someone for whom one wishes good, whose existence one values, whose company one enjoys, and with whom one shares a life.

Second, self-love provides the conceptual model for understanding the friend as “another self.” The friend is not simply an external object of benevolence. The friend is another person whose good is apprehended with a degree of immediacy and intimacy comparable to one’s apprehension of one’s own good. The agent’s concern crosses the boundary between self and other without abolishing the distinction between them.

This account challenges the idea that altruism must originate in indifference to oneself. Aristotle sees self-regard as morally ambiguous rather than inherently corrupt. Bad self-love seeks the largest share of external goods and thereby competes with others. Good self-love seeks noble activity and thereby becomes compatible with generosity. The person who loves himself properly is not less capable of concern for friends; he is more capable, because he possesses an ordered conception of the good.

## The Friend as “Another Self”

The phrase “another self” is among the most famous and philosophically suggestive expressions in Aristotle’s discussion of friendship. It appears to indicate that the friend is closely connected to the agent’s own identity. To love a friend is, in some important respect, to love another version or manifestation of oneself.

This idea should not be interpreted as meaning that the friend is literally identical with the self or that friendship is a form of narcissism. Aristotle is not claiming that friends have no independent lives or interests. Rather, he is describing a practical and ethical relation in which the friend’s good becomes intimately connected with one’s own. The friend is “another self” because the friend is another center of rational and virtuous activity whose flourishing matters to me in a way that resembles the importance of my own flourishing.

The concept has several dimensions.

First, the friend is another self through recognition. In a good friendship, each person sees the other’s character and responds to it. The friend helps make the agent’s own moral identity visible. A person may discover what kind of person he is through the way he acts toward a friend and through the judgments of the friend. The friend is therefore not merely an object of concern but a partner in self-knowledge.

Second, the friend is another self through shared activity. Aristotle repeatedly emphasizes that friendship involves living together. Shared life does not mean constant physical proximity; it means participating in common perceptions, conversations, practices, and projects. The friend’s activities become part of the context in which one’s own life is lived. When friends deliberate together, admire the same things, and pursue noble actions, their lives are not simply adjacent. They are coordinated expressions of a common form of existence.

Third, the friend is another self through the extension of agency. I can act for my friend in ways that resemble acting for myself. I can protect the friend, deliberate about the friend’s good, and make sacrifices for the friend without experiencing these actions as alien to my own identity. This does not mean that I confuse my interests with the friend’s. It means that the friend’s good has entered the scope of my practical concern.

Fourth, the friend is another self through emotional participation. Aristotle suggests that the virtuous person is pleased by the friend’s existence and saddened by the friend’s misfortune. The friend’s condition matters not merely because it affects the agent externally but because the agent’s emotional life is responsive to it. This responsiveness is not an accidental weakness. It is part of loving someone as a person.

The phrase also exposes a possible danger. If the friend is another self, then the friend may be valued only insofar as he resembles the agent. The theory could appear to make similarity a condition of moral worth and to exclude radical difference. Aristotle does say that friendship is facilitated by likeness, especially likeness in virtue. People who share no values or practices cannot easily form the intimate association that friendship requires. But likeness does not have to mean sameness. A friend can be another self precisely by embodying the same human capacities—reason, agency, and the pursuit of the good—while remaining a distinct individual with a distinct perspective.

The friend as another self thus expresses both continuity and difference. The friend is sufficiently like me that his good can become intimately mine, but sufficiently distinct that my concern is directed toward him rather than merely back toward myself. If the friend were wholly identical with me, friendship would disappear into self-love. If the friend were wholly alien, intimate friendship would be impossible. Aristotle’s formulation attempts to articulate the intermediate relation in which another person is both other and near.

## Does “Another Self” Preserve Genuine Otherness?

The central problem for the interpretation of friendship as self-referential altruism is whether the friend’s good remains valuable for its own sake. If the friend is integrated into my identity, perhaps I care about the friend only as I care about a part of myself. That kind of concern may be intense, but intensity is not the same as altruism. A person may protect a possession, reputation, or bodily organ because it belongs to him, without valuing it independently.

Aristotle avoids this reduction by maintaining that the friend is loved as a person who is good in his own right. In virtue friendship, the friend is not merely useful to the agent’s self-development. The friend’s character is admired, and the friend’s flourishing is desired. The agent does not simply want the friend to remain available for companionship or moral assistance. He wants the friend to flourish even when the friend’s flourishing does not produce an immediate benefit.

At the same time, Aristotle does not articulate this independence in the language of modern moral impartiality. The friend’s value is not detached from the agent’s own conception of the good. The good friend is valued because the friend possesses virtue, and virtue is the form of activity that constitutes human flourishing. Thus, the friend’s good is pursued for the friend’s sake, but it is recognized within a shared ethical world. The friend’s good is not alien to my good because we participate in the same human form of life.

This is why the term “self-referential altruism” is useful but potentially misleading. It is useful if it indicates that the structure of other-concern is modeled on self-concern and that the friend’s good becomes a constituent of the agent’s own flourishing. It is misleading if it implies that all concern for the friend is ultimately reducible to personal benefit. Aristotle’s virtuous person does not ask whether every sacrifice will increase his own happiness. He may act for the friend at great personal cost, and what makes the action choiceworthy is its nobility.

Still, the account does not make self-seeking elements disappear. A friend’s existence gives pleasure; a friend provides help; a friend contributes to one’s moral activity; a friend’s success may confirm one’s own identity. These features are not merely incidental. They are part of what makes friendship desirable. Aristotle’s position is therefore not that pure altruism is achieved when self-interest vanishes. Rather, he denies that the presence of self-related benefit disproves the reality of other-regarding concern.

## The Limits of Concern: Friends, Family, and the Problem of Partiality

A major limitation of Aristotle’s account lies in the restricted scope of its strongest form of concern. Friendship is personal, selective, and generally partial. The language of “another self” is most naturally applied to close friends, family members, and perhaps fellow citizens with whom one shares a form of life. It does not generate an equal concern for every human being.

Aristotle’s ethics recognizes different degrees and forms of association. The household, village, and city are organized through various relationships of kinship, utility, affection, and political cooperation. Family members receive special concern because they share origin, identity, and history. Citizens may owe one another justice and a form of civic friendship. Close friends receive the deepest personal care because they share activity and character.

From one perspective, this partiality is a realistic and defensible feature of human morality. Human beings have limited time, attention, and emotional capacity. Intimate concern requires knowledge and sustained interaction. We cannot have thousands of relationships that involve the same depth of trust and mutual understanding. A moral theory that demands equal intimacy with all persons may be psychologically impossible and ethically counterproductive. Partiality can also have moral value: parents should care for their children in ways they are not required to care for strangers, and friends may reasonably prioritize one another in emergencies.

From another perspective, however, Aristotle’s restriction raises concerns about the universality of altruism. If the strongest form of other-regarding concern arises only where the other is sufficiently like me—virtuous, familiar, reciprocal, and integrated into my life—then those who fall outside these circles may receive weaker consideration. The poor, distant, foreign, socially excluded, or morally imperfect person may not count as “another self.” Aristotle’s framework risks making moral concern depend upon similarity and proximity.

This limitation is especially significant because “another self” can become a principle of exclusion. The friend is another self because he resembles me in virtue or shares my way of life. But those who differ radically may remain merely “others.” The theory can therefore support a hierarchy of concern: intense care for friends and family, civic concern for fellow citizens, and less developed concern for outsiders. Such a hierarchy may be compatible with Aristotle’s social and political context, but it sits uneasily with universalist ideals of human dignity.

Aristotle does not completely lack resources for extending concern beyond intimate friendship. His account of justice, civic association, and the common good suggests that human beings are not morally indifferent to strangers. Political community requires shared recognition of advantage and a willingness to act for the common good. Yet political friendship is not identical to personal friendship. It operates through general norms, reciprocity, and institutions rather than through intimate knowledge of another as an individual.

This distinction reveals both the strength and weakness of Aristotle’s synthesis. Personal friendship can generate an unusually deep form of altruism because it allows another person’s good to become part of one’s own practical identity. But the same mechanism makes the concern partial. The closer the relation, the more readily the other becomes another self; the farther away the person, the more difficult it is to sustain equivalent concern. Aristotle explains intimate moral bonds better than he explains universal moral obligation.

## Self-Seeking and the Unselfishness of Good Friendship

The question remains whether persistent self-seeking elements undermine the unselfish nature of good friendship. Aristotle’s answer depends on what counts as self-seeking. If self-seeking means any attention to one’s own good, then no friendship is unselfish, including the best friendship. Friends seek companionship, pleasure, assistance, understanding, and shared activity. They value how the relationship affects their own lives. On this definition, friendship is necessarily self-referential.

But if self-seeking means treating another person merely as a means to private advantage, then virtuous friendship is not selfish. The friend is not valued only for what he provides. The relationship can survive periods in which one friend cannot offer utility or pleasure. More importantly, the good friend acts for the other’s sake even when the action involves sacrifice. Aristotle’s examples of virtuous self-love imply that a person may relinquish wealth, honor, or even life for a friend or for a noble cause. Such action would be unintelligible if every motive were reducible to immediate personal gain.

One might object that sacrifice itself can serve the agent’s deeper self-interest. The person who sacrifices external goods gains moral nobility, self-respect, or the satisfaction of acting in accordance with virtue. In that case, even heroic friendship might be interpreted as enlightened egoism. Aristotle would likely accept part of this analysis. He does think that virtuous action is choiceworthy for the virtuous person and contributes to eudaimonia. But this does not make it egoistic in the objectionable sense. A virtuous agent does not perform noble action as a calculated transaction in which moral worth is exchanged for psychological reward. The agent’s good is constituted by the activity of virtue itself. To say that virtue fulfills the agent is not to say that the agent uses others for fulfillment.

This point can be expressed through the distinction between motive and consequence. A friend may experience joy because the friend has helped another. That joy does not prove that the help was motivated by self-interest. Indeed, it may be the appropriate emotional response of a virtuous person. Aristotle’s ethics does not demand that moral action be performed against one’s inclinations. The best agent takes pleasure in noble action. If concern for a friend is integrated into the agent’s character, acting for the friend’s sake can also be an expression of the agent’s own happiness.

The deeper issue is whether the self and the other are best understood as competing centers of value. Aristotle’s account suggests that they are not. The self is constituted through rational activity, relationships, practices, and commitments. A friend can become part of that self without becoming a possession. The good of the friend can be internal to the agent’s flourishing while remaining the friend’s good. This is not a logical contradiction unless we assume that goods must belong exclusively either to me or to another.

Shared goods make this possible. Conversation, friendship itself, common projects, civic life, and mutual recognition are not goods that one person must possess at another’s expense. They are realized through participation. When friends undertake a noble activity together, the activity benefits each without being divided into separate portions. The existence of mutual benefit therefore does not undermine the other-directedness of the relationship. It may instead reveal that some human goods are essentially relational.

Still, Aristotle’s theory should not be idealized. Friends can rationalize possessiveness as concern, confuse their own conception of the good with the friend’s good, or impose obligations in the name of intimacy. The idea that a friend is another self may encourage overidentification and failure to respect the friend’s independence. A person may claim to know what is best for a friend while actually seeking to preserve the relationship or control the friend’s choices. Genuine friendship requires not only shared identity but also recognition of the friend as a distinct agent.

Aristotle’s emphasis on virtue helps limit this danger. The virtuous person does not simply want the friend to conform to personal preferences. He wishes the friend to flourish according to the friend’s own rational and virtuous activity. Yet because Aristotle does not sharply distinguish autonomy from virtue in the modern sense, his account leaves room for paternalism. The friend’s good is objective, but the virtuous agent may presume to identify it.

## Beyond the Dichotomy of Egoism and Altruism

The opposition between pure egoism and pure altruism is often treated as exhaustive. Either a person acts for himself or he acts for others; either concern for the self is morally basic or concern for others is morally basic. Aristotle’s account demonstrates why this dichotomy is inadequate.

Pure egoism faces a conceptual problem in the context of friendship. If every other person is valued only as a means, then there can be no genuine friendship, only strategic alliances. Even relationships that appear intimate would be fragile, since the agent would abandon the other whenever a more advantageous option arose. Such a life would also undermine the agent’s own flourishing, because human beings require stable relationships involving trust and shared activity.

Pure altruism faces a different problem. If altruism requires acting without any relation to one’s own good, then it may demand an impossible division between the agent’s motives and identity. A person who never valued his own life, character, or happiness would lack the basis for sustained concern for anyone else. Furthermore, a morality that treats self-concern as inherently suspect would fail to explain why relationships, commitments, and personal attachments have legitimate value.

Aristotle’s alternative is not a compromise between two independent principles but a different account of the self. The self is not an isolated individual whose interests happen to intersect with those of others. It is a rational, social, and ethical being whose flourishing is realized through activities and relationships. Proper self-love and proper love of others are therefore internally connected. The virtuous person seeks his own good by pursuing virtue, and virtue includes justice, generosity, loyalty, courage, and concern for friends.

This synthesis can be called “self-referential altruism” insofar as altruistic concern originates in the agent’s relation to himself. I learn to wish good for another by understanding what it is to wish good for myself. I recognize the friend as another self because the friend is another being whose virtuous activity and flourishing can be incorporated into the shared horizon of my life. My concern for the friend refers back to the kind of self I am and the kind of life I am trying to live, but it does not thereby cease to be concern for the friend.

The success of the synthesis depends on distinguishing two senses of self-reference. In the first sense, self-reference is genetic or structural: self-love provides the model and foundation for friendship. In the second sense, self-reference is reductive: concern for the other is merely a disguised concern for oneself. Aristotle’s account supports the first but rejects the second. The friend’s good is connected to the agent’s good, yet the friend is still loved as someone whose good matters independently within the relationship.

The Aristotelian synthesis also shows that altruism may be relational rather than impartial. Modern ethical theories often treat impartiality as the measure of genuine moral concern. On that view, if I favor my friend merely because he is my friend, my concern is morally suspect. Aristotle does not share this assumption. He regards special relationships as ethically significant. A friend is not just one human being among many; he is someone with whom one shares a history, obligations, knowledge, and activity. To respond to a friend differently from a stranger is not necessarily to be unjust. It may be to acknowledge the moral reality of a particular relationship.

At the same time, the Aristotelian model cannot fully answer the demand for universal concern. Its most powerful language of identification applies to friends, relatives, and fellow citizens, not to humanity as a whole. The theory overcomes the egoism-altruism dichotomy locally and relationally, but not universally. It explains how I can care for another person without abandoning myself, but it does not by itself establish why I should care equally for persons with whom no bond exists.

## Conclusion

Personal friendship in the *Nicomachean Ethics* can plausibly be described as self-referential altruism, provided the phrase is interpreted with precision. Aristotle grounds friendship in self-concern: one must first possess a relation to oneself involving self-wishing, self-knowledge, enjoyment of one’s own existence, and commitment to one’s own flourishing. The virtuous form of self-love then becomes the model for concern for another. The friend is “another self” because the friend’s good is integrated into one’s practical identity through recognition, shared activity, emotional participation, and mutual pursuit of the good.

This account does not reduce friendship to egoism. In virtue friendship, each person wishes good for the other for the other’s sake. Utility and pleasure may accompany the relationship, and the friendship may contribute to each person’s eudaimonia, but these benefits do not exhaust its value. The friend is not merely a means to happiness; friendship is one of the activities through which a human being flourishes. Concern for another can therefore be constitutive of self-realization without being selfish.

Nor does Aristotle embrace pure altruism. He does not imagine that a good person must become indifferent to personal happiness, pleasure, or advantage. The friend is valued within a shared structure of flourishing, and the relationship involves reciprocity and mutual benefit. Self-seeking elements remain present, but they are transformed by virtue. The question is not whether the agent gains from friendship, but whether the agent treats the friend only as a means. Aristotle’s answer is that a virtuous person can gain from friendship while also valuing and serving the friend for the friend’s own sake.

The idea of the friend as “another self” is thus both the strength and the vulnerability of the theory. It explains how another person’s good can become intimately one’s own without being merely instrumental. Yet it also risks reducing otherness to similarity and limiting the deepest form of concern to close associates, family, and fellow citizens. Aristotle offers a compelling account of partial, intimate, and reciprocal altruism, but not a fully universal ethics of concern.

Ultimately, Aristotle’s achievement is to show that egoism and altruism are not the only available categories. The self can be constituted through relations, and concern for another can be an expression of a perfected self rather than a denial of selfhood. Good friendship is neither the annihilation of self-interest nor its disguised triumph. It is a form of shared flourishing in which the self reaches beyond its isolated boundaries while remaining itself. The persistence of self-related benefits does not undermine its unselfishness, unless unselfishness is defined so strictly that no human relationship could satisfy it. Aristotle’s friendship is genuinely other-regarding, but its other-regard is rooted in a conception of the self as social, virtuous, and capable of making another person’s good part of its own life.
