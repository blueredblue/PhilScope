## The Rule as a Dance: Wittgenstein, Music, and the Grammar of "Going On"

What is a rule? At first glance, it appears to be the most abstract of entities—a ghostly directive hovering above our actions, a self-contained, crystalline structure that dictates what we must do. We imagine that to follow a rule is to consult a hidden formula, a precise specification that, once grasped, leaves no room for ambiguity. Yet, the moment we attempt to articulate what this formula *is*, we find ourselves grasping at air. Is a rule a string of words? A mental image? A Platonic form? Or is it something far more mundane, yet far more profound: a pattern of behavior embedded in the fabric of our shared lives? This essay will evaluate the claim that rules are family-resemblance concepts—concepts that resist analytic definition and can only be understood through the practice of learning by example. Drawing heavily on Ludwig Wittgenstein’s later philosophy, particularly his account of rule-following as illuminated by musical composition and performance, I will argue that this claim is not only correct but constitutes the only viable escape from the philosophical paralysis induced by the "rule-following paradox." The tension between our craving for an essence of rules and the recognition that instruction, imitation, and the capacity to "go on" exhaust their content is not a problem to be solved, but a misunderstanding to be dissolved. The "essence" of a rule, I will contend, is not a hidden entity *behind* the practice, but the very normative regularity *of* the practice itself—a regularity that is revealed, tested, and lived in the act of application. Music, with its intricate blend of rigid notation and infinite interpretive freedom, serves as the perfect laboratory for demonstrating this truth.

### Part I: The Illusion of the Analytic Definition

The philosophical impulse to define rules analytically stems from a deep-seated desire for certainty. We want to say that a rule, such as "one must move the knight in an L-shape" or "one must not pass a red light," possesses a fixed, immutable content that exists independently of any particular application. This content, we imagine, can be captured by a set of necessary and sufficient conditions. An analytic definition would specify the rule's essence—that which makes it *this* rule and not another—in terms that are prior to and independent of the messy contingencies of human behavior.

Wittgenstein systematically dismantles this desire. In *Philosophical Investigations* (§143-242), he exposes the fatal flaw in this picture: the regress of interpretations. Suppose we try to define a rule by a proposition, say, "Add 2." We can interpret this proposition in infinitely many ways. Does "add 2" mean adding 2 to the current number, or adding 2 to the number of the step in the sequence, or something else entirely? To resolve this ambiguity, we would need a further rule for interpreting the first rule—say, "Interpret 'add 2' as adding 2 to the current number." But this second rule is itself open to interpretation. We are caught in an infinite regress, where no amount of explicit formulation can ever pin down the rule's meaning. As Wittgenstein famously states: "This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule. The answer was: if everything can be made out to accord with the rule, then it can also be made out to conflict with it. And so there would be neither accord nor conflict here."

The paradox arises because we assume that the rule is a *thing*—a mental state, a Platonic object, or a piece of text—that must be consulted and *interpreted* before it can be applied. But if the rule is a thing, then the application is always one step removed from the thing itself, requiring an act of interpretation that is itself ungrounded. The "thing" cannot contain its own application; it is inert, dead, awaiting the spark of a conscious mind to bring it to life. But what if the mind's spark is not an interpretation but an *action*? What if understanding a rule is not a matter of decoding a symbol, but of possessing a *technique*—a practical ability to respond in a certain way?

Consider the most basic example: a child learning to count. We do not teach a child the rule of counting by giving them a definition of "number" or "successor." We say, "1, 2, 3, 4... now you try." The child imitates. They make mistakes. We correct them. Eventually, they "get it." What have they gotten? They have not grasped a hidden formula; they have acquired a *disposition* to continue a series in a way that we, the community, deem correct. They have learned to "go on" in the same way. The rule "count by twos" is not a mental object they consult; it is a pattern of behavior they have absorbed through training.

This is the first blow to the analytic definition. A rule cannot be defined because any definition is just another sign, another rule, requiring its own application. The definition is not the rule; it is merely a *tool* used within the practice of rule-following. The meaning of the rule is not the definition, but the *use* to which the definition is put.

### Part II: Rules as Family-Resemblance Concepts

If rules cannot be analytically defined, how are we to understand the concept "rule" itself? Wittgenstein's answer, famously applied to concepts like "game" and "language," is that they are *family-resemblance* concepts. We do not find a common essence running through all games—there is no single property shared by chess, tag, solitaire, and the Olympics. Instead, we find a complicated network of overlapping and criss-crossing similarities: competition, skill, luck, amusement, rules, objectives. These similarities are like the features of a family—the build, the eye color, the gait—which overlap and criss-cross without any single feature being common to all members.

The concept "rule" operates in precisely this way. Consider the vast array of things we call rules: traffic laws, grammatical rules, rules of etiquette, mathematical rules, moral rules, rules of chess, rules of a game of tag, and the "rules" of musical interpretation. Do they all share a single essence? A traffic law is a written statute enforced by the state. A grammatical rule is a regularity of language use, often unspoken. A rule of etiquette is a social convention. A mathematical rule is a normative prescription for calculation. A rule of musical performance is an instruction for producing a certain sound.

What do these have in common? We might be tempted to say they all *prescribe* behavior. But a mathematical rule doesn't prescribe in the same way a traffic law does; there is no police force for arithmetic. We might say they all have a *normative* character—they tell us what *ought* to be done. But the "ought" of grammar is different from the "ought" of morality. We might say they are all *general*—they apply to many cases. But some rules are highly specific. The "rules" of a particular sonata are unique to that sonata.

The family-resemblance view does not deny that these things share features. It denies that there is a single, essential feature that *defines* them. The concept "rule" is held together by a web of overlapping similarities: generality, normativity, instruction, correction, evaluation, and the notion of a "standard" against which actions are judged. These features are present in different combinations and to different degrees across the family of rules. The "grammar" of the word "rule" is not a fixed list of properties but a dynamic field of uses.

This has a profound consequence: we cannot understand what a rule *is* by analyzing its definition. We can only understand it by observing how it functions in our lives. To understand the rule of chess, we must watch a game being played. To understand a grammatical rule, we must listen to people speaking. To understand a moral rule, we must see how people judge actions. The meaning of the rule is its *use* in the "language-game" of rule-following.

### Part III: The Musical Paradigm—Schubert's Staccato and Beethoven's Tempo

Nowhere is the inadequacy of analytic definition and the power of family-resemblance practice more evident than in the domain of music. Music is a rule-governed art. A score is a dense web of rules: pitches, rhythms, dynamics, articulation, and tempo markings. Yet, the meaning of these rules cannot be captured by any verbal formula. They can only be understood through the practice of learning by example. Wittgenstein, himself an accomplished musician, was acutely aware of this. He frequently used musical examples to illustrate his philosophical points, noting that understanding a musical phrase is akin to understanding a sentence—both require a "form of life."

Take, for example, the staccato marks in Franz Schubert's piano sonatas. A staccato dot above a note indicates that it should be played short and detached. This is the "definition." But any pianist will tell you that this definition is almost useless. How short? How detached? Is it a crisp, dry articulation or a soft, velvety separation? The answer depends entirely on the musical context. In the lyrical, singing lines of Schubert's *Impromptus*, a staccato mark often indicates a gentle *leggiero* touch, barely lifting the finger from the key, creating a shimmering, ethereal effect. In a dramatic, rhythmic passage of a *Moments musicaux*, the same dot might demand a sharp, percussive attack.

No analytic definition can specify this. The rule "play staccato" does not contain within itself the infinite variations of touch, weight, and timing that constitute a correct performance. The rule is, at best, a *placeholder* for a range of possible actions. The pianist does not *interpret* the rule in their mind and then translate it into sound. Rather, they have learned, through years of listening to recordings, imitating teachers, and experimenting at the keyboard, a *technique* of articulation. They have absorbed the practice of Schubertian performance. They have learned to "go on" from the score in a way that is deemed correct by the community of competent musicians.

The rule, in this case, is not a hidden essence but a *family resemblance* of possible touches. The dot on the page is a member of a family of marks that indicate "shortness," but the specific meaning of each mark is determined by its context within the musical phrase, the harmonic progression, the dynamics, and the overall character of the piece. The pianist's understanding is not a matter of decoding a definition but of *participating* in a practice.

Beethoven's tempo markings provide an even more striking illustration. Beethoven was notoriously obsessive about tempo. He was one of the first composers to use the metronome, and he provided metronome markings for many of his works. Yet, these markings are the subject of endless scholarly debate. The metronome mark for the first movement of the *Hammerklavier* Sonata is ♩ = 138, a tempo that many pianists find nearly unplayable and that seems to contradict the monumental, architectural character of the music. Similarly, the *Eroica* Symphony's first movement is marked ♩ = 60, which, if taken literally, produces a plodding, lifeless rhythm that undermines the heroic sweep of the opening.

What does this mean? Does it mean Beethoven's metronome marks are "wrong"? No. It means that the rule of tempo is not a numerical value. The metronome mark is a *pointer*, a rough guide, but the actual tempo must be derived from the *musical content*. The tempo must be such that the harmonic rhythm, the phrasing, and the dynamic contrasts make musical sense. The performer must find the tempo that allows the music to *breathe*. This is a judgment that cannot be reduced to a formula. It is a skill acquired through listening, playing, and internalizing the style.

The rule "play this movement at ♩ = 138" is not an analytic definition. It is a constraint that, when combined with a thousand other musical considerations, yields a unique, living performance. The performer must "go on" from the metronome mark, using it as a springboard, not a cage. They must understand that the *character* of the music—its *Allegro con brio*—is the deeper rule, and the metronome mark is merely one possible expression of that character.

Wittgenstein captures this perfectly in his *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*: "If a person has not got the faculty of 'going on' in this way, he cannot understand the rule." The "going on" is not a process of consulting a rule book; it is a *creative* act that is nonetheless *constrained*. The pianist who plays Schubert's staccato "correctly" is not merely obeying a law; they are *creating* the law through their performance. The rule is not *behind* the action; it is *in* the action. The meaning of the tempo marking is not the number; it is the *way of moving through time* that the performer embodies.

### Part IV: Resolving the Tension—The Essence is the Practice

We now face the central tension. On the one hand, we have an almost irrepressible intuition that there *must* be an essence to a rule. If there is no essence, how can we say that one performance is *correct* and another is *wrong*? How can we say that a pianist who plays Schubert's staccato with a heavy, legato touch is making a mistake? If the rule is just a family resemblance, if it is exhausted by instruction and imitation, then doesn't this lead to a radical relativism where anything goes?

This is the skeptical worry raised by Saul Kripke in his famous reading of Wittgenstein. Kripke presents the "skeptical paradox": if there is no fact about what a rule means—no essence—then there is no fact about whether my current action is correct. I might have been "quadding" instead of "adding" all along, and no past intention or mental state can rule this out.

Wittgenstein's response to this worry is not to provide a new theory of rules, but to dissolve the problem by shifting our perspective. The key is to recognize that the "essence" we are seeking is not a *thing* but a *practice*. The normativity of a rule—its ability to distinguish correct from incorrect—does not reside in a hidden formula but in the *agreement* of a community of practitioners.

Consider the musical example again. When a piano teacher corrects a student's staccato, they are not consulting a Platonic form of "Schubertian staccato." They are relying on their own trained ear, their knowledge of Schubert's style, and their sense of what "sounds right." This "sounding right" is not a subjective whim; it is grounded in a shared form of life. The teacher and the student belong to a community that has developed a particular way of hearing and producing music. This community has established standards of taste, technique, and interpretation through decades of tradition—through the performances of great pianists, the writings of musicologists, and the reactions of audiences.

This is what Wittgenstein calls "agreement in judgments." He writes: "It is what human beings say that is true and false; and they agree in the *language* they use. That is not agreement in opinions but in form of life." This agreement is not based on a shared essence but on a shared *practice*. We agree on what counts as a correct performance of Schubert because we have been trained in the same way, we listen to the same music, and we react to it in similar ways. This agreement is the bedrock upon which our normative judgments rest. It is not a matter of opinion; it is a matter of the way we live.

The "essence" of the rule, then, is not a hidden property but the *regularity* of the practice. The rule "play staccato" has an essence in the sense that there is a stable, repeatable pattern of behavior that counts as following it. But this stability is not derived from a definition; it is *constituted* by the ongoing activity of the community. The rule is not a thing that exists independently of us; it is a *normative pattern* that we create and sustain through our actions.

This dissolves the tension. We do not have to choose between an essence (a hidden formula) and a practice (a mere collection of behaviors). The practice *is* the essence. The rule is not *behind* the action; it is the *grammar* of the action. To understand a rule is to know how to participate in the language-game of following it. To follow a rule is to act in a way that is recognized as correct by the community.

Wittgenstein's famous aphorism, "I obey the rule blindly" (§219), is crucial here. Following a rule is not a matter of *interpreting* it and then acting. It is a matter of *acting*, period. The pianist does not stop to think, "What does this staccato mark mean?" before playing. They simply play. The rule is not a mental object they consult; it is a *skill* they have mastered. The skill is the rule. The "blindness" is not a lack of awareness but a lack of the need for interpretation. The rule has become part of their flesh and blood.

### Part V: The Normative Payoff—Losing the Essence, Gaining the World

What do we lose by abandoning the search for an analytic essence of rules? We lose the illusion of a transcendental guarantee of correctness. We can no longer say, "The rule dictates that I must do X, and anyone who does Y is objectively wrong, regardless of context." But what do we gain? We gain a much richer, more realistic, and more humane understanding of what it means to follow a rule.

We gain the ability to account for *creativity*. If rules were fixed essences, then there would be no room for innovation. But the history of music is a history of creative reinterpretation. A performer like Glenn Gould redefined the way we hear Bach, not by breaking the rules, but by finding new ways to "go on" from them. Gould's staccato, his tempi, his phrasing were radically different from those of his predecessors, yet they were deemed correct—even revelatory—by a new generation of listeners. How is this possible if the rule has a fixed essence? It is possible precisely because the rule is a family-resemblance concept. The rule provides a framework, a set of constraints, but within that framework there is an infinite space for novel applications. The rule does not dictate a single outcome; it *invites* a range of outcomes, all of which can be said to "accord" with it.

We also gain the ability to account for *learning*. If rules were essences, we could learn them by pure intellectual apprehension—by reading the definition. But we know that this is impossible. We learn rules through practice, imitation, and correction. We learn to play the piano not by reading a manual but by sitting at the keyboard and having a teacher guide our hands. This process of *training* (Wittgenstein uses the German word *Abrichtung*) is not a mere preliminary to understanding; it *is* the understanding. The rule is not something we grasp *after* we have learned; it is something we *are* when we have learned.

Finally, we gain a deeper appreciation for the *normativity* of rules. Normativity is not a mysterious property of abstract objects; it is a feature of our social lives. To say that a rule is normative is to say that it functions as a standard within a community. It is to say that we hold each other accountable for our actions. This accountability is not based on a hidden essence but on our shared commitment to a form of life. When a music critic judges a performance to be "wrong," they are not appealing to a Platonic form; they are appealing to the standards of the musical community. This is a real, powerful form of normativity, even if it is not absolute.

The musical case demonstrates this beautifully. The score is a set of rules. But the score is not the music. The music is the performance. The performance is an action, a "going on" from the score. The rule is the *possibility* of the performance. The essence of the Beethoven sonata is not the ink on the page, nor the sound waves in the air, but the *normative practice* of performing it correctly. This practice is a human creation, a product of our history, our culture, and our shared sensibilities. It is not less real for being created; it is, in fact, the most real thing we have.

### Conclusion: The Rule as a Dance

The claim that rules are family-resemblance concepts that resist analytic definition and can only be understood through the practice of learning by example is not a skeptical denial of rules. It is a positive, constructive account of how rules actually function in our lives. By examining the rule-following paradox, we saw that the search for an essence leads to an infinite regress of interpretations. By examining the family resemblance of rules, we saw that there is no single property that unites all rules. By examining the musical examples of Schubert's staccato and Beethoven's tempo, we saw that the meaning of a rule is not a hidden formula but a *technique*—a way of "going on" that is acquired through imitation and practice.

The tension between the desire for an essence and the recognition that practice exhausts content is resolved when we realize that the practice *is* the essence. The rule is not a ghostly entity behind our actions; it is the pattern of our actions themselves. The normativity of the rule—its power to distinguish correct from incorrect—is grounded not in a metaphysical realm but in the agreement of a community of practitioners. The rule is a dance, and we learn the dance by dancing.

Wittgenstein's philosophy does not give us a theory of rules; it gives us a *therapy* for the confusion that arises when we try to think of rules as things. It shows us that the meaning of a rule is its use, and that the use is a form of life. When we listen to a pianist play Schubert, we are not witnessing the application of a rule; we are witnessing the rule itself, brought to life in the moment of performance. We do not need to find the essence of the rule; we need only to listen. In that listening, we understand that the rule is not a constraint but a liberation—a way of giving shape to silence, of making time audible, of turning a mere mark on a page into a profound expression of the human spirit. The rule is not something we obey; it is something we *are*. And that, perhaps, is the only essence we ever truly needed.
