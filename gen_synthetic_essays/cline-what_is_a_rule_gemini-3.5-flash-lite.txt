# The Grammar of Action: Wittgenstein, Musical Practice, and the Anti-Essentialist Account of Rules

## Introduction: The Temptation of the Essence

There is a persistent, almost irresistible philosophical urge to locate the hidden machinery of meaning. We look at language, games, moral codes, and artistic performances, and we ask: *What makes a rule a rule?* Under the spell of this metaphysical craving, we assume that behind every successful application of a rule lies an explicit formula, an underlying blueprint, a foundational standard, or a mental mechanism that dictates each successive step. If someone correctly follows a rule—whether in arithmetic, chess, or musical performance—it is tempting to suppose they have accessed an abstract proposition that bridges the gap between the general command and the particular instance. 

This essay evaluates the claim that rules are not abstract essences waiting to be discovered, but rather *family-resemblance concepts* that resolutely resist analytic definition. Drawing on the later philosophy of Ludwig Wittgenstein, I shall argue that rules cannot be captured by necessary and sufficient conditions; instead, they are understood solely through the concrete, embodied practices of learning by example. 

To illuminate this anti-essentialist account, I will examine how Wittgenstein’s treatment of rule-following intersects with musical composition and performance practices—specifically the interpretive challenges posed by Franz Schubert’s staccato markings and Ludwig van Beethoven’s tempo indications. These musical cases reveal that the meaning of a rule is not a hidden entity distinct from its performance, but is instead entirely tied to its application in action. Finally, I will resolve the apparent tension between our intellectual desire to discover the essence of rules and the recognition that instruction, imitation, and the sheer human capacity to "go on" exhaust the entire content of rule-following.

---

## I. The Philosophical Anatomy of Rules: Universals, Essentialism, and the Analytical Impasse

Western philosophy has long been haunted by the Socratic question: *What is X?* When applied to normative guides, this question demands an analytical definition—a set of necessary and sufficient conditions that demarcates the extension of the concept "rule" from everything that is not a rule. Traditional epistemology and philosophy of logic sought to treat rules as analogous to mathematical axioms or logical propositions: universal, static, and transparently clear in their implications.

On this traditional view, a rule is an independent object—perhaps a Platonic form, a Cartesian innate idea, or a neurocomputational algorithm—that stands outside of and prior to its applications. When an agent acts in accordance with the rule, they are merely decoding this pre-existing entity. The rule acts as a railroad track laid out to infinity, dictating every future turn before the train ever reaches it. 

Yet, this essentialist model encounters immediate, devastating paradoxes. If a rule requires an interpretation to bridge the gap between the general rule and the particular case, and every interpretation itself requires another rule to govern it, we are launched into an infinite regress. As Wittgenstein famously diagnosed in the *Philosophical Investigations*: 

> "This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule." (§201)

If a rule can be interpreted in infinitely various ways, how does it ever constrain our behavior? The essentialist tries to solve this by postulating mental acts of understanding, intuitions, or inner mechanisms that grasp the rule all at once. But Wittgenstein counters that no inner state—whether a feeling of certainty, a flash of insight, or a mental image—can possess the normative force of a rule. A mental state is just a psychological event; it does not, by its mere existence, tell us what we *ought* to do. 

To escape this impasse, Wittgenstein abandons the search for the metaphysical essence of rules. In its place, he introduces two revolutionary concepts: *family resemblance* (*Familienähnlichkeit*) and the *language-game* (*Sprachspiel*).

When we look at the vast multiplicity of things we call "rules"—grammatical rules, legal statutes, traffic regulations, etiquette, the rules of tic-tac-toe, the rules of composition, and the unwritten rules of conversational turn-taking—we find no single common denominator that unites them all. There is no single core feature called "ruleness." Instead, as Wittgenstein writes regarding "games":

> "...we see a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing: sometimes overall similarities, sometimes similarities of detail. I can think of no better expression to characterize these similarities than 'family resemblances'; for the various resemblances between members of a family: build, features, eyes, gait, temper, etc. overlap and criss-cross in the same way.—And I shall say: 'games' form a family." (§66-67)

Rules are family-resemblance concepts. They form a sprawling, historically situated network of practices. Consequently, any attempt to provide a closed, analytic definition of a rule is doomed to fail because the boundaries of the concept are porous, elastic, and continuously expanding.

## II. Learning by Example and the Capacity to "Go On"

If rules do not possess an analytic essence, and if they cannot be reduced to a mental formula or an infinite series of interpretations, how do we learn and follow them? Wittgenstein’s answer shifts our philosophical gaze from the realm of abstract intellect to the domain of training, custom, and shared human activity (*Lebensform*).

Rules are learned *by example*. A child does not learn what addition is by mastering a formal axiom of set theory; they learn by being shown how to write numerals, how to line up columns, and how to carry the one in concrete instances. The teacher demonstrates: "Look, like this." The student imitates, stumbles, is corrected, and eventually masters the technique. 

This process relies heavily on ostensive definition, drilling, and corrective feedback. But crucially, the examples do not logically entail the future applications; rather, they initiate the learner into a practice. At a certain point in the training, the learner exhibits the ability to "go on" (*weitermachen*). They grasp the rhythm of the practice. 

This capacity to go on is not an intellectual deduction; it is a groundless foundation of human life. As Wittgenstein remarks in *On Certainty*: 

> "At the beginning of being convinced is not a proposition, but it is our acting, which lies at the bottom of the language-game." (§204)

When we teach someone a rule, we are training their reactions. We are shaping their dispositions so that, when presented with a novel case, they naturally, unhesitatingly converge upon the same response as other members of the community. 

This community of practitioners provides the normative anchor for rule-following. To follow a rule is not a private, solipsistic event; it is a public, social action. As Wittgenstein famously asserts in the "private language argument": 

> "‘Following a rule’ is a practice. And to think one is following a rule is not to follow a rule. And hence it’s not possible to follow a rule ‘privately’, because otherwise thinking one was following a rule would be the same as following it." (§202)

The normativity of a rule—the distinction between getting it right and getting it wrong—subsists entirely within the shared practices of a community. There is an external standard, but that standard is not a Platonic form in heaven; it is the established usage, the technique, the tradition of the community of practitioners.

## III. Musical Practice as the Crucible of Rule-Following

To see how anti-essentialism operates outside the domain of formal logic or arithmetic, we must turn to aesthetic practices where rules are notoriously fluid, interpretation is paramount, and formulas constantly break down against the pressures of creation. Music is the ultimate testing ground for Wittgenstein’s philosophy of rule-following.

Music is governed by rules: rules of counterpoint, harmony, voice-leading, meter, notation, and stylistic convention. Yet, every great composer and performer knows that the rules of music are not algorithmic constraints that produce mechanically uniform outputs. A musical score is not a set of instructions for a computer; it is a system of notations that requires human incarnation.

Wittgenstein himself was deeply attuned to the musical dimensions of thought. In his lectures and remarks (as recorded by students and compiled in works like *Culture and Value*), he frequently used musical examples to explain meaning and understanding. Understanding a musical phrase is not distinct from hearing it, playing it, or being moved by it; it is manifested in the *way* one plays it, the phrasing one adopts, the breath one takes, and the gesture one makes.

To demonstrate how the meaning of a musical rule is tied directly to its application in action, we can examine two specific historical cases: **Franz Schubert’s staccato markings** and **Ludwig van Beethoven’s tempo indications**.

---

## IV. Schubert’s Staccato Marks: Dots, Wedges, and the Gestural Context of Notation

Few composers illustrate the ambiguity and context-dependency of musical rules more acutely than Franz Schubert. In Schubert’s manuscripts and early editions, performers and editors have long wrestled with his idiosyncratic notation of staccato markings—specifically, his oscillation between dots ($\cdot$) and wedges ($\vee$ or $>\text{)}$ over notes.

In classical music theory textbooks, the distinction between a dot (indicating a gentle detachment or portato) and a wedge (indicating a sharp, biting staccato) is treated as a clean, analytic rule. Yet, when musicologists examine Schubert’s manuscripts, they discover a maddening inconsistency. Schubert frequently slurs dots together, applies wedges where lyrical flow is required, and uses strokes that blur the boundary between the two symbols. Generations of editors have tried to establish a definitive, rationalized rule for Schubert’s articulation, attempting to codify an essential meaning for every stroke of his pen.

Yet, this essentialist quest founders upon the reality of Schubert’s musical grammar. Schubert’s staccato marks do not possess an isolated, self-contained semantic value. Their meaning is entirely contingent upon the *musical context*, the harmonic tension, the instrumental register, and the overarching expressive character of the movement. A wedge over a note in the agitated opening of the *Death and the Maiden* quartet (*String Quartet No. 14 in D minor*) demands a ferocious, biting aggression. The exact same graphical mark in the melancholic, flowing expanse of the late *Piano Sonata in B-flat major* (D. 960) cannot be executed with mechanical harshness; it must be felt as a delicate, sighing pulsation—a vocal inflection rather than a percussive strike.

Here, the rule ("play staccato") dissolves into the practice of *how* one goes on within the specific stylistic world of Schubert. There is no algorithm that tells the pianist how much weight to drop into the key, how much pedal to apply, or how to shape the decay of the sound. The score provides examples, prompts, and historical clues, but the rule only comes alive in the performance. 

If a pianist asks, "Does this dot mean a ten percent reduction in note-value or a twenty percent reduction?", they are under the spell of the essentialist illusion. They are treating a musical rule as if it were a statute in a legal code. But as Wittgenstein would insist, the rule is the practice itself. Learning to play Schubert’s staccato correctly is not a matter of memorizing a semiotic key; it is a matter of acquiring a musical "form of life" through listening to great interpreters, studying the phrasing of singers (Schubert’s lifelong immersion in *Lied* is crucial here), and developing an ear for the tragic-lyric idiom of Viennese Romanticism.

## V. Beethoven’s Metronome: The Elasticity of Tempo and the Illusion of the Absolute

If Schubert’s staccato exposes the fragility of graphic rules, **Beethoven’s tempo indications** shatter the myth of the algorithmic rule. 

When Johann Nepomuk Mälzel invented the metronome in the early nineteenth century, Beethoven embraced it with revolutionary zeal. He was the first major composer to systematically affix metronome numbers (e.g., $\text{MM} \cdot \mathbf{♩} = 120$) to his scores, believing he had finally found a way to rescue his music from the arbitrary, sluggish, or frantic tempos of incompetent conductors. To the essentialist, Beethoven’s metronome mark appears to be the ultimate realization of a precise, objective rule: an unyielding mathematical standard that anchors the performance to an absolute truth.

Yet, music history and performance practice tell a radically different story. Beethoven’s own metronome markings have been a source of endless controversy among conductors, musicologists, and performers. Many of Beethoven’s indicated tempos—such as the breakneck speed of the finale of the *Hammerklavier Sonata* (Op. 106) or the tumultuous Alla Marcia in the finale of the Ninth Symphony—are so extreme that generations of virtuosos have found them physically unplayable or aesthetically grotesque.

Did Beethoven make mistakes? Were his metronomes broken? Or is there a deeper philosophical lesson here about the nature of musical rules?

Applying Wittgenstein’s framework, we can see that a metronome marking is *not* a rigid rule that dictates a mechanical speed. It is a historical gesture, a point of orientation, a signpost thrown out by the composer to indicate the general character and energetic pulse (*Takt*) of the music. 

A tempo in Beethoven is never merely a numerical frequency; it is an organic quality of movement. A performance at a slightly slower metronome setting can often *feel* faster and more urgent than a mechanical rendering at the exact numerical specification, provided the rhythmic articulation, dynamic contrast, and harmonic tension are managed with intensity. Conversely, a literal adherence to a number without regard for the acoustic resonance of the hall, the phrasing of the instruments, and the emotional arc of the movement results in musical lifelessness.

Beethoven himself reportedly grew ambivalent about metronome marks later in life, remarking that "anyone can feel the right tempo; our tempo indications can only reflect feelings which are related to the signature." He realized that no mechanical device could substitute for the living judgment of the performer. 

The rule "Play at $\text{♩} = 132$" does not contain within itself the secret of its application. The rule is understood only *through* the performance. The performance does not obey a pre-existing numerical essence; rather, the performance *constitutes* the reality of the tempo. As Wittgenstein might put it, to understand the tempo is to be able to dance to it, to breathe with it, to feel the logical necessity of the next downbeat when it arrives. The number on the page is an example, a prompt, a reminder—not a metaphysical rail track.

## VI. Resolving the Tension: The Illusion of the Essence vs. the Reality of Practice

We are now in a position to confront the central tension of this essay: the profound, psychological conflict between our craving for generality and essentialist certainty, on the one hand, and the anti-essentialist recognition that instruction, imitation, and the capacity to "go on" exhaust the entire content of rule-following, on the other.

Why do we resist Wittgenstein’s deflationary account? Why do we constantly yearn for the hidden essence of rules?

The temptation arises from a deep-seated philosophical picture: the picture of language and normativity as a vast, interconnected machine held together by invisible metaphysical rivets. We fear that if rules are "merely" practices, customs, and human habits, then anything goes. We worry that anti-essentialism collapses into radical relativism or nihilism—that if there is no ultimate, foundational essence to a rule, then rules are arbitrary, and we have no objective standard for correctness.

Wittgenstein diagnoses this anxiety in the *Investigations*: 

> "‘—So you are saying that human agreement decides what is true and what is false?’—It is what human beings *say* that is true and false; and they agree in the *language* they use. That is not agreement in opinions but in form of life." (§241)

The resolution of the tension lies in recognizing a fundamental shift in how we conceive of "foundation." The essentialist looks for a *vertical* foundation—a transcendent rule, a mental mechanism, or a logical formula that anchors our practices from above or from underneath. Wittgenstein points us toward a *horizontal* foundation—the shared ground of human action, training, and the "form of life" (*Lebensform*).

Rules do not need an underlying essence to be rigorous, binding, and objective. Consider a game of chess. Are the rules of chess arbitrary? In one sense, yes: humans invented them. There is no law of nature that dictates that a knight must move in an L-shape. Yet, once the game is underway, the rules are absolute, exacting, and unforgiving. If a player moves a rook diagonally, they are not playing chess. 

Where is the "essence" of chess located? Not in a Platonic realm of ideal chess positions, and not in the brain of the inventor, but in the totality of the living practice of chess-players across the globe. The rules are sustained by being played.

When this insight is brought back to artistic practices like Schubert’s staccato and Beethoven’s tempo, the resolution becomes luminous. We no longer need to search for the "true" meaning of Schubert’s wedge or the "correct" metronome click of Beethoven as if they were buried treasures waiting for archaeological excavation. We realize that the meaning of the rule is exhaustively manifested in the ongoing, evolving tradition of musical performance. 

Instruction occurs through example ("Play it like *this*, with this weight, with this breath"). Imitation bridges the gap between teacher and student. And the capacity to "go on" is the manifestation of mastery—a mastery that is neither an unconscious calculation nor a blind reflex, but an intelligent, cultivated sensitivity to the demands of the practice.

---

## VII. Conclusion: The Grammar of the Living Practice

To evaluate the claim that rules are family-resemblance concepts that resist analytic definition and can only be understood through learning by example is to undergo a fundamental conversion of philosophical vision. We must abandon the quest for the metaphysical anchor—the hidden mechanism, the transcendental rule, the essential formula—and look instead at the messy, vital, and irreducible world of human action.

Through Wittgenstein’s lens, we see that rules are not railroad tracks laid out to infinity. They are signposts, reminders, and techniques forged in the crucible of shared human training. As our exploration of Schubert’s staccato markings and Beethoven’s tempo indications demonstrates, musical practice lays bare the bankruptcy of essentialism: notation comes alive only through interpretation, and the rule is inseparable from its execution in sound and gesture.

The tension between our intellectual longing for an analytic essence and the fluid reality of rule-following is resolved when we stop viewing rules as static objects and start viewing them as dynamic practices. Instruction, imitation, and the spontaneous human capacity to "go on" do not leave anything out. There is no secret behind the practice; the practice *is* the foundation. In the end, we find ourselves returned to the simple, humbling truth voiced by Wittgenstein at the conclusion of his reflections on rule-following: 

> "If I have exhausted the justifications I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned. Then I am inclined to say: ‘This is simply what I do.’" (§217)

In the quiet turn of the phrase, in the sudden bite of a Schubertian staccato, in the surging pulse of a Beethovenian crescendo, we do not discover the abstract essence of a rule; we encounter the living grammar of human action itself.
