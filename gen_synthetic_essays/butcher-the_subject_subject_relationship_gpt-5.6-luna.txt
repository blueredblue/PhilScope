# From Mutual Objectification to Mutual Transcendence: Dialogue, Indeterminacy, and “The Two”

The claim that genuine person-to-person communication requires moving beyond Jean-Paul Sartre’s ontological conflict of mutual objectification is compelling, but it must be stated carefully. Sartre’s account of interpersonal existence does not merely describe an unfortunate psychological tendency that communication might overcome through goodwill. It identifies a structural tension within embodied selfhood: I encounter the Other as a freedom who can reveal me to myself, yet I also experience the Other as a perspective that fixes, judges, and objectifies me. The Other is both a subject like me and an object within my world. I cannot simply eliminate this ambiguity. Nor can I achieve a transparent community in which neither person ever objectifies the other. Any serious account of dialogue must therefore preserve some form of objectification as necessary for orientation, interpretation, and practical action.

Nevertheless, Sartre’s model can seem to leave communication trapped in a struggle for control. If each consciousness seeks to secure itself by reducing the other to an object, while simultaneously resisting being reduced to an object itself, then the relation appears organized by defense, rivalry, and domination. Against this impasse, a model of mutual transcendence proposes that persons meet not by attaining a final representation of one another, but by recognizing that each exceeds every representation. Paul Tillich’s account of indeterminacy within the subject-subject relation helps explain why such transcendence is possible: a person is not a closed object with a fully determinable nature, but a freedom whose future cannot be exhaustively predicted. Luce Irigaray’s notion of “the two” adds that genuine relation requires preserving difference rather than seeking either domination or fusion. The two are not incomplete halves awaiting synthesis; they are distinct subjects who sustain an interval in which neither absorbs the other.

The resulting model does not abolish objectification. It transforms the status of objectifying approximations. They become provisional hypotheses rather than definitive judgments, preparations for encounter rather than substitutes for it. Communication begins with a necessarily partial image of the Other, but it becomes genuinely dialogical when that image is exposed to revision by the Other’s response. The relation thereby shifts from a drive to dominate toward a dynamic anticipation of mutual change. Whether this shift is fully achievable is debatable. No conversation escapes embodiment, power, interpretation, or the possibility of manipulation. Yet genuine dialogue can replace domination as the governing orientation of communication when it treats both participants as centers of initiative whose reality is disclosed through, but never exhausted by, the exchange.

## Sartre and the Ontological Conflict of Recognition

Sartre’s analysis of the Other begins from the experience of being seen. In *Being and Nothingness*, the self is not simply a consciousness enclosed within its own interiority. It is also exposed to another consciousness that can apprehend it from outside. The famous experience of shame illustrates the point. I may be absorbed in an activity and understand myself through my own projects; then I hear a sound or notice another person’s gaze and suddenly encounter myself as visible, judged, and situated in a world that is not organized solely by my freedom. The Other’s look reveals me as an object.

This objectification is not merely an error in the Other’s perception. It discloses something real about my being. I am embodied, observable, and available to perspectives other than my own. I cannot make myself into pure subjectivity by denying that I occupy a place in the world. The Other’s gaze therefore gives me a form of access to myself that I could not produce alone. Yet this access is also threatening. The Other’s judgment appears to fix me within a meaning that I did not choose. I am no longer simply the one who acts; I become “the coward,” “the respectable person,” “the ridiculous person,” or some other identity assigned from without.

The conflict follows because the Other is not merely an object in my field of perception. The Other is a subject who can interpret me. I cannot control the meaning that I have for this freedom outside me. In response, I may attempt to recover control by objectifying the Other in turn. If I can make the Other an object for my consciousness, I can perhaps deny the Other’s power to define me. But the Other, being a consciousness, resists permanent objectification. My attempt to possess the Other’s meaning cannot finally succeed because the Other can always exceed the role I assign. The struggle is thus not accidental. Each consciousness desires recognition as a free subject, but each may seek that recognition by subordinating the other.

Sartre’s account of love, desire, and sadism dramatizes variations of this conflict. In love, one wishes to be freely chosen by the Other, not merely compelled. Yet one also wishes to secure the Other’s freedom so that it will continue choosing oneself. This produces a paradox: I want the Other’s freedom, but I want it to be freely devoted to me in a manner that guarantees my value. The attempt to possess the Other’s freedom destroys the very freedom whose recognition I desire. In sadism, the conflict becomes more explicit: the Other is treated as an object to be controlled, while the Other’s subjectivity is suppressed or instrumentalized. But even domination remains unstable, since the victim’s freedom cannot be annihilated by physical control alone. The dominated person can resist, reinterpret, or inwardly refuse the identity imposed upon them.

Sartre therefore presents interpersonal existence as a tension between subjectivity and objectivity that cannot be dissolved by a simple appeal to empathy. To understand another person, I must form some image of that person. I must perceive bodily gestures, hear words, interpret intentions, and place actions within a meaningful context. In this broad sense, I necessarily objectify the Other: I make the Other available as an intelligible figure within my world. But if I mistake this partial figure for the Other’s complete reality, I convert interpretation into possession. I cease to meet a person and instead relate to a fixed representation.

The problem is not objectification as such, but objectification that denies transcendence. A person is objectified when treated as wholly contained by an externally assigned identity. The Other becomes an object in the destructive sense when my description is no longer answerable to the Other’s capacity to respond, surprise, contest, and change. Sartre’s conflict thus points toward the need for a more relational ontology. The Other must be understood as both object-like and subject-like: accessible enough to be encountered, yet free enough never to be fully captured.

## Beyond the Conflict: What Mutual Transcendence Means

“Mutual transcendence” should not mean that two persons float above embodiment and history into a purely spiritual unity. Such a conception would risk reproducing the very abstraction it intends to overcome. Nor should it mean that each person simply respects the other’s privacy while remaining isolated. A relation in which neither person risks interpretation, influence, or transformation would not be communication but parallel solitude.

Mutual transcendence means that each participant recognizes the other as exceeding the identities through which the other is presently encountered. I may know you as a colleague, friend, parent, stranger, or political opponent, but none of these descriptions exhaust your capacity to become otherwise. At the same time, I must recognize that I too am not exhausted by the roles through which I appear. The relation becomes reciprocal when neither person claims a privileged status as the sole interpreter of reality. Each remains capable of being addressed and changed.

This model preserves the Sartrean insight that freedom is relationally exposed. The self does not first become a complete subject and then enter communication; it discovers itself through encounters in which it is seen, addressed, and challenged. But mutual transcendence modifies the conflictual structure by regarding the other’s freedom not primarily as a threat to my self-possession, but as the condition of a richer form of selfhood. If the Other were fully predictable, the Other could not genuinely answer me. If I could completely determine the Other, I would not be communicating with a subject but manipulating an object.

Mutual transcendence therefore involves a form of shared vulnerability. I must allow my interpretation of the Other to be corrected, and I must allow the Other’s response to alter my own position. This does not require surrendering judgment or accepting every claim made by another person. A dialogue can include disagreement, refusal, and boundaries. The key is that disagreement is directed toward a subject capable of response, not toward a caricature whose purpose is to confirm my superiority. The Other is not transcended by being elevated beyond criticism, but by being acknowledged as irreducible to the criticism I make.

## Tillich and the Indeterminacy of the Subject-Subject Relation

Paul Tillich’s concept of indeterminacy provides an important philosophical basis for this model. In Tillich’s account of personhood and the subject-subject relation, the person cannot be treated as a thing whose meaning is completely fixed by causal description or external classification. A subject is an existent who participates in freedom, meaning, and self-transcendence. The human being can be analyzed objectively, but objective analysis does not exhaust the person’s mode of being. There is always an excess between what can be known about a person and what that person is capable of becoming.

Indeterminacy here does not mean randomness or total unintelligibility. If the Other were absolutely indeterminate, no communication would be possible. Words, gestures, memories, and social practices provide patterns through which we anticipate one another. Indeterminacy means instead that the Other is not entirely determined by those patterns. A person has a history, but is not merely the sum of that history. A person occupies social roles, but can reinterpret or resist them. A person displays stable traits, but can respond in ways that disclose an unforeseen possibility.

The distinction between subject-object and subject-subject relations is crucial. In a subject-object relation, I can in principle aim at an exhaustive determination of the object. The object is approached as something to be measured, explained, classified, and controlled. In a subject-subject relation, by contrast, the other participant is not only something I observe but someone who observes, interprets, and responds to me. My knowledge is therefore reflexive and dialogical. The Other’s response can change the conditions under which I understand the Other. The relation has no final external vantage point from which one consciousness can completely survey the other.

This indeterminacy is not a defect in interpersonal knowledge. It is what makes interpersonal knowledge possible as communication rather than mere information transfer. A machine can be described by a complete set of operating specifications, at least in principle, without that description needing to answer to the machine’s freedom. But a person’s response to a description can alter the person’s relation to the description. If I tell someone, “You are afraid,” the statement may provoke denial, recognition, anger, or reflection. The person’s reaction is not an irrelevant addition to my knowledge; it becomes part of the reality I am trying to understand.

For Tillich, the subject-subject relation thus involves an irreducible risk. I cannot guarantee in advance how the Other will respond, nor can I secure a final interpretation that eliminates ambiguity. The relation is characterized by an “open” future. This openness can be experienced negatively as anxiety. To encounter another freedom is to face the possibility that my expectations will fail. Yet the same openness permits trust, forgiveness, creativity, and transformation. If the Other were determined in advance, there would be no basis for genuine love or ethical responsibility, because there would be no meaningful possibility of response.

Tillich’s indeterminacy also corrects an overly romantic understanding of mutual transcendence. The Other’s irreducibility does not make the Other automatically benevolent. A person can use freedom to deceive, exploit, or dominate. The fact that no one is fully predictable does not eliminate the need for practical judgment. Indeed, because persons are indeterminate, they can surprise us for better or worse. Mutual transcendence therefore requires a double attitude: openness to revision and attentiveness to evidence. I must not reduce someone to a static identity, but neither must I abandon all provisional assessments. The subject-subject relation remains ethical precisely because it requires discernment under conditions of incomplete knowledge.

## Irigaray and the Meaning of “The Two”

Luce Irigaray’s notion of “the two” deepens this account by insisting that genuine relation must preserve difference. Much Western philosophy, in her view, has tended to organize relation according to a logic of sameness. The other is either assimilated into the same conceptual order or treated as an inferior deviation from it. Even apparently harmonious unity can conceal the disappearance of one party into the categories of the other. A relation modeled on fusion may seem to overcome domination, but it can do so by erasing alterity.

“The two” names a relation in which neither person is reduced to a function of the other. The two are not two objects standing side by side, nor two fragments that must be reunited into a higher unity. They are two subjects who remain distinct while entering relation. The interval between them is not simply a distance to be eliminated. It is the space that makes address, desire, respect, and response possible. If there were no difference between us, I would not need to speak to you. If you were entirely absorbed into my identity, your reply would merely echo what I already am.

Irigaray’s account is especially significant for the problem of mutual objectification because it challenges the assumption that equality requires interchangeability. If both parties must become the same kind of subject according to a single universal model, then one party may be admitted into subjectivity only by adopting the dominant party’s terms. A relation of “mutual” recognition can therefore remain asymmetrical if the standard of recognition is defined in advance by one side. Irigaray’s emphasis on “the two” asks whether difference can be affirmed without being ranked.

In this context, mutual transcendence is not a movement beyond difference but a movement through difference. The Other transcends my objectification because the Other is not simply another instance of the same kind of self that I already understand. The Other’s difference may expose the limits of my categories. Yet this difference must not be romanticized into an absolute mystery that prevents communication. The point is not that the Other is unknowable, but that the Other is never reducible to my mode of knowing.

Irigaray’s language of sexual difference also introduces an important political and material dimension. The subject is always embodied, situated, and marked by social histories. A purely formal account of two abstract freedoms can conceal inequalities that determine who is allowed to speak, who is believed, whose body is treated as authoritative, and who is expected to adapt. The relation of “the two” cannot be established merely by adopting polite conversational rules. It requires attention to the institutional and symbolic conditions under which one person’s subjectivity may be systematically converted into an object for another.

This point complicates the ideal of reciprocity. In an unequal relation, both people may technically speak, but they do not necessarily possess equal power to define the terms of the conversation. A woman, racialized person, colonized subject, disabled person, or economically subordinate person may be addressed through stereotypes that precede any actual dialogue. The demand to “recognize mutuality” can itself become oppressive if it ignores the history by which one party has been denied subject-status. Irigaray’s “two” therefore requires not only interpersonal openness but a transformation of the structures that make some persons more easily objectified than others.

At the same time, “the two” resists the idea that liberation is achieved by becoming indistinguishable from the dominant subject. The goal is not for every person to occupy a single abstract position called “the human,” if that abstraction silently reproduces one culturally privileged form of subjectivity. Instead, communication must make room for plural forms of embodiment and expression. Mutual transcendence means that each person can appear as a subject in a way that is not required to mirror the other.

## The Necessity of Objectifying Approximations

The demand to recognize the Other as subject can be misunderstood as a prohibition against objectification in every form. Such a prohibition would be impossible. We encounter others through perception, language, memory, and conceptualization. To speak to someone, I need some sense of who that person is, what language they understand, what situation we share, and what their words may mean. Even the use of a proper name is a kind of identification. I cannot engage a person without forming an image, however flexible, of the person with whom I am engaging.

Objectifying approximations are therefore necessary. They allow me to prepare for interaction. Before a difficult conversation, I may consider the other person’s likely concerns, emotional state, history, and possible objections. A teacher anticipates how students may receive an explanation. A physician forms a working model of a patient’s symptoms and needs. A friend remembers what kinds of situations have previously caused another friend pain. Without such anticipations, communication would lack direction. Pure receptivity without any prior horizon would not be openness but incoherence.

The problem arises when preparation hardens into preemption. A working hypothesis becomes a final verdict. Instead of thinking, “This person may be frightened and therefore defensive,” I think, “This person is merely irrational.” Instead of asking how a colleague understands a conflict, I assign the colleague to a fixed type: ambitious, resentful, ignorant, manipulative. The approximation then functions as a substitute for encounter. I no longer listen in order to discover whether my expectation is correct; I listen only to collect evidence for what I have already decided.

The difference between responsible anticipation and domination lies in the possibility of revision. A provisional model respects the Other’s subjectivity by acknowledging that it may fail. A rigid model attempts to make the Other predictable in advance. This is where Tillich’s indeterminacy becomes practically important. Since the Other is capable of response and change, my preparation must remain open to interruption. I may need an initial orientation, but I must not confuse orientation with possession.

The relation between objectification and subjectivity can also be described in terms of levels. At one level, I objectify the Other by attending to observable features: words, facial expressions, conduct, social position. At another level, I recognize that these features are expressions of a subject who can reinterpret them. The first level is indispensable, but the second prevents it from becoming totalizing. The Other is an object of perception and interpretation, but also the source of a perspective that can contest my perception and interpretation.

This balance is particularly difficult in practical contexts requiring rapid judgment. In emergency situations, institutions, or political decisions, one cannot always wait for a fully reciprocal conversation before acting. Categorization may be necessary for coordination and protection. Yet the more consequential the judgment, the greater the danger that a provisional classification will become an identity imposed upon the person. A legal, medical, educational, or bureaucratic system can begin with useful abstractions and gradually treat those abstractions as more real than the people they were meant to serve.

The ethical task is not to abandon categories but to maintain their provisionality. One must ask: What does this description enable me to see? What does it conceal? How would the Other describe the situation? What evidence would force me to revise my account? These questions do not guarantee justice, but they interrupt the movement from interpretation to domination.

## The Moment of Dialogue: Relinquishing the Approximation

Actual dialogue requires a moment in which preparatory approximations are relinquished—not necessarily discarded, but surrendered as authorities. I approach a conversation with expectations, but I allow the conversation to become something other than the enactment of those expectations. This relinquishment is a form of risk. It means that I consent to the possibility that the person before me is not the person I had imagined, that the issue is not what I thought, and that I may be required to change.

This does not mean passively accepting whatever happens in conversation. To relinquish an approximation is not to lose all standards. It is to distinguish between standards and predictions. I may continue to believe that cruelty is wrong, that certain claims are false, or that a boundary must be maintained. But I must allow the Other’s actual speech to determine how those standards apply in the present situation. The conversation cannot be reduced to a script in which the other person’s words merely trigger responses already prepared.

Dialogue is therefore not simply an exchange of propositions. It is an event in which the participants’ identities and positions can be reorganized. I speak from a particular standpoint, and the Other hears me from another. The meaning of my words is not wholly contained in my intention; it emerges within a shared but contested situation. The Other’s response may reveal an implication of my statement that I did not see. I may discover that a phrase I considered neutral carries a history of exclusion. Conversely, I may learn that an accusation directed at me rests on a misunderstanding. In either case, communication is successful not because the parties have eliminated difference, but because each permits the other’s response to have consequences.

This dynamic can be described as anticipation of mutual change. Before speaking, I anticipate that I may influence the Other. In genuine dialogue, however, I also anticipate being influenced. The relation is not a one-way transmission from an already formed self to a passive recipient. Each person enters with projects and commitments, but each remains available to transformation. The anticipated future is not “I will make you understand,” but “we may come to understand the situation differently through what occurs between us.”

Here Irigaray’s “two” is essential. Mutual change does not mean that the participants merge into a single viewpoint. If dialogue succeeds only when one person abandons their distinctiveness, it has reproduced domination under the name of harmony. The two remain two. Their communication creates a shared space without abolishing the interval between them. The goal is not agreement at any cost but a relation in which disagreement can occur without reducing the dissenter to an inferior object.

A conversation can therefore be transformative even when it ends without consensus. Suppose two people disagree about a political practice. If each treats the other as an embodiment of stupidity or evil, the exchange is organized by objectification. If each listens closely enough to understand the other’s reasons, while retaining disagreement, the relation may become dialogical. Each may leave with a revised understanding of the issue, of the other’s position, or of the assumptions underlying their own. Mutual transcendence does not require identical conclusions. It requires that the participants no longer occupy exactly the same relation to their conclusions.

## Can Dialogue Replace Domination?

The strongest version of the claim asks whether genuine conversation can successfully replace the drive toward domination with a dynamic anticipation of mutual change. The answer should be affirmative but qualified.

Dialogue can replace domination as the governing orientation of communication. It can do so by changing what counts as success. In domination, success means securing control over the meaning, conduct, or self-understanding of the Other. In dialogue, success means sustaining a relation in which both participants can speak, respond, and revise. The other person’s independence is no longer an obstacle to communication; it is its condition. I do not seek to eliminate the Other’s unpredictability, because a completely predictable interlocutor would be incapable of genuine response.

Yet dialogue cannot eliminate every instance of objectification or domination. First, communication is embedded in relations of power. A person may be formally invited into dialogue while lacking the material freedom to refuse, leave, or speak without punishment. Second, language itself carries inherited classifications and exclusions. Even a well-intentioned speaker can reproduce objectifying assumptions. Third, people often enter conversation with unequal abilities to interpret, persuade, or control the consequences of speech. Finally, some situations involve deliberate manipulation, where one participant performs openness in order to gain information or legitimacy.

For these reasons, “genuine conversation” cannot be identified simply with conversation in which both parties take turns speaking. It requires ethical and political conditions: the possibility of response, some protection against retaliation, a willingness to revise, and recognition that the other’s account is not merely raw material for my purposes. It may sometimes require interruption rather than exchange, especially when dialogue is being used to delay justice or extract emotional labor from the subordinated party. The ideal of mutual transcendence must not become a demand that victims patiently converse with those who are actively denying their subjectivity.

Moreover, a single conversation may not overcome established patterns of domination. Someone who has habitually objectified others may momentarily speak the language of reciprocity without changing their underlying orientation. Mutual transcendence is better understood as a practice and discipline than as a permanent achievement. It must be renewed whenever an interpretation becomes rigid, whenever the Other is reduced to a type, and whenever the desire for certainty becomes a desire for control.

Nevertheless, the limitations of dialogue do not make the ideal empty. Even within imperfect conditions, the distinction between domination and mutual change remains morally decisive. A person can ask, before and during communication: Am I trying to understand, or merely to classify? Am I prepared to be answered, or do I only want confirmation? Do I regard the other’s freedom as a danger to be neutralized, or as the source of a reality I could not reach alone? Such questions reorient the encounter from possession toward participation.

## Conclusion: A Nonviolent Ontology of Encounter

Sartre is right that interpersonal relation contains an ontological conflict. I cannot meet another person without making the person appear within my world, and this appearance carries the danger of fixation. The Other’s look can objectify me, just as my look can objectify the Other. Since each consciousness seeks recognition as free, each may attempt to secure itself by controlling the other’s meaning. Any theory of communication that ignores this conflict will mistake courtesy for reciprocity and agreement for genuine relation.

But Sartre’s conflict need not be the final ontology of communication. Tillich’s concept of indeterminacy shows why the Other cannot be finally captured by objectifying description. The Other is a subject whose capacity for response keeps the future open. Irigaray’s notion of “the two” shows why this openness must not be converted into fusion. The aim is not to overcome difference by producing a single shared subject, but to sustain a relation between distinct subjects whose difference makes address possible.

The self must therefore balance two requirements. It must objectify the Other sufficiently to form provisional interpretations, anticipate needs, and enter the concrete situation of communication. At the same time, it must recognize that every such interpretation is partial and revisable. The Other-as-object is necessary for orientation; the Other-as-subject is necessary for dialogue. To deny the first is to abandon practical understanding. To deny the second is to turn understanding into domination.

The transition from preparation to dialogue marks the ethical center of the process. I may prepare by imagining who the Other is, but I must relinquish the authority of that image when the Other actually speaks. I must permit the encounter to interrupt my expectations. This does not require abandoning judgment, standards, or self-protection. It requires recognizing that the Other’s response can disclose something that my prior model could not contain. Communication succeeds when anticipation includes the possibility of mutual change.

Genuine conversation, then, does not abolish the struggle described by Sartre. It inhabits that struggle differently. Instead of treating the Other’s freedom as a threat to be mastered, it treats that freedom as the condition of a relation that can disclose more than either participant possessed alone. Instead of seeking final recognition from a person whose freedom must be controlled, it accepts the vulnerability of being known by someone who cannot be controlled. Instead of dissolving “the two” into unity, it creates an interval in which two persons can remain distinct and nevertheless become answerable to one another.

Mutual transcendence is thus not a miraculous escape from objectification. It is the ongoing transformation of objectification from a final verdict into a fallible approximation. The Other remains visible, interpretable, and situated, but never exhausted by visibility, interpretation, or situation. In that unfinished excess lies the possibility of conversation: not the replacement of difference with sameness, nor the elimination of conflict, but the shared anticipation that what each person is—and what the relation itself can become—has not yet been finally decided.
