The dramatic architecture of Plato’s *Republic* presents a profound philosophical puzzle: does the text prioritize the inculcation of virtue over metaphysical doctrine, utilizing its theoretical models as pedagogical instruments to shape the characters of Glaucon and Adeimantus? The claim holds a compelling surface validity. Throughout the dialogue, Socrates constructs the just city (Kallipolis) and the tripartite soul not as abstract discoveries of a transcendental reality, but as heuristic devices designed to move his interlocutors from a life of mere opinion to one of behavioral harmony. Yet, to conclude that the *Republic* ultimately abandons metaphysical truth in favor of moral pedagogy is to misread the dialogue’s underlying architecture. While the dramatic interaction between Socrates and his interlocutors subordinates metaphysical doctrine to the immediate task of character formation, the very theoretical models employed as pedagogical tools are structurally dependent upon the ontological claims they appear to supersede. The *Republic* does not succeed in abandoning metaphysical truth; rather, it reveals that the inculcation of virtue is inextricable from the metaphysical order, demonstrating that the word (logos) of the pedagogical model must be realized into the deed (praxis) of imitative practice precisely because both are anchored in the transcendental Good.

The genesis of the just city in Book II is explicitly framed as a pedagogical strategy, a response to the dramatic demands of Glaucon and Adeimantus. These interlocutors are not passive recipients of philosophical truth; they are provocateurs who insist that justice is merely a social compromise, a "second-best" good chosen by the weak to avoid suffering injustice. Glaucon demands that Socrates defend justice for its own sake, stripping away the reputational rewards that typically motivate the just. Socrates’ response is to construct the city-in-speech, a model that he introduces as a "large" argument designed to make the nature of justice "visible" and easier to apprehend. The city is not merely a political treatise; it is a didactic tool engineered to transform the dispositions of Glaucon and Adeimantus. By abstracting away the complexities of the actual city, Socrates strips the interlocutors of the psychological distractions of wealth, reputation, and luxury, forcing them to confront justice in its pure, structural form. The city is built as a mirror for the soul, but it is built as a mirror specifically because Glaucon and Adeimantus require a visible scaffold upon which to hang their ethical transformation.

This pedagogical function becomes even more pronounced in the construction of the tripartite soul. When Socrates locates the three parts of the soul—reason, spirit, and appetite—he is not conducting a psychological survey of pre-existing human nature in the manner of a modern empiricist. Rather, he is constructing a theoretical model to operationalize the concept of justice. The city provided the macrocosm; the soul provides the microcosm. The structural isomorphism between the two is not presented as a discovery of an independent metaphysical reality, but as a functional mapping designed to cultivate behavioral harmony. In the just city, the rational rulers command, the spirited auxiliaries obey, and the appetitive producers remain focused on production. In the just soul, the rational principle rules, the spirited part enforces its dictates, and the appetitive part finds its proper satisfaction. By aligning the interlocutors with this model, Socrates aims to reshape Glaucon and Adeimantus from individuals who pursue appetitive desires under the guise of rational self-interest into individuals who experience the harmony of a well-ordered soul. The tripartite soul is thus a pedagogical technology, a tool for the inculcation of virtue that renders the abstract concept of justice tangible and actionable.

However, the text immediately introduces a profound tension that undermines any simplistic reading of the *Republic* as a purely pedagogical treatise that jettisons metaphysics. Socrates explicitly claims ontological priority for his theoretical models, blurring the line between a constructed heuristic and a discovered reality. When the city is completed, Socrates declares it the "true" city, while the actual cities of the world are merely "fictitious" (592c). Furthermore, the city is said to exist "only in speech" (592b), yet Socrates insists that its parts correspond to the parts of the soul, which possess an ontological reality independent of the model. The tension lies in the fact that the city is a pedagogical tool *precisely because* it purports to reflect the true nature of reality. If the tripartite soul were merely a convenient fiction, it could not ground the moral imperative to be just. The pedagogical tool is given the weight of metaphysical doctrine; it is presented as a window into the fundamental structure of being. The text thus oscillates, at times elevating the model to the status of transcendental truth, and at other times demoting it to a mere heuristic for the benefit of its interlocutors.

This oscillation is most vividly illustrated in the dialogue’s treatment of the relationship between word and deed, and the role of mimesis in the realization of theory into imitative practice. The prompt highlights the necessity of examining how the *Republic* moves from the theoretical to the practical, from the word to the deed. Socrates acknowledges the inadequacy of a city that remains only in word; he notes that the true test of the city lies in its realization as a deed (592b). The dramatic interaction between Socrates and Glaucon and Adeimantus is itself an exercise in moving from word to deed. Through the dialectical exchange, the interlocutors undergo a psychological shift—a transformation of their desires and beliefs that constitutes a behavioral change. Yet, the dialogue does not end with the construction of the model; it demands that the interlocutors imitate the model in their own lives. The philosopher, having seen the Form of the Good, must return to the cave and rule, translating the theoretical insight into the deed of just action. The pedagogical model shapes the character of Glaucon and Adeimantus not merely by convincing them of an argument, but by compelling them to internalize the model and enact it. The word constructs the city; the deed requires the philosopher to live as a citizen of that city, thereby collapsing the distance between the theoretical model and the lived reality of virtue.

The dramatic interaction further complicates the hierarchy between metaphysical doctrine and virtue by introducing the element of myth and narrative, which undermines the purely rational, theoretical discourse. In Book X, Socrates famously argues that the poets must be banished from the ideal city because their imitative art (mimesis) presents a shadow of a shadow, thrice removed from the truth. Yet, the *Republic* itself concludes with the Myth of Er, a narrative that is explicitly non-dialectical and metaphysical in its cosmological scope. The myth describes the immortality of the soul, the cosmic order of reward and punishment, and the necessity of choosing the right life. This narrative is not a pedagogical tool for Glaucon and Adeimantus in the same way the city was; it is an ontological claim about the structure of reality. By ending the dialogue with a myth that reinforces the metaphysical consequences of justice, Plato undermines the earlier subordination of metaphysics to pedagogy. The behavioral harmony cultivated through the rational discourse of the city and the soul is ultimately dependent upon the metaphysical truth of cosmic justice. If the soul is not immortal and justice is not cosmically enforced, the inculcation of virtue loses its ultimate rational foundation. The myth of Er reasserts the ontological priority of metaphysical doctrine, revealing that the pedagogical tools of the earlier books are insufficient without the metaphysical anchor of the afterlife and the Good.

To assess whether the *Republic* succeeds in abandoning metaphysical truth in favor of cultivating behavioral harmony through rational discourse, one must look to the ultimate telos of the dialogue: the Form of the Good. The entire pedagogical apparatus of the just city and the tripartite soul is oriented toward the Good, which is introduced in Book VI as the ultimate metaphysical reality, the cause of being and the object of knowledge. The sun metaphor in the Allegory of the Cave establishes that the Good illuminates the intelligible realm, making knowledge possible, just as the sun illuminates the visible realm, making sight possible. Without the Form of the Good, the just city is merely a clever architectural and political arrangement, and the tripartite soul lacks its ultimate object of desire and knowledge. Behavioral harmony, in the Platonic sense, is not a self-sufficient state achieved through rational discourse alone; it is the alignment of the soul with the ontological order of the Good. The rational discourse of Socrates is effective precisely because it guides Glaucon and Adeimantus toward an apprehension of this metaphysical truth. The inculcation of virtue is thus not a replacement for metaphysical doctrine, but its consequence. The *Republic* does not succeed in abandoning metaphysical truth; it demonstrates that virtue cannot be cultivated without it.

Nevertheless, the dramatic interaction ensures that the metaphysical truth is never presented as a static dogma to be accepted, but as a living reality to be embodied. The pedagogical models of the city and the soul serve as the necessary bridge between the transcendental Good and the behavioral reality of the interlocutors. Glaucon and Adeimantus cannot simply be told that the Good exists; they must be shown how justice operates in the microcosm of the soul and the macrocosm of the city before they can grasp its metaphysical necessity. The tension between the ontological priority of theory and the pedagogical subordination of that theory to virtue is resolved not by choosing one over the other, but by recognizing that the pedagogical model is a necessary mediation. The word of the theoretical model is the vessel through which the metaphysical truth is transmitted, and the deed of imitative practice is the proof that the transmission has succeeded. The city-in-speech is a pedagogical tool, but it is a tool that works because it mirrors the metaphysical structure of the Good. The tripartite soul is a behavioral model, but it is a model that functions because it participates in the ontological order of the rational cosmos.

In conclusion, the claim that Plato’s *Republic* prioritizes the inculcation of virtue over metaphysical doctrine through the dramatic interaction between Socrates and his interlocutors captures a vital methodological truth of the text, but ultimately fails to account for the dialogue’s philosophical depth. The just city and the tripartite soul are indeed constructed as pedagogical tools, designed to shape the characters of Glaucon and Adeimantus and to cultivate behavioral harmony through rational discourse. The explicit statements regarding the ontological priority of theory are frequently undermined by the text’s reliance on constructed models and its acknowledgment of the city as a "fictitious" city. Yet, the role of word and deed in the realization of theory into imitative practice reveals that the pedagogical tool is inextricably bound to its metaphysical foundation. The *Republic* does not abandon metaphysical truth in favor of virtue; rather, it subordinates the *presentation* of metaphysical truth to the *pedagogical inculcation* of virtue, demonstrating that the latter is only possible because of the former. The dialogue succeeds not in separating virtue from metaphysics, but in showing that virtue is the behavioral realization of metaphysical truth, and that the philosophical life is the continuous, dramatic interplay between the rational word and the just deed.
