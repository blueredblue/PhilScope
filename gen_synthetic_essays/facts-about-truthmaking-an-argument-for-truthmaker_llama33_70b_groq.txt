The debate surrounding truthmaker necessitarianism has been a longstanding and contentious issue in the realm of metaphysics. At its core, truthmaker necessitarianism posits that for every true proposition, there exists a corresponding truthmaker that necessitates its truth. This thesis has been most notably defended by David Armstrong, who argues that truthmakers are ontological entities that make true propositions true. These entities can take various forms, including states of affairs, properties, and relations. The key idea is that for every true proposition, there exists a corresponding truthmaker that necessitates its truth. For instance, if the proposition "the apple is red" is true, then there must exist a truthmaker, such as the state of affairs of the apple being red, that makes this proposition true. This necessitation relation is understood as a modal relation, where the truthmaker ensures the truth of the proposition in all possible worlds.

Non-necessitarians, on the other hand, reject the idea that truthmakers must necessitate the truth of propositions. They argue that truthmakers can be contingent, meaning that they do not guarantee the truth of the proposition in all possible worlds. This rejection of necessitating truthmakers is often motivated by concerns about the ontological commitments of truthmaker necessitarianism. Non-necessitarians argue that Armstrong's formulation of truthmaker necessitarianism leads to an overly expansive ontology, where every true proposition requires a corresponding truthmaker.
