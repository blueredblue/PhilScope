### The Instantaneous Referent and the Tensed Predicate: Why Stage Theory Fails Compositional Semantics

**Introduction**

The philosophy of persistence has long been dominated by a tripartite division: endurantism, perdurantism, and, more recently, stage theory. While perdurantism (or "worm theory") holds that ordinary objects are four-dimensional sums of temporal parts, and endurantism holds that objects are wholly present at each time they exist, stage theory—championed most forcefully by Ted Sider and Katherine Hawley—offers a radical alternative. According to stage theory, an ordinary object like Kant is not a four-dimensional worm, nor a three-dimensional entity enduring through time. Rather, Kant is an instantaneous temporal stage, a momentary slice of reality. Persistence through time is then understood not as the identity of an object across times, but as a matter of counterpart relations holding between distinct instantaneous stages. The name "Kant" refers to a single stage, and statements about Kant’s past or future are made true by the existence of other stages bearing the appropriate temporal-counterpart relation to the stage in question.

This metaphysical picture is elegant and powerful, offering a unified account of time and modality. However, when we turn to the compositional semantics of natural language, we encounter a fundamental and seemingly intractable mismatch. The requirements that Sider and Hawley impose on referring expressions—namely, that they denote instantaneous stages—stand in direct conflict with the syntactic and semantic structure of natural language tense. Specifically, sentences containing multiple tensed verbs governed by a single referring expression, such as "Kant was born and died in Königsberg," generate a problem that neither the standard quantificational nor the operator-based analyses of tense can solve without either abandoning compositionality or collapsing stage theory into perdurantism. In this essay, I will argue that the stage theorist cannot successfully resolve this syntactic and semantic tension without abandoning the core metaphysical thesis that everyday objects are instantaneous temporal stages. The problem is not a mere technicality; it reveals a deep structural incompatibility between the ontology of stage theory and the compositional demands of natural language.

**Section 1: The Metaphysics of Stage Theory**

To understand the semantic challenge, we must first precisely articulate the metaphysical commitments of stage theory. Sider, in his seminal work *Four-Dimensionalism*, argues for a particular version of four-dimensionalism that he calls the "stage view." Unlike the "worm view" (perdurantism proper), which identifies an ordinary object with a maximal, temporally extended sum of stages, Sider claims that the ordinary object *is* one of these stages. The other stages are not parts of the object; they are distinct objects—temporal counterparts. Sider’s primary motivation is the analogy between temporal and modal discourse. Just as we say that I *could have been* a philosopher (where the modal predicate is true of me in virtue of a modal counterpart in another possible world), we say that I *was* a child (where the temporal predicate is true of me in virtue of a temporal counterpart at an earlier time). On this view, the semantic function of tense operators is analogous to modal operators: they shift the evaluation context to a counterpart.

Katherine Hawley, in *How Things Persist*, defends a similar view, though she is careful to distinguish between "stage theory" and "perdurantism." She argues that the fundamental entities are instantaneous stages, and that the apparent persistence of an object is a matter of a primitive "stagehood" relation—a relation of being a stage of the same "career." For Hawley, the name "Kant" refers to a particular stage, and the sentence "Kant was born" is true if and only if the stage referred to by "Kant" bears the appropriate temporal relation to a distinct stage that is born. The crucial point for both Sider and Hawley is that the referent of a proper name is *not* a temporally extended entity. It is a single, instantaneous slice. This is the "core metaphysical thesis" that I will argue is ultimately incompatible with compositional semantics.

The primary appeal of stage theory is its simplicity in handling certain indexical and cross-temporal predications. For instance, the sentence "I am hungry" is straightforwardly true of my current stage. The sentence "I was hungry" is true because my current stage has a temporal counterpart that is hungry. This elegantly avoids the problem of an object having incompatible properties (e.g., being hungry and not hungry) at different times by distributing these properties across distinct entities. However, this elegance comes at a severe cost when we consider the syntactic structure of natural language, which frequently forces a single referring expression to serve as the argument for multiple tensed predicates.

**Section 2: The Compositional Semantics of Tense**

Natural language tense is a compositional phenomenon. The meaning of a complex sentence is a function of the meanings of its constituent parts and their syntactic combination. Standard semantic theories treat tense in one of two ways. The first, the *operator analysis* (associated with Arthur Prior and Hans Reichenbach), treats tense as a sentential operator. The past tense operator, PAST, is a function from a proposition to a proposition. Thus, "Kant was born" is represented as `PAST(Born(kant))`. The operator shifts the evaluation time to some time in the past. The second, the *quantificational analysis* (often associated with David Lewis and others in the tradition of temporal logic), treats tense as a quantifier over times. "Kant was born" is represented as `∃t (t < t_now & Born(kant, t))`. Here, the predicate "Born" is a two-place relation between an individual and a time, and the existential quantifier binds the time variable.

Both analyses face a well-known challenge when dealing with multiple tensed verbs sharing a single subject. Consider the sentence "Kant was born and died in Königsberg." Under the operator analysis, if we treat the conjunction as a simple Boolean conjunction of two tensed clauses, we get `PAST(Born(kant)) & PAST(Died(kant))`. This is semantically well-formed. However, it does not specify the relative order of the two events. We know that birth precedes death, but the operator analysis as stated does not capture this. We could stack operators, but `PAST(PAST(Born(kant) & Died(kant)))` is nonsensical, implying both events occurred at the same past time. The standard fix is to introduce a more complex operator semantics, such as Reichenbach's reference times, but even this struggles with the fact that the subject term "Kant" must be evaluated at two different times.

Under the quantificational analysis, the sentence is represented as `∃t1 ∃t2 (t1 < t_now & t2 < t_now & t1 < t2 & Born(kant, t1) & Died(kant, t2) & In(kant, Konigsberg, t1) & In(kant, Konigsberg, t2))`. This correctly captures the temporal ordering and the fact that Kant was in Königsberg at both times. The crucial point is that the individual constant "kant" appears as an argument to predicates evaluated at *different* times. For the worm theorist, this is unproblematic: "kant" refers to a four-dimensional worm that has a temporal part at t1 which is born, and a temporal part at t2 which dies. The worm itself persists through both times, and the predicates apply to its parts.

But for the stage theorist, this is a disaster. If "kant" refers to an instantaneous stage, then it exists at exactly one time. It cannot be the subject of a predicate evaluated at t1 and also the subject of a predicate evaluated at t2. The stage is simply not present at t2. To make the sentence true, the stage theorist must invoke the counterpart relation. The sentence is true if and only if the stage denoted by "kant" has a temporal counterpart at t1 that is born, and a temporal counterpart at t2 that dies. This is a plausible metaphysical reading. However, it introduces a profound semantic mismatch, which I will now examine in detail.

**Section 3: The Intractable Problem of Multiple Tensed Predicates**

The fundamental problem is that natural language syntax requires a single referring expression to occupy a single argument position, but the semantics of multiple tensed verbs demands that this argument be evaluated at multiple times. The stage theorist's requirement—that the referring expression denotes an instantaneous stage—makes it impossible for that single entity to satisfy the predicates at different times. Let us examine why both the quantificational and operator analyses fail to bridge this gap without semantic distortion.

Consider the quantificational analysis again. The formalization `∃t1 ∃t2 (... & Born(kant, t1) & Died(kant, t2) & ...)` assumes that the constant "kant" can be substituted into a two-place predicate `Born(x, t)`. But if "kant" refers to a stage, then the predicate `Born(x, t)` is only true of a stage if that stage *is* born at time t. But a stage exists at only one time. If the stage is at time t*, then it can only satisfy `Born(x, t*)`. It cannot satisfy `Born(x, t1)` for t1 ≠ t*. To fix this, the stage theorist must change the semantics of the predicate. They might suggest that `Born(x, t)` is true of a stage x iff x has a temporal counterpart at t that is born. But this changes the meaning of the predicate "Born" from a simple property of an object at a time to a relational property involving a hidden existential quantifier over counterparts. This is no longer the standard semantics of "Born". The predicate "Born" in natural language does not mean "has a counterpart that is born." It means "was born."

This is not merely a technical quibble. It violates the principle of compositionality. Compositionality requires that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined. If the meaning of "Born" includes a hidden quantification over counterparts, then the syntactic structure of the sentence does not reflect its semantic structure. The sentence "Kant was born" would have a logical form like `∃y (Counterpart(y, kant) & Born(y))`, but this logical form is not recoverable from the surface syntax. The subject term "Kant" does not introduce a variable ranging over counterparts; it introduces a singular term. The stage theorist is forced to posit a massive divergence between surface grammar and logical form, a move that undermines the entire project of providing a compositional semantics for natural language.

The operator analysis fares no better. Suppose we treat "Kant was born and died" as `PAST(Born(kant)) & PAST(Died(kant))`. The operator PAST shifts the evaluation time. But the referent of "kant" is a stage. At the time of evaluation (say, t1), the referent of "kant" is still the same stage—the stage we picked out in the actual world. But that stage does not exist at t1 (if t1 is in the past relative to the stage's own time). So the predicate `Born(kant)` is evaluated at t1, but the entity "kant" is not present at t1. The operator cannot change the referent of the term; it only changes the time of evaluation. Thus, the operator analysis fails because it leaves the individual constant rigidly fixed to an entity that does not exist at the shifted time.

One might object that the stage theorist can simply claim that the name "Kant" refers to a stage, and the tense operator shifts the *counterpart* relation. That is, `PAST(Born(kant))` is true if the stage denoted by "kant" has a past counterpart that is born. But this is exactly the problem. The operator PAST is no longer a simple sentential operator; it is now a relation between an individual and a property. The semantic contribution of PAST is not just to shift the time, but to introduce a quantifier over counterparts. This makes PAST a non-compositional operator, as its meaning depends on the specific individual argument it applies to. In standard operator semantics, PAST is a unary function on propositions. Here, it must be a binary relation between an individual and a property, which is a radical departure from the syntax.

The intractability of the problem is most clearly seen when we consider the conjunction "and." In "Kant was born and died," the conjunction combines two verb phrases. The subject "Kant" is shared. In a compositional semantics, we must assign a single semantic value to "Kant" that can combine with both "was born" and "died." If "Kant" refers to a stage, that stage has a single time of existence. The property "was born" is a property that the stage can have only in virtue of its relation to a counterpart. The property "died" is a property it can have only in virtue of its relation to a *different* counterpart. But the conjunction "and" requires that the *same* entity satisfy both properties. The stage cannot satisfy both properties directly, because they are properties of its counterparts, not of itself. The stage can only satisfy them in a derivative sense—by being related to other entities. But this derivative sense is precisely what breaks compositionality, because the syntactic subject "Kant" does not denote the entity that actually has the property of being born or dying.

**Section 4: Sider's and Hawley's Proposed Solutions and Their Inadequacy**

Sider and Hawley are not unaware of this problem. Sider, in particular, has addressed the issue of cross-temporal predication. His solution is to adopt a "temporal counterpart theory" for the semantics of tense. On this view, the sentence "Kant was born" is true if and only if the stage denoted by "Kant" has a temporal counterpart that is born. Sider explicitly compares this to modal counterpart theory: just as "I could have been a doctor" is true if I have a modal counterpart who is a doctor, "I was a child" is true if I have a temporal counterpart who is a child. This is a coherent metaphysical picture.

However, as a *semantic* theory, it is deeply problematic. The issue is that Sider's account requires us to reinterpret the predicates "born" and "died" as relational properties. The predicate "born" no longer denotes a property of individuals; it denotes a relation between an individual and its counterparts. This is a radical revision of the semantics of natural language. In ordinary English, "Kant was born" does not mean "Kant has a counterpart that is born." It means that Kant himself underwent the event of being born. The stage theorist must claim that our intuitive understanding of the sentence is systematically mistaken, and that the true logical form involves a hidden quantifier over counterparts. This is an extremely high cost, as it undermines the idea that natural language is a reliable guide to ontology—a cost that is particularly ironic given that stage theory is often motivated by a desire to align metaphysics with our best scientific and logical theories.

A stage can have the property of *being such that it was born* or *being such that it will die*. These are, in a sense, properties of the stage itself, not of its counterparts.
