# Rationality, Adaptation, and the Limits of the Intentional Stance

Daniel Dennett’s Intentional Stance is one of the most influential attempts to explain how we predict and interpret the behavior of complex systems. Its central idea is both simple and powerful: when a system’s behavior is best predicted by attributing to it beliefs, desires, and rational strategies, we should adopt the “intentional stance” toward it. We may predict a chess-playing computer by assuming that it wants to win, believes certain facts about the board, and will choose the best move available to it. We may predict a person in the same way. The intentional stance is therefore not necessarily a metaphysical claim that minds contain little internal sentences called “beliefs” and “desires.” It is, at least initially, a methodological claim about successful prediction.

Dennett distinguishes the intentional stance from the physical stance and the design stance. From the physical stance, we predict a system by appealing to its physical constitution and laws: the movement of a planet, the electrical activity of a circuit, or the chemical reactions in a body. From the design stance, we treat a thing as having been designed to perform a function: we predict that a clock will indicate the time or that a thermostat will activate the furnace when the temperature falls. From the intentional stance, we treat the system as a rational agent. We identify its relevant beliefs and desires, and then infer what it ought to do given those beliefs and desires.

This strategy has genuine explanatory virtues. It provides a compact way of describing behavior that would otherwise require enormous physical or computational detail. It makes sense of linguistic action, planning, practical deliberation, deception, and cooperation. It also avoids a simplistic division between “real” beliefs and “merely metaphorical” beliefs. If attributing beliefs and desires reliably predicts a system’s behavior, Dennett argues, then those intentional states have a legitimate status in our explanatory practices.

Nevertheless, the Intentional Stance encounters a serious problem when it is applied to human beings. Its predictive success appears to depend on a presumption of rational optimality: the agent will act in the way that best satisfies its desires, given its beliefs and available information. This presumption is useful for idealized systems, such as chess programs or thermostats, whose relevant functions are narrowly specified. It is much less plausible for human beings, whose behavior is marked by confusion, self-deception, weakness of will, cognitive bias, conflicting motives, social conditioning, and outright mistakes. Dennett’s strategy can accommodate some deviation from rationality by adding further beliefs and desires, but this risks making the intentional stance unfalsifiable or explanatorily empty. It can also invoke evolutionary design to explain why human cognitive mechanisms generally approximate rationality. Yet this move depends on adaptationist assumptions that are themselves controversial.

The challenge becomes especially sharp in light of Steven Gould and Richard Lewontin’s critique of adaptationism. Not every trait is an adaptation; some traits are by-products, historical accidents, constraints, or “spandrels” generated by the structure of an organism rather than selected for the function they happen to serve. If human cognition contains non-adaptive traits, maladaptive tendencies, or accidental by-products, then we cannot assume that every recurrent pattern of behavior reflects an underlying rational design. Stephen Stich’s critique of Dennett develops a related point from the standpoint of cognitive fallibility: humans frequently reason badly, violate principles of rational choice, and fail to choose the means most likely to achieve their ends. A theory that treats rationality as the default risks predicting not actual human beings, but idealized agents who exist only in the theorist’s model.

An alternative is to adopt an empathetic or interpretive approach: instead of asking what an ideally rational person would do, we ask, “What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?” This approach does not abandon intentional explanation. It retains the importance of beliefs, desires, character, and perspective, but models the particular agent rather than an abstractly optimal one. It is not without difficulties: imagination can be egocentric, projection can distort interpretation, and “being like him” may be too vague to generate precise predictions. Still, when compared with a strict assumption of optimality, the empathetic approach offers a more coherent framework for understanding human behavior, especially when joined to empirical knowledge about the person’s cognitive limitations and social environment.

## The Logic of the Intentional Stance

Dennett’s Intentional Stance is best understood as a strategy of idealized abstraction. To predict the behavior of a system, we need not trace every causal detail. We can instead treat the system as an agent whose actions are organized by reasons. The procedure normally involves three steps. First, we attribute beliefs to the system about relevant features of the world. Second, we attribute desires or goals. Third, we predict that the system will act in a way that is rationally appropriate to those beliefs and desires.

Suppose I want to cross a river and believe that a bridge is nearby. If I believe that the bridge is intact and that crossing it will get me to the other side, then I will walk toward the bridge. The intentional explanation is not a complete causal history of my neural activity. It is a pattern-level explanation that identifies my action as intelligible in relation to my reasons. The point is not that the action must be consciously preceded by a neatly formulated syllogism. Rather, the agent’s behavior is organized in a way that can be captured by the structure of practical reasoning.

The strategy is particularly effective because human actions are often open-ended. Physical descriptions may tell us how a body moved, while intentional descriptions tell us why the movement mattered. The same bodily movement may be a greeting, an attack, an attempt to attract attention, or an accident. The intentional stance distinguishes these possibilities by situating behavior within a network of beliefs and desires.

Dennett also emphasizes that intentionality is not restricted to human beings. Animals, corporations, thermostats, and computers can all be treated as intentional systems to the extent that this stance successfully predicts their behavior. A thermostat can be said to “believe” that the temperature is below the desired level and to “want” the room to be warmer. Such language is not necessarily a claim that the thermostat has conscious experiences. It is a useful strategy for identifying the functional organization of the system.

The central difficulty is that intentional prediction is not merely the attribution of any arbitrary mental state. The strategy is constrained by rationality. We are supposed to attribute beliefs and desires in such a way that the agent’s behavior becomes predictable as a rational response. If an agent wants an outcome, believes that a particular action is the best available means, and is able to perform that action, we generally predict that the agent will perform it.

This requirement gives the intentional stance its power. Without some assumption of coherence, beliefs and desires would not explain behavior. But the same requirement produces a problem. Human beings often do not behave as rational-choice theory says they should. They do not always act on their strongest desires, update beliefs in accordance with evidence, or choose the most effective available means. Consequently, the stance must either weaken its rationality requirement or explain away human irrationality as a special case.

## Rationality and the Adaptationist Background

One way to defend the intentional stance is to claim that human cognitive systems are generally designed for rational problem-solving. Evolution, on this view, has shaped organisms to pursue goals effectively in environments where successful action improves survival and reproduction. Even if an individual sometimes makes mistakes, the underlying architecture is broadly adaptive. The intentional stance works because organisms have been organized by selection to behave in ways that are, on average, suited to their interests.

This argument is not necessarily explicit in every formulation of Dennett’s theory. Dennett distinguishes the intentional stance from a biological theory of natural selection, and he often presents it as a general predictive method applicable to artifacts and artificial systems. Yet when the stance is used to explain the systematic organization of human psychology, adaptationist reasoning becomes difficult to avoid. Why should we suppose that human beliefs and desires are sufficiently coordinated for rational prediction? Why should mental mechanisms be expected to track environmental structure? The evolutionary answer is that cognitive capacities were selected because they contributed to fitness.

There is a legitimate insight here. Organisms are not random collections of matter. Their capacities have been shaped by developmental and evolutionary histories. Vision, memory, motivation, and planning are not arbitrary. They are constrained by the problems organisms have had to solve. A theory of human behavior that ignored this organization would be incomplete.

But evolutionary history does not entail optimality. Natural selection does not produce perfect rational agents. It produces populations whose inherited traits have, under particular historical conditions, contributed sufficiently to reproductive success. Selection operates under constraints imposed by developmental architecture, phylogenetic history, energetic costs, available variation, and environmental change. A trait can be advantageous in one environment and harmful in another. It can persist because its costs are outweighed by benefits elsewhere. It can be a by-product of a selected structure rather than an adaptation in its own right. It can also be neutral or nearly neutral.

The distinction between adaptation and optimality is crucial. To say that a trait is adaptive is to say, roughly, that it contributed to fitness in a particular context. To say that a system is rationally optimal is to say that it selects the best action relative to its goals, beliefs, and constraints. These are not equivalent. A cognitive bias may have been useful in ancestral environments while producing systematically irrational judgments in modern contexts. A preference may increase reproductive success while undermining the individual’s reflective aims. A mechanism may be good enough for survival without being good at truth, consistency, or long-term welfare.

Dennett’s predictive strategy therefore faces a dilemma. If the rationality assumption is interpreted strongly, it is empirically false of human beings. If it is interpreted weakly, so that any behavior can be rendered rational by supplying suitable beliefs and desires, it loses predictive force. Adaptationist explanations do not automatically resolve this dilemma because evolution itself does not guarantee that all human traits are optimally designed for rational action.

## Gould, Lewontin, and the Challenge of Spandrels

Steven Gould and Richard Lewontin’s famous critique of adaptationism sharpens this concern. In “The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm,” they objected to the tendency of evolutionary theorists to explain every trait as though it had been directly selected for its present function. Their architectural analogy is important. In a cathedral, spandrels are the roughly triangular spaces that arise necessarily when arches support a dome. They may later be decorated or used for some purpose, but they were not necessarily designed as independent functional elements. Their existence is a consequence of architectural constraints.

Gould and Lewontin used this example to argue that biological traits may be by-products of other adaptations, products of developmental constraints, or historical residues. A trait’s current usefulness does not demonstrate that it was selected for that usefulness. Nor does the fact that a trait contributes to fitness show that it is an adaptation. Evolutionary explanation must consider alternatives such as structural constraint, drift, correlation, exaptation, and historical contingency.

Applied to the Intentional Stance, the lesson is that human cognitive patterns should not automatically be treated as components of an optimally designed rational system. Some aspects of human psychology may be spandrels. Language, for example, may have effects that extend far beyond whatever capacities originally supported its evolution. Emotional reactions, symbolic behaviors, aesthetic preferences, and forms of social reasoning may be by-products of mechanisms selected for other purposes. Even when a capacity contributes to survival, its particular errors and distortions may not have been selected for any function at all.

This creates tension with Dennett’s requirement that intentional systems be interpretable as rational agents. If a human trait is a by-product, it may not fit neatly into the structure of means-end rationality. A person may procrastinate not because procrastination is the best means to any consciously or unconsciously represented end, but because competing control systems, temporal discounting, affective states, and learned habits interact in a historically contingent way. A person may cling to a false belief not because the belief coheres with a hidden desire, but because the mechanisms responsible for belief fixation are imperfect products of a general learning system. A person may make a perceptual or moral error because the cognitive architecture is constrained in ways that were never “designed” to produce the truth in that domain.

The intentional stance can respond that even a by-product can be given an intentional interpretation. If procrastination is predictable as the result of a desire for immediate comfort, a belief that there is still enough time, and an inability to resist temptation, then no evolutionary adaptation is required. The behavior can be made intelligible through a sufficiently rich psychological description.

That response is partly successful, but it exposes another problem. The more irrational or structurally constrained the agent is, the more detailed and ad hoc the intentional attributions must become. The explanatory work shifts from a general principle of rational action to a retrospective construction of idiosyncratic motives. Instead of predicting that a person will do what best satisfies her desires, we say that she desired immediate comfort, underestimated future costs, had a habit of delay, felt anxious about beginning, and possessed conflicting second-order preferences. These claims may be true, but they are often inferred after the behavior has occurred. They do not necessarily provide an independent prediction.

The point is not that intentional explanations become impossible when adaptationism is rejected. Rather, they lose the presumption that the mind is organized as a unified, functionally rational system. Human beings may be intentional systems in a broad interpretive sense while remaining poor rational agents in the strict predictive sense. Gould and Lewontin’s critique therefore does not refute the intentional stance outright, but it undermines the evolutionary justification for expecting optimality as a default.

## Stich on Human Error and the Failure of Ideal Rationality

Stephen Stich’s critique focuses directly on the mismatch between rational-agent models and actual human cognition. Human beings routinely commit mistakes in reasoning. They affirm the consequent, ignore base rates, display framing effects, overvalue vivid evidence, maintain contradictory beliefs, and choose short-term rewards despite judging long-term alternatives superior. They are vulnerable to wishful thinking, self-deception, conformity, and motivated reasoning.

These patterns are not merely occasional accidents at the margins of otherwise ideal rationality. They are stable and widespread features of human cognition. If a theory predicts action by assuming that agents select the optimal means to their ends, it will regularly generate false predictions about human beings.

Stich’s challenge can be formulated in terms of practical reasoning. Suppose a person desires to remain healthy and believes that smoking increases the risk of serious illness. Suppose further that she has an opportunity to avoid smoking. A simple intentional-stances analysis predicts that she will refrain. Yet she smokes. One might reply that she has another desire—to experience immediate pleasure—or that she discounts the future, or that she believes she can quit later. These additions may improve the explanation, but they do not restore the original ideal of rationality. The person’s preferences are now divided, temporally unstable, or poorly integrated. The prediction depends on identifying which desire will control behavior, and that is precisely what the rationality assumption was supposed to tell us.

Similarly, consider someone who wishes to pass an examination but spends the evening watching irrelevant videos. The action may be explained by a desire for entertainment and a belief that studying can be postponed. Yet the person may also sincerely desire to study, believe that delay will harm performance, and judge the entertainment less valuable than the academic goal. The behavior is not simply the rational satisfaction of a coherent preference ordering. It reflects weakness of will, conflict between motivational systems, failures of self-control, and temporal inconsistency.

Stich’s criticism is especially damaging to the idea that irrationality can always be absorbed into the intentional vocabulary. If every mistake is redescribed as the result of some belief or desire, the theory becomes protected from counterexample. A person who acts against her stated interests is said to have had an unacknowledged desire. A person who reasons badly is said to have had a mistaken belief. A person who behaves inconsistently is said to have had changing preferences. But these maneuvers may merely redescribe the behavior rather than predict it.

There is also a distinction between intelligibility and rational justification. We can understand why a person made a mistake without treating the mistake as rational. A frightened person may flee from a harmless object because of a phobia. We can explain the flight by identifying fear, perceptual distortion, and learned association. But it would be misleading to say that fleeing was the optimal response to the actual situation. Intentional interpretation can make behavior psychologically intelligible while still acknowledging that the agent’s response was irrational, maladaptive, or based on false assumptions.

Stich’s point, then, is not that humans have no beliefs or desires. It is that a theory of human behavior must accommodate cognitive dysfunction, not merely reinterpret it as rational action under a more elaborate description. The human mind may be sufficiently organized for intentional explanation while falling far short of the ideal rationality needed for straightforward prediction.

## Can Dennett Escape the Problem Through Levels of Stance?

Dennett might reply that the intentional stance is not committed to perfect rationality. The physical stance is always available when the intentional stance fails. The design stance may also explain why a system has certain capacities without implying that it will use them perfectly. A chess computer can be designed to play well but still blunder because of limited search depth. A human can be generally rational while making local mistakes. Prediction is always relative to a level of description and a purpose.

This reply is important. Dennett’s theory need not demand that every agent be perfectly rational. In practice, interpreters use “bounded rationality.” They attribute only as much competence as is required for successful prediction. We know that people have limited information, limited time, limited memory, and limited computational resources. The intentional stance can therefore treat an agent as rational within those constraints.

However, bounded rationality does not eliminate the central issue. There is a difference between predictable limitation and arbitrary failure. If a chess program has a known search depth, we can predict the kinds of errors it will make. If a human has a stable tendency to discount the future, we can incorporate that tendency into a model. But many human errors are context-sensitive, emotionally triggered, socially mediated, and unstable across time. People are not merely rational agents with fixed computational limits. They are assemblages of partially integrated systems.

Moreover, once bounded rationality becomes sufficiently flexible, the stance risks becoming a post hoc interpretive tool rather than a robust predictive method. To say that an agent did what was rational given its exact limitations is often to say little more than that the behavior had some causal explanation. The original promise of the intentional stance was that beliefs, desires, and rationality would allow us to predict behavior without calculating every physical detail. If each departure from ideality requires a new set of special assumptions, the compression gained by the stance may be too small to justify its theoretical ambitions.

Dennett could also argue that prediction is pragmatic rather than absolute. We do not need a theory to predict every action with certainty. A useful strategy may be successful on average even if it fails in individual cases. This is true, but it does not settle whether rational optimality should be the default model of human beings. The question is not whether rational descriptions are sometimes useful. They plainly are. The question is whether they offer a superior general framework for predicting human behavior compared with models that begin from actual cognitive limitations and individual patterns.

## The Empathetic Alternative

The proposed alternative is to ask: “What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?” This formula combines simulation and interpretation. It asks the observer to imagine the situation from the other person’s perspective, while also recognizing that the other person is not simply identical to the observer. The phrase “if I were like him” is essential. It demands that the observer incorporate the other person’s fears, habits, knowledge, temperament, social position, and characteristic weaknesses.

This approach is more modest than the Intentional Stance’s rational ideal. It does not begin by asking what the best means to the agent’s ends would be. It asks what action would appear likely from within the agent’s psychological and social configuration. A jealous person may interpret an ambiguous remark as threatening. A risk-averse person may reject a profitable opportunity. A person raised in a rigid moral culture may experience a harmless choice as shameful. Someone who is exhausted or frightened may choose immediate relief over long-term benefit. Empathetic prediction takes these features not as deviations to be explained away, but as central components of the explanation.

The advantage of this method is that it preserves the intentional dimension of behavior without imposing ideal rationality. To understand a person is not necessarily to regard the person as optimally rational. We can ask what the situation looks like to the agent, what options are emotionally salient, what action feels possible, and what patterns of response the person has exhibited in the past. This produces a model of situated agency rather than abstract rational choice.

The phrase “if I were like him” also guards against a simplistic form of empathy. It does not ask the observer to imagine what the observer, with unchanged preferences and capacities, would do. That would be projection. Instead, it asks the observer to alter the imagined self in accordance with the target person’s characteristics. The prediction is therefore conditional: if I possessed his beliefs, dispositions, history, vulnerabilities, and social expectations, how would I respond?

This can provide a more realistic account of mistakes. Suppose someone refuses medical treatment because of an irrational fear of hospitals. The intentional stance might say that the person desires to avoid discomfort and believes the treatment is dangerous. The empathetic approach asks how the hospital appears to someone whose fear is intense, whose past experiences have reinforced it, and whose bodily sensations are interpreted catastrophically. The refusal then becomes predictable not because it is optimal, but because it is psychologically compelling within that person’s perspective.

Empathy is also better suited to understanding self-division. A person may want to lose weight and want to eat a dessert. She may judge one desire more important while being moved by the other. An ideal rational model tends to represent preferences as a unified ordering. Empathetic interpretation can represent the person as internally conflicted. It can predict not only the final action but also hesitation, regret, rationalization, and later reversal. These phenomena are central to human agency and are poorly captured by the assumption that one coherent preference set determines action.

## Limits of Empathetic Prediction

The empathetic alternative should not be romanticized. It has serious weaknesses. First, empathy may be unreliable when the observer lacks relevant experience. A person who has never experienced addiction, severe depression, cultural alienation, or chronic discrimination may be unable to simulate the perspective of someone who has. The phrase “if I were like him” may conceal an impossible demand.

Second, empathy can produce projection. Observers may attribute their own motives to others while believing they are imaginatively entering the other’s perspective. They may assume that everyone values autonomy, family, honor, safety, or success in the same way. Empathy can therefore reproduce cultural and personal bias.

Third, empathy may explain behavior after the fact without improving prediction in advance. Once we know what someone did, we can often construct a plausible narrative of how the action made sense from that person’s perspective. But several different narratives may fit the same behavior. Empathy needs constraints supplied by psychological research, biographical knowledge, observation of past behavior, and information about the situation.

Fourth, empathy can become morally dangerous if understanding is confused with justification. To see why a cruel action appeared compelling to someone is not to excuse it. A successful empathic interpretation may make an action intelligible while preserving the judgment that it was harmful or unjust.

These limitations show that empathy cannot simply replace all systematic modeling. It should be supplemented by empirical knowledge of cognitive biases, social structures, developmental history, and neural and behavioral mechanisms. A good predictive framework would combine several levels of explanation. It would use intentional language to represent the agent’s perspective, psychological science to identify predictable limitations, biological knowledge to explain capacities and constraints, and physical or computational descriptions where necessary.

The superiority of empathy is therefore comparative and methodological, not absolute. It is more coherent than strict optimality as a framework for human prediction because it begins with situated, imperfect agents. But it is most effective when disciplined by evidence rather than treated as unconstrained imaginative identification.

## Is the Intentional Stance Still Valuable?

The critique of Dennett should not lead to the conclusion that the Intentional Stance is useless. Rational interpretation remains indispensable. When someone tells us she is thirsty and reaches for a glass of water, it is reasonable to predict that she will drink it. When a person makes a promise, forms a plan, or pursues a project, intentional vocabulary identifies patterns that physical description alone would obscure. Rationality is often a regulative ideal in human interaction. We expect people to respond to reasons, and this expectation makes communication, cooperation, and moral responsibility possible.

The problem arises when this ideal is treated as an empirical default without sufficient attention to human cognitive architecture. Dennett is right that a system can be understood at the intentional level even if its internal mechanisms are complicated. But the intentional level need not be identified with optimal rationality. It can instead include distorted beliefs, unstable preferences, compulsive motives, social scripts, and predictable biases. An intentional system may be one whose behavior is organized around representations and goals, not one that invariably selects the best action.

This broader formulation would weaken the adaptationist assumptions behind Dennett’s strategy. It would recognize that natural selection produces organisms with mixed inheritances: adaptations, spandrels, constraints, historical residues, and mismatches between old mechanisms and new environments. It would also recognize that mental organization can be intentional without being unified. Human beings may have multiple systems of motivation, each with its own partial logic. Their behavior may be intelligible as the outcome of conflict rather than as the expression of a single optimal policy.

The revised stance would therefore be pluralistic. At the intentional level, we ask what the person believes, wants, fears, expects, and imagines. At the psychological level, we identify systematic biases, self-control failures, and emotional mechanisms. At the social level, we consider norms, roles, institutions, and power relations. At the evolutionary level, we distinguish genuine adaptations from by-products and constraints rather than assuming that every feature was selected for its present function. No single level should be allowed to dictate the conclusions of the others.

## Conclusion

Dennett’s Intentional Stance offers a compelling account of why beliefs and desires are indispensable to the explanation of behavior. It captures the fact that agents are not merely physical objects but systems whose actions are intelligible in relation to goals and representations. Its difficulty lies in the role assigned to rationality. The strategy predicts most effectively when an agent is assumed to choose the action that best satisfies its desires given its beliefs and circumstances. Yet actual human beings are not reliably optimal. They make mistakes, act against their considered judgments, cling to false beliefs, succumb to temptation, and behave in ways shaped by conflicting and partially unconscious motives.

Appeals to evolution do not resolve this problem. Gould and Lewontin’s critique of adaptationism shows why we should not assume that every cognitive trait is an optimally designed adaptation. Human psychology may contain spandrels, by-products, developmental constraints, historical residues, and maladaptive consequences of mechanisms that were useful in other contexts. Evolutionary explanation can illuminate the origins of cognitive capacities, but it cannot justify the presumption that those capacities produce rationally optimal behavior.

Stich’s critique reinforces this conclusion. Human error is not an occasional disturbance in an otherwise perfectly rational system. It is a pervasive feature of cognition. If every error is reinterpreted as rational relative to some hidden desire or mistaken belief, the intentional stance risks becoming a flexible redescription rather than a predictive theory. It can preserve intelligibility, but only by abandoning the strong claim that rationality reliably generates behavior.

The empathetic question—“What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?”—offers a more coherent starting point. It models agents as situated, historically formed, emotionally divided, and cognitively limited. It seeks to understand behavior from the agent’s perspective without requiring that the behavior be optimal. Although empathy is vulnerable to projection, cultural blindness, and post hoc storytelling, these problems can be addressed through evidence and psychological knowledge. Its central virtue is that it treats imperfection not as an anomaly to be rationalized away but as part of the subject to be understood.

The best conclusion is not that intentional explanation should be abandoned, but that it should be detached from adaptationist idealization. Human beings are intentional systems, but they are not necessarily optimally rational systems. A successful theory of their behavior must interpret beliefs and desires while also recognizing spandrels, cognitive biases, emotional conflict, and historical contingency. In that enlarged framework, empathy is not an alternative to intentionality. It is a corrective to the unrealistic assumption that intentionality always takes the form of rational optimality.
