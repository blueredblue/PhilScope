### The Paradox of Universal Revisability: Quine, S5, and the Limits of Modal Revision

Willard Van Orman Quine’s famous assertion in “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” that “no statement is immune to revision” stands as one of the most radical and influential challenges to classical epistemology. By dismantling the analytic-synthetic distinction, Quine argued that the entirety of human knowledge—from the most mundane empirical observation to the laws of logic and mathematics—forms a seamless web of belief. Confronted with recalcitrant experience, we may revise any part of this web, provided we make sufficient adjustments elsewhere. This thesis of universal revisability (UR) appears liberating, yet its formalization within a rigorous modal framework reveals deep and troubling paradoxes. Specifically, when we attempt to interpret UR within alethic modal logic S5—the standard logic for metaphysical necessity—we discover that both the wide-scope reading (all statements, including logical laws, are revisable) and the narrow-scope reading (only empirical statements are revisable, while logical laws are exempt) generate formal contradictions. These contradictions stem from the fundamental incompatibility between the dynamic, pragmatic nature of belief revision and the rigid, absolute structure of S5 necessity. Furthermore, attempts to resolve these contradictions by introducing ad hoc exceptions to the revisability thesis prove futile, as such exceptions inevitably fall prey to the same self-referential paradoxes they seek to avoid. Ultimately, the failure of UR within S5 does not merely invalidate a particular formalization; it reveals a deep tension between Quinean holism and any logic that treats necessity as an unassailable, fixed operator.

#### I. Quine’s Holism and the Two Scopes of Revisability

To understand the formal paradoxes, we must first clarify what Quine’s thesis entails. In Quine’s holistic empiricism, individual statements do not face the tribunal of sensory experience alone; rather, it is the totality of our beliefs that confronts experience as a corporate body. This leads to his famous dictum: “Any statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system.” Conversely, “no statement is immune to revision.” This is not merely a claim about psychological possibility but a normative epistemological principle: for any statement *p*, there exists some possible experiential or logical pressure that could rationally justify abandoning *p*.

However, the phrase “no statement” is ambiguous. A **narrow-scope** reading interprets Quine as saying that while empirical statements (e.g., “There are bricks on the lawn”) are revisable, certain foundational statements—particularly the laws of logic and mathematics—are *de facto* immune in practice, or even *de jure* unrevisable because they constitute the very framework of reasoning. This reading aligns with a more conservative holism, akin to Carnap’s distinction between internal and external questions, where the logical framework is presupposed. In contrast, a **wide-scope** reading takes Quine at his most radical: absolutely every statement, including the law of non-contradiction, the law of excluded middle, and the axioms of set theory, is subject to revision. If we adopt a paraconsistent logic, for instance, we might revise the law of non-contradiction. The wide-scope reading is the purest expression of Quine’s attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction, for it denies any principled boundary between the “constitutive” and the “empirical.”

The choice between these readings is not merely interpretive; it determines how we formalize the thesis in modal logic. To do so, we need a logic that can express modality—possibility and necessity—and the concept of revision itself. Alethic modal logic S5 provides a natural, albeit powerful, framework. S5 is characterized by the axioms: (K) □(A→B)→(□A→□B); (T) □A→A; (5) ◇A→□◇A; and (4) □A→□□A. Semantically, S5 corresponds to frames where the accessibility relation between possible worlds is an equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, transitive). This means that every world is accessible from every other world; there is no privileged “actual” world. Consequently, in S5, necessity is absolute: if a proposition is necessary, it is true in *all* possible worlds, and it is *necessarily necessary* (□□A). Similarly, possibility is absolute: if a proposition is possible, it is necessarily possible (□◇A). This rigidity is precisely what creates the tension with revisability, as we shall see.
