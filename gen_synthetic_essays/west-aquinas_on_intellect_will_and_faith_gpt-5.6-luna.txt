# Faith, Will, and the Transformation of Desire in Aquinas

## Introduction

The claim that St. Thomas Aquinas advances an *indirect descriptive volitionalism* regarding the acquisition of Christian faith captures something important in his account of belief, but it also risks misrepresenting the relation among intellect, will, grace, and evidence. Aquinas is not a voluntarist in the sense that the will arbitrarily manufactures conviction or chooses truth independently of intellectual apprehension. Faith is, for him, principally an act and habit of the intellect: it is the intellect’s assent to divine truth. Yet this assent is not produced by an intellectual demonstration that compels belief in the manner of a mathematical proof. The will moves the intellect to assent, and it does so under the influence of an apprehended good—ultimately, the universal and perfect good offered in God. The will does not replace evidence, but it determines whether the intellect will commit itself to a truth whose evidence is not sufficient to force assent.

This position raises a serious epistemological problem. If the evidence available to the believer does not compel belief, and if the will contributes decisively to assent, then does faith become epistemically unjustified? Does it amount to an act of desire imposed upon the intellect? Aquinas’s defense depends in part on his metaphysical doctrine that being and goodness are convertible. Whatever is, insofar as it is actual, is good; and the will is naturally ordered toward being under the aspect of goodness. Since God is the fullness of being and goodness, the will’s movement toward God is not an arbitrary preference but the deepest fulfillment of rational appetite. Nevertheless, the identity of being and goodness cannot by itself establish that a particular Christian proposition is true, nor can it turn insufficient evidence into sufficient evidence. The metaphysical doctrine explains why the will can be rightly attracted to divine truth, but it does not function as a substitute for demonstration.

The deepest resolution of the tension lies in Aquinas’s theological account of the transformation of the human agent. Faith is neither a purely theoretical conclusion nor a naked act of will. It is an intellectual assent elicited under the command of a will being moved by grace toward the supreme good. The will’s movement is therefore morally and spiritually significant: it reflects a reordering of love, desire, and self-understanding. Christian faith becomes intelligible not as an irrational preference but as the beginning of a transformation in which the heart is made receptive to a truth that exceeds natural intellectual proportion. The account remains vulnerable to objections concerning circularity, evidence, and the risk of motivated belief, but it avoids crude epistemic voluntarism by preserving the intellect’s essential role and by grounding the will’s movement in the objective goodness of its end.

## I. Aquinas and the Meaning of Volitionalism

To describe Aquinas as a volitionalist requires care. The human person, in Aquinas’s psychology, possesses distinct but interdependent powers. The intellect apprehends truth, while the will tends toward the good as apprehended by the intellect. The will cannot desire what is wholly unknown, and the intellect cannot be commanded to affirm a proposition whose content it does not in some way grasp. Thus neither faculty operates in isolation. The intellect presents objects to the will, and the will moves the intellect in the execution of its acts.

Aquinas accordingly distinguishes between the formal subject of faith and the cause that moves it to assent. Faith resides formally in the intellect because faith consists in assent to revealed truth. To believe is not simply to desire God, to admire Christianity, or to obey a religious practice. It is to affirm as true propositions concerning God and his saving action. Yet the intellect’s assent is not generated solely by the immediate evidence of the propositions themselves. The believer does not see the divine mysteries directly. The truths of the Trinity, Incarnation, and sacramental economy are not demonstrated by the natural intellect in the manner in which one demonstrates a conclusion from self-evident principles.

The will therefore has a distinctive role. Aquinas frequently describes the will as moving the intellect through command or *imperium*. This movement is indirect. The will does not enter the intellect and produce an assent as an efficient cause might produce an external effect. Rather, the will directs the intellect to consider, attend, investigate, and finally assent. The intellect remains the power that assents, but it does so because the will has commanded it to adhere to the revealed truth.

The qualification “descriptive” is useful here. Aquinas is not primarily offering a recommendation that people should believe whatever they choose. He is describing the psychological and metaphysical structure of faith: the intellect assents without being compelled by the intrinsic evidence of the object, and this assent occurs because the will moves it. His account is therefore descriptive in the sense that it identifies the actual mode by which faith is acquired. It is not a theory according to which all belief is justified merely because someone wills it.

The qualification “indirect” is equally important. The will does not directly assent to the proposition “Christ is risen” in place of the intellect. Nor does it directly determine the truth of that proposition. It indirectly moves the intellect by presenting faith as a good worth pursuing, by directing attention toward the signs of revelation, and by sustaining the intellect in assent where the proposition is not self-evident. This distinction prevents Aquinas’s view from collapsing into a simple doctrine of arbitrary choice.

Faith, on this account, has a structure involving at least three elements. First, there is the content of revelation: the propositions believed. Second, there is the intellectual apprehension of that content and of the reasons that make belief reasonable, such as testimony, miracles, the Church’s witness, and the coherence of the revealed order. Third, there is the will’s movement toward assent under the attraction of divine goodness. The intellect is not empty or wholly unmotivated; yet neither is it compelled by an exhaustive demonstration. The will supplies the decisive movement toward commitment.

## II. How the Will Moves the Intellect

The central philosophical question is how one power can move another without destroying the latter’s proper activity. Aquinas’s general psychology offers a framework for understanding this. The will moves the bodily powers as an agent moves instruments, but it moves the intellectual powers in a more interior and complex way. It can direct the intellect’s attention, regulate inquiry, command consideration, and determine whether the intellect will settle upon a conclusion when competing possibilities remain open.

This is familiar even outside religion. A person may understand a difficult argument but refuse to follow its implications because accepting them would threaten a cherished identity or interest. Conversely, a person may decide to pursue truth despite discomfort, social pressure, or fear. In both cases the will affects intellectual activity without changing the formal object of intellect. The will cannot make a contradiction true, but it can determine whether one attends to the relevant evidence, whether one investigates patiently, and whether one accepts a conclusion that is not demonstratively compelled.

Faith operates through this kind of movement, though in a more elevated form. The propositions of faith are not self-evident to the believer. Their truth is mediated through divine revelation, and the signs of revelation do not eliminate all possible doubt in the way a direct vision of the divine essence would. The believer must therefore choose to assent. Yet the choice is not made in a vacuum. It is made in response to the perceived authority and goodness of God, together with the credibility of the signs through which revelation is communicated.

Aquinas’s account also depends upon a distinction between the sufficient cause of assent and the compelling cause of assent. A proposition may be sufficiently grounded in the authority of a truthful witness without being so evident that the intellect is psychologically or logically forced to affirm it. In ordinary life, much knowledge depends upon testimony. One believes historical, scientific, and personal claims because trustworthy witnesses report them, even though one does not personally observe every fact. Divine testimony is unique because God is the First Truth and cannot deceive or be deceived. The problem is not whether divine testimony is reliable in itself but how the human subject comes to recognize that God is indeed the speaker.

Here the so-called motives of credibility become important. Miracles, prophecy, the holiness of the Church, the intelligibility of Christian teaching, and the witness of the Christian community can provide rational grounds for believing that revelation has occurred. These grounds may establish that faith is reasonable without making the mysteries themselves evident. They function as signs pointing to divine revelation. The intellect sees that the act of believing is not arbitrary; the will then moves the intellect to assent to the revealed content.

This arrangement gives faith a distinctive form of certainty. Aquinas insists that faith is certain because its formal object is God’s truthfulness. The believer does not believe because the proposition has become intrinsically evident but because God, who reveals it, cannot err. Faith is therefore more certain in its ground than many ordinary opinions, even though it is less evident to the human knower than truths directly seen or demonstrated. Aquinas can consequently distinguish the certainty of the object and cause from the weakness of the subject’s mode of access. The believer’s assent is firm because of whom one trusts, not because one comprehends the divine mystery.

This distinction, however, does not entirely solve the epistemological problem. If the believer’s recognition of divine revelation is itself uncertain, then the certainty of faith may appear to rest on an uncertain foundation. The appeal to God’s authority presupposes that the subject has adequate reason to identify a purported revelation as divine. Aquinas can respond that grace elevates the intellect and will, and that the signs of revelation make the act reasonable. Still, from the standpoint of philosophical analysis, the transition from “these signs are credible” to “I must assent to all that God reveals” involves more than ordinary inference. It involves trust, commitment, and a reorientation of desire.

## III. Being and Goodness: The Metaphysical Defense

Aquinas’s metaphysics of goodness supplies the background for understanding why the will is capable of moving toward divine truth. In the Thomistic account, being and goodness are convertible. This does not mean that every existing thing is morally good in every respect. Rather, whatever exists has actuality, perfection, and therefore some goodness insofar as it is. Evil is not a positive being opposed to good as an equal rival; it is a privation of a due perfection. Goodness is being insofar as it is desirable, perfective, or suitable to an appetite.

The will is rational appetite. It does not merely seek particular sensible pleasures; it seeks the good under a universal aspect. Every particular choice is made under some conception of what is beneficial, desirable, or fulfilling. Even wrongful action involves an apparent good. The thief desires money, security, status, or pleasure, although the manner in which these goods are pursued is disordered. The will’s fundamental orientation is toward goodness as such, and only finite and partial goods can fail to satisfy it completely.

God, as subsistent being itself, is also the fullness of goodness. Since created goods participate in being and goodness, they can attract the will. But they do so imperfectly and incompletely. The will’s ultimate end cannot be a finite object, because finite goods are limited and cannot satisfy the universal reach of rational appetite. The will is therefore naturally ordered to the universal good, even when the person does not consciously identify that good with God. Human desires are often scattered among partial goods, but their underlying dynamism points beyond those goods to a complete fulfillment.

This metaphysical structure helps Aquinas defend the rationality of the will’s movement toward faith. The will is not choosing a proposition merely because it happens to like it. It is responding to a perceived relation between the proposition and the ultimate good of human life. To believe in God is, at least implicitly, to recognize that one’s fulfillment lies not in finite self-possession but in participation in divine truth and goodness. The will’s movement toward revealed truth is thus connected with the will’s deepest orientation.

This argument can be formulated as follows. First, the will is ordered to the good. Second, the universal good is not adequately realized in finite objects. Third, God is the universal and perfect good. Fourth, the human person is incapable of attaining the fullness of this good through natural powers alone and therefore requires divine self-communication. Faith is the intellectual beginning of participation in that self-communication. The will can consequently move the intellect toward faith because faith concerns the good that fulfills the will’s deepest desire.

Yet the argument must be handled carefully. The convertibility of being and goodness does not entail the truth of any particular theological proposition. From the fact that the will seeks being as good, one cannot deduce the Trinity or the Incarnation. Nor does the fact that God is the supreme good prove that Christianity, rather than another religious tradition, is the true revelation. The metaphysical identity between being and goodness explains the desirability of the divine end, but it does not supply the historical and propositional content of revelation.

More importantly, ontological goodness is not identical with epistemic warrant. A thing may be good, and the desire for it may be healthy, without one’s belief about it being justified. Someone may desire justice but hold false beliefs about how justice is achieved. Someone may long for transcendence but adopt an incoherent theology. The will’s orientation toward the good can motivate inquiry and openness, but the truth of a belief still depends upon the intellect’s relation to reality.

Thus the metaphysics of being and goodness can defend Aquinas against a charge of sheer irrationality, but not by itself against every charge of epistemic unjustifiedness. It shows that the will’s movement is not foreign to reason or opposed to truth. It does not show that every willing of assent is legitimate. The will must be ordered to the true good, not merely to an imagined good. This is why Aquinas’s account requires moral and spiritual formation in addition to bare volitional decision.

## IV. The Problem of Insufficient Evidence and the Absence of Certainty

The most serious objection to Aquinas’s account concerns the status of assent when evidence is insufficient to compel belief. If the intellect assents to a proposition without seeing its truth, is the assent epistemically irresponsible? In modern terms, one might say that Aquinas permits the will to believe beyond the evidence. If so, faith appears to be an example of doxastic voluntarism: the person decides to believe what cannot be established.

Aquinas’s response begins by denying that faith lacks every rational ground. The absence of compelling evidence is not the absence of evidence altogether. Christian faith is supported by signs that disclose the credibility of revelation. The intellect can recognize that God’s revelation is a reasonable object of trust even though it cannot comprehend the content of revelation. There is therefore a distinction between evidence sufficient to establish credibility and evidence sufficient to produce direct intellectual vision. Faith requires the former but not the latter.

This distinction is significant. A person can be rationally entitled to trust a witness without possessing an argument that makes the witness’s report unavoidable. In interpersonal knowledge, confidence often depends upon the character, competence, and circumstances of the speaker. Faith in God is analogous but not identical. The divine speaker is perfectly truthful, while the human subject’s recognition of divine speech is mediated through history, testimony, and communal discernment. The believer’s certainty concerns God’s reliability; it does not mean that the believer has eliminated every psychological possibility of doubt.

Aquinas’s notion of certainty is therefore layered. Faith has a firm quality because the intellect adheres to the First Truth. But the believer does not have the same kind of evident certainty as one who directly sees an object or demonstrates a conclusion. The object is certain in itself; the mode of apprehension is obscure. This explains how Aquinas can affirm both the certainty of faith and the absence of intellectual compulsion.

The distinction also clarifies the role of the will. The will moves the intellect precisely because the intellect is not compelled by the object’s intrinsic visibility. If the truth were directly evident, the will would not need to command assent in this way. A person cannot coherently choose whether to affirm an immediately self-evident contradiction or a directly perceived object. But where evidence points without forcing, the will determines whether the intellect will commit itself. The will’s role is not a defect added to a complete act of knowledge; it is part of the structure of faith as a mode of knowing what exceeds natural vision.

Still, this solution invites the charge of epistemic unjustifiedness. Why should a person be permitted to assent when the evidence does not compel? One answer is that rationality does not always require compulsion. Many reasonable beliefs are based upon testimony, inference, practical trust, and cumulative evidence. Another answer is that the will has a legitimate role in beliefs concerning the meaning and ultimate direction of one’s life. The decision to trust is not equivalent to a random selection among unsupported propositions.

But these answers do not remove all danger. The will can be moved by fear, tribal loyalty, pride, wishful thinking, or the desire for security. A person may interpret ambiguous signs as divine confirmation because one wants them to be true. If the will’s influence on the intellect is not disciplined by evidence and self-criticism, faith can degenerate into projection. Aquinas’s theory must therefore distinguish the will’s movement by grace toward the true good from the will’s movement by passion toward an apparent good.

This distinction is not merely psychological. It is moral. The will’s orientation affects the quality of intellectual assent. A person attached to domination may resist truths that require humility; a person attached to wealth may reject teachings that demand generosity; a person attached to self-sufficiency may refuse dependence upon grace. Conversely, a person who seeks truth and goodness may become more receptive to revelation. The moral condition of the agent does not create the truth, but it can affect the agent’s ability to recognize and accept it.

## V. The Philosophical Tension: Universal Good Rather Than Demonstration

A genuine tension arises when faith is said to operate through the will’s inclination toward the universal good rather than through direct intellectual demonstration. On one side, this model appears philosophically attractive. Human beings are not disembodied intellects. Their beliefs are connected to their practical orientation, loves, hopes, fears, and sense of the good. A truth concerning the ultimate end of life cannot be encountered as a neutral fact unrelated to the agent’s existence. To understand Christianity is, in part, to confront a claim about what human beings are for and how they should live. It is therefore unsurprising that faith involves desire and commitment.

On the other side, this model threatens to make truth dependent upon attraction. If the will is inclined toward the universal good, and if faith is acquired when this inclination moves the intellect, then might one believe simply because one finds Christianity existentially satisfying? Would the believer’s “certainty” be nothing more than the psychological stabilization of a desired worldview? The danger is that the universal good becomes a subjective ideal projected upon reality. The will would then determine belief according to what it wants life to mean.

Aquinas can resist this danger by insisting on the objective character of the good. The good is not defined by whatever an individual happens to desire. It is grounded in being and perfected in God. The will is naturally ordered to the good, but it can be misdirected by ignorance, sin, and disordered passion. The fact that a person desires something does not make it good; rather, desire is judged by the real perfection of its object. The universal good is not whatever is universally desired, nor whatever satisfies the agent psychologically. It is the objective fullness of being in which human beings find their final completion.

Even so, a difficulty remains. How does the subject know that the good toward which the will is inclined is genuinely universal and not merely an elevated form of personal preference? Aquinas’s answer would require the cooperation of natural reason, moral experience, the order of creation, the signs of revelation, and divine grace. No single subjective feeling is sufficient. The will’s inclination must be tested by truth, by the rational content of the faith, by the fruits of holiness, and by the wider order of reality.

This is why the phrase “the will moves the intellect” should not be interpreted as “the will chooses the truth.” The will chooses to adhere to a truth proposed by the intellect as credible and by grace as salvific. It does not create the proposition or determine its content. The will’s freedom consists not in inventing the object but in consenting to it. This consent is free because the object is not fully evident; it is not forced by direct vision. Yet it is not arbitrary because the object is presented as divine truth and supreme good.

The tension can therefore be restated. Faith must be more than demonstration because the God revealed in Christianity exceeds the natural intellect’s capacity. But faith must also be more than desire because the Christian mysteries make objective claims about reality. If faith is reduced to demonstration, it ceases to be faith and becomes a form of natural knowledge. If it is reduced to desire, it ceases to be knowledge and becomes preference. Aquinas’s model attempts to occupy the middle position: faith is knowledge through testimony, made possible by grace, and completed by an act of will.

This middle position is philosophically unstable only if one assumes that knowledge must either be directly evident or wholly voluntary. Aquinas rejects that dichotomy. There are forms of rational assent grounded in trust, testimony, and practical commitment. Faith is a distinctive instance of such assent because the witness is divine and the content surpasses natural reason. The will does not compensate for a total absence of reasons; it enables a response to reasons that are insufficient to compel but sufficient to invite trust.

## VI. Grace, Charity, and the Transformation of the Heart

The theological resolution of the tension becomes clearer when faith is understood within Aquinas’s broader account of grace. Faith is not simply a psychological act produced by human effort. It is a supernatural habit infused by God. The human intellect and will are elevated so that the person can participate in a truth and goodness beyond the capacities of nature. The initiative belongs to grace, even though the human faculties genuinely act.

This point is essential. If the will moved the intellect entirely by its natural power, faith might appear to be a self-generated leap toward a desired ideal. But Aquinas holds that divine grace moves the will from within without abolishing freedom. The will is attracted by God as the supreme good, and this attraction heals and elevates the will’s natural orientation. Faith is therefore not merely a decision to accept a proposition; it is the beginning of participation in divine life.

The transformation involves the “heart,” understood not merely as emotion but as the center of practical orientation, love, and desire. Human beings are often divided within themselves. They know certain truths but do not want to live according to them. They recognize the vanity of finite goods but remain fascinated by them. They seek happiness while pursuing objects incapable of satisfying them. The moral life is consequently a struggle over what the will loves most deeply.

Christian faith reorders this struggle by presenting God not as one good among others but as the final good in which all other goods find their proper place. The believer begins to see created things as participations in divine goodness rather than as absolute possessions. Wealth, honor, pleasure, and power may retain legitimate value, but they no longer function as ultimate ends. This reordering of desire changes the conditions under which the intellect operates. The person becomes less resistant to truths that demand conversion and less dependent upon beliefs that protect disordered attachments.

In this respect, faith is morally transformative before it is fully intellectually comprehensive. The believer may not understand the mysteries in themselves, but assent to them begins to reshape the agent’s way of inhabiting the world. The human person learns to trust rather than control, to receive rather than possess, and to hope beyond the limits of present success. The will’s movement toward faith is therefore not an isolated choice but the beginning of a new pattern of desire.

Aquinas’s account of charity deepens this point. Faith without charity is possible as an intellectual assent, but it is not yet the perfect form of Christian life. Charity unites the soul to God through love and gives faith its living orientation. Faith tells the intellect who God is and what God has revealed; charity transforms the will so that God is loved for God’s own sake. In the mature Christian, faith and charity are not competitors. Faith provides the form of truth to which the intellect adheres, while charity orders the will to the divine good.

This relation also answers the concern that faith might be merely motivated belief. The proper end of faith is not emotional comfort or social belonging but communion with God. The grace that produces faith also tends toward humility, repentance, justice, and love of neighbor. These moral fruits do not prove every proposition of Christian doctrine, but they reveal whether the agent’s assent is participating in the transformation faith is meant to initiate. A purported faith that reinforces hatred, domination, vanity, or cruelty contradicts the goodness toward which faith is ordered.

The moral implications are therefore double. First, the will’s role means that belief is not morally neutral. The refusal to believe may arise from culpable attachment, just as belief may be corrupted by pride or fear. Second, because faith is supernatural, one cannot simply manufacture it by force of will. The person can seek, attend, pray, examine evidence, and dispose oneself toward truth, but the actual gift of faith depends upon grace. This preserves both responsibility and humility. The believer cannot boast that faith is an achievement of autonomous reason, while the nonbeliever cannot be treated as simply choosing falsehood in a morally transparent manner.

Aquinas’s theory thus points toward a moral epistemology in which intellectual assent is connected to the formation of the person. The question is not only whether the evidence is sufficient in abstraction but also what kind of agent one is becoming through one’s response to truth. This does not permit believers to excuse poor reasoning by appealing to personal transformation. Rather, it indicates that the highest truths may require not merely intellectual capacity but also purification of desire.

## VII. Evaluating the Claim

Does Aquinas advance an indirect descriptive volitionalism regarding the acquisition of Christian faith? The answer is qualified but affirmative. He is descriptive because he identifies the will’s actual causal role in assent where evidence does not compel the intellect. He is indirect because the will moves the intellect through command, attention, and orientation toward the good rather than by directly producing intellectual assent. The claim is therefore a useful characterization, provided that it does not suggest that Aquinas makes belief arbitrary or independent of truth.

The strongest feature of this view is its psychological realism. Human beings do not form ultimate beliefs through detached calculation alone. Their intellectual lives are shaped by their loves, fears, and practical commitments. A doctrine concerning God and the final end of human existence necessarily engages the whole person. Aquinas’s account recognizes that the will can either open or close the intellect to a truth whose evidence is not coercive.

A second strength is its compatibility with the nature of testimony. Much human knowledge depends upon trust in witnesses. Aquinas extends this structure to divine revelation while radically intensifying its ground: God is the First Truth. The will’s movement toward faith is thus not a leap into evidential emptiness but a response to a purported witness whose credibility is mediated through signs and whose authority, if recognized, is absolute.

A third strength is its integration of epistemology and ethics. Belief is not treated as an isolated mental state but as part of a conversion of life. The universal good toward which the will moves is not a merely theoretical object. It is the final perfection of the person. Faith accordingly involves a transformation of desire, and the rationality of faith is inseparable from the question of what kind of life fulfills human nature.

The weaknesses of the view are equally significant. The metaphysical identity between being and goodness does not provide a direct epistemic bridge to Christian revelation. It explains why God is desirable but not how one can infallibly identify a particular revelation as divine. Furthermore, appealing to grace may explain the believer’s experience from within theology without satisfying a philosophical demand for publicly accessible justification. The theory risks becoming circular if the signs of revelation are interpreted as credible only by someone already inclined to believe.

There is also a danger in emphasizing the will’s role. If insufficient evidence is understood too loosely, the theory could license motivated belief. Individuals might claim that their desire for transcendence or moral transformation justifies assent to any doctrine that satisfies them. Aquinas’s own framework resists this through the objective order of being, the discipline of reason, the communal mediation of revelation, and the moral fruits of charity. But the resistance must be actively maintained. The theory does not automatically prevent abuse.

Finally, the claim that faith is certain requires a careful distinction between metaphysical, epistemic, and psychological certainty. Faith may be certain in its formal ground—God’s truthfulness—without being evident to the believer in the way a demonstrated conclusion is evident. It may also coexist with emotional uncertainty, temptation, and intellectual difficulty. If “certainty” means the elimination of every possibility of doubt, then Aquinas’s account cannot plausibly provide it through ordinary human experience. If it means firm adherence grounded in the authority of divine truth, then faith can possess certainty without direct vision.

## Conclusion

Aquinas’s account of Christian faith is best understood as a mediated cooperation of intellect, will, and grace. Faith belongs formally to the intellect because it is assent to truth, but the will moves the intellect to assent when the object is not evident in itself. This movement is indirect: the will does not create truth or substitute preference for knowledge. It directs the intellect toward a proposition that is proposed through revelation and supported by signs, while the ultimate motive is the good of union with God.

The convertibility of being and goodness explains why this movement can be more than arbitrary desire. The will is rational appetite, naturally ordered toward the good, and God is the fullness of being and goodness. The movement toward divine truth therefore corresponds to the deepest orientation of human nature. Yet this metaphysical identity cannot by itself establish the truth of Christian propositions or remove every epistemic concern. Ontological goodness is not identical with evidential warrant, and desire for the universal good can be confused with attachment to an imagined good.

The tension is resolved, though not dissolved, by the theological account of grace and moral transformation. Faith is not a detached choice to believe unsupported claims. It is the beginning of a reordering of the heart in which the will learns to desire God as the final end and to place finite goods within their proper order. As desire is purified, the intellect becomes more willing to receive a truth that exceeds its natural power. The resulting assent remains non-demonstrative, but it is neither irrational nor merely subjective. It is a trustful adherence to divine truth under the influence of grace.

Aquinas therefore offers neither pure intellectualism nor crude voluntarism. His indirect descriptive volitionalism shows that the will is indispensable to faith because the human person must freely commit to a truth that is not forced by direct evidence. But the will’s proper movement is itself ordered by being, goodness, grace, and charity. Faith becomes epistemically defensible not because desire manufactures certainty, but because the transformed person is drawn toward the objective good that revelation discloses. The decisive issue is consequently not whether the will has entered the process of belief—it plainly has—but whether the will has been converted from the pursuit of partial and illusory goods toward the fullness of truth and goodness in God.
