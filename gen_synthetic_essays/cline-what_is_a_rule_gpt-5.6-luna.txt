# Rules, Resemblance, and the Possibility of Going On

The claim that rules are family-resemblance concepts that resist analytic definition, and that they can be understood only through learning by example, brings together two important but potentially conflicting ideas in Wittgenstein’s later philosophy. On the one hand, the concept of a rule appears to have no single essence shared by every rule. Rules may be grammatical, legal, mathematical, ethical, social, technical, or musical, and the similarities among these practices may be overlapping rather than universal. On the other hand, Wittgenstein’s account of rule-following insists that rules are not mysterious objects hidden behind their applications. A rule has its life in the practices through which it is taught, interpreted, followed, corrected, and extended. To understand a rule is not primarily to possess a definition or an image in the mind, but to have acquired the capacity to “go on” in the appropriate way.

This view does not simply replace definitions with examples. It also does not imply that rule-following is arbitrary, subjective, or reducible to imitation. Rather, it shifts the philosophical question. Instead of asking what inner essence all rules possess, we ask how the concept of a rule functions in different forms of life. Instead of asking what additional fact makes an action an application of a rule, we investigate the training, expectations, standards, and practices in which the distinction between correct and incorrect application has a role. Musical performance makes this especially vivid. A staccato indication in Schubert cannot be understood merely as a pictorial mark or as a general instruction to make notes “short.” Its significance depends on a tradition of performance, on the relation between articulation and phrasing, and on the ability of performers to recognize what counts as an appropriate realization in a particular work. Likewise, Beethoven’s tempo markings do not function as isolated numerical commands. Their force depends on the character of the music, conventions of performance, and the cultivated ability to hear what a tempo indication requires.

The tension between essence and practice can therefore be resolved by distinguishing two senses of “understanding.” If understanding means being able to provide a strict analytic definition, then many rules cannot be understood in that way. But if understanding means mastering a practice—knowing how to apply, explain, correct, and extend a rule—then examples are not inferior substitutes for definitions. They are among the primary vehicles by which the content of a rule is established. The content of a rule is not exhausted by a finite list of examples, but neither is it something over and above the practical capacity to continue in a manner recognized as correct.

## I. The temptation to seek an essence

Philosophical reflection on rules often begins with a model supplied by formal systems. A rule appears to be an abstract instruction that determines what is to be done in any relevant case. The rule of addition, for example, seems to determine how numbers are to be combined; a grammatical rule determines which expressions are permissible; a legal rule determines what conduct is required or forbidden. This model encourages the thought that a rule possesses a fixed content independently of its applications. Applications are then understood as events governed by an already complete standard.

There is nothing wrong with this model within certain contexts. A formal rule can be stated explicitly, and its scope can be carefully delimited. In mathematics, a definition may serve as a rule for transforming one expression into another. In a computer program, an instruction can be encoded in a form that is mechanically executable. In law, a statute may be articulated in general language and applied to particular cases. Such cases make it tempting to treat all rules as though they were explicit formulas, waiting to be consulted and then applied.

Yet even in these apparently precise cases, the philosophical picture becomes unstable when we ask how a rule determines its own application. Suppose someone is told to continue a numerical series according to the rule “add two.” The person writes 2, 4, 6, 8, and so on. What justifies the next step? One might say that the person has grasped the rule. But what is grasping the rule? If it is the occurrence of a mental image, formula, or interpretation, then that mental item must itself be interpreted in order to guide the next application. A further rule would be needed to determine how the first rule is to be understood, and then another rule to interpret the second. The attempt to explain rule-following by appealing to an inner interpretation thus threatens an infinite regress.

This is the central force of Wittgenstein’s discussion of rule-following in the *Philosophical Investigations*. The problem is not that rules fail to guide action. The problem is that we are tempted to imagine that their guidance must consist in some hidden object or process that accompanies every application. Wittgenstein asks what it means to follow a rule when no mental event can by itself determine all future applications. Any image or formula is compatible with indefinitely many ways of continuing. A finite history of performance does not, by itself, logically force a single future action. If one tries to find the determining force in an interpretation, the interpretation itself can be interpreted in multiple ways.

This thought has sometimes been taken to imply radical skepticism: perhaps no action is really determined by a rule, and perhaps every continuation can be made correct by inventing a suitable interpretation. Wittgenstein’s point, however, is not that rules are powerless or that correctness is unreal. It is that the determinacy of a rule does not come from a private mental accompaniment. The distinction between following a rule and merely seeming to follow it belongs to practices of training, correction, agreement, and use. The fact that a person has learned a rule is manifested in what the person can do, how the person responds to instruction, and whether the person can continue appropriately in new circumstances.

The search for an essence of rules therefore fails when it assumes that all rule-governed activity must be governed in the same way. The concept of a rule is not necessarily unified by one internal feature. It is more like a network of overlapping uses. A rule may be an explicit formulation, a customary pattern, a model, a legal standard, a musical convention, or a technique learned through repeated correction. These cases need not share a single defining property. They may resemble one another in different respects: rules are often teachable, normatively assessable, repeatable, socially recognizable, and capable of being extended. But no one of these features need be present in exactly the same form in every case.

This is what it means to regard “rule” as a family-resemblance concept. The idea is not that the concept is vague in a merely defective sense, as though a sharper definition would eventually be found. Nor is it that any activity may be called rule-governed at will. Rather, the unity of the concept is made by overlapping similarities, just as members of a family may resemble one another in build, voice, manner, or facial appearance without possessing one feature common to all. Different kinds of rules are connected by a pattern of analogies and contrasts. The concept is stable enough for ordinary philosophical and practical use, but its stability does not depend on an essential core.

## II. Family resemblance and the limits of analysis

The family-resemblance model offers a powerful critique of the assumption that every legitimate concept must have necessary and sufficient conditions. Yet it must be used carefully. To say that rules resist analytic definition does not mean that nothing can be said about them. Wittgenstein’s method is not a replacement of definitions with silence. It is a grammatical investigation of how words function. We can compare different uses of “rule,” identify characteristic features, and describe the circumstances in which the concept has a role. What we should not assume is that such a description must culminate in an essence common to every instance.

There is an important distinction between a concept’s lacking a single essence and its being unintelligible. Games are another familiar example. Chess, tag, and solitaire do not share one feature that can be extracted as the essence of every game. Still, it would be absurd to conclude that the word “game” has no significance. The concept works because the activities are related through overlapping similarities, and because speakers know how to use the concept in particular circumstances. Rules function similarly. A rule of chess, a rule of etiquette, and a rule of counterpoint may differ dramatically in their sources, explicitness, sanctions, and applications. Nonetheless, each can participate in a practice where actions are distinguished as correct or incorrect by reference to a norm that learners can be trained to recognize.

Analytic philosophy often seeks to isolate the condition that makes an action an instance of rule-following. But the relevant condition may be distributed across a practice rather than located in a single mental or physical event. Consider the act of following a rule in language. A speaker may follow a grammatical rule without consulting a formulation, entertaining a proposition, or imagining a paradigm. The speaker has acquired a competence. This competence is displayed in the capacity to produce and understand sentences, to notice errors, to accept corrections, and to handle novel cases. The rule is not absent merely because it is not consciously represented. Nor is it reducible to a statistical regularity in the speaker’s behavior, since the speaker can distinguish accidental repetition from a norm and can respond to a request such as “Use the past tense here” or “That is not how we say it.”

The same point applies to instruction. An instructor may state a rule, but the statement does not function in isolation. Its force depends on the learner’s background capacities and on examples that show how the formulation is to be understood. “Play this passage lightly,” “stress the first beat,” or “take this tempo with breadth” are not necessarily defective because they lack strict measurement. Their meaning is situated in a practice where teachers demonstrate, students imitate, and both compare performances with established models. Even when the instruction is precise, its practical significance may depend upon unspoken conventions. A numerical tempo marking, for example, does not settle every question about articulation, phrasing, or expressive character.

This does not establish that rules are merely implicit habits. A habit is a regularity in behavior, whereas a rule has a normative dimension. Someone can behave regularly without following a rule. A person might always turn left when leaving a building simply because of an unnoticed physical compulsion. By contrast, one who follows a rule can be corrected, can recognize a deviation, and can often explain what would count as an appropriate continuation. Rule-following belongs to a space of reasons and standards. The standards need not be represented by a formula, but they must be operative in a practice in which “right,” “wrong,” “appropriate,” and “mistaken” have intelligible uses.

The family-resemblance account thus has two negative implications and one positive implication. Negatively, it rejects the search for a universal essence and rejects the idea that a private interpretation can independently determine application. Positively, it directs attention to the forms of life in which rules operate. To understand a rule is to understand its place in a practice: how it is taught, what it contrasts with, how its violation is identified, and what kinds of continuation it licenses.

## III. Learning by example

The importance of examples follows directly from this practical conception of understanding. A learner does not normally acquire a rule by first receiving a complete definition and then deducing every application. Children learn linguistic rules through exposure, repetition, correction, and participation. Apprentices learn crafts by watching a skilled practitioner, attempting the task, and adjusting their performance. Musicians learn articulation, phrasing, and tempo through scores, demonstrations, recordings, rehearsals, and criticism. Examples provide a way of showing what the rule does.

Wittgenstein’s emphasis on examples should not be interpreted as an appeal to induction. A learner does not merely observe a number of cases and generalize a hidden regularity. To learn a rule is to be trained into a normative practice. The teacher’s examples are accompanied by responses such as “yes,” “no,” “not like that,” “again,” or “here the phrase must continue.” The learner gradually acquires a sensitivity to what counts as a relevant similarity between cases. This is crucial. Examples do not explain themselves. They become instructive within an activity of comparison and correction.

Suppose a child is taught the word “red” by being shown red objects. The child is not given a private definition that accompanies each future use. Nor does the child simply memorize a finite set of objects. The child is trained to recognize new cases as red, to distinguish red from orange or pink, and to use the word in appropriate grammatical settings. The examples establish a capacity of discrimination and continuation. Similarly, when a student learns a rule of harmony, the student does not merely store particular chord progressions. The student acquires an ability to hear and produce continuations as fitting or unfitting within a musical idiom.

This explains why imitation is philosophically significant. Imitation is not the opposite of understanding. In many practices, it is the beginning of understanding. The learner first reproduces a model without being able to state the principle behind it. Through repeated attempts and corrections, the learner comes to act with increasing independence. At a later stage, the learner may depart from the model while still remaining faithful to the rule. The ability to innovate appropriately is evidence that the person has not merely copied surfaces.

The capacity to “go on” is therefore more than the ability to repeat. It means being able to extend a practice beyond the exact cases used in instruction. A learner who can only reproduce memorized examples has not yet mastered the rule. A person who has learned to add two must be able to continue beyond the initial sequence. A performer who understands a staccato indication must be able to apply it in a new phrase, at a different dynamic, or in relation to a different accompaniment. The rule’s content is disclosed in this flexible continuity.

Yet “go on” does not mean that all continuations are equally legitimate. The learner’s capacity is constrained by the practice. There is a difference between an unexpected but intelligible continuation and a mere departure. A jazz musician may improvise beyond a written pattern, but not every sound counts as a successful continuation of the style. A grammatically creative sentence may extend a language, but it must still be recognizable as participating in that language. The possibility of innovation presupposes a background of mastery. One can depart from a rule only where there is a practice in which the departure can be understood as a departure.

This is why instruction and imitation do not exhaust rule-following in the sense of reducing it to passive reproduction. They exhaust the content of rule-following in a different sense: there is no further metaphysical ingredient required beyond the acquired ability to participate correctly in the practice. Once the learner can recognize examples as examples, respond to correction, and continue in a way that the practice counts as appropriate, asking for an additional hidden essence of understanding adds nothing.

## IV. Musical rules and the meaning of application

Music provides an especially illuminating field for Wittgenstein’s reflections because it combines explicit notation with embodied, historical, and interpretive practices. A musical score contains marks that function as instructions, but the marks do not determine performance in the manner of a fully specified algorithm. Their significance depends upon conventions and upon the performer’s trained ear. The score is not merely a description of sounds, nor is it an autonomous set of commands whose meaning is fixed independently of performance.

Consider Schubert’s staccato notes. A staccato mark is often translated into a simple instruction: play the note short or detached. But this translation is insufficient. The duration and character of a staccato note vary with tempo, instrument, register, articulation, metrical position, dynamic level, and musical context. A staccato note in a lively accompaniment may be light and buoyant; in another passage it may have a more pointed or tense quality. The mark can affect not only the length of an individual note but also the relation between notes, the shape of a phrase, and the balance between articulation and line.

Wittgenstein’s discussion of Schubert’s staccato is valuable precisely because it resists treating the notation as a self-interpreting command. To ask what the staccato mark “really means” apart from any performance may be to ask a misleading question. Its meaning is shown in the range of performances, corrections, and comparisons through which musicians learn how to realize it. One learns that a particular passage is not to be played as though every note were mechanically clipped. One learns how the articulation contributes to the character of the whole phrase. Such knowledge is not captured by a universal formula such as “reduce every note to half its notated value,” even if a formula can be useful in some pedagogical context.

The musical example also clarifies the relation between a rule and its application. The meaning of the staccato indication is not first constituted in an abstract realm and then transported into sound. The application is part of what gives the indication its practical meaning. This does not mean that the performer invents the rule anew in every performance. The performer is constrained by the score, by historical style, by the work’s internal structure, and by the judgments of a musical community. But those constraints become intelligible only in the activity of performance. The mark means what it does because musicians have learned to respond to it in characteristic ways.

Beethoven’s tempo indications provide a related example. A tempo marking may appear to be more determinate than an articulation mark because it can be expressed numerically or by a conventional term such as *Allegro*, *Adagio*, or *Andante*. Yet a tempo number does not by itself settle the character of a movement. The same metronomic speed may feel radically different depending on meter, texture, phrase structure, resonance, and articulation. Conversely, a performer may preserve the musical character of a marking while making subtle adjustments in ways that a rigid numerical interpretation would not anticipate.

The question of Beethoven’s tempo is therefore not simply whether one should obey the composer’s number or ignore it in favor of subjective expression. The more fundamental question is what it means to obey such an indication. Obedience is not exhausted by matching a number. It requires understanding how the tempo organizes the movement. A Beethoven tempo marking may function as an instruction concerning the energy, proportion, and character of the passage, not merely as a measurement of beats per minute. To grasp it, a performer must hear the relation between tempo and musical structure.

This is an instance of Wittgenstein’s broader point that an instruction can be understood only against a background of practice. A command such as “play at this tempo” does not itself supply the complete criteria for its realization. The performer must know how to coordinate tempo with phrasing, articulation, dynamics, and style. That knowledge is learned through examples: performances, teacher demonstrations, study of related works, and repeated attempts. One comes to know what a Beethoven tempo indication requires by becoming able to hear when a performance is too heavy, too rushed, too static, or otherwise inconsistent with the movement’s character.

The same applies to the relation between composer, score, and performer. A performance can be wrong without there being a single mechanical test that settles the matter. Yet disagreement is not thereby arbitrary. Musicians can point to the relation between a marking and a phrase, compare the passage with analogous passages, invoke historical conventions, and show how a given interpretation distorts the work. The standards are public and teachable, even when they are not reducible to a strict definition.

## V. Rule-following beyond interpretation

The musical cases help illuminate the famous problem of interpretation. When a performer encounters a staccato mark or tempo indication, it may seem that the performer must interpret the mark before applying it. But if interpretation is understood as attaching an explanatory meaning to the mark, then another question arises: how is that interpretation itself to be applied? A second interpretation would be needed, and so on. Wittgenstein’s response is not that interpretation never occurs. Rather, it is that not every case of understanding consists in interpreting.

In ordinary rule-following, one may simply act. A skilled speaker does not normally formulate an interpretation before using a familiar grammatical construction. A chess player does not first produce a philosophical account of the rules before making a move. A musician may not consciously decide what a staccato mark means at every occurrence. The performer sees the mark and plays. This directness is not evidence of an unconscious representation that secretly completes the rule. It is evidence that the person has mastered the practice.

Wittgenstein distinguishes, in effect, between an interpretation and an understanding demonstrated in action. An interpretation is one expression of a rule in other signs. It can be useful when ambiguity arises or when a learner needs clarification. But an interpretation does not constitute the ultimate foundation of rule-following. At some point, explanations come to an end in the practice itself. The learner must be able to proceed. The musician must play the passage; the language learner must use the word; the craft apprentice must perform the gesture.

This stopping point is sometimes described as a “bedrock” of practice. It is not an irrational decision or an arbitrary convention. It is the point at which further requests for justification no longer have a role within the activity. If a student repeatedly asks what justifies each application of a rule, the teacher may eventually say, “That is how we continue here,” while demonstrating the continuation. This does not prove that the rule lacks meaning. It shows that the meaning is embodied in a shared form of activity rather than grounded in an additional proposition.

The possibility of explanation remains important. A teacher can say why a staccato should not be exaggerated in a particular phrase, or why a Beethoven movement requires a broader tempo than a literal reading might suggest. But explanation works by connecting one part of the practice with another. It refers to phrasing, style, rhythm, historical performance, or the relation among voices. It does not ascend to a standpoint outside all practices from which the essence of the rule can be apprehended. At some point, the student must hear and do.

The concept of “going on” thus occupies the space that traditional philosophy assigns to essence. It is not a replacement object. It is a capacity. To understand a rule is to be able to continue in the appropriate way, to recognize when a continuation is called for, and to respond to new circumstances without requiring a new foundational interpretation at every step. This capacity includes the possibility of being corrected and of correcting oneself. It is inherently normative and practical.

## VI. Is this merely conventionalism?

A serious objection remains. If the meaning of a rule is tied directly to its application, and if application depends on communal training, does this reduce correctness to whatever a community happens to accept? Could a whole community be wrong? Could a tradition teach a distorted way of applying a rule? Does Wittgenstein’s emphasis on practice collapse the distinction between following a rule and conforming to a social custom?

These worries arise if “practice” is understood as a mere aggregate of actual behavior. But Wittgenstein’s notion of practice is richer. A form of life includes not only what people do but also how they criticize, teach, justify, compare, and revise what they do. A practice contains standards of correction. The fact that a group regularly produces a certain performance does not automatically make the performance correct. Musicians can criticize a tradition from within the language of the music itself. They can point to inconsistencies, failures of phrasing, disregard for notation, or historical misunderstandings. The possibility of error is internal to a practice.

Nor does communal agreement mean unanimous agreement. Rule-following is compatible with disagreement, innovation, and reform. A performer may challenge an established interpretation and persuade others that a neglected possibility better realizes the work. A language community may change its usage. A legal community may revise its interpretation of a statute. What makes these developments intelligible is not conformity to a fixed essence but continuity of practice. The innovation must be recognizable as a development, reinterpretation, or correction rather than an unrelated activity.

Wittgenstein’s account therefore differs from relativism. Relativism says, roughly, that truth or correctness is nothing more than whatever a group accepts. Wittgenstein instead asks what makes the notions of truth and correctness function in human activities. A rule is not correct because people happen to agree; rather, agreement in judgments is one of the conditions under which the concept of a rule can operate. There must be shared criteria, but those criteria can be applied critically and can evolve.

This also explains why private rule-following is problematic. A person who claims to follow a wholly private rule, whose correct application is determined by an inaccessible inner impression, lacks the distinction between seeming right and being right. The point is not that private experiences are unreal. It is that a norm requires criteria by which application can be distinguished from mere inclination. In music, a performer’s personal feeling may contribute to interpretation, but it is not by itself enough to establish that the interpretation is faithful to the passage. The performer must be able to locate the interpretation within a practice of hearing, comparison, and justification.

The social dimension of rules is therefore not an accidental supplement. It is part of what makes normativity possible. Yet the social need not mean crude conformity. A rule is learned from others, but mastery may enable one to act independently of explicit social prompting. Indeed, the mature practitioner often follows a rule most successfully when no one is present to supervise. The public character of the rule lies not in constant observation but in the availability of standards for teaching and correction.

## VII. The tension between essence and practice

We can now formulate the central tension more precisely. The desire for an essence of rules expresses a legitimate need: we want to know what distinguishes a rule from a mere regularity, command, prediction, or habit. We want to understand how a rule can determine future applications and how correctness is possible. But the attempt to satisfy this need by positing a single abstract essence leads to the very problems Wittgenstein exposes. A hidden formula cannot determine its own interpretation; a mental image cannot fix all its future uses; and a finite set of past actions cannot logically compel a particular continuation without a background of practice.

The alternative is not to abandon the concept of determination. Rules determine action through the conditions of training and participation. A person who has learned a rule is situated in a normative space. Certain responses are now natural, others conspicuously wrong, and still others available as intelligent adaptations. This determination is neither mechanical nor arbitrary. It is comparable to the way a skilled musician is constrained by a score while still exercising judgment. The score does not dictate every acoustic detail, but it does not permit anything whatsoever.

The content of rule-following is therefore exhausted by instruction, imitation, and the capacity to go on—but “exhausted” must be understood carefully. It does not mean that a rule is nothing more than a collection of instructions or imitated performances. It means that there is no further content waiting behind these practical abilities. The learner’s grasp of a rule is not completed by discovering an essence beyond the ability to apply it. If the learner can respond appropriately to examples, distinguish correct from incorrect continuations, explain or demonstrate applications, and extend the practice to new cases, then the rule has been understood.

This conclusion may appear anti-intellectual because it gives priority to doing over stating. But Wittgenstein does not deny the importance of propositions, definitions, or theoretical understanding. In some practices, a formal statement is essential. The point is that the statement derives its role from a practice in which it can be used. A mathematical formula can guide calculation because practitioners know how to manipulate symbols and recognize correct results. A legal definition can guide adjudication because interpreters understand the language, institutions, and procedures in which the definition operates. A musical marking can guide performance because musicians have learned the conventions that give it significance.

Nor is the practice-based account anti-rational. Reasoning itself is a practice of moving from one judgment to another according to standards that are learned and shared. The demand for a reason eventually reaches a point at which further reasons are not called for. This is not a failure of rationality but a feature of the grammar of justification. One can justify a performance by appealing to a score, a stylistic convention, or a structural feature of the music; but one cannot justify every basic form of hearing by another independent hearing. The practice has to be enacted.

## VIII. A qualified conclusion

The claim that rules are family-resemblance concepts is persuasive if it is understood as a warning against treating every rule as an explicit, self-interpreting formula. Rules belong to diverse practices, and their similarities are overlapping rather than essential. Some rules are codified, some tacit; some are enforced institutionally, some sustained by custom; some can be applied mechanically, while others require judgment and sensitivity. The concept has enough unity to be useful, but not the kind of unity demanded by a strict analytic definition.

The claim that rules can be understood only by learning through examples is also substantially correct, provided that “only” does not exclude formulations, explanations, or reflective criticism. Examples are indispensable because they initiate the learner into a practice of recognition and correction. They show not merely what has happened but how to treat relevantly similar cases. Through instruction and imitation, the learner acquires a capacity for continuation. This capacity is the practical manifestation of understanding.

Wittgenstein’s account of rule-following ties meaning directly to application without reducing meaning to a private decision or a mere behavioral regularity. The rule is neither an abstract entity that exists independently of all use nor a subjective interpretation imposed upon inert signs. Its meaning is expressed in the role it plays within a form of life. Schubert’s staccato marks mean what they do through practices of musical articulation and phrasing; Beethoven’s tempo indications acquire their force through the cultivated ability to hear and realize musical character. In both cases, the rule is not fully captured by a definition detached from performance. Its content appears in the difference between a sensitive and an insensitive application.

The search for essence and the recognition of practice can therefore be reconciled by changing the philosophical picture. The essence we seek is not an object behind the rule but the grammar of its use. Yet even “grammar” must not be imagined as a hidden structure independent of practice. It is the network of distinctions, examples, responses, and activities through which a rule is made intelligible. To understand a rule is to know how to move within that network.

Ultimately, rule-following is not a mysterious relation between an inner representation and an external action. It is a learned human capacity. We are taught to respond, we imitate models, we are corrected, and eventually we become able to continue without explicit supervision. We can recognize a new case, judge an unfamiliar performance, and adapt a rule without abandoning it. This ability to go on is not evidence that rules lack determinate content. It is where their determinate content lives. Rules resist analytic definition not because they are empty or irrational, but because their identity is inseparable from the practices in which they guide action.
