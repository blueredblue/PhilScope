### Introduction

The metaphysical trajectory of classical Greek philosophy is defined by an enduring tension: how to reconcile the permanence required for genuine knowledge with the undeniable mutability of the physical world. In the *Phaedo*, Plato resolves this tension through the Theory of Forms, positing a transcendental realm of immutable, eternal essences in which sensible particulars imperfectly participate. Change and predication are thus explained as the transient manifestation of these transcendent entities within particulars. 

Conversely, Aristotle rejects this ontological dualism in the *Categories* and the *Physics*, bringing the explanatory principles of change and predication down to the immanent structure of the sensible world itself. By formulating a systematic classification of categories and opposites, and by introducing the dynamic triad of *substance* (*ousia*), *form* (*morphê*), and *matter* (*hulê*) mediated by potentiality (*dynamis*) and actuality (*energeia*), Aristotle attempts to provide a more cogent account of the physical world. 

This essay evaluates whether Aristotle’s ontological framework and systematic classification of opposites in the *Categories* provide a more cogent account of change and predication than Plato’s theory of forms in the *Phaedo*. It will be argued that Aristotle’s framework is fundamentally superior. By anchoring predication in primary substances and resolving the mechanics of change through immanent potentiality rather than transcendent participation, Aristotle avoids the infinite regresses, logical antinomies, and causal obscurantism that plague Plato’s theory.

### Part I: Plato’s Transcendental Framework and the Metaphysics of the *Phaedo*

Plato’s *Phaedo* establishes a rigorous ontological dualism designed to secure the objects of knowledge against the Heraclitean flux of the sensible world. The dialogue operates against the backdrop of Socrates’ impending death, using this existential horizon to elevate the soul above the corporeal realm. Plato argues that if true knowledge (*epistêmê*) is possible, its objects cannot be subject to the decay, variation, and relativity characteristic of sensible particulars. Thus, he posits the existence of separate Forms (*eidê* or *ideai*)—immaterial, immutable, and self-predicating paradigms (e.g., the Beautiful itself, the Just itself, the Equal itself) that exist apart from the physical cosmos.

In the *Phaedo*, the relationship between these transcendent Forms and the sensible particulars of the empirical world is characterized primarily through the idioms of *participation* (*methexis*) and *presence* (*parousia*). Sensible particulars are what they are—tall, small, beautiful, or wise—only insofar as they participate in the corresponding Form. As Socrates outlines in his methodological autobiography (96a–102a), the cause of any phenomenon is not to be found in mechanical or material interactions alone, but in the ontological debt that the particular owes to the Form. A thing is beautiful because of the Beautiful (100c); it is tall because it partakes of Tallness.

However, this architecture immediately encounters severe difficulties when attempting to account for change and the attribution of contrary properties. In the final argument for the immortality of the soul (102b–107a), Socrates introduces a refined causal mechanism regarding opposites. He distinguishes between the abstract Form of an opposite (e.g., Tallness itself) and the immanent character (*enousia*) within a particular (e.g., the tallness in Simmias). Simmias is taller than Socrates and shorter than Phaedo not because Simmias’s substance inherently contains tallness as an immutable attribute, but because the immanent character of Tallness is temporarily present in him.

Crucially, Plato insists that while opposites cannot admit each other while remaining what they are (the Form of the Tall can never become the Form of the Short), the particulars that house these immanent opposites undergo transition. Simmias, who is now tall, can become short. Plato accounts for this by arguing that when the opposite quality approaches, the particular either retreats or perishes. In the case of organic change, the old immanent opposite is driven out or destroyed, and the new immanent opposite takes its place, driven by the presence of the incoming Form.

Yet, this account of transition relies heavily on Plato’s epistemology of *recollection* (*anamnêsis*). Because sensible particulars are constantly shifting, displaying mixed and contradictory properties—Simmias is tall relative to Socrates, but short relative to Phaedo—they cannot provide the stable foundation for knowledge. Instead, our encounters with sensible items prompt the soul to recollect the pure Forms it contemplated before its incarnation. Predication in the physical world is thus an imperfect reflection of a dialectical relationship between immanent characters and transcendent archetypes.

### Part II: The Platonic Impasse: Participation, Predication, and the Third Man

Despite its systematic ambition, Plato’s account of change and predication in the *Phaedo* suffers from profound metaphysical instability, much of which Aristotle exposes and targets in his critiques across the *Metaphysics* and *Physics*.

#### The Obscurity of Participation
The first major vulnerability is the metaphor of *methexis* (participation). Plato is famously vague about *how* a sensible particular participates in a transcendent Form. Is the Form spatially present in the particular? If so, the unitary Form must be divided among myriad particulars, compromising its simplicity and immutability. Or is the Form present *as a whole* in many distinct places simultaneously? Plato recognizes this difficulty in the *Phaedo* and the *Parmenides*, yet the *Phaedo* offers little conceptual machinery to bridge the chasm between the sensible and the intelligible realms. By segregating the explanatory cause (the Form) entirely from the thing explained (the sensible particular), Plato creates a two-world ontology that renders the causal mechanism of change mysterious. To say that Simmias becomes tall through the "presence" or "communion" of Tallness is essentially to restate the phenomenon in honorific metaphysical language rather than to explain its underlying mechanics.

#### The Third Man Argument and Self-Predication
Furthermore, Plato’s commitment to self-predication—the doctrine that the Form of $F$ is itself $F$ (e.g., Justice is just, the Beautiful is beautiful)—creates logical anomalies that threaten the coherence of predication. As Aristotle later formalizes in the *Third Man Argument* (critiqued in *Metaphysics* A.9 and M.4), if a collection of particulars is called $F$ because they share a common Form $F$, and that Form itself is $F$, then there must be a *further* Form (often designated as *Form 2*) by virtue of which both the original particulars *and* the first Form are $F$. This generates an infinite regress, stripping the Forms of their foundational explanatory power.

#### The Problem of Substratum Stability
Most critically regarding change, Plato’s *Phaedo* struggles to explain what remains numerically one during a transition between opposites. If Simmias-tall transitions to Simmias-short, what is the *substratum* of this change? Plato speaks of the immanent character retreating or perishing, but if the immanent character of tallness is destroyed and replaced by the immanent character of shortness, it is unclear what continuity remains. Plato’s ontology tends to treat properties almost as quasi-substances (*choriston* entities) that migrate in and out of particulars. Consequently, the individual particular risks dissolving into a mere meeting place for transient, warring Forms, undermining the ontological stability of primary substances. Without a robust theory of material potentiality and an underlying matter that persists through qualitative alteration, Plato’s account of how an individual endures while changing its attributes remains conceptually incomplete.

### Part III: Aristotle’s Immanent Framework: The *Categories* and the *Physics*

Aristotle addresses these metaphysical deficiencies by dismantling Plato’s transcendent dualism and relocating the principles of existence, change, and predication within the immanent structure of the physical world. This project is articulated across two foundational texts: the *Categories* (which establishes the logical and ontological grammar of predication) and the *Physics* (which provides the dynamic framework for natural change).

#### The *Categories*: Primary Substance and Predication
In the *Categories*, Aristotle begins not with abstract, separated universals, but with the concrete realities of everyday discourse and being. He establishes a foundational fourfold division of entities based on two axes: *said of a subject* (*katêgoreitai hypokeimenou*) and *in a subject* (*en hypokeimenou*):

1. **Universals not in a subject** (e.g., *Man*, *Animal*).
2. **Particulars in a subject** (e.g., *this individual grammatical knowledge*, *this specific pale color*—non-substance accidents).
3. **Universals in a subject** (e.g., *Grammar*, *Whiteness* as universal qualities).
4. **Particulars not in a subject and not in a subject** (*Primary Substances*, e.g., *this individual man*, *this individual horse*).

At the apex of this taxonomy is **primary substance** (*prôtê ousia*). Primary substances are the foundational ontological anchors of the universe. Everything else—quantities, qualities, relations, places—is either *said of* primary substances as universals (secondary substances) or *in* primary substances as accidents. 

This framework revolutionizes the theory of predication. For Plato, to predicate "tall" of Simmias is to invoke a vertical, vertical-transcendent relation to a separated Form. For Aristotle, predication is an immanent affair. When we say "Socrates is pale" or "Socrates is a man," we are linking a subject (primary substance) to a predicate (a universal substance or an accident inhering in that substance). Primary substances do not change their ontological status by acquiring or losing accidents; Socrates remains numerically one and the same individual whether he is pale or tanned, sitting or standing.

#### The *Physics*: The Triadic Structure of Change
While the *Categories* maps the static logical architecture of being, the *Physics* (specifically Book I, Chapters 7–9) provides the dynamic mechanism for how change occurs. Aristotle observes that all change (*metabolê*)—whether qualitative alteration, quantitative growth, or substantial generation—requires a triadic structure:

1. **The Substratum** (*hypokeimenon* or matter, *hulê*).
2. **The Privation** (*sterêsis*).
3. **The Form** (*morphê* or *eidos*).

In every instance of change, there must be something that persists through the process. If nothing persisted, change would reduce to sheer annihilation and creation ex nihilo, which Aristotle rejects as unintelligible. The substratum is that which persists while the extremes (the contraries) succeed one another. 

For example, when an unmusical man becomes musical, the man (*anthrôpos*) is the underlying substratum that persists. The change occurs between two contraries: the initial state of *privation* (being unmusical) and the final state of *possession* (having the form of musicality). The privation is not absolute non-being, but the absence of a characteristic that the subject is naturally apt to possess. Thus, change is rendered fully intelligible without appealing to transcendent realms: it is the actualization of what a substance is potentially capable of becoming.

### Part IV: The Mechanics of Transition: Potentiality (*Dynamis*) and Actuality (*Entelecheia*) vs. Recollection and Participation

The divergence between Plato and Aristotle becomes most pronounced when we examine how each thinker accounts for the dynamic transition of an individual between opposite states—such as an individual growing taller, or changing in temperature, size, or quality.

#### Plato’s Participation in Flux
In the *Phaedo*, an individual grows taller through the shifting presence of immanent characters governed by the transcendent Forms. When a child grows, the immanent character of "smallness" retreats, and the immanent character of "tallness" takes its place. Plato must resort to a complex dialectical machinery where opposites are mutually exclusive yet dynamically linked through the soul’s capacity for *recollection*. Because the sensible world is in constant flux, the empirical observation of a child growing taller does not yield a scientific understanding of *why* or *how* change occurs; rather, it serves as a psychological trigger for the intellect to recall the unchanging Form of Tallness. The transition itself remains metaphysically opaque—a shadow play cast by transcendent archetypes.

#### Aristotle’s Potentiality and Actuality
Aristotle sweeps away this Platonic machinery by introducing the correlative concepts of **potentiality** (*dynamis*) and **actuality** (*energeia* or *entelecheia*). These concepts solve the Parmenidean dilemma—how something can come to be from what is not—without needing a realm of separated Forms.

1. **Potentiality (*Dynamis*)**: This is not mere logical possibility or privative non-being; it is a real, inherent capacity within a substance to undergo a specific change or to actualize a specific form. A bronze statue exists potentially in the bronze block; a child has the potential to grow taller or to become educated.
2. **Actuality (*Energeia* / *Entelecheia*)**: This is the fulfillment or realization of that potentiality. When the potentiality is actualized, the substance takes on a new form or accident.

Let us apply this directly to the problem raised in the prompt: an individual growing taller. 
*   **Plato’s Account**: The individual participates in the Form of Smallness; when growth occurs, Smallness is driven out by the arrival of the immanent character of Tallness, mediated by the soul's recollection of the eternal Form of Tallness.
*   **Aristotle’s Account**: The individual (primary substance, e.g., Callias) possesses an inherent *potentiality* for quantitative increase (growth) bounded by his species-specific nature. Growth is not the migration of a transcendent Form into matter, but the continuous, immanent *actualization* of the matter's potential for extension. The underlying substratum (Callias) remains numerically one throughout the process. The opposite states (short vs. tall) are not external entities battling for possession of a passive vessel, but terminal points of a continuous spectrum inherent to the category of quantity.

By framing change as the transition from potentiality to actuality, Aristotle eliminates the need for Platonic recollection as a bridge for physical predication. We do not need to recollect a transcendent realm of Tallness to understand that Callias is taller today than he was last year; we need only analyze the immanent biological processes wherein potentiality is systematically converted into actuality by an efficient cause (e.g., nutrition and metabolic development).

### Part V: The Problem of the Substratum and Contrary Qualities

A rigorous test for any ontological system is its ability to solve the foundational paradox of change: *How can a single, numerically identical entity admit contrary qualities at different times without losing its identity?*

#### Plato’s Treatment of the Substratum
In the *Phaedo*, Plato struggles to articulate a robust theory of a material substratum. Because his ontological focus is on the immaterial Forms, the sensible particular is often treated as a transient receptacle or an unstable nexus of shifting immanent characters. When Simmias changes from tall to short, the continuity is secured primarily by the soul or by the nominal identity of "Simmias," but metaphysically, Simmias is merely the intersection where opposite characters arrive and depart. Plato does not yet possess the fully developed concept of *prime matter* (*hylê*) as an indeterminate, purely potential substratum that we find in Aristotle’s later works (though embryonic versions appear in the *Timaeus* with the *chôra*). Consequently, the integrity of the sensible particular is constantly threatened by the ontological weight of the Forms: particulars are pale shadows, derivative and unstable.

#### Aristotle’s Resolution via Substance and Accident
Aristotle solves this problem cleanly in the *Categories* and the *Physics* by maintaining two vital ontological principles:
1. **Numerical Identity through Qualitative Alteration**: In *Categories* 4 (4a10–20), Aristotle explicitly notes that primary substances possess the unique capacity to admit contrary qualities while remaining numerically one and the same. For example, an individual human being, while remaining numerically one, becomes pale at one time and dark at another, hot and cold, good and bad. This is the defining mark of primary substance: "It is most distinctive of substance that, while remaining numerically one and the same, it is able to admit contrary qualities."
2. **The Substratum as Matter and Substance**: Unlike Plato’s particulars, which risk dissolving into bundles of characters, Aristotle’s primary substance is a concrete composite (*synholon*) of form and matter. When accidental change occurs (such as an individual growing taller or becoming tanned), the primary substance itself remains the stable *hypokeimenon*. When substantial change occurs (such as a living body perishing), the underlying *prime matter* persists, shedding one substantial form to receive another.

Thus, Aristotle resolves the problem of contrary qualities without invoking transcendent entities. Contraries do not inhabit the substance as autonomous guests; rather, the substance *itself* is the enduring subject that possesses the capacity (*dynamis*) to host now this quality, now its contrary. The logical grammar of the *Categories* perfectly mirrors the ontological reality: the primary substance is always the ultimate subject of predication and never a predicate itself, securing absolute stability amidst the flux of accidents.

### Part VI: Evaluating Cogency: Does Aristotle Successfully Avoid Platonic Burdens?

Having examined both frameworks, we must now evaluate whether Aristotle’s system successfully avoids the metaphysical burdens and inconsistencies that plague Plato’s treatment in the *Phaedo*.

#### 1. Eliminating the Separated Metaphysical Realm
Plato’s commitment to transcendent Forms creates an unnecessary ontological duplication—the famous "Third Man" problem and the yawning chasm between the sensible and intelligible worlds. By banishing separated Forms and locating universals *in re* (in things) or as conceptual abstractions derived from sensible particulars, Aristotle slashes Ockham’s razor across Platonic dualism. Aristotle’s ontology is ontologically parsimonious: it requires only the physical world of substances, properties, and relations, operating according to immanent principles. This avoids the obscure mechanics of *methexis* and *parousia*.

#### 2. Solving the Problem of Regress
Because Aristotle’s forms are immanent (*morphê* inhering in *hulê*), he avoids the infinite regresses inherent in self-predicating Forms. A horse is a horse not because it imitates a transcendent, eternal Equinity, but because it actualizes the substantial form of *equus* inherent in its matter, received through generational continuity from prior horses. Predication describes the structural arrangement of these immanent features rather than attempting to map an empirical item onto a divine paradigm.

#### 3. A Coherent Account of Becoming
Plato’s *Phaedo* treats change with a certain metaphysical anxiety—change is a symptom of the sensible world’s inferiority, a pale substitute for true intellectual contemplation. Aristotle, by contrast, embraces change as a fundamental, rational feature of the natural world. By introducing potentiality and actuality, Aristotle converts the Parmenidean riddle of non-being into a systematic science of biology and physics. Privation is not a terrifying descent into nothingness, but the natural baseline of potentiality waiting to be actualized. An individual growing taller is not orchestrating a delicate dance with distant mathematical Forms; they are realizing the biological potentiality encoded within their primary substance.

#### Potential Criticisms and Limitations of Aristotle’s System
Fairness requires acknowledging that Aristotle’s framework is not without its own internal difficulties. Critics (such as medieval nominalists and modern empiricists) have argued that Aristotle’s reliance on teleology and unmovable first movers reintroduces a metaphysical teleology that borders on the anthropomorphic. Furthermore, Aristotle’s concept of *prime matter* (*hylê prôtê*)—pure potentiality devoid of all form and quality—is notoriously elusive, functioning almost as a negative limit concept rather than a positively graspable entity (a difficulty strikingly analogous to Plato’s *chôra* in the *Timaeus*).

However, even if prime matter strains the limits of empirical verification, it remains functionally superior to Plato’s separated Forms. Prime matter is an internal, immanent limit of physical potentiality, whereas Plato’s Forms are external, transcendent entities that threaten to render the physical world fundamentally unintelligible and causally inert.

---

### Conclusion

In the final analysis, Aristotle’s ontological framework and systematic classification of opposites in the *Categories*, supported by the dynamic mechanics of the *Physics*, provide a vastly more cogent account of change and predication than Plato’s theory of forms in the *Phaedo*. 

Plato’s *Phaedo* establishes a brilliant, visionary metaphysics, but it founders upon the unbridgeable gulf of *methexis*, the logical perils of self-predication, and an inability to account for the stability of particulars undergoing transition without reducing them to ephemeral vessels of warring immanent characters. 

Aristotle resolves these impasses by turning his gaze from the heavens to the immanent structure of reality. By centering his ontology on primary substances that remain numerically one while admitting contrary qualities, and by explaining change not as the migratory dance of transcendent Forms, but as the rigorous transition from potentiality (*dynamis*) to actuality (*energeia*), Aristotle constructs an account that is logically coherent, empirically grounded, and metaphysically economical. In doing so, he successfully banishes the ghost of the Third Man and establishes a framework where change is not a metaphysical defect to be escaped through philosophical asceticism, but the very pulse of a rational, intelligible natural world.
