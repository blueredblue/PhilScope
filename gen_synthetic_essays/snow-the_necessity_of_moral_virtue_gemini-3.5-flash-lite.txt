# The Architecture of Mortal Flourishing: Moral Virtue, Intellectual Contemplation, and the Structural Tension of Aristotelian Eudaimonia

## Introduction

At the heart of Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* lies a foundational, yet profoundly vexing, structural ambiguity concerning the nature of human happiness (*eudaimonia*). Aristotle defines *eudaimonia* as the ultimate end (*telos*) of human action, an activity of the soul in accordance with complete virtue (*kat’ areten teletean*). 

Yet, as the treatise unfolds across its ten books, a profound tension emerges between two distinct ideals of the good life. On the one hand, Books I through IX present a comprehensive, socially embedded account of human flourishing. This account integrates moral virtue (*arete ethike*), practical wisdom (*phronesis*), political engagement, friendship (*philia*), and external goods (*euporia*) into an integrated, self-sufficient (*autarkes*) whole. 

On the other hand, Book X, Chapter 7 elevates *theoria*—contemplative intellectual activity—as the highest, most divine, and most complete realization of human potential, appearing to subordinate or render instrumental the entire domain of moral virtue and practical action.

This textual tension has generated one of the most enduring debates in classical scholarship: the divide between the **inclusive (or comprehensive) interpretation** and the **dominant intellectualist interpretation**. 

The inclusive view, championed by commentators such as J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche, argues that *eudaimonia* comprises a plurality of intrinsic goods, wherein moral virtue and its corresponding activities are constitutive elements of the happy life. Under this reading, human happiness is a comprehensive life-plan that harmonizes various worthwhile pursuits. 

Conversely, the intellectualist view, classically articulated by John Cooper and supported by figures such as W. F. R. Hardie, maintains that *eudaimonia* in its primary and strict sense is identical with *theoria*. In this view, moral virtue and practical reasoning do not constitute happiness itself. Instead, they serve as necessary developmental prerequisites and practical foundations that secure the external and psychological conditions required to engage in philosophical contemplation.

This essay evaluates the necessity of moral virtue within Aristotle’s account of human happiness by navigating this exact interpretive divide. It will be argued that while Book X’s intellectualism poses a formidable challenge to any simple harmonizing of Aristotle’s texts, a rigorous reading of the function argument (*ergon*), the nature of completeness (*teleion*), and the psychological continuity between *phronesis* and *sophia* reveals a profound, dialectical synthesis. 

Moral virtue is neither merely instrumental nor entirely co-extensive with theoretical contemplation. Rather, it is the indispensable structural bridge that allows a mortal, composite creature to approximate the divine life without violating the ontological limits of human nature.

## I. The Inclusive Interpretation: Ackrill, Roche, and the Architecture of *Teleion*

To understand the inclusive reading of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, one must begin with the structural demands of *eudaimonia* as Aristotle outlines it in Book I. Aristotle posits that *eudaimonia* is the supreme good: that which is always chosen for itself and never for the sake of something else (1097a30–35). It is also characterized by *autarkeia* (self-sufficiency); not the isolation of the solitary ascetic, but a state that makes a life choice-worthy and lacking in nothing. Crucially, Aristotle applies the term *teleion* (complete or final) to this good.

Historically, interpretations of *teleion* have fractured the scholarly landscape. J. L. Ackrill, in his seminal 1974 essay "Aristotle on Eudaimonia," launched a radical challenge against the traditional intellectualist consensus. Ackrill argued that *teleion* can be understood in two distinct ways:
1. **The Dominant End view**, which holds that happiness consists of a single, overarching activity (such as *theoria*), to which all other activities are subordinated as means.
2. **The Inclusive view**, which maintains that happiness is a comprehensive life comprising a plurality of activities and goods, each chosen for itself, which together make up a complete life.

Ackrill contends that Aristotle’s initial definition of *eudaimonia* in Book I, Chapter 7—that it is an activity of the soul in accordance with *complete* (*teleian*) virtue—strongly supports the inclusive view. If there are several virtues, and happiness requires the exercise of complete virtue, then happiness must embrace the exercise of moral virtues (courage, temperance, justice, liberality) alongside intellectual ones. 

To reduce happiness to the solitary exercise of theoretical contemplation in Book X, Ackrill argues, is to render the detailed ethical investigations of Books II through IX largely irrelevant, reducing them to mere prolegomena for the true life of the mind.

Timothy Roche has advanced and refined this inclusive framework by rigorously analyzing the teleological structure of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, particularly through the lens of the *ergon* (function) argument in Book I, Chapter 7. Roche argues that the human *ergon*—defined as the active exercise of the soul's faculties in accordance with, or implying, reason (*logos*)—cannot be restricted solely to the theoretical intellect without violating Aristotle’s own methodological commitments. 

Because humans are composite beings possessing both a rational soul (*logos*) and an irrational yet reason-responsive appetite (*orexis*), the human function must encompass the actualization of *both* parts of the soul.

Roche utilizes the concept of *teleion* not merely as "complete" in the sense of lacking nothing, but as "final" or "fully realized" across the temporal span of a complete human life. He emphasizes Aristotle's famous caveat: 

> "One swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy" (1098a18–20). 

This temporal extension implies that *eudaimonia* is a narrative unity. It is a life structured by the exercise of moral virtues in social, political, and familial spheres. 

For Roche, moral virtue is not a mere instrumental tool used to quiet the passions so that the intellect can escape into contemplation. Instead, moral virtue is an **intrinsic constituent** of the good life. 

To live a happy life is to experience the fine (*kalon*) through courageous actions, just transactions, and generous distributions. These actions are choice-worthy for their own sakes. They are not merely steps toward a contemplative end. Consequently, the inclusive interpretation insists that a life devoid of moral action and interpersonal engagement—no matter how intellectually brilliant—falls short of the Aristotelian standard of a fully realized human life.

## II. The Dominant Intellectualist Interpretation: John Cooper and Book X

In stark opposition to the inclusive reading stands the dominant intellectualist interpretation, most systematically defended by John Cooper in *Reason and Human Good in Aristotle* (1975). Cooper argues that Ackrill and other inclusive commentators conflate the *conditions* of happiness with happiness itself. Cooper draws a sharp distinction between what is choiceworthy as a component of a good human life and what constitutes the supreme focal point (*telos*) of that life.

Cooper anchors his interpretation in Book X, Chapters 7 and 8, where Aristotle explicitly identifies *eudaimonia* with *theoria*. Here, Aristotle argues that theoretical contemplation is the highest activity because:
* The intellect (*nous*) is the highest element in us.
* Its objects (the divine and unchanging truths of metaphysics and nature) are the highest objects of knowledge.
* Contemplation is the most continuous, pleasant, self-sufficient, and leisurely of human activities.

Crucially, Aristotle states that a life lived in accordance with *theoria* will be happy, but only "insofar as [man] has something divine in him" (1177b30). He contrasts this life with the secondary, subordinate happiness found in the exercise of moral virtue: 

> "The life in accordance with the other kind of virtue is happy in a human way [kat' anthropon]; for the activities in accordance with this are characteristic of us in relation to our relations" (1178a9–11).

Cooper seizes upon this distinction to dismantle the inclusive view. He argues that Aristotle operates with a focal meaning of *eudaimonia*. While moral virtue and practical wisdom produce and sustain the conditions under which *theoria* is possible, they are not intrinsic constituents of happiness *in its primary sense*. 

Instead, moral virtue belongs to the composite human nature—the soul-body compound struggling with passions, social relations, and political contingencies. *Theoria*, by contrast, actualizes the divine spark within us: the intellect, which transcends mortal contingency.

Under Cooper's intellectualist reading, the moral virtues (*arete ethike*) are strictly instrumental or structural prerequisites. Just as health is a prerequisite for athletic excellence or artistic creation, moral virtue creates the psychological equilibrium and social stability necessary for the philosopher to engage in contemplation. 

A temperate person is not distracted by immoderate bodily pleasures; a courageous person is not paralyzed by fear in times of political turmoil; a just person lives in peaceful harmony with fellow citizens. 

Yet, these moral states do not constitute the *content* of the highest good. If a philosopher were granted a life of uninterrupted, god-like contemplation, the exercise of courage in battle or liberality in wealth distribution would not add to the intrinsic value of their happiness. In fact, these moral activities would often represent a distraction from the supreme activity of the intellect. 

Therefore, Cooper concludes that the *Nicomachean Ethics* presents a rigorous, uncompromised intellectualism: human happiness, properly understood, is the life of theoretical contemplation, and moral virtue is merely the necessary practical foundation that makes such a life attainable.

## III. Moral Virtue: Intrinsic Constituent or Instrumental Prerequisite?

The fault line between Ackrill/Roche and Cooper forces a deeper philosophical interrogation: *What is the exact ontological and causal status of moral virtue in relation to eudaimonia?* 

To resolve this tension, one must move beyond the stark binaries of the inclusive-intellectualist debate and examine how Aristotle conceptualizes human agency, the structure of the soul, and the relationship between *phronesis* (practical wisdom) and *sophia* (theoretical wisdom).

### The Limits of Pure Instrumentality
Cooper’s intellectualist reading risks reducing moral virtue to an external, technological tool—akin to a ladder that is discarded once the roof of contemplation has been reached. However, this interpretation struggles to account for Aristotle’s insistent claims regarding the intrinsic value of moral actions. 

In Book III, Aristotle argues that virtuous actions are chosen for their own sake because they are *kalon* (fine or noble). The courageous person faces death not as a calculated means to secure leisure for later philosophizing, but because it is noble to do so. 

If moral virtue were merely instrumental, its value would be entirely derivative. Yet Aristotle explicitly categorizes virtues among things chosen both for themselves and for something else (like honor or happiness), yet ultimately constitutive of the noble life.

Furthermore, the radical instrumentalization of moral virtue severs the psychological continuity that Aristotle painstakingly establishes across the *Ethics*. Aristotle does not view the human being as a disembodied intellect awkwardly trapped inside an animal body. We are composite substances (*synolon*). 

The irrational part of the soul (the appetitive and desiderative faculties) is not merely a recalcitrant beast that must be chained by moral habituation; it is capable of listening to and participating in reason (*to echon logon* in a secondary sense, 1102b25–30). 

When moral virtue is acquired, the passions are harmonized with reason. This harmonization produces a state of psychic order (*kosmios*) that is intrinsically pleasant and good in itself. To claim that this state of profound psychic health is merely a utilitarian device for philosophy is to drain human moral agency of its intrinsic dignity within Aristotle’s system.

### The Developmental and Foundational Necessity of Moral Virtue
Conversely, the inclusive view often struggles to account for the unmistakable hierarchy Aristotle establishes in Book X. It is undeniable that Aristotle treats *theoria* as the *megiston* (greatest) good and explicitly demotes moral virtue to a secondary, "human" form of happiness. How can moral virtue be both an intrinsic constituent of a comprehensive life and yet subordinate to intellectual contemplation?

The resolution lies in understanding moral virtue as a **developmental prerequisite and practical foundation** that is simultaneously **architectonically continuous** with intellectual wisdom. 

Moral virtue is the causal and developmental condition for the actualization of *sophia*. One cannot jump straight to theoretical contemplation without first undergoing the rigorous moral education (*ethismos*) detailed in Books II through V. 

The child must be habituated to feel pleasure and pain at the right things; the adolescent must develop *sophrosyne* (temperance) and *andreia* (courage) to prevent the tyrannical appetites from hijacking the deliberative faculty.

Without moral virtue, practical wisdom (*phronesis*) cannot exist. As Aristotle argues in Book VI, Chapter 12, *phronesis* and moral virtue are reciprocally entailing: 

> "It is not possible to be good in the strict sense without practical wisdom, nor practical wisdom without moral virtue" (1144b30–32). 

*Phronesis* provides the correct grasp of the mean in practical matters, determining what is to be done here and now in the fluctuating realm of human affairs. It coordinates the pursuit of noble ends within the *polis*. 

Yet, *phronesis* does not rule over *sophia*; rather, practical wisdom is the servant of theoretical wisdom. As Aristotle states in Book VI, Chapter 13: 

> "Practical wisdom does not use [theoretical wisdom], but provides for its coming to be; it issues orders, then, for its sake, but not to it" (1145a6–9).

Herein lies the structural key. *Phronesis* and moral virtue organize the composite life of the mortal human being, creating an external and internal environment of stability, leisure (*schole*), and emotional balance. 

This environment is not merely a causal means to an external end; rather, it is the necessary soil out of which the flower of *theoria* blooms. Just as a healthy physical body is not merely a tool for the mind, but an intrinsic aspect of a complete human organism, moral virtue is the vital, functioning health of the practical soul without which the divine activity of the intellect is impossible for a mortal creature.

## IV. Reconciling Mortal Existence: Practical Reasoning and Divine Contemplation

This brings us to the ultimate philosophical challenge of the *Nicomachean Ethics*: How does a mortal creature, bound by the messy contingencies of political life, physical vulnerability, and emotional impulses, reconcile the demands of practical reasoning (*phronesis*) with the pure, god-like intellectual activity (*theoria*) described in Book X?

### The Paradox of the Mortal God
Aristotle famously asserts in Book X, Chapter 7 that we must not follow those who advise us, being human, to think of human things, but must "immortalize as much as is in our power" (*athanatizein hos epi to pasan*, 1177b33). 

This injunction creates a profound anthropological paradox. If the highest happiness consists in an activity that is divine and immortal, and if actualizing this activity requires transcending the mortal sphere, what happens to our mortal responsibilities? What happens to the citizen, the friend, the parent, and the moral agent?

To resolve this paradox, we must reject both an exclusive reduction of happiness to moral virtue and an exclusive reduction of happiness to pure, unbroken contemplation. Aristotle’s human being is an amphibious creature living on the borderlands between the animal kingdom and the divine realm. We possess a mortal body, passions, and social bonds, yet we harbor within us an immortal spark—the *nous*.

Because we are composite (*synolon*), our *eudaimonia* cannot be exclusively divine without denying our mortal reality, nor can it be exclusively political without denying our divine potential. 

Therefore, the *Nicomachean Ethics* presents a **dual-aspect or hierarchical harmony** of the good life. 

### The Architectonic Role of *Theoria* and the Integration of *Phronesis*
In a fully realized, flourishing human life, *theoria* is the supreme, governing apex. It is the activity for the sake of which all other noble activities are ultimately ordered. 

However, *theoria* is not an activity that can occupy every waking hour of a mortal life. As Aristotle concedes, continuous divine contemplation is beyond human endurance: 

> "Such a life would be too high for man; for it is not in so far as he is man that he will live so, but in so far as something divine is present in him" (1177b26–28).

Because we tire, because we must eat, sleep, govern cities, and maintain friendships, our lives are necessarily filled with practical action. 

Here is where the integration of moral virtue and practical reasoning (*phronesis*) occurs. The person of practical wisdom does not view moral action as a frustrating interruption of their contemplation. Rather, they execute their moral and political duties with grace, nobility, and proportion precisely because their character has been shaped by virtue. 

The moral virtues regulate our interactions with others, ensuring that our social existence is harmonious, just, and beautiful (*kalon*).

When the philosopher steps down from the heights of contemplation to engage in civic life, judge a dispute, or act with courage in a crisis, they do not abandon their happiness. Instead, they exercise that secondary, "human" happiness which is intrinsically bound up with moral virtue. 

The two forms of happiness are not mutually exclusive alternatives; they are harmonized within a single biographical narrative through the architectonic ordering of the soul. 

*Theoria* provides the ultimate orienting vision of truth and order, while *phronesis* and moral virtue manage the terrestrial household of mortal existence, ensuring that the human instrument remains tuned, healthy, and capable of occasionally reflecting the divine light of contemplation.

---

## Conclusion

The tension between the inclusive view and the dominant intellectualist interpretation of Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* is not a mere scholastic puzzle. It is a profound reflection of the inherent complexity of the human condition. 

As we have seen, J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche rightly emphasize that human happiness, as a *teleion* good encompassing the full scope of an *ergon* suited to a composite creature, must embrace the rich texture of moral virtue, social action, and emotional nobility. A life stripped of these elements is subhuman. 

At the same time, John Cooper and the intellectualist tradition correctly insist that Book X cannot be explained away. *Theoria* remains the supreme, focal *telos*—the highest actualization of the human intellect that bridges the mortal and the divine.

The reconciliation of these two seemingly divergent paths lies in recognizing moral virtue not as an isolated, purely instrumental tool, nor as the whole of happiness itself, but as the **indispensable structural foundation and intrinsic harmonic expression of the practical soul**. 

Moral virtue and practical wisdom create the psychological stability and social order required for the emergence of *theoria*. Yet, because we are mortal, we live out our days in the realm of action, where moral nobility shines forth as an essential component of a well-lived human life. 

Ultimately, Aristotle’s account of *eudaimonia* does not ask us to choose between the citizen and the philosopher. Instead, it invites us to achieve a difficult, magnificent synthesis: to cultivate moral virtue so that we may master the turbulent passions of our mortal frame, thereby creating the sacred leisure required to touch, if only for brief and blinding moments, the eternal life of the mind.
