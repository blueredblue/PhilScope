**The Unbearable Tension of Tense: Stage Theory and the Semantic Demands of Natural Language**

The stage theory of persistence, most notably articulated by Ted Sider and defended in various forms by Katherine Hawley, represents one of the most robust metaphysical frameworks for understanding how everyday objects persist through time. According to this view, ordinary objects are four-dimensional spacetime worms, but crucially, they are constituted entirely by instantaneous temporal parts, or stages. A persisting object is not a three-dimensional entity that endures through time, nor is it a single four-dimensional block that persists in a mysterious sense; rather, it is simply the temporal counterpart relation linking distinct, instantaneous stages. This metaphysical thesis offers elegant solutions to traditional puzzles of persistence, such as the problem of temporary intrinsics and the ship of Theseus. However, the stage theorist faces a profound semantic challenge when attempting to integrate this metaphysics with the compositional semantics of natural language tense. The fundamental mismatch between what Sider and Hawley require of referring expressions and what natural language syntax provides generates an intractable semantic tension. This tension is brought into sharp relief by sentences containing multiple tensed verbs and a single referring expression, such as "Kant was born and died in Königsberg." For the stage theorist, this sentence exposes a deep incompatibility: the syntactic unity of natural language referring expressions demands a semantic unity that the instantaneous, multiplicity-laden ontology of stage theory cannot provide, and the stage theorist cannot resolve this tension without abandoning the core metaphysical thesis that everyday objects are instantaneous temporal stages.

To understand the depth of this problem, one must first examine the specific semantic demands that stage theory places upon referring expressions. In the stage theorist’s ontology, an object at a given time is merely one instantaneous stage of a four-dimensional whole. When we make an assertion about an object, we are fundamentally making an assertion about a particular temporal part. Consequently, Sider and Hawley require that referring expressions in natural language must, at the level of semantic composition, be capable of picking out these instantaneous stages. A proper name like "Kant" cannot simply refer to a three-dimensional object that endures; rather, it must refer to the entire four-dimensional spacetime worm, or it must function as a rigid designator that selects a specific stage relative to the time of utterance. 

Sider, in *Four-Dimensionalism*, argues that the semantics of reference must be tied to the metaphysics of persistence. If everyday objects are constituted by temporal parts, then the semantic value of a singular term must reflect this constitution. Sider suggests that names rigidly designate four-dimensional objects—spacetime worms—which are logically constructed out of their temporal parts. Katherine Hawley, in her work on persistence and natural philosophy, emphasizes that our ordinary thought and language treat objects as enduring through change, yet our metaphysics must account for how these enduring objects are semantically referenced. For the stage theorist, a referring expression must be semantically equipped to distribute its reference across the various instantaneous stages that constitute the object, or it must refer to the object as a whole while allowing predicates to be relativized to specific stages. The requirement is thus a delicate balancing act: the semantics must respect the instantaneous nature of the ontology while also capturing the unified, persisting subject of our discourse.

However, natural language syntax provides neither the distributed semantic distribution nor the unified whole that the stage theorist requires. Syntax provides a single noun phrase (NP) as the grammatical subject of a sentence, and this NP is semantically interpreted as a singular referring expression that occupies the subject position across all predicates in the sentence. Consider the sentence: "Kant was born and died in Königsberg." Syntactically, "Kant" is the single subject of both "was born" and "died." Compositional semantics dictates that the semantic value of the subject must remain constant across the conjunction of predicates. The same entity that was born must also have died. 

This syntactic unity generates an intractable problem for the stage theorist when combined with the temporal semantics of tensed verbs. To see why, we must evaluate how both quantificational and operator analyses of tense handle this sentence, and why both fail to reconcile the syntactic unity of "Kant" with the instantaneous ontology of stage theory.

First, consider the quantificational analysis of tense, which treats tense as a quantifier over times. On this view, "Kant was born" is analyzed as something like "There exists a time t such that Kant was born at t." The tensed verb is replaced by a quantifier that binds a temporal variable, and the sentence is evaluated at a time relative to which the past tense is interpreted. For the stage theorist, "Kant" in this quantificational framework would have to refer to a temporal stage. However, the conjunction "Kant was born and died" would then require a single quantifier (the referring expression "Kant") to bind to two distinct times—the time of birth and the time of death. In quantificational logic, a single variable cannot be bound to two different times simultaneously unless the quantifier ranges over a set of times, but "Kant" is a singular referring expression, not a plural quantifier. If "Kant" refers to a specific instantaneous stage, that stage cannot be both born and died, because an instantaneous stage has no duration in which to undergo these processes. If "Kant" is interpreted as a quantifier over the stages of Kant, then the sentence means "There is a stage of Kant that was born and there is a stage of Kant that died." But this violates the syntactic unity of the subject; the conjunction requires the *same* subject to satisfy both predicates, not merely two stages of the same object. The quantificational analysis thus forces the stage theorist to either abandon the singular reference of "Kant" (making it a mereological sum or plurality) or accept that an instantaneous stage can possess temporally extended properties like being born and dying, which is metaphysically incoherent.

Second, consider the operator analysis of tense, which treats tensed verbs as operators that shift the time of evaluation. On this view, "Kant was born" is analyzed as the proposition "It was the case that Kant was born," where the operator "it was the case that" shifts the evaluation time to the past. In operator semantics, the subject "Kant" retains its reference, but the truth conditions of the predicate are relativized to a different time. For the stage theorist, this creates an even more acute problem. The operator "was" requires the predicate "born" to be evaluated at the time of birth, but the subject "Kant" must still refer to something. If "Kant" refers to an instantaneous stage, the operator must select a stage at the time of birth. But then the conjunction "Kant was born and died" requires the same operator to shift the evaluation time to the time of death, while the subject "Kant" must still refer to the *same* entity. The syntactic unity of "Kant" as the subject of both conjuncts implies a single referent that spans the temporal gap between birth and death. The operator analysis, however, localizes the evaluation of predicates to specific times, leaving the subject as a static referent. If the subject is a stage, it cannot span the gap. If the subject is the whole four-dimensional worm, then the operator analysis collapses into a perdurantist framework where the whole object is the subject, but this violates the stage theorist's core thesis that the whole is nothing over and above its instantaneous parts. The operator analysis thus fails to provide a compositional semantics that respects both the syntactic unity of the subject and the instantaneous nature of the ontological constituents.

The fundamental mismatch, therefore, lies at the intersection of syntax and metaphysics. Natural language syntax provides a unified, singular referring expression that persists across multiple tensed predicates. Stage theory requires that the ontological constituents of an object be instantaneous temporal parts. The stage theorist is caught between a rock and a hard place: if "Kant" refers to a single instantaneous stage, the sentence "Kant was born and died in Königsberg" is false, because no single stage was both born and died, and the syntactic unity of the subject is violated by forcing the two predicates to apply to different stages. If "Kant" refers to the whole four-dimensional worm, then the syntactic unity is preserved, but the ontological thesis is compromised, because the whole worm is not an instantaneous stage; it is a temporally extended entity.

Sider and Hawley have attempted to navigate this tension, but their attempts reveal the intractability of the problem. Sider’s approach, as outlined in *Four-Dimensionalism*, often relies on a distinction between the object as a whole and its temporal parts. He argues that proper names rigidly designate four-dimensional objects, and that predicates in tensed sentences are interpreted as applying to the temporal parts of those objects. On this view, "Kant was born and died" is true because there is a four-dimensional object (the Kant-worm) such that at the time of birth, a temporal part of that object satisfies the predicate "was born," and at the time of death, a temporal part satisfy "died." However, this move effectively abandons the instantaneous stage thesis at the level of reference. By rigidly designating the whole worm, Sider treats the everyday object as a four-dimensional entity whose identity is the identity of the worm, not the identity of a single stage. This is a form of perdurantism, which the stage theory was originally formulated to avoid or nuance. Furthermore, this view struggles to account for the fact that in ordinary language, we do not speak of the "worm" but of the object as it endures. The semantic value of "Kant" becomes a four-dimensional abstract entity, severing the tight connection between the syntax of referring expressions and the instantaneous ontology of the world.

Hawley’s approach, while emphasizing the naturalness of persistence and the continuity of objects, faces a similar impasse. Hawley argues that everyday objects persist by having temporal parts, but she emphasizes that our concept of persistence is tied to the continuity of the object's nature. When we say "Kant was born and died," we are attributing properties to the same persisting entity. For the stage theorist, this persisting entity must be a whole constituted by stages. But if "Kant" refers to the whole, the sentence attributes the property of being born to the whole, which is a category mistake if the whole is merely a sum of instantaneous stages. A sum of instantaneous stages cannot be born; only a specific stage can be born. Thus, the stage theorist is forced to either treat "Kant" as a mereological sum of stages (which makes it a temporally extended entity, not an instantaneous stage) or to treat "Kant" as a single stage (which makes the sentence false). Neither option is palatable. The former abandons the instantaneous nature of stages, and the latter abandons the truth of our ordinary tensed discourse.

Can the stage theorist successfully resolve this syntactic and semantic tension without abandoning the core metaphysical thesis? The answer must be a resounding no. Any resolution that preserves the compositional semantics of natural language—where a single noun phrase maintains its referential identity across a compound predicate—must treat "Kant" as a referent that spans the temporal gap between birth and death. But spanning a temporal gap means being temporally extended, which means being a four-dimensional object or a mereological sum of stages, not an instantaneous stage. If the stage theorist insists that "Kant" must refer to an instantaneous stage, then the syntax of natural language is systematically misleading, and the compositional semantics of tense must be radically revised so that a single syntactic subject can refer to different stages at different times. But this revision destroys the unity of the subject, making "Kant was born and died" logically equivalent to "A stage of Kant was born and a stage of Kant died," which is a fundamentally different—and philosophically impoverished—sentence.

The stage theorist might attempt a third path: adopting a complex semantic decomposition where "Kant" is not a simple referring expression but a function from times to stages. On this view, "Kant" applied to the time of birth yields the birth stage, and "Kant" applied to the time of death yields the death stage. This would preserve the instantaneous ontology while explaining the truth of the sentence. However, this move fundamentally alters the syntactic and semantic category of a referring expression. In standard compositional semantics, a noun phrase is a referential expression that denotes an object (or a set of objects) in the domain of discourse. Treating "Kant" as a function from times to stages transforms it from a noun phrase into a complex temporal operator or a predicate. This violates the syntactic unity of the sentence; "Kant" would no longer be a single referential subject but a distributed semantic function. The syntax of natural language does not provide the machinery for such a decomposition; it presents "Kant" as a singular, unified subject. To impose a functional decomposition on it is to read a complex semantic theory into the syntax, which is a violation of the compositional principle that the semantics should be built from the syntax, not imposed upon it from a metaphysical agenda.

Ultimately, the tension between stage theory and the compositional semantics of tense is not a mere technicality; it is a fundamental philosophical crisis. The core metaphysical thesis of stage theory—that everyday objects are instantaneous temporal stages—requires that referring expressions pick out these instantaneous parts. But the syntax of natural language provides referring expressions that function as unified subjects across multiple tensed predicates, requiring a semantic unity that instantaneous parts cannot provide. The quantificational analysis fails because a single stage cannot satisfy temporally extended predicates like being born and dying, and the operator analysis fails because it cannot bridge the temporal gap while maintaining the singular reference of the subject. Sider and Hawley’s attempts to resolve this by treating names as designating the whole worm or by emphasizing the continuity of the object effectively abandon the instantaneous stage thesis, collapsing into a perdurantist or endurantist framework that the stage theory was designed to supersede. 

Therefore, the stage theorist is left with an impossible choice: either abandon the instantaneous nature of everyday objects, thereby forfeiting the core metaphysical thesis that makes stage theory distinctive, or abandon the compositional semantics of natural language, thereby severing the connection between our ordinary linguistic practices and our metaphysics. The sentence "Kant was born and died in Königsberg" stands as an intractable proof of this dilemma. It demonstrates that the syntax of natural language tense is fundamentally incompatible with the semantic demands of a metaphysics composed entirely of instantaneous temporal stages. Until the stage theorist can provide a compositional semantics that allows a single syntactic subject to refer to multiple instantaneous stages without reducing it to a mereological sum or a temporal function, the theory will remain semantically inadequate, unable to account for the very language we use to describe the world it seeks to explain.
