## The Illusion of Optimality: Why Dennett’s Intentional Stance Fails Human Suboptimality and Empathy Prevails

Daniel Dennett’s Intentional Stance stands as one of the most influential and elegant contributions to the philosophy of mind and cognitive science. It offers a pragmatic, third-personal strategy for predicting and explaining the behavior of complex systems—from thermostats to chess computers to human beings—by treating them as rational agents with beliefs and desires. The strategy’s power lies in its simplicity: rather than peering into the murky internal machinery of a system, we simply assume that it will act in the way that is most rational given its environment and its goals. This assumption, however, is not a neutral heuristic. It is a deeply normative and empirically loaded claim, one that Dennett grounds explicitly in a neo-Darwinian framework of adaptationism. The central thesis of this essay is that Dennett’s Intentional Stance, while a brilliant tool for predicting ideal or designed systems, systematically fails as a predictive framework for human behavior precisely because it relies on an indefensible assumption of optimal rationality inherited from adaptationist evolutionary biology. This assumption is refuted by the existence of non-adaptive cognitive spandrels, as argued by Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin, and by the robust empirical evidence of systematic human irrationality, as highlighted by Stephen Stich. Consequently, I will argue that substituting an empathetic approach—asking “What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?”—offers a more coherent and empirically adequate framework for predicting human behavior, precisely because it jettisons the normative demand for optimality in favor of a descriptive simulation of actual cognitive architecture.

### I. The Anatomy of the Intentional Stance

To understand the critique, one must first appreciate the precise mechanics of Dennett’s strategy. In *The Intentional Stance*, Dennett distinguishes three levels of prediction. The *physical stance* predicts behavior based on physical laws—a dropped stone will fall. The *design stance* predicts behavior based on the system’s designed function—a thermostat will turn on the furnace when the temperature drops. The *intentional stance* goes a step further: it predicts behavior by attributing to the system beliefs and desires and assuming it will act rationally to fulfill those desires given those beliefs. The intentional stance is not a claim about the internal reality of mental states; it is an instrumentalist strategy. A system is an intentional system if, and only if, treating it as such yields successful predictions. As Dennett famously puts it, we attribute to the system “those beliefs and desires it *ought* to have, given its place in the world and its purpose.” The predictive algorithm is simple: (1) determine the system’s goals (desires) and its informational state (beliefs); (2) assume the system will choose the action that maximizes the satisfaction of its desires given its beliefs; (3) predict that action.

The crucial element here is the *rationality assumption*. Dennett is explicit that the intentional stance treats the system as an “ideal” rational agent. It is a normative idealization. We do not predict what a *particular* person will do based on their actual psychological quirks or cognitive limitations; we predict what a *rational* agent with those beliefs and desires would do. This is why the stance works so well for chess computers: we assume the program will select the best move, and we are often right because the program was designed to be optimal. The same logic extends to humans. Dennett argues that human beings, as products of natural selection, have been “designed” by evolution to be rational in their environments. Just as a chess program is optimized to win, humans are optimized to maximize fitness, and thus their behavior approximates rational choice. The intentional stance works on humans because evolution has endowed us with a rational cognitive architecture.

This grounding in evolutionary biology is not incidental; it is the *sine qua non* of the stance’s applicability to biological organisms. In *Darwin’s Dangerous Idea*, Dennett elaborates on this connection. Natural selection is an algorithmic process that generates “free-floating rationales”—reasons for behavior that exist without any agent explicitly representing them. A mother bird’s instinct to feed her chicks is a free-floating rationale for the survival of her genes. Evolution designs organisms to be rational in the sense that their behaviors are, on average, fitness-enhancing. Therefore, when we adopt the intentional stance towards a human, we are implicitly invoking this evolutionary design. We are predicting that the human will act in a way that is *optimal* relative to their evolved goals. The rationality assumption is thus an adaptationist assumption: it presumes that natural selection has produced an optimally designed cognitive system, one that reliably tracks truth and maximizes utility.

### II. The Adaptationist Foundation: A House of Cards

The problem with this foundation is that the adaptationist program, at least in its strong form, has been subject to devastating critique. The most famous challenge comes from Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin in their seminal paper, “The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm: A Critique of the Adaptationist Programme.” Gould and Lewontin argue that not every trait of an organism is an adaptation—a feature shaped by natural selection for its current function. Many traits are *spandrels*: structural byproducts of other adaptations, or features that arise due to developmental constraints, genetic drift, or historical contingency. They use the architectural metaphor of the spandrels of San Marco—the triangular spaces between arches in a cathedral. These spaces were not designed as decorative areas, but once they existed, artists filled them with mosaics. Similarly, many biological traits are inevitable byproducts of other design features, and they may have no adaptive function whatsoever, or they may have been co-opted for a function they were not originally selected for.

If this is true for morphology, it is almost certainly true for cognition. The human mind is not a single, perfectly optimized rational engine. It is a patched-together collection of heuristics, biases, modular processes, and structural byproducts. Consider, for example, the phenomenon of *apophenia*—the tendency to perceive meaningful patterns in random data. This may be a spandrel of our highly developed pattern-recognition system, which was adaptive for detecting predators or food sources. But the same system that helps us find a camouflaged animal in the underbrush also leads us to see faces in clouds or conspiracies in random events. From the adaptationist perspective, our propensity to believe in superstitions or commit the gambler’s fallacy is not rational. It is a byproduct of a cognitive architecture that is not optimal for truth-tracking in all domains.

Dennett is aware of this critique and often dismisses it. He argues that even if a trait originated as a spandrel, it can be co-opted and refined by subsequent selection. He also argues that the intentional stance is a *predictive* tool, not an *explanatory* one. It does not matter if the underlying mechanism is a spandrel; what matters is whether the stance yields good predictions. However, this response conflates *predictive utility* with *explanatory truth*. If a cognitive mechanism is a spandrel—if it is not designed to be rational—then the intentional stance’s core assumption of optimality will lead to systematic predictive failures. The stance predicts that a person will act rationally to maximize their desires. But if their cognitive architecture includes a spandrel that systematically distorts their perception of probabilities (e.g., the conjunction fallacy), then their behavior will deviate from the rational ideal in a predictable, but non-rational, way. The intentional stance cannot accommodate this deviation without abandoning its normative premise. If we simply adjust the person’s “beliefs” to include the distorted probabilities, we are no longer using the intentional stance as Dennett defines it; we are engaging in a *post-hoc* rationalization, attributing to the person whatever beliefs are necessary to make their behavior appear rational. This renders the stance vacuous—it can explain anything but predict nothing.

### III. Gould and Lewontin’s Spandrels: The Achilles Heel of Optimality

To make this critique concrete, let us examine specific cognitive spandrels that directly undermine the intentional stance’s predictive power. Kahneman and Tversky’s work on prospect theory provides a rich source of examples. Consider *loss aversion*: humans systematically overweight losses relative to equivalent gains. A rational agent, as modeled by expected utility theory, should treat a loss of $100 and a gain of $100 as symmetric in magnitude (though opposite in sign). Yet humans consistently exhibit a strong asymmetry—the pain of losing $100 is far greater than the pleasure of gaining $100. This is not a mere performance error; it is a robust, replicable feature of human cognition. If I adopt the intentional stance towards a person, I would predict that they will choose the option that maximizes their expected utility. But loss aversion means they will often choose a suboptimal option (e.g., refusing a bet with positive expected value because the potential loss looms too large). The intentional stance fails to predict this behavior because it assumes optimality.

Dennett might respond that we can simply attribute to the person a desire to avoid losses. But this is cheating. The intentional stance’s predictive power comes from the *constraint* that beliefs and desires must be rationally connected to the environment. If we can arbitrarily posit any desire to fit any behavior, the stance becomes unfalsifiable. Moreover, loss aversion is not a *desire* in the intentional sense; it is a structural feature of the cognitive system. It is a spandrel—a byproduct of how our neural systems process gains and losses, likely evolved for reasons unrelated to rational choice under uncertainty. The intentional stance cannot model this because it treats the mind as a transparently rational system, not as a physical mechanism with quirky constraints.

Gould and Lewontin’s critique goes deeper than just pointing out exceptions. They argue that the adaptationist program—and by extension Dennett’s intentional stance—is a “Panglossian paradigm” that assumes everything is for the best in the best of all possible worlds. It treats natural selection as an omnipotent optimizer, ignoring the constraints of history, development, and physics. The human cognitive system is not a blank slate that evolution has filled with optimal solutions; it is a historical accretion of modifications, many of which are suboptimal or maladaptive in current environments. For instance, our craving for sugar and fat was adaptive in an environment of scarcity but is maladaptive in an environment of abundance. The intentional stance would predict that a rational agent would not overeat, because it leads to poor health and reduced fitness. Yet millions of people do exactly that. The intentional stance fails because it assumes the person’s desires are aligned with their evolutionary fitness, but the actual desires are the product of a cognitive system designed for a different environment. The spandrels of our ancestral past—our sweet tooth, our fear of snakes, our tendency to procrastinate—are not rational adaptations to the modern world; they are historical baggage. And the intentional stance cannot predict the behavior that baggage produces.

### IV. Stich’s Critique: The Systematic Irrationality of Human Beings

While Gould and Lewontin undermine the evolutionary foundation of the intentional stance, Stephen Stich attacks its psychological core. In works like *The Fragmentation of Reason* and his various critiques of the “pragmatic” theory of rationality, Stich argues that human beings are systematically irrational—not just occasionally, but in predictable and replicable ways. He points to a vast literature in cognitive psychology demonstrating that humans routinely violate the basic canons of logic and probability. The Wason selection task, for example, shows that most people fail to apply *modus tollens* (if P then Q; not Q; therefore not P) when the content is abstract, even though they are perfectly capable of doing so when the content is concrete and social. Base-rate neglect shows that people ignore statistical information in favor of vivid, anecdotal evidence. The conjunction fallacy shows that people judge a conjunction of two events as more probable than either event alone, violating the conjunction rule of probability theory.

Stich’s key argument is that these errors are not random noise; they are systematic and often immune to correction, even among highly educated individuals. More importantly, they are not easily explained by the intentional stance. If a person commits the conjunction fallacy, a rational agent would not. The intentional stance would predict that the person assigns a higher probability to the conjunction being true than to the individual conjunct, which is impossible. To accommodate this, Dennett would have to claim that the person has a *desire* to commit logical errors, or that they have *beliefs* that are inconsistent. But this is absurd. The person does not *believe* that Linda is both a bank teller and a feminist is more probable than Linda is a bank teller; they simply *judge* it to be so due to a cognitive heuristic (representativeness). The intentional stance, which operates at the level of beliefs and desires, cannot capture this because the error occurs at a sub-intentional level—the level of cognitive architecture.

Stich’s critique is more radical than simply saying humans make mistakes. He argues that the very concept of rationality used by Dennett is a normative ideal that does not correspond to any natural kind. There is no single, unified “rational” standard that humans are approximating. Instead, there are multiple, competing notions of rationality, and the human mind is a collection of various cognitive mechanisms, some of which are truth-tracking, some of which are pragmatic, and some of which are just plain quirky. If this is the case, then the intentional stance’s assumption of a single optimal rationality is not just empirically false; it is conceptually confused. The stance asks us to predict what a rational agent would do, but there is no such thing as *the* rational agent. There are only agents with specific cognitive architectures, and those architectures are full of biases, heuristics, and spandrels. The intentional stance is a tool for predicting the behavior of a mythical creature—the perfectly rational human—which does not exist.

### V. The Empathetic Alternative: A Coherent Framework for Suboptimality

Given the failures of the intentional stance, what alternative offers a more coherent predictive framework? I propose the empathetic approach, encapsulated in the question: “What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?” This is not merely a variant of the intentional stance; it is a fundamentally different epistemic strategy. The intentional stance is *third-personal* and *normative*. It asks: “What should a rational agent do?” The empathetic stance is *first-personal* and *descriptive*. It asks: “What would *I* do, given that I have a specific cognitive architecture, history, and set of constraints?” Crucially, the empathetic stance does not assume optimality. It assumes *simulation*.

This approach aligns with the simulation theory of mindreading, championed by philosophers like Alvin Goldman and Robert Gordon. According to simulation theory, we predict and explain others’ behavior by mentally simulating their cognitive processes. We put ourselves in their shoes, but we do not just imagine having their beliefs and desires; we imagine having their *cognitive quirks* as well. We use our own cognitive architecture as a model for theirs, adjusting for known differences. This allows us to predict suboptimal behavior directly. If I know that I have a tendency to be anchored by the first number I see, I can predict that he will also be anchored, even if the anchor is irrelevant. If I know that I am susceptible to loss aversion, I can predict that he will overreact to a potential loss. The empathetic stance embraces the spandrels and biases that the intentional stance tries to ignore.

This approach is more coherent because it is grounded in a realistic ontology of the human mind. It does not posit an idealized rational agent; it posits a real, flawed, historically contingent cognitive system. This aligns with Herbert Simon’s concept of *bounded rationality*. Humans do not maximize; they *satisfice*. They use heuristics, which are fast and frugal but often lead to systematic errors. The empathetic stance can model satisficing: I predict that he will choose the first option that meets a certain threshold of acceptability, not the optimal option, because that is what I would do. The intentional stance cannot model this because satisficing is, by definition, suboptimal.

Furthermore, the empathetic stance handles individual differences far better than the intentional stance. The intentional stance treats all humans as interchangeable rational agents. The empathetic stance allows us to adjust for personality, intelligence, cognitive style, and emotional state. “If I were like him” is a flexible parameter. I can simulate a person who is more impulsive, more anxious, or more optimistic than I am. This is not possible under the intentional stance, which forces us to attribute to the person the beliefs and desires they *ought* to have, regardless of their actual psychological makeup.

### VI. Addressing Objections to the Empathetic Stance

One might object that the empathetic stance is prone to egocentric bias. I assume that he is like me, but he might not be. This is a valid concern. However, this is a *practical* limitation, not a *conceptual* one. The intentional stance’s failure is conceptual: it is built on a false assumption of optimality. The empathetic stance’s failure is merely epistemic: I might not have accurate knowledge of his cognitive architecture. This is a problem that can be mitigated through learning, observation, and cultural knowledge. I can learn about cognitive biases, personality types, and cultural differences to refine my simulations. But the intentional stance cannot be refined in this way; its core premise is simply wrong.

Another objection is that the empathetic stance is just a “thicker” version of the intentional stance. Dennett might argue that when I ask “What would I do if I were like him?”, I am still attributing beliefs and desires, just more detailed ones. But this misses the crucial distinction. In the intentional stance, the *rationality constraint* is normative and global. It dictates that beliefs and desires must be rationally connected to the environment. In the empathetic stance, the *cognitive architecture constraint* is descriptive and local. It dictates that the simulation must reproduce the specific cognitive processes of the target. The intentional stance says: “Predict what a rational agent would do.” The empathetic stance says: “Predict what this specific agent, with his specific biases and spandrels, would do.” The former cannot account for suboptimality without collapsing into *post-hoc* rationalization. The latter embraces suboptimality as a primary predictive feature.
