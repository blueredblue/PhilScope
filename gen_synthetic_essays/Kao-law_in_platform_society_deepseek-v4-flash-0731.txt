### The Digital Leviathan: Reconciling Public Governance, Private Property, and Expressive Freedom

**Introduction**

The modern public square is no longer a physical plaza but an algorithmic feed. The agora of the twenty-first century is a privately owned digital platform—a social network, a search engine, or an app store—whose architecture dictates the flow of information, the formation of public opinion, and the very conditions of social and economic participation. This transformation presents a profound philosophical crisis. We have inherited legal and political frameworks designed for a world where the state was the sole sovereign, and the market was a realm of voluntary private action. Yet, digital platforms have collapsed this distinction, wielding power that is simultaneously economic, social, and political. They are not merely businesses; they are the infrastructural conduits of modern life.

This essay evaluates whether digital platforms should be governed as quasi-public actors, subject to constitutional and public law principles. I will argue that they must be. The functional reality of platforms as the primary arena for public discourse and essential services renders them akin to public utilities, and their governance cannot be left solely to private contract. To substantiate this claim, I will engage with the foundational tension between John Rawls’s theory of distributive justice—which mandates treating access to digital communication as a primary good—and Robert Nozick’s libertarian defense of private property rights and voluntary exchange. I will demonstrate that Nozick’s defense collapses when confronted with the realities of market power and network effects. Furthermore, I will utilize Helen Nissenbaum’s framework of contextual integrity to show that the absence of meaningful exit options renders user consent illusory, thereby delegitimizing the contractual basis of platform governance. I will then apply Philip Pettit’s republican conception of freedom as non-domination to argue that the arbitrary, unaccountable power of corporate content moderation constitutes a structural violation of liberty. Finally, I will formulate a concluding argument that public law can impose transparency and accountability on algorithmic governance without violating Ronald Dworkin’s principles of expressive freedom, provided that regulation targets the procedural architecture of platforms rather than the substantive content of speech. The goal is not to make platforms democratic, but to make them non-dominating, ensuring that their immense power is exercised in a manner that is transparent, contestable, and respectful of the equal moral standing of all users.

**Part I: The Quasi-Public Nature of Digital Platforms**

To argue for the application of public law, one must first establish that digital platforms are not simply private actors. The legal distinction between “state action” and “private action” is enshrined in constitutional law, limiting the application of rights like free speech to government entities. However, this binary is anachronistic. A platform like Facebook or X (formerly Twitter) does not merely host content; it curates it through proprietary algorithms that determine visibility, reach, and virality. This algorithmic curation is a form of editorial judgment, but unlike a newspaper, it is not guided by a coherent editorial philosophy. Instead, it is driven by engagement metrics designed to maximize advertising revenue. The platform thus functions as a *de facto* legislator of speech, establishing rules of conduct, defining acceptable discourse, and enforcing these rules through automated and human moderation.

The quasi-public nature of these platforms is further underscored by their infrastructural monopoly. Network effects create a powerful lock-in: the value of a platform increases with the number of users, making it extraordinarily difficult for a challenger to emerge. A user who wishes to leave Facebook to protest its policies faces the social cost of losing contact with friends, family, and professional networks. This is not a simple market choice; it is a constraint on social existence. In many jurisdictions, platforms have become the primary means for accessing essential services—job listings, government information, news, and civic engagement. When a platform deplatforms a user, it is not merely revoking a service; it is severing them from the public sphere itself. This functional role mirrors that of a public utility (like electricity or water) or a public forum (like a town square). As Jürgen Habermas theorized, the public sphere is the space where citizens deliberate on matters of common concern. When that sphere is privately owned and algorithmically mediated, the public character of the deliberation is compromised.

Therefore, a functionalist approach is warranted. We should classify actors based on the function they perform in society, not merely their legal incorporation status. A private company that controls the infrastructure of public communication exercises a form of sovereign power. As such, it should be subject to the normative constraints that we place on sovereign power: due process, non-arbitrariness, and accountability to the public interest. This does not mean nationalizing platforms or treating them as government agencies, but it does mean that their governance should be constrained by public law principles, such as transparency, non-discrimination, and procedural fairness.

**Part II: Rawls vs. Nozick: The Battle for the Digital Primary Good**

The foundational philosophical dispute over the governance of digital platforms can be framed as a clash between the egalitarian liberalism of John Rawls and the libertarianism of Robert Nozick. This clash is not merely academic; it determines whether we view algorithmic bias as an injustice to be corrected or a private choice to be tolerated.

John Rawls’s theory of justice as fairness is built upon the idea of the “original position,” a hypothetical scenario where individuals choose the principles of justice from behind a “veil of ignorance,” unaware of their own social status, talents, or conception of the good. From this impartial standpoint, Rawls argues, rational individuals would choose two principles: (1) equal basic liberties for all, and (2) the difference principle, which states that social and economic inequalities are permissible only if they benefit the least advantaged members of society. Central to this framework is the concept of “primary goods”—things that every rational person wants regardless of their life plan, such as rights, liberties, opportunities, income, and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect.

In the digital age, access to communication and information must be considered a primary good. Without it, an individual cannot exercise their basic liberties of speech and assembly, cannot pursue fair equality of opportunity in education and employment, and cannot participate in the democratic process. A person who is digitally excluded is effectively a second-class citizen. Applying Rawls’s difference principle to digital platforms yields a powerful mandate. The algorithmic architecture of these platforms—which determines who gets heard, who gets seen, and who gets marginalized—must be arranged so that it benefits the least advantaged. This means that platforms cannot simply optimize for engagement if that optimization systematically silences minority voices, amplifies misinformation harmful to vulnerable groups, or creates filter bubbles that entrench social divisions. A Rawlsian framework demands that platforms ensure a fair distribution of communicative power, treating the digital public sphere as a common resource that must be justly allocated.

Conversely, Robert Nozick offers a formidable defense of private property rights that would reject any such distributive mandate. In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, Nozick argues that individuals have inviolable rights, and that any state intervention beyond the minimal night-watchman state (which protects against force, fraud, and theft) is unjust. His entitlement theory of justice holds that a distribution is just if it arises from just acquisition (e.g., through labor) and just transfer (e.g., through voluntary exchange). Digital platforms are private property. They were built by founders, investors, and employees who voluntarily entered into contracts. Users who sign up voluntarily agree to the Terms of Service. Therefore, the platform owners have the right to set the rules of their property, including who is allowed to speak and what content is allowed. For Nozick, forcing a platform to host certain speech or to distribute communicative power more equally is a violation of the owner’s property rights. It is akin to forcing a homeowner to allow strangers to use their living room for political rallies.

Nozick’s argument, however, rests on a crucial empirical assumption: that the exchanges are truly voluntary. He acknowledges that property rights are subject to the Lockean proviso—that acquisition must leave “enough and as good” for others. In a competitive market with many platforms, a user might be said to have a meaningful choice. If Facebook is too restrictive, one can join Mastodon or Gab. However, the reality of the digital economy is characterized by natural monopolies. Network effects create a situation where the dominant platform is not merely one option among many but the *only* viable venue for reaching a certain audience. The Lockean proviso is violated because the acquisition of the platform’s market dominance has not left enough communicative space for others to meaningfully compete. Furthermore, Nozick’s conception of voluntary exchange is abstruse. A worker who accepts a hazardous job because they will starve otherwise is not, in a meaningful sense, acting voluntarily. Similarly, a user who accepts a platform’s Terms of Service because they need to stay in contact with their elderly parents or find a job is not exercising a free choice; they are capitulating to a structural necessity.

Thus, while Nozick’s framework provides a powerful caution against heavy-handed state intervention, it fails to account for the coercive nature of platform monopolies. Rawls’s framework, which prioritizes the basic structure of society and the fair distribution of primary goods, offers a more compelling normative foundation for the digital age. If access to digital communication is a primary good, then the state has a legitimate interest—indeed, a duty—to ensure that this good is distributed fairly. This does not require abolishing private ownership of platforms, but it does require that this ownership be constrained by public principles designed to secure justice.

**Part III: Nissenbaum and the Illusion of Consent**

A central pillar of the Nozickian defense is the notion of user consent. Users agree to the Terms of Service, thereby voluntarily surrendering certain rights in exchange for access to the platform. This contractual model presumes that users are autonomous agents capable of making informed, voluntary choices. Helen Nissenbaum’s framework of contextual integrity fundamentally challenges this presumption.

Nissenbaum argues that privacy is not merely about secrecy or control over personal information, but about the appropriate flow of information within specific social contexts. We have different expectations of privacy in a doctor’s office, a family dinner, and a public park. Information that is appropriate to share in one context may be inappropriate in another. Digital platforms systematically violate contextual integrity by harvesting vast amounts of personal data and using it for purposes that users did not anticipate or consent to—targeted advertising, behavioral profiling, and algorithmic manipulation. The problem is not just that platforms collect data; it is that they collect data in a way that breaks the contextual norms that govern social life.

The concept of consent is central to Nissenbaum’s framework. For consent to be valid, it must be informed, voluntary, and specific to the context. However, the consent obtained by digital platforms is none of these things. The Terms of Service are lengthy, jargon-filled legal documents that few users read. Even if they did read them, they would not fully understand the complex algorithms and data-sharing arrangements described. The consent is therefore not *informed*. It is also not *voluntary*. As I argued in the previous section, users lack meaningful exit options. The platform is not a luxury good; it is a necessity for social and economic participation. When a user is forced to accept terms they do not understand in order to access an essential service, their consent is coerced.

Nissenbaum’s most potent insight is that the absence of reasonable exit options renders consent *illusory*. If the only way to participate in modern society is to use a platform, and the platform’s terms are non-negotiable, then the user’s “choice” is a Hobson’s choice—a choice with no real alternative. This illusory consent undermines the moral legitimacy of the contractual relationship. Nozick’s argument relies on the idea that the user has voluntarily alienated their rights. But if the user had no realistic alternative, if the power imbalance is so vast that the user is essentially at the mercy of the platform, then the contract is a form of domination, not freedom. The state, in such cases, has a legitimate role in intervening to ensure that the terms of exchange are fair, just as it intervenes in other contexts where there is a massive power asymmetry, such as in labor law or consumer protection law.

Applying Nissenbaum’s framework, we can see that the platform’s governance is not merely a private matter. It is a matter of public concern because it violates the contextual integrity of public life. The platform has transformed the public sphere into a data-extraction machine, and the user’s “consent” to this transformation is a legal fiction. Public law must step in to restore contextual integrity, not by dictating what content can be shared, but by regulating the flow of information and the terms of the exchange. This means mandating clear, accessible privacy policies, prohibiting the use of dark patterns that manipulate user choices, and requiring algorithmic transparency so that users understand how their data is being used to shape their experience.

**Part IV: Pettit and the Arbitrary Power of Moderation**

The most compelling philosophical argument for subjecting platforms to public law comes from the republican tradition, particularly as articulated by Philip Pettit. In his theory of freedom as non-domination, Pettit defines liberty not as the absence of interference (negative liberty) but as the absence of *domination*. Domination occurs when an agent has the capacity to interfere arbitrarily in the choices of another, on an ad hoc basis, without regard to the interests or opinions of the dominated party. A slave master who is benevolent and never actually interferes with a slave is still dominating the slave because the master possesses the *capacity* to interfere at will. This capacity renders the slave’s status precarious and unfree.

Pettit’s framework is devastatingly applicable to digital platforms. Content moderation is a form of interference. Platforms can delete posts, shadowban accounts, demonetize channels, and deplatform users entirely. The key question is whether this interference is *arbitrary*. Pettit argues that interference is arbitrary when it is not forced to track the interests of the affected party. In a legitimate state, interference (e.g., criminal law) is non-arbitrary because it is constrained by the rule of law, subject to due process, and accountable to the public. The state must justify its actions, provide reasons, and offer avenues for appeal.

Corporate content moderation, however, is profoundly arbitrary. The rules are often vague and inconsistently applied. Algorithms are opaque, and their decisions are frequently inscrutable even to their own engineers. When a user is deplatformed, they are rarely given a clear explanation of the specific rule they violated, nor are they afforded a meaningful opportunity to appeal. The decision is made by a private corporation whose primary interest is profit, not justice. The platform has the capacity to interfere with a user’s access to the public sphere on a whim, driven by an algorithm or a low-paid moderator in a different country. This is the very definition of domination. The user is not free, regardless of whether the platform actually interferes with them most of the time. The mere fact that the platform *could* arbitrarily silence them at any moment creates a chilling effect that undermines their autonomy.

Pettit’s solution to domination is the constitutionalization of power. Power must be checked by an external authority that forces it to track the interests of the governed. For the state, this is the constitution, the separation of powers, and judicial review. For digital platforms, this requires a form of public law that imposes due process constraints on their moderation practices. This does not mean the state dictates what content must be allowed or banned. Rather, it means that the *process* of moderation must be subject to public scrutiny and procedural fairness. Platforms should be required to publish clear, non-contradictory rules. They should be required to provide users with specific, individualized reasons for moderation decisions. They should be required to establish independent appeals processes. And critically, they should be required to make their algorithms transparent and auditable, so that we can determine whether the interference is systematic or arbitrary.

By imposing these procedural constraints, we transform the platform’s power from arbitrary to non-arbitrary. The platform can still moderate content, but it must do so in a way that is predictable, reason-giving, and contestable. This is the essence of republican freedom. A user on a well-regulated platform is like a citizen in a constitutional republic—subject to rules, but free from domination because the rules are publicly known and applied equally.

**Part V: Dworkin and the Limits of Regulation**

Having established the need for public law intervention, we must confront a significant objection: does such regulation violate freedom of expression? This is where Ronald Dworkin’s principles of expressive freedom become crucial. Dworkin argued that the state must not restrict speech on the grounds that it finds the speech offensive, unjust, or wrong. He distinguished between *content-based* restrictions (which the state cannot impose) and *time, place, and manner* restrictions (which it can). The state cannot ban a political protest because it disagrees with the message, but it can require a permit for the protest to ensure public safety.

The challenge is to design public law for digital platforms that respects Dworkin’s prohibition on content-based censorship. We cannot have the state telling platforms to ban certain viewpoints or to promote others. That would be a gross violation of expressive freedom and would turn the state into a propagandist. However, we *can* regulate the *architecture* of the platform. Dworkin’s principles allow for regulation of the conditions under which speech occurs, as long as the regulation is viewpoint-neutral and serves a compelling public interest.

Therefore, the transparency and accountability measures I have proposed are compatible with Dworkin’s framework. Requiring algorithmic transparency does not tell a platform *what* to say; it tells the platform *how* to govern its speech space. It demands that the platform disclose the criteria it uses to rank, promote, or suppress content. This is analogous to requiring a government agency to publish its rulemaking procedures. It does not dictate the outcome of the agency’s decisions, but it ensures that the decisions are made in a reasoned, non-arbitrary manner. Similarly, requiring an independent appeals process for moderation decisions does not dictate what content is allowed; it ensures that the platform’s application of its own rules is fair and consistent.

Crucially, Dworkin also emphasized the importance of “equal concern and respect.” The state must treat all citizens as equals. Applying this to platforms, we can argue that algorithmic systems that systematically disadvantage certain groups—based on race, gender, or political affiliation—violate the principle of equal concern. Public law can require platforms to conduct audits to detect and correct algorithmic bias. This is not censorship; it is a demand for non-discrimination. Just as a public utility cannot discriminate in providing water or electricity, a quasi-public digital platform cannot discriminate in providing access to the public sphere.

The distinction between regulating the *modality* of speech and regulating its *content* is the key to reconciling public accountability with expressive freedom. We can impose a “procedural constitution” on platforms, mandating transparency, due process, and non-discrimination, without ever telling the platform which ideas are true or false, good or bad. This respects Dworkin’s core insight that the state must remain neutral on the *value* of ideas while still ensuring that the *marketplace* of ideas is not corrupted by arbitrary power. The state is not a censor; it is a referee. It ensures that the game of public discourse is played fairly, even if it does not determine who wins.

**Conclusion**

The digital revolution has fundamentally altered the relationship between the individual, the state, and the market. We have ceded control of our public sphere to private corporations whose power rivals that of sovereign states. To cling to the outdated legal fiction that these platforms are merely private actors engaged in voluntary exchanges is to abandon the very principles of liberty and justice that underpin our constitutional order.

The philosophical case for governing digital platforms as quasi-public actors is overwhelming. Rawls’s theory of justice compels us to recognize access to digital communication as a primary good, demanding a fair distribution of communicative power. Nozick’s libertarian defense of private property, while intellectually powerful, is rendered untenable by the reality of monopolistic markets and the absence of meaningful choice. Nissenbaum’s framework of contextual integrity reveals that user consent is illusory, a legal fiction that masks a profound power asymmetry. Pettit’s republican conception of freedom as non-domination exposes the arbitrary nature of corporate content moderation, demonstrating that such power is a structural violation of liberty.

The concluding argument is that public law can and must impose transparency and accountability on algorithmic governance without violating Dworkin’s principles of expressive freedom. The solution lies in a procedural, rather than substantive, approach. We must regulate the *process* by which platforms govern speech—their algorithms, their moderation procedures, their data practices—while remaining strictly neutral on the *content* of that speech. This means mandating algorithmic audits, requiring due process in moderation appeals, and prohibiting discriminatory design. Such measures do not censor any viewpoint; they merely ensure that no viewpoint is silenced through the arbitrary, unaccountable exercise of corporate power.

Ultimately, the goal is not to make digital platforms democratic, for they are not governments. The goal is to make them *non-dominating*. We seek to ensure that users are not subjects of a digital Leviathan, but free citizens in a digitally mediated public sphere. By applying the principles of public law—transparency, accountability, and non-arbitrariness—we can reclaim the promise of a free and open internet, not by freeing it from all constraints, but by subjecting its immense power to the rule of law. The digital square must be governed, but it must be governed justly, with the same respect for human dignity and autonomy that we demand of our own political institutions.
