# Beyond Inner Display: Knowledge, Consciousness, and the Architecture of Cognition

The traditional philosophical conception of knowledge often begins with an apparently innocent picture. A subject knows something when the subject possesses an internal representation with a determinate content, and that content is somehow available to the subject as content. The mind is imagined as containing inner states that are, at least in principle, transparent to introspection: if I know that snow is white, there is something in me that has the meaning *snow is white*, and the meaning is available for inspection, deployment, or report. Even when philosophers reject the classical definition of knowledge as justified true belief, they frequently retain this deeper picture. Knowledge remains a kind of inner possession, and cognition remains a matter of accessing the contents of an internal theatre.

This essay evaluates the claim that such a conception relies upon a false assumption of internal transparency. The claim is not that people never have conscious access to their thoughts, nor that introspection is wholly unreliable. It is that cognitive systems need not contain a single, privileged, internally displayed version of what they know. What a person knows may instead be realized in a distributed, temporally extended, and format-dependent organization of dispositions, discriminations, memories, inferential capacities, perceptual skills, and action-guiding processes. On this view, knowing is not necessarily the possession of an inner sentence whose meaning is transparently present to a central observer. It is the stable availability of information within an organized cognitive economy.

Daniel Dennett’s Multiple Drafts Model provides a powerful framework for developing this proposal. The model denies that consciousness consists in the production of a single final representation in a central Cartesian theatre. Instead, the brain continuously generates multiple, revisable interpretive drafts. Different processes extract information at different times and in different formats; some become available for verbal report, some guide action, some influence memory, and some disappear without entering explicit awareness. If this model is applied to the nature of knowledge, it undermines the demand for a single internally transparent locus in which the subject’s knowledge is fully present.

The consequences extend beyond debates about consciousness. The Multiple Drafts perspective helps dissolve Frank Jackson’s knowledge argument, weakens the force of John Searle’s Chinese Room analogy, and reframes the dispute between syntax and semantics. It also offers a way of responding to Colin McGinn’s thesis of cognitive closure: rather than treating consciousness as a metaphysical mystery lying outside human cognitive reach, we can understand the apparent mystery as a consequence of demanding a form of self-representation that the mind’s architecture does not provide. Finally, the same approach can reconcile conceptual and perceptual knowledge by treating them as members of one unified category realized in different representational formats. This reconception has important implications for scientific progress in the philosophy of mind, because it shifts the central question from “Where is the meaning inside the mind?” to “What kinds of organized capacities make meaningful cognition possible?”

## I. The Assumption of Internal Transparency

The assumption of internal transparency takes several forms. In one form, it says that a mental state has a determinate content only if the content is internally available as such. In another, it says that a subject’s knowledge must be encoded in a format that could, in principle, be inspected by a central self. In its strongest form, it treats the mind as a theatre containing representations before an inner spectator. The spectator may be called the self, the subject, or consciousness, but the structure remains the same: an internal item is presented, and the subject apprehends its content.

This picture can be associated with several familiar philosophical assumptions. First, knowledge is often treated as a relation between a subject and a proposition. The subject knows that *p* by standing in a cognitive relation to the proposition *p*. Second, propositions are often implicitly modeled as sentence-like objects. The mind possesses an internally available assertion, and reasoning consists in manipulating or endorsing such assertions. Third, conscious awareness is treated as the standard of cognitive reality. If a process is not available to awareness, it seems not to count as genuine understanding or knowledge.

These assumptions are not entirely arbitrary. Human beings can entertain propositions, give reasons, make judgments, and report what they believe. Explicit conceptual knowledge has a linguistic or language-like character. Introspection also provides some real access to our experiences and thoughts. But it does not follow that every form of knowledge has the same format, or that every cognitive process depends on an internally displayed content.

Consider the knowledge involved in recognizing a familiar face, riding a bicycle, parsing a sentence, identifying a melody, or navigating a room. Such capacities may depend on highly structured information, yet the information need not be represented as a sentence that the subject can inspect. A skilled pianist knows how to execute a passage without possessing an explicit proposition that specifies every finger movement. A person may know that a face is familiar without being able to identify whose face it is. An infant can distinguish linguistic patterns before possessing the concepts required to describe those patterns. The knowledge is real because it structures behavior, discrimination, anticipation, and learning, not because it appears in a single transparent format.

Internal transparency is therefore a misleading standard. It makes explicit, reportable, propositionally organized cognition the measure of all cognition. It also encourages a regress. If the mind contains a representation whose meaning must be understood, then there must be an inner interpreter who understands it. But if that interpreter operates on representations, the same problem arises again. A central observer does not explain understanding; it merely relocates the problem.

Rejecting internal transparency does not amount to rejecting mental representation. It means rejecting the idea that representation requires a final, centrally presented, self-interpreting object. Mental content can be realized by the functional organization of many processes. A representation need not be a sentence in an inner language; it can be a pattern of activation, a perceptual model, a learned association, a motor policy, or a network of inferential dispositions. The relevant question is not whether a representation is transparently present to an inner observer, but what the representation enables the system to do.

## II. Dennett’s Multiple Drafts Model

Dennett’s Multiple Drafts Model offers a direct alternative to the Cartesian theatre. According to the model, there is no single moment at which all perceptual and cognitive information is gathered into one definitive conscious representation. Instead, the brain performs parallel and successive processes of interpretation. Stimuli are analyzed through multiple pathways; partial results are revised; later information can affect how earlier events are categorized; and different outputs become available to different cognitive systems.

The metaphor of “drafts” is important. A draft is not necessarily a private image or sentence. It is an evolving interpretation embodied in the brain’s ongoing activity. Some drafts influence verbal report, others guide behavior, others affect memory or emotional response. There may be no further fact about consciousness beyond the content and consequences of these distributed processes. The conscious event is not an additional inner presentation over and above the relevant cognitive organization.

This model challenges internal transparency in two ways. First, it denies that there must be one canonical format in which a cognitive event is finally and completely represented. The same stimulus can be encoded in visual, semantic, motor, emotional, and linguistic formats. Second, it denies that there must be a central processor who surveys these formats and combines them into a unified experience. Unity is achieved functionally, through coordination among processes, rather than through inspection by an inner spectator.

Knowledge can be understood in an analogous way. To know that a person is approaching may involve visual categorization, memory-based recognition, emotional appraisal, linguistic identification, and preparation for interaction. There need not be a single internal sentence that collects all of this information. What matters is that the system possesses an organized capacity to distinguish the person, anticipate relevant events, answer appropriate questions, and act accordingly. Knowledge is distributed across drafts and dispositions.

This does not make knowledge vague or subjective. A system either has or lacks certain capacities relative to a context and a standard of correctness. Nor does it imply that all interpretations are equally valid. The drafts are constrained by sensory input, bodily structure, learned regularities, causal interactions, social practices, and the world itself. A person’s knowledge that there is a tree outside is not merely a free-floating construction. It is sustained by perceptual access, memory, environmental coupling, and the ability to successfully navigate and report the relevant situation.

The Multiple Drafts Model therefore replaces a storage metaphor with an organizational metaphor. Knowledge is not primarily a hidden object stored in a mental container. It is a pattern of availability distributed across the cognitive system. Some knowledge is accessible to explicit reflection; some is accessible only through performance; some influences behavior without being reportable; and some may be activated only under particular environmental conditions. These are not necessarily different metaphysical kinds of knowledge. They are different ways in which one cognitive economy can store and deploy information.

## III. Jackson’s Knowledge Argument and the Dissolution of the Inner Fact

Frank Jackson’s knowledge argument is designed to establish that physical information is insufficient for complete knowledge of consciousness. Mary, a brilliant color scientist, is imagined to have complete physical knowledge about color vision while living in a black-and-white environment. When Mary leaves the room and sees a red object, Jackson argues, she learns something new: what red looks like. Since she already knew all the physical facts, it appears that there are nonphysical facts about experience.

The argument gains much of its force from an image of knowledge as the possession of a determinate inner content. Mary knows every physical description, but she lacks an experiential item. Upon seeing red, she acquires the missing item: the qualitative character of red experience. The new knowledge seems to be a fact or content presented to her consciousness in a way that physical descriptions could not capture.

The Multiple Drafts Model does not deny that Mary undergoes a significant cognitive change. It denies that the best description of this change is the acquisition of a nonphysical inner object or a newly revealed intrinsic property. Before leaving the room, Mary possesses extensive conceptual, theoretical, and inferential knowledge about color vision. What she lacks is a set of perceptual capacities and formats that can be acquired only through the relevant kind of interaction with the world. When she sees red, new perceptual dispositions are established: she can recognize red objects directly, imagine or remember red experiences, compare red with other colors in a perceptually grounded way, and use these capacities in new forms of reasoning and action.

The distinction here is between knowing *about* an experience and being able to undergo, recognize, remember, imagine, or use that experience. Mary’s knowledge changes because her cognitive system acquires a new format of access. That change is genuine, but it need not involve the apprehension of a nonphysical fact. The perceptual draft generated by exposure to red becomes integrated with her conceptual and inferential resources. She now possesses abilities that cannot be obtained merely by reading more descriptions, just as one cannot acquire the skill of riding a bicycle through a complete propositional account of balance and motion.

This response does not trivialize the intuition behind the argument. Mary does learn something. The mistake lies in interpreting learning as the discovery of an additional fact over and above all physical and functional facts. The argument assumes that if Mary’s post-release state differs cognitively from her pre-release state, then she must have acquired a new proposition or nonphysical quale. But the difference can instead consist in a new capacity, a new representational format, and a new integration among existing processes.

The argument also presupposes a sharp division between “physical information” and “experience.” Yet an embodied perceptual process is itself a physical process with a distinctive functional and representational role. A red experience is not exhausted by a verbal description, but it does not follow that it is nonphysical. There are many physical states whose significance depends on how a system is organized and what it can do. A map is physical, but its navigational significance cannot be identified with the paper and ink considered apart from their use. Likewise, a perceptual state has experiential significance because it participates in a complex system of discrimination, memory, attention, action, and report.

The Multiple Drafts Model thus dissolves the knowledge argument by rejecting its hidden premise: that every genuine addition to knowledge must be the acquisition of a centrally inspectable fact. Mary acquires a new way of representing and using information. The change is epistemically important, but it is compatible with physicalism and with a distributed account of consciousness.

## IV. Searle’s Chinese Room and the Limits of Symbol-Centered Understanding

John Searle’s Chinese Room analogy targets the claim that a computer executing the right program thereby understands Chinese. The person inside the room manipulates Chinese symbols according to formal rules without understanding their meanings. From this, Searle concludes that syntax is not sufficient for semantics and that computational processes, considered as formal symbol manipulation, cannot generate genuine understanding.

The argument exposes a real danger. If cognition is described merely as the transformation of syntactic symbols according to rules, then it is difficult to see where meaning enters. A purely formal system appears capable of preserving structural relations while remaining semantically empty. The person in the room does not understand Chinese, even though the system produces appropriate Chinese outputs.

However, the Chinese Room gains its force by accepting the same central assumption that supports internal transparency: understanding must be located in a particular internally accessible representation or process. Searle asks whether the person understands, whether the room understands, or whether the whole system understands. But each option is considered as though understanding required an entity that internally possesses Chinese meanings in a form available for inspection. If no such entity can be identified, understanding appears absent.

A Multiple Drafts approach changes the question. Understanding need not be an inner semantic glow added to syntax. It is a capacity realized by a whole system’s ongoing organization. The relevant system must be able to connect linguistic inputs with perception, memory, action, inference, learning, social interaction, and world-directed correction. A room containing a person who mechanically follows rules may lack understanding if the system is isolated from these wider capacities. But this does not show that computation as such is insufficient. It shows that a narrow conception of computation—one consisting of ungrounded symbol shuffling—does not amount to the full architecture of understanding.

The system reply to Searle is often criticized because the person could memorize the entire rulebook and internalize the room’s operations. Searle responds that the person would still not understand Chinese. The deeper issue, however, is not whether the person has an introspectively accessible Chinese meaning. It is whether the organized system has the functional and world-involving capacities characteristic of understanding. If the system genuinely integrates perception, memory, learning, action, and linguistic interaction, then denying its understanding merely because no inner spectator experiences Chinese semantics risks begging the question.

Rejecting internal transparency also helps clarify the syntax/semantics problem. Syntax and semantics are not necessarily two separate ingredients, one formal and one mysterious, that must be connected by an additional mental act. Syntax concerns the structural organization of representations and transformations. Semantics concerns the role those representations play in a larger system’s successful engagement with the world, other agents, goals, and norms of correctness. Meaning can therefore be an emergent property of organized use rather than a private substance added to formal structure.

This does not mean that every syntactic system has meaning. A random symbol manipulator lacks semantics because it lacks the relevant causal, functional, historical, and normative connections. But the absence of an inner semantic display is not the reason. Meaning is not missing because no homunculus can look at the symbols and read their content. It is missing when the symbols fail to participate in the right kind of integrated cognitive economy.

The Chinese Room consequently identifies an important limitation of disembodied, contextless models of cognition but does not establish that artificial systems are inherently incapable of understanding. If a system can develop multiple drafts that interact with perception, memory, action, learning, and communication, then its semantic capacities may be realized in the organization of the whole system. The appropriate question is empirical and architectural: what kinds of integration are necessary for understanding?

## V. Syntax and Semantics as Format and Function

The traditional syntax/semantics dichotomy often assumes that syntax belongs to the physical or computational side of mind, while semantics belongs to a separate realm of conscious interpretation. On this view, formal symbols have shapes and relations but no meanings; a mind must somehow assign meanings to them. Yet if meaning assignment requires an inner interpreter, the explanatory problem reappears inside the interpreter.

The rejection of internal transparency offers a different resolution. Syntax and semantics can be distinguished without being metaphysically separated. Syntax describes relations among states within a representational format. Semantics describes how those states function within an organism’s broader engagement with the world. A state can have semantic significance because it is reliably connected to conditions, goals, inferences, expectations, and actions. The semantic property is not a further inner object; it is a property of the organized relation between representation, system, and environment.

Different formats can carry overlapping semantic content. A written sentence, a visual image, a remembered melody, and a motor routine may all encode information relevant to the same object or event, but they do so differently. The meaning is not identical with any one format, yet neither is it independent of format. There is no abstract content floating free of all realization. To know that a cup is on the table may involve a visual representation of the cup, a linguistic judgment, a motor plan for reaching toward it, and a practical expectation about what will happen if one grasps it. These formats are not interchangeable, but they can be semantically coordinated.

This approach also explains why translation between formats is often possible but never perfect. A proposition can describe an experience, but it cannot replace the perceptual or embodied capacities through which the experience is encountered. A motor skill can embody knowledge that is difficult to articulate. A diagram can make spatial relations available in a way that prose does not. The existence of multiple formats is not evidence for multiple kinds of mind-stuff. It is evidence that cognition is heterogeneous in realization.

Meaning, then, is neither reducible to bare syntax nor opposed to it as an immaterial supplement. It is a functional and historical achievement of systems whose formal structures are embedded in patterns of learning and use. This is compatible with Dennett’s broader naturalism: the intentional vocabulary remains legitimate when it captures stable patterns in an agent’s behavior and internal organization, even though no single internal sentence or spectator possesses the meaning in isolation.

## VI. McGinn and Cognitive Closure

Colin McGinn’s thesis of cognitive closure holds that the human mind may be constitutionally incapable of solving the problem of consciousness. Just as a dog cannot understand higher mathematics, human beings may be unable to form the concepts required to understand how consciousness arises from the brain. McGinn’s position is not necessarily that consciousness is supernatural. It is that the relevant explanatory relation may lie beyond the cognitive powers supplied by our biological architecture.

The rejection of internal transparency provides a way to reinterpret both the appeal and the limitations of cognitive closure. The appeal arises because consciousness seems to demand a self-revealing explanation. We want to know how physical processes produce subjective experience, but we also expect the explanation to be available in the same conceptual and introspective terms in which experience is given. If no physical account reproduces the felt character of experience as an internally presented object, the explanatory gap appears absolute.

But this demand may be mistaken. The brain does not need to contain a transparent representation of its own total operation. Indeed, Dennett’s model predicts that it will not. Consciousness is generated by distributed, competing, revisable processes, not by a single system whose entire operation is displayed to itself. The fact that the mind cannot produce a final, complete, first-person presentation of how it produces consciousness may therefore reflect architectural limits without implying metaphysical closure.

In this sense, rejecting internal transparency weakens McGinn’s argument from inscrutability. Consciousness may be difficult to understand because our ordinary concepts are adapted to describe unified objects and inner appearances, whereas the underlying processes are distributed and format-dependent. Scientific explanation often succeeds by replacing familiar categories rather than satisfying them directly. Temperature was not explained by discovering a hidden “warmth substance”; it was reconceived in terms of molecular motion. Life was not explained by locating a vital force; it was reconceived in terms of biological organization. Consciousness may similarly require a shift from inner presentation to globally coordinated cognitive function.

Still, this response should not claim too much. A distributed account does not automatically solve every problem about phenomenal consciousness. It may explain reportability, attention, memory, perceptual integration, self-modeling, and flexible control without settling every dispute about why these processes are accompanied by experience. Nor does the rejection of transparency show that human beings can answer every question about consciousness. It establishes a more modest point: the apparent impossibility of explanation may result from a false explanatory demand.

Cognitive closure should therefore be treated as a defeasible empirical hypothesis, not as a philosophical conclusion drawn from the failure of introspective transparency. We can investigate neural mechanisms, computational architectures, developmental processes, and behavioral capacities even if no explanation takes the form of a single inner disclosure. Scientific knowledge often concerns structures that are not directly transparent to the systems that instantiate them. The immune system does not understand immunology; a telescope does not understand optics; and the brain need not possess a complete conscious model of consciousness in order for science to explain its operations.

## VII. Conceptual and Perceptual Knowledge

A persistent difficulty for theories of knowledge is the apparent divide between conceptual and perceptual knowledge. Conceptual knowledge is typically associated with propositions, reasons, language, and generality. Perceptual knowledge is associated with sensory experience, recognition, discrimination, and direct acquaintance. If knowledge is defined as possession of a proposition, perceptual knowledge appears derivative or incomplete. If knowledge is defined as a perceptual capacity, conceptual reasoning appears to belong to another category.

The better solution is to defend one unified category of knowledge stored and deployed in format-dependent ways. Knowledge is information integrated into a system such that it supports reliable discrimination, anticipation, inference, evaluation, memory, or action under appropriate conditions. Conceptual and perceptual knowledge differ in format, accessibility, and use, but not in their fundamental status as knowledge.

Conceptual knowledge often has a language-like format. It can be explicitly entertained, combined through inference, communicated, and justified by reasons. Perceptual knowledge has a sensory or modality-specific format. It makes distinctions available through visual, auditory, tactile, or interoceptive processing. Procedural knowledge has a motor format; social knowledge may be encoded in learned patterns of attention and response; emotional knowledge may involve dispositions to appraise situations and prepare for action. These formats can interact without collapsing into one another.

The unity of knowledge lies in functional integration. A person knows that a lemon is sour not merely by possessing a sentence, but through a network that connects the concept *lemon* with visual recognition, taste memory, linguistic descriptions, expectations, and practical behavior. A person knows how to ride a bicycle through sensorimotor coordination, but this knowledge can become conceptually accessible when the rider explains balance or teaches another person. Conceptual and perceptual formats can transform one another through learning, attention, language, and practice.

This account also clarifies the role of conscious access. Explicit awareness is one route by which knowledge becomes available, but it is not the defining criterion. Conscious conceptual knowledge may be reportable and inferentially manipulable. Perceptual knowledge may be conscious in the form of experience without being verbally articulated. Some knowledge may remain unconscious yet still guide behavior. These differences concern access profiles, not a fundamental division between genuine and merely apparent cognition.

The format-dependent view avoids two opposite errors. The first is intellectualism, which treats all knowledge as hidden propositions and therefore struggles to explain skill and perception. The second is anti-intellectualism, which treats perceptual and practical capacities as wholly nonconceptual and therefore struggles to explain their integration with reasoning and language. A single category of knowledge can accommodate both by recognizing that the same informational economy can be realized across multiple representational formats.

This position also revises the traditional distinction between “knowing that” and “knowing how.” The distinction remains useful for describing differences in expression and deployment. Knowing that a road is dangerous is not the same as knowing how to drive safely on it. But the difference need not imply two unrelated epistemic substances. Both involve learned, world-sensitive organization that supports appropriate responses. The relevant contrast is between formats and capacities, not between knowledge and a non-knowledge mental residue.

## VIII. Implications for Scientific Progress

This redefinition of knowledge has methodological consequences for the philosophy of mind. If knowledge is assumed to be an internally transparent proposition, then philosophical inquiry may focus excessively on conceptual analysis, introspective certainty, and the search for necessary inner ingredients. Scientific findings that reveal distributed processing, unconscious inference, perceptual variation, or neural plasticity may then appear to threaten the very idea of cognition. The alternative framework makes such findings central rather than disruptive.

First, it encourages a pluralistic science of representation. Researchers should not assume that all meaningful information is encoded in a single, sentence-like format. Visual, auditory, motor, affective, linguistic, and interoceptive representations may have different computational properties. Their relations can be investigated through neuroscience, psychology, artificial intelligence, and comparative cognition. The question becomes how information is transformed and integrated across formats.

Second, it supports a process-based account of consciousness. Instead of searching for a single neural location where experience is “present,” researchers can examine recurrent interactions, global availability, attention, working memory, report, valuation, and action control. Dennett’s Multiple Drafts Model suggests that conscious content may be identified through the consequences of competing and converging processes rather than through the discovery of a final internal image.

Third, the framework reduces the temptation to treat first-person and third-person perspectives as mutually exclusive. First-person reports remain important data, but they are outputs of cognitive systems, not infallible windows into intrinsic mental objects. Third-person methods can investigate the mechanisms that produce reports, discriminations, memories, and behavior. The distinction between appearance and mechanism is not a conflict between two ontological realms. It is a distinction between levels and formats of description.

Fourth, the approach provides a more productive standard for evaluating artificial cognition. Instead of asking whether a machine contains private experiences in exactly the human format, we should examine whether it develops integrated capacities for perception, learning, self-monitoring, flexible reasoning, action, and social interaction. This does not guarantee that an artificial system is conscious, but it replaces intuition-driven verdicts with architectural questions. The Chinese Room’s isolated rule-following system is a poor model of cognition precisely because it lacks the broader organization that gives symbols their functional significance.

Finally, the rejection of internal transparency promotes explanatory humility without intellectual defeatism. We should expect that some familiar questions will dissolve rather than receive direct answers. “Where is the final representation?” may have no answer because there is no final representation. “What is the hidden semantic object?” may be misguided because semantics is a property of system-world organization. “What nonphysical fact does Mary acquire?” may be based on a false opposition between description and perceptual capacity. Philosophical progress often consists in recognizing that a problem was generated by an inadequate conceptual framework.

## Conclusion

The traditional definition of knowing relies, often implicitly, on the assumption that cognition contains internally transparent contents. To know is imagined as possessing a proposition, image, or qualitative object that is available to a central subject for inspection. This picture is attractive because it reflects the character of explicit human thought. But it cannot account adequately for perception, skill, unconscious cognition, distributed processing, or the diversity of representational formats.

Dennett’s Multiple Drafts Model provides a compelling alternative. The mind is not a theatre containing one definitive presentation; it is a dynamic system in which multiple drafts are generated, revised, coordinated, and made available for different purposes. Knowledge can be understood within this architecture as organized informational competence rather than as possession of a single transparent inner item.

On this basis, Jackson’s knowledge argument loses its force. Mary acquires new perceptual capacities and a new format of access when she sees red, but she need not acquire a nonphysical fact. Searle’s Chinese Room shows that isolated formal manipulation is insufficient for the full organization of understanding, but it does not prove that semantics requires a mysterious inner ingredient. Syntax and semantics can be distinguished as structure and function without being divided into separate ontological realms. Meaning arises through the role of representations in an integrated, world-involving system.

The same framework moderates McGinn’s cognitive closure thesis. Consciousness may be difficult to explain, but the difficulty need not show that its nature is permanently beyond human understanding. It may instead reflect our mistaken expectation that the mind should transparently display its own mechanisms. Once that expectation is abandoned, scientific investigation can proceed through neural, computational, behavioral, and developmental analysis.

Conceptual and perceptual knowledge can likewise be unified. Both are forms of information integrated into capacities for discrimination, inference, memory, anticipation, and action; they differ in format rather than in metaphysical kind. Knowledge is therefore neither exclusively propositional nor merely experiential. It is a distributed achievement of an organized cognitive system.

The philosophical significance of this redefinition is substantial. It shifts the study of mind away from the search for an inner observer and toward the investigation of processes, formats, functions, and relations. The result is not an elimination of consciousness or meaning, but a naturalization of both. By rejecting internal transparency, philosophy of mind can stop asking how a hidden content becomes meaningful to an inner spectator and begin asking how complex systems acquire the capacities that make knowledge, understanding, and conscious life possible.
