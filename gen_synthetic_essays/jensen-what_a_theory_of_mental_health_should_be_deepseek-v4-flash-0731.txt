### The Anomalous Mind and the Biological Norm: Boorse, Davidson, and the Limits of a Physiological Psychiatry

**Introduction**

The question of what constitutes mental illness is one of the most intractable in philosophy and medicine. Is a mental disorder merely a biological dysfunction, akin to a failing organ or a metabolic imbalance? Or does it involve something qualitatively different—a disturbance in the very fabric of rationality, meaning, and intentionality? Christopher Boorse’s Biostatistical Theory (BST) offers a robust and influential answer to the first question. For Boorse, health is the absence of disease, and disease is defined as a statistical deviation from the species-typical functional organization of an organism, where function is understood in terms of causal contributions to survival and reproduction. Mental health, on this view, is simply a subset of biological health, concerning the proper functioning of the brain and its subsystems.

This physiological model promises an objective, value-free foundation for psychiatry. However, it collides head-on with a formidable challenge from the philosophy of mind, specifically from Donald Davidson’s principle of the anomalism of the mental. Davidson argues that there are no strict deterministic laws governing mental events—no psycho-physical laws and no psychological laws—because the mental realm is governed by the normative constraints of rationality and the holistic interdependence of beliefs, desires, and intentions. If Davidson is right, Boorse’s project faces a fundamental crisis: how can we identify a *causal* dysfunction in a mental mechanism if mental states are not the kind of things that can be captured by causal laws? Furthermore, Robert Klee’s penetrating critique of anomalous monism—the *ceteris paribus* objection—demonstrates that even the attempt to salvage psychological laws through hedged generalizations fails, because the background conditions for psychological explanations are irreducibly normative and cannot be shared with physical explanations.

In this essay, I will argue that Boorse’s physiological model of mental health cannot succeed given Davidson’s anomalism of the mental. The tension is not merely superficial but strikes at the core of Boorse’s reliance on causal laws governing mental disease. Klee’s objection deepens this failure by showing that the gap between psychological and physical explanation is unbridgeable, even at the level of *ceteris paribus* laws. However, I will conclude that this does not doom the possibility of an autonomous science of mental health. Rather, it demands a radical reconceptualization. Such a science remains viable, but only if it abandons Boorse’s monistic physiological reductionism and embraces a pluralistic, non-reductive framework where physiological mechanisms serve as causal constraints, while the *definition* of mental disorder is irreducibly tied to normative rationality. The autonomy of mental health science is secured not by its possession of its own strict laws, but by the irreducibility of its normative core.

**I. Boorse’s Biostatistical Theory: The Physiological Foundation of Mental Health**

To understand the stakes, we must first appreciate the meticulous architecture of Boorse’s model. In his seminal papers, Boorse aims to provide a naturalistic, value-free definition of health and disease that applies equally to somatic and mental conditions. His central concept is that of a *function*. A function, for Boorse, is a causal contribution to the goals of an organism—specifically, survival and reproduction. A trait or process is functional if it makes a statistically typical contribution to these goals. Consequently, a disease is a state of the organism that interferes with the performance of these functions, representing a deviation from the species-typical design.

Boorse’s model is explicitly physiological. He argues that the brain and its psychological capacities are just as much biological organs as the heart or the liver. Just as the heart’s function is to pump blood, the function of a cognitive system might be to process information or regulate emotion. A mental disorder, then, is a breakdown in these specific functional systems. For instance, clinical depression could be understood as a dysfunction of the neural mechanisms responsible for mood regulation, resulting in a statistically abnormal affective state that impairs the organism’s ability to pursue survival and reproduction. Similarly, schizophrenia might be a dysfunction of the cognitive mechanisms governing perception and reality-testing.

This approach has immense appeal. It promises to place psychiatry on the same solid scientific footing as cardiology or endocrinology. It avoids moralistic judgments about "bad behavior" by grounding illness in objective biological facts. The determination of whether someone has a mental disease is a matter of empirical investigation into the causal machinery of the brain, not a matter of cultural preference or ethical evaluation. Boorse is careful to distinguish between a disease (a biological dysfunction) and a disability or a harm. A person might have a disease that causes no harm, or a condition that is harmful but not a disease. The definition of disease is purely descriptive.

Crucially for our purposes, Boorse’s model is *nomological*—it assumes that mental functions are governed by causal laws. To say that a mental mechanism is dysfunctional is to say that it deviates from the causal pathways that are typical for the species. The psychiatrist, in diagnosing a mental disorder, is identifying a causal anomaly in a biological system. This presupposes that mental states and processes can be subsumed under causal laws, at least in principle. If a specific neural circuit is supposed to regulate serotonin reuptake and it fails to do so, this is a causal failure. The model requires that we can identify the *etiology* of a mental disorder in terms of discrete, law-governed physical events.

**II. Davidson’s Anomalous Monism and the Holism of the Mental**

It is precisely this nomological presupposition that Donald Davidson attacks. Davidson’s philosophy of mind, known as Anomalous Monism (AM), presents a sophisticated attempt to reconcile the physical nature of the world with the irreducibility of the mental. AM holds three key principles: (1) mental events are causally related to physical events; (2) causal relations are governed by strict laws, and these laws are physical; and (3) there are no strict deterministic laws connecting mental events to physical events or to other mental events.

The third principle—the anomalism of the mental—is the crux. Davidson argues that mental events (beliefs, desires, intentions) are anomalous because they are constituted by their relations to other mental events. This is the *holism of the mental*. A belief that "Paris is the capital of France" is not an isolated atomic entity; its identity is determined by its logical and inferential connections to countless other beliefs (e.g., that France is a country, that Paris is a city, that capitals are political centers). To have one belief is to have a vast web of related beliefs.

Furthermore, this web is governed by a *normative* principle: the principle of rationality, or what Davidson calls the "principle of charity." When we interpret another person’s behavior, we must assume that their beliefs are largely true and their desires are largely coherent and rational. We cannot make sense of a creature as having a belief that is wildly inconsistent with all its other beliefs, nor can we attribute to it a desire that directly contradicts its most basic goals. The attribution of mental states is constrained by the need to *maximize rationality* in the subject. This is not an empirical discovery about how people actually behave; it is a constitutive rule of what it means to have a mind.

This has profound consequences. Because the identity of a mental state is tied to its rational, holistic relations, and because these relations are normative (they prescribe how beliefs *ought* to fit together), mental states cannot be captured by the kind of strict, exceptionless causal laws that govern physical phenomena. Physical laws are descriptive; they state what happens given certain initial conditions. Psychological "laws" would have to be normative; they would have to state what a rational agent *should* do. But the connection between rationality and causation is broken. A person might have a belief that rationally entails another belief, but still fail to form that belief due to irrationality, wishful thinking, or simple oversight. The causal chain from one mental state to another is always vulnerable to interference from irrational factors, which cannot be predicted by a strict law.

Davidson’s conclusion is that while mental events are token-identical to physical events (each specific mental event is a specific physical event), there are no type-type identities. The mental kind "belief that it is raining" does not correspond to a single physical kind, because the same belief can be realized in countless different physical configurations across different brains, or even within the same brain at different times. More importantly, there are no bridging laws that would allow us to deduce psychological facts from physical facts.

**III. The Fundamental Tension: Boorse’s Causal Dysfunction vs. Davidson’s Holism**

The tension between Boorse and Davidson is now starkly visible. Boorse’s model of mental health requires that we can identify a *causal dysfunction* in a mental mechanism. To say that a person is mentally ill is to say that some sub-system of their cognitive or affective apparatus is not performing its biological function, and that this failure is due to a specific causal breakdown. This presupposes that mental states can be individuated and analyzed in isolation, like the components of a machine.

Davidson’s holism destroys this picture. A mental state, such as a belief or a desire, is not a discrete component with a fixed causal role. Its "function" cannot be specified independently of its place in the overall rational economy of the mind. Consider the example of clinical depression. Boorse would want to say that depression is a dysfunction of a mood-regulation system. But what is the *function* of mood? A purely physiological account might say that mood serves to modulate energy expenditure or approach-avoidance behavior. However, the *phenomenology* and *content* of depression are irreducibly mental. A depressed person has beliefs like "I am worthless" and "Nothing will improve." These beliefs are not just epiphenomenal symptoms of a biological malfunction; they are partially constitutive of the disorder. The person is not just sad; they are *irrationally* sad, or their sadness is tied to a distorted cognitive appraisal of themselves and the world.

Davidson would argue that we cannot understand the dysfunction of depression without understanding the rational (or irrational) connections between these beliefs. The belief "I am worthless" is not just a neural event; it is a mental state that is logically connected to other beliefs (e.g., "I failed at my job") and desires (e.g., "I want to be successful"). The malfunction is not merely a causal breakdown in a neural circuit; it is a breakdown in the *rational coherence* of the person’s mental life. The person is failing to maximize rationality—they are drawing unwarranted conclusions, overgeneralizing, and engaging in cognitive distortions.

Boorse might respond that these rational failures are merely the *symptoms* of an underlying biological dysfunction. The real disease is the neurochemical imbalance; the irrational beliefs are just downstream effects. But this response misses Davidson’s point. The identity of the mental state is not exhausted by its physical realization. The belief "I am worthless" has a specific *content*, and it is this content that is causally relevant to the person’s behavior (e.g., they stop going to work, they isolate themselves). A purely physiological explanation, which cites only neural firings and chemical concentrations, cannot capture the causal role of the *content* of the belief. The content is governed by rational norms, not by physical laws.

To put it bluntly, Boorse needs to identify a *causal law* that connects a specific neural state to a specific behavioral output, and then show that this law is broken in a diseased state. But Davidson shows that there is no such law for mental states. The connection between a belief and a behavior is mediated by the entire web of other beliefs and desires. The same belief can lead to different behaviors depending on the context of other mental states. Therefore, we cannot isolate a single "dysfunction" in a causal mechanism; we can only identify a global failure of rational coherence.

**IV. Klee’s Ceteris Paribus Objection: The Unbridgeable Gap**

One might think that Davidson has left a loophole. While there are no *strict* laws, perhaps there can be *ceteris paribus* laws—laws that hold "all other things being equal." A psychological generalization like "If a person desires X and believes that doing Y will bring about X, then, ceteris paribus, they will do Y" seems plausible. Boorse could argue that his physiological model of mental health relies on such *ceteris paribus* laws. We can say that a specific neural dysfunction causes depression, provided that no other interfering factors are present.

Robert Klee’s powerful critique, however, closes this loophole. Klee argues that the *ceteris paribus* clauses in physical explanations and those in psychological explanations are fundamentally different in kind. In physics, a *ceteris paribus* clause typically refers to a closed set of physical conditions. For example, Newton’s laws hold "ceteris paribus" meaning "in the absence of other gravitational fields or frictional forces." These background conditions are themselves physical and can, in principle, be specified and controlled for. The clause is a placeholder for a finite, physical list of potential disturbances.

In psychology, however, the *ceteris paribus* clause is irreducibly open-ended and normative. To say that a rational person will act in a certain way "all other things being equal" means that they will act in that way *provided they are rational*. But rationality is not a physical condition. It is a normative standard. The background conditions for a psychological law include things like "the person is not suffering from a cognitive bias," "the person is not overwhelmed by emotion," or "the person has correctly processed all relevant information." These conditions cannot be exhaustively specified in physical terms. There is no finite list of physical states that corresponds to "being rational." Rationality is a holistic, normative property that supervenes on, but is not reducible to, physical facts.

Consequently, Klee argues, psychological explanations and physical explanations do not share identical background conditions. A physical explanation of a behavior might cite the firing of neurons and the release of dopamine. A psychological explanation of the same behavior might cite the person’s belief that they are being threatened. The *ceteris paribus* clauses for these explanations are different. The physical explanation requires that no other physical forces interfere. The psychological explanation requires that the person’s reasoning is not distorted by irrationality. These are not the same conditions.

For Boorse, this is devastating. His model attempts to explain mental disease in purely physiological terms. He wants to say that a mental disorder is a physical dysfunction that causes psychological symptoms. But if Klee is right, the psychological symptoms cannot be explained by the physical dysfunction alone, because the psychological explanation carries its own normative *ceteris paribus* conditions. To say that a person is depressed because of a serotonin imbalance is to offer a physiological explanation. But to say that they are depressed because they irrationally believe they are worthless is to offer a psychological explanation. These two explanations are not competing descriptions of the same underlying causal law; they are different kinds of explanations with different background conditions. The physiological explanation is incomplete because it cannot account for the *rational* connection between the belief and the behavior. The psychological explanation is incomplete because it cannot account for the *physical* substrate. Neither can be reduced to the other, and Boorse’s attempt to privilege the physiological one fails.

**V. The Viability of an Autonomous Science of Mental Health**

If the arguments of Davidson and Klee are sound, then Boorse’s physiological model of mental health is untenable. It fails because it rests on the false premise that mental dysfunction can be understood as a purely causal, physiological anomaly. The mental realm resists this kind of reduction because it is constituted by normative rationality and holistic interdependence.

But does this mean we must abandon the project of a scientific psychiatry? Is an autonomous science of mental health impossible? I believe the answer is no, but it requires a fundamental shift in how we conceive of such a science.

First, we must reject the demand for type-to-type physical reductions. We cannot hope to find a single physical signature for every mental disorder. Depression is not just a serotonin deficiency; it is a complex pattern of neural activity, cognitive schemas, and behavioral dispositions. Schizophrenia is not just a dopamine excess; it is a profound disruption of the rational integration of thought and perception. The mental disorder is a *higher-level* phenomenon that is multiply realized in physical states.

Second, we must accept that the science of mental health is *pluralistic*. It requires both a *causal-mechanical* component (neurobiology, genetics, endocrinology) and a *hermeneutic-normative* component (psychology, phenomenology, ethics). The neurobiological component studies the physical substrate of mental processes. It can identify correlations between neural states and psychological states, and it can offer causal explanations for how physical interventions (e.g., drugs) affect mental states. However, it cannot *define* what counts as a mental disorder. The definition of a mental disorder must come from the normative component.

What does this normative component look like? It draws on Davidson’s insight that mental states are governed by rationality. A mental disorder, in this view, is a failure of the person to function as a rational agent. It is a breakdown in the ability to form coherent beliefs, to pursue consistent desires, or to integrate emotions with cognition. This is not a purely statistical deviation from a biological norm; it is a violation of a normative standard. The standard is not arbitrary or culturally relative; it is grounded in the very nature of what it means to be a thinking, acting agent.

For example, consider the diagnosis of delusional disorder. A person with a delusion holds a belief that is firmly maintained despite overwhelming evidence to the contrary. The belief is not merely false; it is *irrational* because it is resistant to rational revision. The dysfunction is not just a neural misfiring; it is a failure of the person’s cognitive system to adhere to the norms of evidence and logic. This failure cannot be captured by a purely physiological description of the brain. It requires an evaluation of the person’s rational capacities.

Therefore, an autonomous science of mental health is viable, but it is not autonomous in the sense of having its own strict laws or its own irreducible ontology. It is autonomous in the sense that its core concepts—disorder, dysfunction, health—are irreducibly normative. They cannot be reduced to physical facts. The science is *autonomous* because the normative rationality constraint cannot be eliminated or translated into physical terms.

This has practical implications. A psychiatrist must be both a scientist and a humanist. They must understand the neurochemistry of the brain, but they must also understand the patient’s life story, their beliefs, their values, and their reasons. The diagnosis of a mental disorder is not a purely objective, value-free judgment; it is a clinical judgment that involves both empirical evidence and normative evaluation. The psychiatrist must ask not only "Is this neural circuit functioning properly?" but also "Is this person able to live a flourishing, rational life?"
