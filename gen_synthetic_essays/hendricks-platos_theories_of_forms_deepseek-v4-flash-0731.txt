**The Incommensurable Forms: Universals, Paradigms, and the Unresolved Tension in Plato’s Middle Dialogues**

The philosophical architecture of Plato’s middle dialogues, particularly the *Phaedo* and the *Republic*, rests upon a concept that is simultaneously the source of its explanatory power and its most profound internal instability: the Theory of Forms. The Forms are invoked to solve a dizzying array of problems—the possibility of knowledge, the nature of predication, the foundation of ethics, and the structure of reality itself. Yet, a persistent and powerful critical tradition has argued that Plato does not present a single, coherent theory but rather conflates two fundamentally incommensurable conceptions. On the one hand, a Form appears to be a *universal* (UF), a common property fully instantiated in all its particulars, enabling us to say that a thing *is* beautiful or just. On the other hand, a Form appears to be a *standard* or *paradigm* (SF), a perfect exemplar against which particulars are measured and found wanting, implying that particulars are *not* truly beautiful or just but merely approximate them. This essay will evaluate the claim that this conflation is real and problematic. I will argue that the textual evidence in the *Phaedo* and *Republic* genuinely supports both readings, creating a logical chasm. Alexander Nehamas’ Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars Objection (IA) decisively demonstrates that the paradigmatic reading destroys the possibility of determinate predication. Furthermore, R.S. Bluck’s valiant attempt to reconcile the two by positing a distinction between ontological deficiency and predicative deficiency ultimately fails, as it merely relocates the contradiction rather than resolving it. Consequently, while Plato’s framework does not collapse into utter incoherence—it retains a powerful axiological and explanatory appeal—it is fundamentally unstable as a unified logical system. The dual characterization forces a choice between a logicist interpretation and a paradigmatic interpretation, and the middle dialogues, unable to make this choice, leave a tension that Plato himself later grappled with in the *Parmenides*.

**I. Textual Evidence for Universals and Standards**

The claim of conflation is not a mere scholarly invention; it is grounded in the very language Plato uses. The *Phaedo* provides the clearest articulation of the Form as a universal, particularly in the “safe” answer to the problem of causation. When Socrates is asked why a thing is beautiful, he rejects the elaborate physical explanations of the pre-Socratics and offers a simple, foolproof answer: “nothing else makes it beautiful other than the presence of, or the communion with, or the way of grasping at, that beautiful itself” (*Phaedo* 100d). This formulation is explicitly predicative. The Form of Beauty is that which, when present in a particular, makes that particular *be* beautiful. The relation is one of instantiation or participation, where the Form functions as the property ‘F’ that is applied to the subject. The Form is the *logos* or the reason *why* the predicate applies. This is the classic understanding of a universal: a one-over-many that is fully present in each of its instances. If a thing is beautiful, it is because it partakes of Beauty itself, and this participation grants it the property of beauty *simpliciter*.

However, in the very same dialogue, Plato introduces language that points decisively toward the paradigm theory. In the argument from opposites, Socrates discusses the case of equality. He notes that equal sticks and stones, while appearing equal to one observer and unequal to another, “fall short” (*endea*) of the Equal itself (*Phaedo* 74d-e). The sticks are not simply equal; they are *deficiently* equal. They strive to be equal but never achieve the perfect, absolute equality of the Form. This language of “falling short” is not the language of instantiation. A universal is not something that particulars fall short of; it is something they possess. A red apple does not “fall short” of the universal Redness—it simply *is* red. The concept of deficiency implies a continuum of approximation, where the particular is a copy or image of an original that it can never fully replicate. This is the paradigm model. The Form is not a property but a perfect model, and particulars are imperfect likenesses. The recollection argument reinforces this: we see equal sticks, and they trigger the memory of Equality itself. But if the sticks were simply equal (as instantiations), we would not need to recollect a separate standard; we would just perceive the property. The fact that the sticks are *both* equal and unequal (in different respects) forces us to posit a Form that is purely equal, a standard against which the deficient sticks are judged.

The *Republic* exhibits the same bifurcation. The Form of the Good is explicitly described as the ultimate object of knowledge, “the brightest” of all beings, and the cause of all truth and reality (*Republic* 508e-509a). This suggests a universal—the property of goodness that makes all other things good. Yet, in the political project of the *Republic*, the Forms are most prominently described as *paradeigmata*, or patterns. In Book VI, Socrates says that the philosopher, like a painter, uses the Forms as models to “establish the laws of the beautiful, the just, and the good” in the city (*Republic* 484c-d). The ideal city is a *copy* of the Form of Justice; it is not Justice itself. The philosophers must look to the Form as a standard to guide their actions, but the resulting city will always be an approximation. This is made explicit in Book V, where Socrates distinguishes between the lovers of sights and sounds, who believe in beautiful things, and the philosophers, who believe in Beauty itself. The lovers of sights see beautiful things that are “both beautiful and ugly,” while the philosopher sees the Form, which is “always the same” (*Republic* 479a). The particulars are *both* beautiful and ugly; they are not purely beautiful. This purity is the exclusive property of the Form. If a particular is both beautiful and ugly, it cannot be said to *be* beautiful in the unqualified sense that the Form is. It is, rather, an approximation of beauty, a deficient copy.

The textual evidence, therefore, is genuinely dual. In some passages, the Form is the predicate that makes a particular *be* F. In others, the Form is the perfect exemplar that the particular *resembles* but never fully achieves. The question is whether these two functions can coexist.

**II. The Incommensurability and Nehamas’ Indeterminacy Objection**

The logical incompatibility between UF and SF becomes starkly apparent when we consider the truth-conditions of predication. If the Form is a universal (UF), then the statement “S is F” is true if and only if S participates in the Form of F-ness. Participation is an all-or-nothing relation; either S has the property or it does not. There are no degrees of being beautiful, just as there are no degrees of being red. A thing is either red or it is not.

If the Form is a standard (SF), then the statement “S is F” is problematic. If S is a deficient copy of the Form, then S is not truly F; it is merely *like* F. The sticks are not truly equal; they are approximately equal. This introduces a degree of approximation. But what does it mean to say that S is *approximately* F? This is precisely the point of Alexander Nehamas’ Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars Objection. Nehamas argues that if the Forms function primarily as standards, then the particulars that participate in them become indeterminate with respect to the properties they are supposed to ground. We cannot say *what* a particular is; we can only say *what it is like*.

To elaborate: If the Form of Equality is a perfect standard, then no collection of sticks is equal. They are merely more or less equal. But “more or less equal” is not a determinate property. It is a comparative relation that requires a fixed point of reference—the standard itself. However, the standard is *not* a property; it is an ideal. Therefore, to say that the sticks are “equal-ish” is to say nothing determinate about them. We cannot specify the exact degree of equality they possess without invoking the standard, but the standard is not something that can be quantified or instantiated in degrees. The particular is left in a state of ontological and epistemic limbo. It is not F, and it is not not-F; it is an indeterminate approximation.

Nehamas applies this objection specifically to the epistemic function of the Forms in the *Phaedo*. The recollection argument requires that the perception of equal sticks *remind* us of Equality itself. But what triggers this recollection? If the sticks are merely approximate equals, they do not possess the property of equality. They possess a vague resemblance to equality. However, a vague resemblance cannot serve as a reliable trigger for the recollection of a precise, determinate Form. If I see a roughly drawn triangle, it may remind me of a perfect triangle, but only if I already know what a perfect triangle is. The recollection argument presupposes that the sensible particulars *are* F (in a qualified sense) to serve as the occasion for recollection. But if they are merely approximate, they are indeterminate, and the recollection becomes groundless. We cannot move from an indeterminate image to a determinate original without already possessing the original, which is what the argument is trying to prove.

Furthermore, the IA objection devastates the moral and political framework of the *Republic*. If the Form of Justice is a perfect standard, and no just action or just city fully instantiates it, then we cannot say that any action *is* just. We can only say that it is more or less justice-like. But this undermines the very possibility of moral judgment. To call an action “just” is to predicate a property of it. If the predicate “just” can only be applied to the Form, then no human action can be truly just. The standards become so transcendent that they are wholly disconnected from the world of becoming. The philosopher-kings, who are supposed to use the Form of Justice as a model, would be unable to determine whether any proposed law is just, because the law will always fall short of the standard. The standard provides a direction for approximation, but it does not provide a criterion for determinate classification. Nehamas’ point is that a standard is not a predicate. It cannot tell us what a thing *is*; it can only tell us what it *ought to be*. And in a world where nothing truly *is* just, the language of justice becomes indeterminate and meaningless.

Thus, the incommensurability is evident. UF requires that particulars *are* F, making the Form a logical property. SF requires that particulars *are not* F, making the Form an ontological ideal. These two claims are logically contradictory. A particular cannot both possess a property and fail to possess it in the same respect. Plato’s language of “falling short” and “participation” attempts to bridge this gap, but it does so only by equivocating on the meaning of “being.”

**III. Bluck’s Defense and Its Failure**

In response to this perceived tension, R.S. Bluck offered a sophisticated defense aimed at reconciling the two theories. Bluck argued that Plato’s Forms are *both* universals and standards, but that the “falling short” language refers not to the *predication* of the property but to the *ontological status* of the particular. According to Bluck, a particular beautiful thing *is* beautiful—it fully instantiates the universal Beauty. However, it falls short of the Form in a different sense: it is subject to change, generation, and decay. The Form is eternal and immutable; the particular is temporal and mutable. The “deficiency” is thus a deficiency of *being*, not a deficiency of *predication*. The sticks are equal, but their equality is not stable or pure; they will eventually become unequal, or they will cease to exist. The Form of Equality is the perfect, eternal instance of equality, whereas the sticks are imperfect, perishable instances.

Bluck’s defense attempts to preserve the logical function of the universal while accommodating the metaphysical hierarchy of the paradigm. The particular is F (universal), but it is not *purely* F (standard). The “purity” is a matter of ontology, not logic. A beautiful flower is beautiful, but it is also ugly in comparison to a more beautiful flower, or it will wither and become ugly. The Form is beautiful *simpliciter*, without any admixture of ugliness.

However, Nehamas’ IA objection strikes directly at the heart of Bluck’s reconciliation. The problem lies in the concept of “purity” or “degrees” of a property. If the flower is beautiful, then it is beautiful. The fact that it is also ugly in another respect (e.g., it is wilting) does not make it *less* beautiful; it means that it has *two* properties: beauty and ugliness, in different respects. A red apple is red, even if it is also round. The redness is fully present. Bluck’s notion of “falling short” as ontological deficiency fails because it conflates the *mode of existence* of the particular with the *instantiation of the property*. The particular exists in a deficient mode (it is finite, mutable), but this does not affect the property it instantiates. The property “beauty” is fully present in the beautiful flower, just as the property “redness” is fully present in the red apple.

If Bluck insists that the flower is *less* beautiful than the Form, then he is reintroducing degrees of beauty. But degrees of beauty are incoherent for a universal. If beauty is a property, then either a thing has it or it does not. Saying that something is “less beautiful” is either a comparative judgment (it is less beautiful than another particular) or it is a claim that the property itself is not fully instantiated. The latter is nonsensical. A property is either instantiated or it is not. There is no middle ground. Nehamas’ IA shows that if we adopt Bluck’s position, we cannot specify what it means for a particular to be beautiful. It is not fully beautiful (because it falls short), but it is also not not-beautiful (because it participates). This indeterminacy is fatal. The particular becomes an indeterminate entity, neither F nor not-F, and thus we cannot predicate anything of it.

Moreover, Bluck’s defense fails to address the epistemic problem. If the sticks are equal, then they can trigger the recollection of Equality. But if they are only *ontologically* deficient, they are still equal in the logical sense. The recollection argument works. However, if we accept Bluck’s distinction, we must ask: what does the recollection of Equality give us that the perception of equal sticks does not already provide? If the sticks are fully equal (logically), then we already know what equality is. The Form becomes redundant as a standard. If the sticks are not fully equal (because they are deficient), then we cannot use them to learn equality. Bluck’s defense either makes the Form unnecessary (if particulars are fully F) or makes the particulars unknowable (if they are not fully F). He cannot have it both ways.

Bluck’s attempt to separate the logical and ontological dimensions of the Forms is a valiant effort, but it ultimately fails because it misidentifies the source of the tension. The tension is not between the *mode of existence* of the particular and the *mode of existence* of the Form; it is between the *relation of predication* and the *relation of approximation*. Plato’s language of “falling short” is not merely a statement about the particular’s temporality; it is a statement about the particular’s *failure to be F*. When Socrates says the sticks fall short of Equality, he is not saying that the sticks are equal but perishable; he is saying that the sticks are *not* equal in the way that Equality itself is equal. This is a logical claim, not merely an ontological one. Bluck’s attempt to shift the burden to ontology does not resolve the logical contradiction; it merely disguises it.

**IV. The Collapse of the Framework?**

Given the failure of Bluck’s defense and the force of Nehamas’ IA, we must confront the central question: does Plato’s metaphysical framework collapse under this dual characterization?

If we demand a strictly logical consistency, the answer is yes. The middle dialogues present a theory that cannot simultaneously maintain that particulars are F (universal) and that particulars fall short of F (standard). The framework is internally inconsistent. The Third Man Argument, which Plato himself articulates in the *Parmenides*, is a direct consequence of this conflation. If the Form of F is itself F (as a universal must be, to ground predication), and if the Form is a standard that particulars resemble, then we must posit a new Form to explain the resemblance between the particular and the first Form, leading to an infinite regress. The *Parmenides* is Plato’s own recognition that the middle-period theory, as it stands, is logically unstable.
