## The Divine Dialectic: Socratic Piety and the Refashioning of the Divine

In the twilight of the fifth century BCE, Athens was a city of gods and men, where the marble temples of the Acropolis cast long shadows over the bustling agora. The Athenians worshipped a pantheon that was as capricious as it was powerful—a family of anthropomorphic deities who feasted on nectar, quarreled with petty jealousy, and intervened in mortal affairs with a moral ambiguity that often mirrored the passions of their human subjects. Yet, within this very city, a stonemason turned philosopher, Socrates, stood trial for impiety, charged with not believing in the gods of the state and for corrupting the youth. The trial and the dialogues that frame it—the *Apology* and the *Euthyphro*—present a radical philosophical revolution. Socrates does not merely reject the traditional mythological conception of divinity; he redefines it. The claim that Socratic piety transcends traditional mythology by redefining the service of the gods as the philosophical improvement of human souls is a profound and largely accurate reading of his philosophy. However, this transcendence is not a simple rejection of the divine, but a profound reconstruction that reorients the relationship between the human and the divine from one of transactional sacrifice and appeasement to one of rational, moral self-cultivation. Yet, in this very redefinition, Socrates establishes a link between divine purpose and human moral agency that is philosophically coherent only at the cost of dissolving the personal, providential, and anthropomorphic nature of the gods, reducing them to an abstract principle of rationality and goodness. Thus, while Socratic piety successfully transcends the mythological, it does so by forging a new, austere, and deeply problematic theological framework, one where the elenchus—the method of cross-examination—becomes the sole authentic mode of divine worship.

To understand the radical nature of Socrates’ project, one must first grasp the traditional Athenian conception of the divine. The gods of Homer and Hesiod were not distant, abstract principles; they were superhuman persons, possessed of bodies, emotions, and desires. Zeus, the king of the gods, was subject to lust, anger, and deception. Hera was vengeful and jealous. Apollo could be cruel and ambiguous. These deities were not primarily moral exemplars; they were powerful forces to be appeased, honored, and occasionally manipulated through sacrifice, prayer, and ritual observance. The relationship between mortal and immortal was largely transactional. The human offered *thysia* (sacrifice) and *euche* (prayer) in exchange for *charis* (favor). The gods demanded recognition of their power and status—a form of *timē* (honor)—and in return, they offered protection, victory, fertility, and prosperity. This was a civic religion, embedded in the political and social fabric of the polis. To neglect the gods was to risk their wrath, not only upon oneself but upon the entire community. The fickleness of these gods was a source of existential anxiety; one could never be entirely sure of their disposition, and their moral standards were often as variable as the weather. This worldview is predicated on a fundamental asymmetry: the gods are powerful, humans are weak, and the divine will is ultimately inscrutable, requiring blind obedience and ritualistic appeasement.

Socrates fundamentally challenges this paradigm. In the *Euthyphro*, a dialogue set on the steps of the king-archon’s court, Socrates encounters Euthyphro, a self-proclaimed religious expert who is prosecuting his own father for murder. When Socrates asks him to define piety (*hosiotēs*), Euthyphro offers a series of definitions that epitomize the traditional view. His first definition is that piety is "what I am doing now, prosecuting the wrongdoer" (5d-e). This is a particular action, not a universal definition. More importantly, his second definition—that piety is "what is dear to the gods" (*theophiles*)—encapsulates the very essence of the traditional transactional model. It defines the holy as that which is loved by the gods, making the divine will the ultimate arbiter of moral value.

Socrates immediately seizes upon this definition with his characteristic elenchus. He poses the famous dilemma: "Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?" (10a). This is not a mere logical puzzle; it is a revolutionary metaphysical question. The first horn of the dilemma—that the pious is loved because it is pious—implies that there is an objective standard of piety that exists independently of the gods. The gods recognize and love this standard, but they do not create it. The second horn—that the pious is pious because it is loved—implies that the gods’ arbitrary whims determine what is good. If the gods loved murder, then murder would be pious. Given the morally ambiguous behavior of the Homeric deities, this second horn leads to a radical moral relativism where justice is nothing more than the will of the strongest. Socrates, through his relentless questioning, pushes Euthyphro toward the first horn. He demonstrates that the traditional conception of the gods as fickle and anthropomorphic cannot serve as a foundation for morality, because their loves and hates are as contradictory as their own internal squabbles. The gods themselves must be subject to a higher standard of goodness, a standard that is rational and unchanging.

This is the first step in Socrates’ transcendence of mythology. He implicitly posits a divinity that is perfectly wise and perfectly good, a being that cannot will anything contrary to the Good. This is a stark departure from the Olympians. The Socratic god is not a character in a myth; it is a rational principle, a cosmic intelligence that orders the universe according to the Form of the Good. The *Euthyphro* does not explicitly articulate this theology, but the logical pressure of the dilemma forces the reader to conclude that the gods must be perfectly rational and moral, otherwise their love would be an unreliable guide to piety.

Having established that piety cannot be merely what the gods love, Socrates proceeds to interrogate the nature of piety as a service to the gods. Euthyphro, pushed to a corner, suggests that piety is a kind of service (*hyperetike*) to the gods (13d). Socrates, with his characteristic irony, applies the analogy of human service. A service to a horse is aimed at the improvement of the horse; a service to a doctor is aimed at the health of the patient. What, then, is the aim of service to the gods? The gods are already perfect; they lack nothing. They cannot be improved upon. Therefore, Socrates argues, the service we render to the gods cannot be for their benefit, but must be for the benefit of their ends or projects. What is the great project of the gods? It must be the production of good, the cultivation of virtue, and the perfection of the human soul. Thus, piety is not the offering of sacrifices to appease a hungry deity; it is the active participation in the divine project of making human souls as good as possible. Euthyphro’s definition collapses because it reduces piety to a form of flattery or bribery, which is incompatible with the notion of a perfectly good god who needs nothing from us. Socrates’ conclusion, though not fully stated in the dialogue, is clear: true piety is the care of the soul, the pursuit of wisdom and virtue, for this is the only service that a perfect being would desire from an imperfect one.

This redefinition of piety as the philosophical care of the soul reaches its fullest expression in the *Apology*. Here, Socrates stands before the Athenian jury, facing the death penalty. He explicitly claims that his philosophical mission is a divine command. He recounts the story of his friend Chaerephon, who went to the Oracle at Delphi and asked if anyone was wiser than Socrates. The Oracle replied that no one was wiser. Socrates, initially baffled, set out to test the Oracle by examining those reputed to be wise—politicians, poets, and artisans. He discovered that, while they possessed some knowledge, they all believed they knew what they did not know. Socrates concluded that he was wiser than them only in this one respect: he was aware of his own ignorance. This realization became the foundation of his mission. He tells the jury: "I was attached to this city by the god—though it seems a ridiculous thing to say—as upon a great and noble horse which was somewhat sluggish because of its size and needed to be stirred up by a kind of gadfly" (30e). He is the gadfly, sent by the god to awaken the sleeping Athenian soul.

The crucial point here is that Socrates interprets the divine command not as a call to perform specific rituals or sacrifices, but as a call to engage in philosophical inquiry. The service of the god, the *latreia* that he performs, is the elenchus—the relentless cross-examination of himself and others to expose false beliefs and compel the search for truth. He explicitly equates his philosophical activity with the service of the god: "I shall never cease from the practice and teaching of philosophy, exhorting anyone whom I meet, and saying to him: 'My friend, why do you, who are a citizen of the great and mighty and wise city of Athens, care so much about laying up the greatest amount of money and honor and reputation, and so little about wisdom and truth and the greatest improvement of the soul, which you never regard or heed at all?'" (29d-e). This is the core of his divine mission. The god does not want sacrifices; the god wants human beings to be virtuous. And virtue, for Socrates, is a form of knowledge—knowledge of the Good. Therefore, the highest form of piety is the acquisition of that knowledge, which is achieved through the elenchus.

Furthermore, Socrates’ relationship with the divine is mediated by his *daimonion*, a divine sign or inner voice. He describes it as "something divine and spiritual" that "began when I was a child, a sort of voice that comes to me, and when it comes, it always dissuades me from what I am about to do, but never encourages me" (31c-d). This *daimonion* is not a source of positive commands or revelations; it is a negative check, a guardian that prevents him from committing errors. This is profoundly significant. The traditional gods communicated through oracles, omens, and prophets, delivering clear, albeit often cryptic, commands. The Socratic *daimonion* does not tell him what to do; it only tells him what *not* to do. This aligns perfectly with the Socratic conception of a perfectly wise and rational divinity. A perfectly good god would not need to micromanage human affairs with arbitrary commands; instead, it would provide a rational capacity for discernment (the intellect) and a negative sign to prevent catastrophic errors. The *daimonion* is thus not a supernatural intrusion but a symbol of the rational, moral order of the universe, a divine spark within the human soul that guides it toward the Good by preventing it from falling into falsehood.

The tension between this Socratic standard and the traditional Athenian view is stark. The Athenians believed in gods who demanded obedience to their specific cultic practices and who could be angered by *hybris* (overweening pride) or neglect. Socrates’ god demands not obedience to a set of specific commands, but the cultivation of a certain intellectual and moral disposition. The Athenians’ gods were immanent and personal; Socrates’ god is transcendent and rational. When Socrates says, "I owe my obedience to the god rather than to you" (29d), he is not simply choosing one deity over another. He is choosing a conception of the divine that is defined by moral wisdom over a conception defined by civic ritual. He is claiming that the true service of the divine consists in the relentless pursuit of truth, even if that pursuit brings him into conflict with the laws and traditions of the city. This is why he refuses to stop philosophizing even when threatened with death. To stop would be to disobey the god, and for Socrates, the moral imperative of the divine command outweighs the political imperative of the state.

Now, we must assess whether this Socratic redefinition successfully establishes a coherent link between divine purpose and human moral agency. On one level, it does. If we accept the premise that the divine is perfectly wise and perfectly good, then the divine’s purpose must be the realization of the Good in the world. The human soul, being the locus of moral agency, is the primary instrument through which the Good can be realized. Therefore, the divine’s purpose is for human beings to become virtuous. The elenchus, by eliminating false beliefs and clarifying concepts, is the most effective method for achieving this virtue. By engaging in philosophical inquiry, the human agent aligns their own rational will with the divine rational will. The link is thus established: divine purpose = the perfection of human souls; human moral agency = the activity of rational inquiry (elenchus) that perfects the soul. The elimination of false beliefs is not merely an intellectual exercise; it is a moral and religious act, because false beliefs are the root of vice. If someone believes that justice is the advantage of the stronger, they will act unjustly. If they believe that the gods are fickle and immoral, they will have no reason to pursue virtue. The elenchus purifies the soul of these falsehoods, thereby enabling it to act in accordance with the Good, which is the will of the divine.

However, this coherence is achieved at a significant metaphysical price. The first problem lies in the *Euthyphro* dilemma itself. By positing that the pious is loved by the gods *because* it is pious, Socrates establishes that the standard of goodness is logically and ontologically prior to the gods. The gods are not the source of the Good; they are merely its most perfect exemplars and lovers. This effectively removes the need for the gods as a *source* of moral authority. If the Good is objective and discoverable through reason, then the divine becomes, at best, a redundant middleman. Human moral agency can function perfectly well without any reference to the divine, provided one has the intellectual capacity to grasp the Forms. The link between divine purpose and human agency becomes purely formal: the divine wills what is Good, and the human agent, through reason, does what is Good, but the two are connected only by their shared adherence to an abstract principle, not by any substantive relationship of command and obedience, or love and grace.

The second problem is the dissolution of the personal nature of the divine. Traditional piety was a relationship—a personal, emotional, and often terrifying relationship with beings who cared about human affairs, who could be entreated, who could show mercy, and who could be angered. Socrates’ divinity, stripped of all anthropomorphism, becomes an abstract ideal of rationality and goodness. It cannot be prayed to in a meaningful sense, because it has no personal will or desires that could be swayed by supplication. It cannot offer comfort in times of suffering, because it is not a conscious agent that responds to human emotion. The *daimonion* is a poor substitute for the Olympian gods; it is a logical safeguard, not a companion. This loss of the personal is a profound alienation. The human soul is left alone with its reason, facing a universe that is rationally ordered but emotionally sterile. The Socratic link between divine purpose and human agency is thus a link of *identity*—the human rational soul *is* the divine spark—rather than a link of *communion* between two distinct persons. This collapses the transcendent into the immanent, making the divine a function of human cognition.

The third problem concerns the nature of the elenchus itself. The elenchus is a purely negative process. It destroys false beliefs, but it does not, in the early dialogues, provide positive content for the definition of the Good. Socrates is famous for concluding his investigations with *aporia*—a state of confusion and perplexity. He does not tell us what piety *is*; he only tells us what it is *not*. If the service of the gods is the elimination of false beliefs, then this service is never completed. It is an eternal process of purification, an endless striving toward a goal that is never attained. This creates a paradox. If the divine purpose is the perfection of the soul, and perfection requires knowledge of the Good, and the elenchus only provides knowledge of ignorance, then the divine purpose can never be fulfilled. Human moral agency is condemned to a Sisyphean task. The link between divine purpose and human agency becomes a link of *frustration*, not of fulfillment. The gods (or the divine principle) demand perfection, but the human instrument (the elenchus) can only ever provide the awareness of imperfection.

Furthermore, one must question whether the elenchus is a sufficient basis for moral action. Socratic intellectualism holds that no one does wrong willingly; all wrongdoing is a result of ignorance. If this is true, then eliminating ignorance is sufficient for virtue. However, human psychology is more complex than this. The will is not simply a slave to the intellect. Passions, desires, and habits can override rational judgment. The elenchus may reveal to a person that their actions are wrong, but it does not provide the motivational force to change those actions. Traditional religion, with its rituals, its myths, and its communal practices, provided a powerful emotional and social reinforcement for moral behavior. The fear of divine punishment, the hope of divine favor, and the sense of belonging to a sacred community all served to motivate virtuous action. Socratic piety, in its austere rationalism, strips away these supports. It leaves the individual alone with their reason, demanding that they be virtuous purely out of a rational recognition of the Good. This is a heroic ideal, but it is an ideal that is psychologically unrealistic for most human beings. The coherence of the link between divine purpose and human agency thus depends on a highly idealized and unrealistic view of human nature.

Finally, we must address the tension between Socrates’ apparent piety and his radical redefinition. Socrates did perform sacrifices and participate in civic religious festivals. He believed in the gods, or at least in *a* divine principle. Does this mean he was merely a conventional believer? No. It means he was a philosophical reformer who sought to reinterpret the meaning of traditional practices. He participated in the rituals, but he gave them a new, rational meaning. The sacrifice was not an attempt to bribe a deity but a symbolic acknowledgment of the divine order. The prayer was not a petition for selfish favors but a meditation on the Good. In this sense, Socrates is not an atheist; he is a philosophical monotheist or a rationalist theist. He is attempting to rescue the concept of the divine from the corruption of myth and superstition, to purify it through reason. This is a noble endeavor, but it is one that ultimately leads to a conception of divinity that is so abstract and rational that it ceases to be a "god" in any traditional sense. It becomes the Form of the Good, the *Nous* (Mind) of Anaxagoras, the rational order of the cosmos. This is a philosophical principle, not a personal deity.

In conclusion, the claim that Socratic piety transcends traditional mythological conceptions of divinity is profoundly true. Socrates dismantles the anthropomorphic, fickle, and morally ambiguous gods of Homer and Hesiod, replacing them with a standard of perfect wisdom and perfect goodness. He redefines the service of the gods not as ritual sacrifice but as the philosophical improvement of the human soul. In the *Euthyphro*, he demonstrates that piety cannot be merely what the gods love, but must be based on an objective standard of Goodness, and that service to a perfect being must consist in the cultivation of virtue. In the *Apology*, he presents his philosophical mission as a divine command, and he equates the elenchus—the elimination of false beliefs—with the highest form of worship. This successfully establishes a coherent link between divine purpose and human moral agency *if* we accept that the divine is essentially rational and that human reason is the image of that divine rationality. The elimination of false beliefs aligns the soul with the divine order, making human agency an instrument of divine will.

However, this coherence is purchased at an exorbitant cost. By subordinating the divine will to an objective standard of the Good, Socrates renders the gods metaphysically redundant. By stripping the divine of its personal, providential, and anthropomorphic features, he alienates the human soul from any possibility of a personal relationship with the divine. And by relying on the purely negative elenchus, he condemns human moral agency to an endless, and ultimately unfulfillable, pursuit of a perfection that can never be defined. Socratic piety is a magnificent philosophical achievement, a radical purification of religious thought that elevates the human intellect to the highest plane of moral and spiritual activity. Yet, it is a piety that ultimately dissolves the very concept of a personal god, leaving in its place a cold, rational, and impersonal principle of cosmic order. It is a transcendence that leads not to a richer theology, but to a philosophical secularism, where the service of the gods becomes indistinguishable from the autonomous exercise of human reason. The gods of Athens are dead, and in their place stands the Socratic ideal of the wise and virtuous soul—a noble, heroic, and deeply lonely ideal, which serves as the eternal foundation of Western philosophy, but which offers little comfort to the human heart that yearns for a divine presence that is more than a logical necessity.
