## The Unlawful Kingdom: Why Kantian Ethics Cannot Justify Political Rebellion

The philosophy of Immanuel Kant presents a formidable paradox. On one hand, his moral philosophy is built upon the unyielding bedrock of the categorical imperative, demanding that all actions be universalizable, respect rational agency, and emanate from a good will. On the other hand, his political philosophy contains an absolute, seemingly unqualified prohibition against revolution. Yet, famously, Kant—the philosopher of rigid duty—openly expressed enthusiasm for the French Revolution, calling it a "phenomenon" that reveals a "moral disposition of the human race." This apparent contradiction—the condemnation of the act alongside the celebration of its historical significance—has vexed scholars for centuries. Can Kantian ethics, through its own internal logic, accommodate a moral justification for political rebellion? A rigorous application of the Formulation of Universal Law (FUL) and the Formulation of the Kingdom of Ends (FKE) demonstrates that it cannot. Despite the allure of a "revised maxim" that might seek to justify the overthrow of evil governments, and despite Christine Korsgaard's nuanced framework distinguishing strict from broad duties, the Kantian system ultimately forecloses rebellion. The appeal to a good will fails because the revolutionary maxim cannot be universalized without generating a practical contradiction, and the exclusion of happiness-based counsels cannot rescue an act that fundamentally undermines the very condition of public right. The paradox of Kant's enthusiasm is resolved not by finding a moral loophole for the rebel, but by recognizing that his admiration was directed at the *spectators'* moral disposition, not the *actors'* moral permissibility.

### I. The Architecture of Kantian Morality: FUL, FKE, and the Taxonomy of Duties

To assess the possibility of a Kantian justification for rebellion, one must first establish the precise mechanics of the moral law. The categorical imperative is the supreme principle of morality, and its first formulation—the FUL—commands: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." This test is not merely a thought experiment; it is a logical and practical filter. A maxim fails the FUL if its universalization leads to a *contradiction in conception*—where the very idea of the universalized maxim is self-defeating, destroying the possibility of the action it describes—or a *contradiction in will*—where the agent could not rationally will the universalized maxim because it would thwart their own fundamental purposes as a rational being. The FUL demands that my subjective principle of action be compatible with the objective principle of law as such.

The second formulation, the FKE, provides a teleological and intersubjective dimension: "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means." The FKE further elaborates this into the idea of a systematic union of rational beings under common laws—a "kingdom of ends." In this kingdom, every rational being is both a sovereign legislator (giving the law to themselves) and a subject (bound by that law). This formulation emphasizes the mutual recognition of autonomy and the absolute prohibition against using another’s rationality as a mere instrument for one’s own projects.

Within this framework, Kant distinguishes between two types of duties, a distinction that Christine Korsgaard has powerfully explicated. *Strict* or *perfect* duties are negative, absolute, and admit of no exception for inclination or circumstance. They prohibit specific actions (e.g., lying, theft, murder) because those actions are inherently contradictory. *Broad* or *imperfect* duties are positive, requiring us to adopt certain ends (e.g., the happiness of others, our own perfection), but they allow latitude in *how* we fulfill them. We must promote these ends, but we are not obligated to perform any specific act to do so. Korsgaard argues that perfect duties are those whose maxims cannot even be *conceived* as universal laws, while imperfect duties involve maxims that cannot be *willed* as universal laws without contradiction of the will.

Crucially, Korsgaard emphasizes that broad duties are constrained by strict duties. The latitude of imperfect duties does not permit us to violate a perfect duty to achieve a broad end. For instance, I have a broad duty to promote the happiness of others, but I cannot fulfill this by murdering a wealthy person to redistribute their wealth. The strict duty against murder absolutely trumps the broad duty of beneficence. This hierarchical structure is essential for understanding the rebellion problem: the duty to establish a just society (a broad, teleological duty) cannot be used to justify the violation of the strict duty of non-rebellion against the sovereign.

### II. Kant’s Explicit Condemnation: The Absolute Prohibition of Rebellion

Kant’s political writings are unambiguous in their rejection of revolution. In the *Metaphysics of Morals*, he writes that "all resistance to the supreme legislative power, all incitement of the subjects to violent expressions of discontent, all defiance which breaks out into rebellion, is the highest and most punishable crime in a commonwealth, for it destroys its very foundation." He goes further, stating that even if the sovereign has violated the original contract, "the people have no right to coerce the sovereign." The people may "complain" and "express their grievances" through legal channels, but they cannot actively resist. Kant argues that the very concept of a civil society presupposes a supreme authority to adjudicate disputes. If the people could unilaterally decide when the sovereign has erred and then act to depose him, they would be setting themselves up as judges in their own case, which is a contradiction of the very idea of a legal order.

This prohibition is not merely a pragmatic concern for stability; it is a *moral* necessity rooted in the categorical imperative. The sovereign is the embodiment of the general will, the condition for the possibility of external freedom. To rebel is to act on a maxim that places one’s own judgment of right above the formal structure of law. Kant argues that the maxim of rebellion, if universalized, would lead to a state of nature—a war of all against all—where no one is secure in their rights. The state is not a means to an end (like happiness); it is the *sine qua non* of rightful coexistence. Therefore, the duty to obey the sovereign is a *perfect* duty, admitting no exceptions based on the sovereign's character or actions.

To test this, we can formulate a "revised maxim" that the rebel might propose to justify their action: *"When a government persistently and egregiously violates the fundamental principles of justice and the rights of its citizens, I will forcibly overthrow it in order to establish a just constitutional order."* This maxim appears to be grounded in moral concern, not mere self-interest. It seeks to align political reality with the dictates of practical reason. The question is whether this maxim can survive the FUL.

### III. The Universalization Test: The Practical Contradiction of the Revolutionary Maxim

Applying the FUL to the revised maxim reveals a profound and insurmountable problem. The test asks: Can I will that everyone act on this maxim? Let us universalize it: *Everyone, when they believe their government is unjust, will forcibly overthrow it.*

The first step is to identify the contradiction in conception. The concept of a "government" or a "civil society" logically requires a supreme, coercive authority that has the final say in resolving disputes. The state exists precisely to escape the state of nature, where individuals use their own private judgment and force to secure their rights. The universalized maxim of rebellion posits a world where every citizen retains the right to unilaterally judge the government's actions and use force to overturn it. In such a world, the state is not a stable institution; it is a provisional arrangement contingent upon the ongoing approval of every individual. But this is a contradiction. The universalized maxim destroys the very concept of a state (a stable, supreme authority) that it presupposes in its antecedent (a "government" that can be overthrown). If everyone reserves the right to rebel, then no government truly exists, because no authority is final. The maxim, when universalized, undermines the condition of its own possibility. It is a performative contradiction: to will the overthrow of a government is to will the existence of a government, but universalizing the right to overthrow makes the existence of any government impossible.

One might object that the maxim can be narrowed: *"When a government commits atrocities that violate the natural rights of its citizens, I will overthrow it."* But this narrowing does not escape the logical problem. The universalization of this maxim still leaves the *judgment* of what constitutes an "atrocity" or a "violation of rights" in the hands of each individual. Since there is no higher authority to adjudicate these claims (that is what the government is for), the universalized maxim again leads to a situation where any individual or faction can claim a moral right to armed resistance. The state becomes a hostage to the most aggrieved or the most fanatical. The universalization of this narrower maxim still results in a condition where the legal order is perpetually vulnerable to unilateral abrogation, which is a contradiction of the concept of a legal order as such.

This is where Korsgaard's distinction between strict and broad duties becomes crucial. Kant classifies the duty to obey the sovereign as a *strict* or *perfect* duty. It is a duty of right (juridical) that is also morally binding. The duty to establish a just society, on the other hand, is a *broad* or *imperfect* duty. We are obligated to work towards a more just world, but we have latitude in how we do so. We can promote justice through education, public discourse, legal reform, and moral self-cultivation. The revolutionary, however, attempts to fulfill a broad duty (establishing justice) by violating a strict duty (obedience to the sovereign). Korsgaard's framework shows that this is categorically impermissible. The strict duty provides an absolute side-constraint on our pursuit of broad ends. The end of justice cannot justify the means of lawlessness, because the means are themselves a violation of the very principle of right that justice aims to instantiate. The revolutionary maxim fails the FUL not because it aims at a bad end, but because the *means* it employs—the unilateral, coercive overthrow of the sovereign—cannot be universalized without destroying the possibility of any lawful order.

### IV. The Good Will and the Exclusion of Happiness: The Failure of Moral Motivation

A Kantian apologist for rebellion might argue that the rebel possesses a *good will*, the only thing good without qualification. The rebel, they might claim, acts not from self-interest or a desire for happiness, but purely from duty—the duty to establish a just constitution. Kant defines a good will as one that acts from duty, motivated by respect for the moral law, not by inclination. The rebel, in this view, is a moral hero who sacrifices personal safety for the sake of the categorical imperative.

However, this appeal to the good will is illusory within the Kantian system. The good will is not determined by the *content* of the agent's intentions alone; it is determined by the *form* of the agent's maxim. A good will acts in accordance with the moral law. If the moral law, as established by the FUL and FKE, prohibits rebellion, then any act of rebellion, no matter how noble its intention, is not an act of a good will. The rebel's maxim fails the universalization test, and therefore the action is objectively wrong, regardless of the agent's subjective motivation. Kant famously states that "duty is the necessity of an action done from respect for the law." If the law (the categorical imperative) forbids the action, then acting on it cannot be an act of respect for the law, no matter how much the agent believes they are serving a higher moral purpose.

Furthermore, the exclusion of happiness-based empirical counsels is a double-edged sword. The rebel cannot justify their action by appealing to the happiness that would result from a better government—less oppression, more prosperity, greater freedom. These are empirical, contingent ends that cannot ground a categorical moral imperative. But the rebel also cannot justify their action by appealing to a non-empirical duty to establish justice, because that duty is a broad, imperfect duty that must be pursued through lawful means. The Kantian agent is caught in a bind. If they rebel for happiness, they are acting heteronomously, making their will determined by pathological inclinations. If they rebel for justice, they are attempting to fulfill a broad duty through a means that violates a strict duty, which is a contradiction in the willing itself. The good will is not a magic wand that sanctifies an unlawful act; it is a will that legislates universal law for itself. A will that legislates rebellion for itself is not a good will; it is a will that has failed to subject its maxim to the test of universalizability.

Kant’s distinction between the *moral* and the *juridical* is also relevant here. The juridical realm concerns external actions and their conformity with law, while the moral realm concerns internal maxims and their conformity with duty. Rebellion is a juridical crime—a violation of public right. But Kant insists it is also a moral crime, because the maxim of rebellion is a violation of the categorical imperative. The rebel cannot claim to be acting morally simply because they intend a good outcome. The moral worth of an action lies in its maxim, and the maxim of rebellion is inherently defective.

### V. The Kingdom of Ends and the Paradox of Enthusiasm

The FKE provides an even more profound obstacle to justifying rebellion. The Kingdom of Ends is a systematic union of rational beings under common laws that they give to themselves. In this kingdom, each person is an end in themselves, and the laws are those that could be agreed upon by all rational beings. The revolutionary act, however, is a unilateral act of force. It treats the sovereign not as a co-legislator in a kingdom of ends, but as a means to an end—the end being the establishment of a new order. Even if the sovereign is a tyrant, the rebel is still treating the *institution* of sovereignty as a mere instrument to be discarded when it no longer serves their purpose. This violates the fundamental principle of the FKE: that we must treat humanity, in every person, as an end and never merely as a means.

Moreover, the FKE demands that we act in a way that could be consistent with a realm of ends. Could a rebellion be a law in a kingdom of ends? No. A kingdom of ends is a realm of peace and mutual respect, where disputes are resolved through law, not force. Revolution is the antithesis of this ideal. It is a regression to the state of nature, a declaration that law is subordinate to private will. To bring about a kingdom of ends, one must act in accordance with the principles that make such a kingdom possible—namely, respect for law and the rejection of unilateral coercion. Rebellion, by its very nature, destroys the possibility of a lawful union, and thus cannot be a means to the kingdom of ends.

This brings us to the heart of the paradox: Kant’s enthusiasm for the French Revolution. How can the philosopher who called rebellion "the highest crime" celebrate a rebellion? The answer lies in a distinction Kant makes between the *act* and the *spectacle*. In the *Conflict of the Faculties*, Kant argues that the French Revolution, despite its horrors, reveals a "moral disposition of the human race" among the *spectators*—the onlookers who, at their own risk, express a universal and disinterested sympathy for the revolutionaries' goal of a republican constitution. This enthusiasm is not an endorsement of the act of rebellion; it is an acknowledgment of the *moral progress* of humanity. The spectators' sympathy demonstrates that humanity has a natural disposition towards republicanism and the rule of law. This is a sign that the moral idea of a rightful constitution is becoming a part of human culture.

Kant is not saying that the revolutionaries were morally justified. He is saying that their actions, however wrongful, serve as an empirical *indicator* of a latent moral capacity in humanity. The enthusiasm is for the *idea* of a republic, not for the *method* of achieving it. In fact, Kant explicitly warns against using revolution as a means to achieve this goal, stating that reform must come from the sovereign, not from the people. The revolution is a "phenomenon" that allows us to observe human nature, but it is not a model for moral action. The spectators' enthusiasm is a judgment about the future of humanity, not a judgment about the moral permissibility of the revolutionaries' deeds.

To resolve the paradox, we must recognize that Kant's moral philosophy operates on two distinct levels: the level of the *actor* and the level of the *spectator*. For the actor, the categorical imperative is a strict test of the maxims of action. The revolutionary's maxim fails this test, and thus the act is wrong. For the spectator, the revolution is an event in the world that can be interpreted through the lens of moral teleology. The spectator sees in the revolution a sign of humanity's progress towards a rightful condition, but this does not mean the spectator approves of the means. The spectator's enthusiasm is a rational hope for the future, not a moral justification of the present. Kant's own framework demands this distinction. A good will is judged by its maxims, not by its consequences. The consequences of the French Revolution—the spread of republican ideals—may be laudable, but the maxim of the revolutionaries—to overthrow the sovereign by force—is not.

### VI. Resolving the Paradox: The Incompatibility of Rebellion and Kantian Duty

The attempt to use a "revised maxim" to justify rebellion fails at every level of the Kantian system. The maxim cannot be universalized without generating a practical contradiction, because it destroys the very concept of a legal order it presupposes. The appeal to a good will fails because a good will is defined by its conformity to the moral law, and rebellion violates that law. The exclusion of happiness-based counsels fails because the alternative—a duty to establish justice—is a broad duty that cannot override the strict duty of obedience. The FKE fails because rebellion treats the sovereign and the legal order as a mere means to an end, and it cannot be a law in a kingdom of ends.

Korsgaard's framework of strict and broad duties is the key to unlocking this entire problem. The strict duty of obedience to the sovereign is a perfect duty. It is a duty of right that is also a moral duty. It admits of no exceptions. The broad duty to promote justice is an imperfect duty, requiring us to adopt the end of justice but allowing latitude in how we pursue it. We can pursue justice through lawful reform, public criticism, and the cultivation of moral virtue. We cannot pursue it through revolution, because revolution violates the strict duty that is the condition for the possibility of any lawful pursuit of justice. The revolutionary is like someone who tries to cure a disease by poisoning the patient. The end is good, but the means are destructive of the very thing they seek to preserve.

Thus, the paradox of Kant's condemnation and enthusiasm is resolved not by finding a moral justification for rebellion, but by recognizing that Kant's moral philosophy is fundamentally incompatible with such a justification. Kant's enthusiasm for the French Revolution is a testament to his hope for human progress, but it is a hope that must be realized through the slow, arduous work of legal and moral reform, not through the violent overthrow of the state. The revolutionary, in Kant's eyes, is a tragic figure: they may be motivated by the noblest ideals, but their actions are objectively wrong because they violate the categorical imperative. The spectators, who cheer from the sidelines, are the true bearers of moral progress, because their enthusiasm is a judgment of the *idea* of justice, not the *act* of rebellion.

In conclusion, Kantian ethics cannot accommodate a moral justification for political rebellion. The application of the FUL and the FKE, combined with Korsgaard's distinction between strict and broad duties, demonstrates that the revolutionary maxim is inherently contradictory and violates the fundamental principles of right. The appeal to a good will and the exclusion of happiness-based counsels cannot rescue the revolutionary act, because the act itself is a violation of the moral law. Kant's philosophy, despite its revolutionary implications for the autonomy of the individual, ultimately prioritizes the stability of the legal order over the immediate redress of grievances. The path to a just society, for Kant, is not through rebellion, but through the gradual, lawful evolution of the state. The French Revolution may have been a magnificent spectacle, but it was a spectacle of moral failure, redeemed only by the rational hope of the spectators. The Kantian rebel is a contradiction in terms—a being who seeks to establish the kingdom of ends by destroying the only means through which that kingdom can be realized: the rule of law.
