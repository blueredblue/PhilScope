The question of what constitutes the highest human good—*eudaimonia*—has anchored the tradition of Western philosophy, and nowhere is this inquiry more rigorously pursued than in Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics*. At the heart of Aristotle’s account lies the assertion that human happiness is an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue. However, a profound tension emerges when we consider the role of moral virtue within this framework. Is moral virtue an intrinsic constituent of the good life, inseparable from the very fabric of human flourishing? Or is it merely a necessary developmental prerequisite and practical foundation, a scaffolding that must eventually be set aside in favor of the pure intellectual activity of theoretical contemplation? This tension pits the inclusive view of the chief good against the dominant intellectualist interpretation, a debate that continues to animate Aristotelian scholarship. By examining the defenses of J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche, who argue for the comprehensive nature of the chief good through the concepts of *teleion* and the function argument, and contrasting their positions with John Cooper’s distinction between human happiness and ultimate divine contemplation, we can resolve whether moral virtue operates as an intrinsic constituent or merely a preparatory condition. Ultimately, this analysis reveals that moral virtue is not merely a prerequisite for theoretical wisdom but an intrinsic constituent of human happiness, and that mortal existence reconciles practical reasoning with pure intellectual activity precisely because the complete human good requires the actualization of our full rational nature, both practical and theoretical.

The dominant intellectualist interpretation of the *Nicomachean Ethics* draws its primary impetus from the culminating chapters of Book X, where Aristotle famously declares that the life of contemplation is the most divine and the most self-sufficient. On this reading, moral virtue is relegated to the status of a necessary condition for the good life—a foundation that stabilizes the soul and provides the external circumstances required for the contemplative life, but which does not itself constitute the highest human good. The intellectualist argues that because the activity of the intellect (*nous*) is the most divine element in us, and because contemplation is the highest exercise of that element, the ultimate object of human happiness must be the theoretical activity of the intellect. In this view, moral virtue is instrumental; it is the necessary prerequisite that allows us to pursue theoretical wisdom, but it is not part of the ultimate fulfillment of the human function. The tension between this intellectualist reading and the inclusive view—which insists that the chief good is comprehensive and requires the exercise of both intellectual and moral virtue—is thus fundamentally a debate about the completeness of the human good. If the intellectualist is correct, then the life of the virtuous but non-contemplative person is incomplete, lacking the highest component of the good. If the inclusive view is correct, then moral virtue is not merely a stepping stone to contemplation but an intrinsic part of the good life itself.

J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche mount a robust defense of the comprehensive, inclusive nature of the chief good, arguing that moral virtue is an intrinsic constituent of *eudaimonia* rather than a mere developmental prerequisite. Their defense hinges critically on the concepts of *teleion* (completeness or perfection) and the function argument (*ergon*). Ackrill, in his seminal essay "Aristotle on the Human Good," argues that the function argument in Book I of the *Nicomachean Ethics* cannot be satisfied by intellectual virtue alone. The human function, Aristotle claims, is rational activity in accordance with virtue (1098a16). For Ackrill, the phrase "in accordance with virtue" (*arete*) must encompass both the intellectual and the moral virtues, because the human function is not merely the capacity for theoretical reasoning, but the full actualization of human capacities in a complete life. If we were to strip the function argument down to intellectual virtue alone, we would be defining the human function in a truncated manner, ignoring the practical and ethical dimensions that constitute the specifically human mode of life. Thus, for Ackrill, moral virtue is intrinsic because the function it actualizes is intrinsically human.

Timothy Roche further bolsters this inclusive reading by focusing on the requirement that the chief good must be *teleion*. The chief good, Aristotle argues, must be complete, self-sufficient, and lacking nothing (1097a30-34). If the chief good were merely the life of contemplation, then the removal of moral virtue and practical activity would not diminish the completeness of the good, provided contemplation remained. But this leads to an absurdity: a life devoid of moral virtue is not a complete human life, for it lacks the practical actualization of our rational and social nature. Roche emphasizes that *teleion* demands the actualization of the whole human being, not just the intellect. The comprehensive nature of the chief good means that it must include the full range of human activities that we value for their own sakes. Moral virtue, therefore, is not a prerequisite to be outgrown but an intrinsic constituent of the complete human good. The life of the virtuous person who engages in both practical and contemplative activity is *teleion* because it actualizes the totality of human excellence, whereas the life of pure contemplation, divorced from moral virtue, is incomplete because it fails to actualize the practical, ethical dimension of our humanity.

Contrasting this inclusive defense is the position articulated by John Cooper, who draws a sharp distinction between human happiness and ultimate divine contemplation. Cooper, a prominent interpreter of Aristotle, argues that while Aristotle does indeed place a high premium on theoretical contemplation, human happiness cannot be equated with the pure intellectual activity of the gods. Cooper notes that because humans are mortal, embodied, and social beings, our happiness must be appropriate to our nature. In his interpretation, Aristotle distinguishes between the ultimate object of desire (divine contemplation) and the human happiness that is achievable within the constraints of mortal existence. Cooper suggests that while theoretical wisdom is the highest form of activity, human happiness requires a life of moral virtue to provide the necessary stability, practical orientation, and social context in which contemplation can occur. 

However, it is precisely at this juncture that the tension between Cooper’s view and the inclusive interpretation becomes most acute. Cooper’s distinction risks reducing moral virtue to a necessary developmental prerequisite and practical foundation for theoretical wisdom. If moral virtue is merely the foundation that supports the edifice of contemplation, then it is instrumental rather than intrinsic. Cooper’s view implies that once the contemplative life is achieved, the moral life recedes in importance; the virtuous person is virtuous not because virtue is constitutive of happiness, but because virtue is the necessary scaffolding that allows the intellect to reach its highest potential. This stands in stark contrast to Ackrill and Roche, who argue that the function argument and the requirement of *teleion* make virtue constitutive of the good life. For the inclusive view, the moral and the intellectual are not hierarchically arranged such that the intellectual merely uses the moral as a tool; rather, they are co-constitutive elements of a complete human existence. Cooper’s distinction, while sensitive to the limitations of mortality, ultimately subordinates the ethical to the theoretical, thereby failing to capture the comprehensive nature of Aristotle’s chief good.

The question of whether moral virtue operates as an intrinsic constituent of the good life or merely as a necessary developmental prerequisite and practical foundation for theoretical wisdom is thus the crux of the matter. If moral virtue is merely a prerequisite, then the life of the contemplative philosopher who lacks moral virtue could, in principle, be happier than the life of the virtuous citizen, provided the philosopher achieves the highest theoretical insights. But this conclusion is deeply problematic for Aristotle’s ethics. The function argument explicitly states that a good human being is one who performs the human function well, and the human function is rational activity in accordance with virtue. If we remove moral virtue from the definition of the human function, we remove the ethical dimension of human excellence, reducing the human good to a purely cognitive achievement. This reduction is inconsistent with the *teleion* nature of the chief good, which must be complete for a human being. Ackrill and Roche compellingly demonstrate that moral virtue is intrinsic because it actualizes the specifically human capacity for practical reasoning and ethical action. Without moral virtue, the good life is not merely difficult to achieve; it is conceptually incomplete.

Furthermore, the resolution of how mortal existence reconciles practical reasoning with the pure intellectual activity described in Book X depends on recognizing the intrinsic necessity of moral virtue. In Book X, Aristotle extols the life of contemplation as the most divine and the most self-sufficient, yet he immediately qualifies this by stating that we must not live like beasts, seeking only sensory pleasure, nor like mere calculators, seeking only honor (1179a15-18). The reconciliation of practical reasoning and pure intellectual activity in mortal existence is not a matter of using practical reasoning as a mere tool to facilitate contemplation, but rather a matter of integrating the two modes of rational activity into a unified human life. Practical wisdom (*phronesis*) and moral virtue are not stepping stones to be discarded once contemplation is achieved; they are the very modes through which rational activity is embodied and lived out in the mortal world. 

Mortal existence is defined by its temporality, its social embeddedness, and its practical demands. We cannot simply abandon our ethical lives to become pure contemplators, for we are not pure intellects; we are embodied, social beings whose rational activity must manifest in action. The inclusive view, defended by Ackrill and Roche, resolves this tension by arguing that the complete human good is a life of activity in accordance with complete virtue, which includes both the practical and the theoretical. Moral virtue is not a prerequisite that is outgrown but a permanent constituent of the good life because it is the actualization of our practical rational capacities. The contemplative life is the highest component of the good life, but it is not the only component. The good life is a composite, and just as a body requires both soul and flesh to be complete, the human good requires both the intellectual and the moral to be *teleion*. 

In conclusion, the necessity of moral virtue in Aristotle’s account of human happiness cannot be overstated. The tension between the inclusive view and the dominant intellectualist interpretation is resolved by recognizing that the chief good must be comprehensive and complete. J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche successfully defend the inclusive nature of the chief good through the concepts of *teleion* and the function argument, demonstrating that moral virtue is an intrinsic constituent of the good life. John Cooper’s distinction between human happiness and ultimate divine contemplation, while highlighting the supremacy of theoretical activity, ultimately reduces moral virtue to a mere developmental prerequisite, thereby failing to capture the full actualization of the human function. Mortal existence reconciles practical reasoning with pure intellectual activity not by subordinating the former to the latter, but by integrating them into a unified life of virtue. Moral virtue is not the scaffolding upon which the temple of contemplation is built; it is the very foundation and fabric of the temple itself, without which the human good remains incomplete.
