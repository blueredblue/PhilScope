# Change, Predication, and the Problem of Opposites: Aristotle’s *Categories* and Plato’s *Phaedo*

The problem of change is inseparable from the problem of predication. When an individual grows taller, becomes pale, or moves from health to sickness, we appear to say that one and the same thing first has one property and later has its contrary. Yet if the subject remains numerically one while its predicates alter, it becomes necessary to explain both what persists and what changes. If nothing persists, change would seem to be the replacement of one thing by another rather than the alteration of a single thing. If everything persists unchanged, however, genuine change becomes unintelligible. The philosophical question is therefore not merely how a quality changes, but how a subject can remain itself while admitting contrary qualities at different times.

Plato and Aristotle approach this problem from sharply different ontological perspectives. In the *Phaedo*, Plato explains intelligibility through Forms: stable, non-sensible realities such as the Beautiful itself, the Just itself, and the Equal itself. Sensible things are what they are by participating in these Forms. The Forms provide the standards by which sensible things are known, but they are also introduced as imperishable and resistant to contrary alteration. Plato’s account thus gives a powerful explanation of why knowledge cannot be reduced to the fluctuating world of sense experience. At the same time, it leaves obscure the precise relation between Forms and particulars, especially when a particular appears to possess opposite properties at different times.

Aristotle’s *Categories* offers a more articulated account of this relation. Its distinction between primary substances and their accidental predicates, its classification of qualities, relatives, contraries, contradictories, and privation and possession, and its claim that substance is capable of receiving contrary qualities all directly address the structure of predication. A particular human being can remain numerically one while becoming taller because the human being is the underlying substance and tallness is an accidental quality that can come to be or pass away in it. Aristotle’s later doctrines of potentiality and actuality, developed most fully in the *Physics* and *Metaphysics*, deepen this account by explaining change as the actualization of a determinate potential in an enduring subject.

Aristotle therefore provides a more cogent account of change and predication than Plato’s theory of Forms as presented in the *Phaedo*, especially because he locates contrary properties within an organized ontology of subjects, qualities, and modes of being. Aristotle’s framework does not make Forms unnecessary in every philosophical sense, nor does it eliminate all metaphysical difficulty. His notions of potentiality, actuality, and underlying matter raise their own questions. Nevertheless, he successfully avoids several burdens in Plato’s account: the ambiguity of participation, the difficulty of explaining how unchanging Forms are related to changing particulars, and the tendency to treat contrary properties as if they were governed primarily by the presence or absence of separate transcendent entities.

## Plato’s Forms and the Problem of Opposite Properties

The central motivation of Plato’s theory of Forms is epistemological as well as ontological. Human beings encounter beautiful objects, just actions, equal sticks, and courageous deeds, but sensible instances are unstable, imperfect, and liable to appear differently to different observers. The Beautiful itself, by contrast, is not beautiful at one time and ugly at another. The Equal itself does not become unequal. The Form is therefore supposed to provide a stable object of knowledge amid the instability of sensible experience.

In the *Phaedo*, this theory is connected with the doctrine of recollection. Socrates argues that when we perceive equal sensible objects, we recognize that they fall short of perfect equality. Two sticks may appear equal in one respect and unequal in another; they may cease to appear equal when viewed from another angle. Yet we possess the notion of equality itself, which cannot have been derived simply from sensory encounters with imperfectly equal objects. Sense perception occasions the recollection of the Form. The argument is not merely that sensible objects resemble Forms. It is that our ability to judge sensible things as deficient instances of equality presupposes some prior grasp of perfect Equality.

This structure gives Plato a persuasive explanation of the normative dimension of predication. To call something beautiful is not merely to report a subjective response. It is to compare the thing, however imperfectly, with Beauty itself. Similarly, to call an act just is to measure it against Justice itself. Plato’s Forms thus explain how predicates can have stable meanings even though their sensible instances are variable.

But the same theory creates a difficulty concerning change. If a particular is beautiful by participating in Beauty, what happens when it becomes ugly? Does Beauty itself enter and leave the object? Does the object acquire and lose a relation to the Form? Is participation a causal process, a resemblance relation, a presence, or something else? Plato’s language in the *Phaedo* does not settle the issue. The Forms are treated as causes of things being what they are, but the mechanism by which a Form accounts for a changing particular remains underdescribed.

The problem becomes particularly acute when the same individual is said to possess contrary properties. Simmias, for example, may be taller than Socrates but shorter than Phaedo. The relevant point is not simply that Simmias has two properties, tallness and shortness, but that his description depends on comparison. He is tall relative to one person and short relative to another. Plato’s discussion indicates that a thing may be called by contrary predicates without the contrary Forms themselves undergoing change. Simmias does not make Tallness itself taller or Shortness itself shorter. Rather, Simmias participates in or is associated with different Forms under different relations.

Plato also insists that a Form does not admit its contrary. The Form of Tallness cannot become Shortness, and the Form of Life cannot admit Death. In the final argument of the *Phaedo*, Socrates argues that certain things exclude their opposites because they participate in Forms or possess essential characteristics that entail exclusion. Fire brings heat, snow brings cold, and the number three cannot admit evenness. The Form of Life cannot admit death because death is contrary to what it is to be alive.

This doctrine provides a striking solution to one aspect of the problem: the Forms themselves remain pure and stable. No Form changes into its contrary. But it does not yet explain the full structure of change in particulars. A particular can become taller or shorter, hotter or colder, alive or dead, but Plato’s account often explains this by saying that one Form is present and then absent, or that a thing comes to participate in one Form rather than another. That language identifies a difference in the object’s condition, but it does not fully explain the ontological process by which the difference occurs.

## Recollection and Participation

Recollection gives Plato an account of how we know stable essences, but it is not primarily a theory of physical change. In the *Phaedo*, recollection explains why the soul can recognize equality, beauty, or justice despite the inadequacy of sensible examples. The soul’s prior acquaintance with the Forms makes intelligible judgment possible. Yet the fact that the soul recollects the Form of Equality does not itself explain how a particular sensible thing becomes equal or unequal. Recollection explains the knower’s access to an intelligible standard, not the subject’s transition between contrary states.

Participation is intended to perform the ontological work. Sensible objects are beautiful because Beauty is present in them, equal because they participate in Equality, and just because they share in Justice. But participation remains metaphorically rich and conceptually ambiguous. If a Form is wholly present in many things, it seems divided or multiplied. If it is only partially present, then the Form appears to have parts or degrees. If participation means resemblance, the theory must explain what grounds the resemblance without appealing to another Form. If it means causal dependence, the kind of causality involved is unclear. Plato’s Forms are not ordinary efficient causes that act at a particular time; they are more like intelligible grounds or paradigms.

The problem is not that Plato’s theory is meaningless. It expresses a deep insight: particular things are intelligible only in relation to stable structures that are not reducible to the particular things themselves. Nevertheless, when the question is specifically how a numerically one individual passes from one contrary state to another, participation is less precise than Aristotle’s account. It tells us that the individual’s relation to a Form changes, but not what sort of entity the individual is, what persists through the change, or what actualizes the new condition.

The *Phaedo*’s argument from opposites further complicates matters. Socrates argues that opposites come from opposites: the greater comes from the smaller, the more just from the less just, the living from the dead, and the dead from the living. This cyclical argument is meant to support the immortality of the soul. Yet the principle is difficult to apply uniformly. In some cases, an individual undergoes a gradual alteration: a person becomes taller, hotter, or more just. In other cases, the relation between opposites seems to involve coming to be and passing away rather than simple alteration. The movement from life to death is not obviously the same kind of transition as movement from shorter to taller.

Plato’s theory tends to group these cases under the general structure of opposites without providing a fully differentiated classification of the ways in which opposites relate. The *Phaedo* distinguishes certain Forms that exclude their opposites, but it does not offer a systematic account of substances, qualities, relatives, privations, and contradictory predicates. As a result, the theory can illuminate the logical and metaphysical importance of opposites while leaving the grammar of change insufficiently analyzed.

## Aristotle’s Ontology of Substance and Accident

Aristotle begins from a different question: what are the fundamental things about which we speak? In the *Categories*, he distinguishes what is said of a subject from what is present in a subject. A universal such as “human” may be said of Socrates, while a particular quality such as a certain shade of pale may be present in Socrates. The most fundamental entities are primary substances, individual things such as an individual human being or an individual horse. They are not said of a subject in the way universals are, nor are they present in a subject as accidents are.

This distinction immediately clarifies the relation between identity and change. Socrates is a primary substance. His being pale, tall, seated, or knowledgeable involves predications that depend upon him. If Socrates becomes taller, Socrates does not thereby cease to exist and get replaced by a new entity. Rather, the substance remains while one of its accidental qualities changes. The quality is real, but it is not an independent substance. It exists in the substance and is predicated of it.

Aristotle’s claim that substance is “most of all” what is a subject is crucial. A subject is not merely a grammatical bearer of predicates. It is an ontological bearer: a being whose existence does not depend upon being in another subject in the same way that an accidental quality does. A particular quality cannot exist independently as the pale of something. It is the pale thing that exists primarily, while the quality exists in it.

This structure avoids a problem in the Platonic account. Plato separates the stable Form from the changing particular, but Aristotle explains the stability and the change within one ontological arrangement. The individual substance is stable enough to remain the same thing, while its accidents are changeable enough to account for alteration. The explanation of change does not require the movement of a transcendent Form into or out of the object. It requires only that a subject possess, lose, or acquire an accidental determination.

The example of an individual growing taller can be analyzed in this way. Let the individual be Callias. At an earlier time, Callias has a certain height; at a later time, he has a greater height. Callias is numerically one through the process. Height is not a separate substance that becomes another height in itself. Rather, the quantity or bodily determination of Callias changes. The individual remains the subject of the predication, while the specific measure of his height is altered.

Aristotle’s language about substance receiving contraries makes the point explicitly. Substance can receive contrary qualities while remaining numerically one: the same body may be hot and later cold, pale and later dark, healthy and later sick. The contrary qualities cannot coexist in the same subject at the same time and in the same respect, but they may succeed one another in the same subject. Substance is therefore not itself one of the contrary qualities. It is that which is capable of receiving both.

This is a significant advance over a simple theory of Forms. Plato can say that a thing participates first in one Form and then in another, but Aristotle can distinguish the enduring subject from the qualities that change in it. The numerical identity of the subject is not inferred from a relation to an external paradigm; it is grounded in the subject’s own ontological status.

## Aristotle’s Classification of Opposites

Aristotle’s systematic classification of opposites makes his account of predication more precise. In the *Categories*, he distinguishes several forms of opposition, including relative opposition, contrary opposition, contradictory opposition, and opposition between possession and privation.

Relatives are opposed in a way that depends upon correlation. Knowledge is knowledge of something, and the greater is greater than something. A person may be taller than one individual and shorter than another without possessing an absolute contradiction. The example of Simmias can therefore be analyzed through the category of relation. “Taller than Socrates” and “shorter than Phaedo” are not straightforward contraries predicated in the same respect. They are relational descriptions whose truth depends on different standards.

Contraries, by contrast, are qualities or states that occupy opposed positions within a relevant field: hot and cold, white and black, virtue and vice, health and sickness. Contraries cannot both belong to the same subject simultaneously in the same respect, but they can belong to the same subject successively. This distinction is exactly what is needed to explain alteration. The individual subject remains, while contrary accidents are gained and lost.

Contradictories are different. “Socrates is sitting” and “Socrates is not sitting” cannot both be true at the same time and in the same respect, nor can they both be false. Contradictory opposition concerns affirmation and negation rather than two positive qualities in a shared field. Aristotle’s classification prevents the mistake of treating every pair of opposed predicates as if they involved the same kind of metaphysical transition. To become not-sitting is not necessarily to acquire a positive contrary quality equivalent to sitting.

Privation and possession provide another important distinction. A subject can possess a capacity or feature and later lose it, or lack it while being capable of having it. Blindness and sight, for example, are not simply contraries in the same sense as white and black. Blindness is a privation of sight in a subject naturally fitted for sight. This means that the absence of a property can be ontologically significant without being a positive entity corresponding to the property’s contrary.

These classifications improve the analysis of change because they separate several issues that Plato tends to associate under the broad heading of opposites. The change from hot to cold is an alteration between contraries. The change from seeing to blindness is a loss of possession or the acquisition of a privation. The change from sitting to not sitting involves contradictory predication, although the physical transition may involve standing, lying, or some other positive state. The statement that Simmias is taller than Socrates and shorter than Phaedo concerns relations rather than absolute contrary qualities.

Aristotle’s distinctions also improve the logic of predication. A subject may bear different predicates because the predicates belong to different categories, because they are true at different times, or because they are true relative to different objects. The apparent contradiction can therefore be dissolved by specifying the respect in which each predicate is true. This does not trivialize change; rather, it shows that many apparent contradictions arise from failing to distinguish kinds of opposition.

## Potentiality and Actuality

The *Categories* identifies substance as the subject capable of receiving contrary qualities, but its treatment of change remains relatively schematic. Aristotle’s more developed solution appears in the *Physics* and *Metaphysics*, through the distinction between potentiality and actuality.

Change is not the emergence of something from absolute nothing, nor is it the replacement of one completely unrelated thing by another. It is the actualization of a potential in a subject. A thing is potentially F when it has the capacity to become F under appropriate conditions, and it is actually F when that capacity has been realized. A child is potentially adult; a piece of bronze is potentially a statue; a person who is currently seated is potentially standing. The potentiality is not itself the actuality, but neither is it sheer nothingness. It is a determinate capacity belonging to a subject.

The example of growing taller illustrates this structure. An immature human being possesses a natural potential for bodily growth. At an earlier stage, the person is potentially taller than he is then. Through biological development, this potential is progressively actualized. The person does not become taller because the Form of Tallness enters him. Nor does he acquire a wholly new substance at every stage. The same organism remains the subject whose bodily capacities are being actualized.

Potentiality explains both continuity and direction. Continuity arises because the potential belongs to an already existing subject. Direction arises because not every change is possible in every subject. A human being can grow to a humanly appropriate height, but a stone does not have the same biological potential. The potential is structured by the nature of the thing. Change is thus intelligible as a process in which what a thing can be becomes what it actually is.

Actuality also clarifies why contrary states are not simultaneously present in the same respect. A subject may be potentially hot while actually cold, or potentially standing while actually sitting. The relevant potential does not amount to the simultaneous possession of the contrary actuality. It indicates an ordered capacity for a future state. In this way Aristotle can affirm that the subject remains one while its actual determination changes.

The distinction also helps distinguish accidental from substantial change. When Socrates becomes pale or taller, Socrates remains the same substance and undergoes accidental alteration. When one living organism dies and its matter is reorganized, the change may be described as substantial: the previous living substance no longer exists as the same kind of substance. The *Categories* by itself does not fully develop the metaphysics of matter and form required to explain substantial generation and destruction, but Aristotle’s broader philosophy supplies that account. Matter functions as what is potentially many forms, while form determines what the thing actually is.

This gives Aristotle a more comprehensive response to Plato’s opposition argument. Plato asks how the living can come from the dead and how opposites can succeed one another. Aristotle distinguishes the kinds of coming to be. In accidental change, an enduring substance acquires a contrary accident. In substantial generation, underlying matter or a prior subject becomes the matter of a new substance. The two processes are not treated as identical. By distinguishing alteration from generation and destruction, Aristotle avoids the need for a single undifferentiated principle governing all opposites.

## Numerical Identity and the Underlying Substratum

Both Plato and Aristotle recognize that change requires some account of what remains. Yet they identify the underlying unity differently.

For Plato, the Forms provide the stable reality in relation to which sensible things are understood. A beautiful object changes, decays, or ceases to be beautiful, but Beauty itself remains. The Form functions as a stable measure. This is powerful if the primary concern is the objectivity of knowledge. The changing particular does not exhaust the meaning of beauty; it is intelligible because it participates in something stable beyond itself.

However, the Form is not obviously the substratum of the particular’s change. Beauty does not remain in the beautiful object as a material or substantial bearer when the object changes. If the particular becomes ugly, the Form of Beauty remains unaffected, but the theory must explain what in the particular accounts for the continuity of the object. Participation connects the particular to the Form, but the Form does not seem to be the internal subject that undergoes alteration. Plato therefore explains the persistence of the standard more clearly than the persistence of the changing individual.

Aristotle, by contrast, locates the substratum in the individual substance for accidental change and in matter for substantial change. The same human being remains while becoming taller, darker, healthier, or less knowledgeable. The underlying subject is not a Form existing separately from the particular; it is the concrete entity whose properties are modified. This makes the numerical unity of the changing thing an internal feature of the ontology.

The distinction between universal and particular is also important here. Aristotle does not deny that there are forms or essences in some sense. But the form of a human being is not a separately existing individual alongside Socrates. It is the actuality or organizing principle of the living individual. The universal “human” can be predicated of many individuals, but it does not exist as a separate substance in the way Plato’s Form of Humanity would. This prevents an infinite regress in which one needs another Form to explain the resemblance between a particular and the Form.

Aristotle’s substratum is not simply an empty receptacle. Substance is already a determinate kind of being. Socrates is not bare matter waiting to receive any arbitrary quality. His nature sets limits on what changes are possible. A human being can become taller, healthier, or more learned, but cannot become a literal triangle while remaining the same human being in the same respect. The subject persists through changes that are compatible with its nature. In the case of substantial change, matter’s potentiality is actualized into a new substantial form, so the numerical identity of the earlier substance is not preserved in exactly the same way.

This differentiation is a major strength. Plato’s account tends to place the stable element outside the changing particular, whereas Aristotle distinguishes several levels of persistence: the individual substance through accidental change, and matter or material continuity through substantial change. Not every kind of change requires the same substratum or preserves identity in the same manner.

## Does Aristotle Avoid Plato’s Metaphysical Burdens?

Aristotle’s framework avoids several difficulties associated with Plato, but it does not eliminate metaphysical problems altogether.

The first Platonic burden concerns participation. How can one Form be present in many particulars? How can a Form remain itself while being participated in by changing things? If participation is treated as literal presence, the Form seems divisible or multiply located. If it is treated as resemblance, the basis of resemblance may require another Form. Aristotle avoids this problem by refusing to make separately existing Forms the immediate bearers of particular properties. The property belongs to the substance as an accident, and the substantial form is internal to the thing rather than externally participated in.

The second burden concerns the causal efficacy of Forms. Plato presents Forms as causes of intelligibility and, in some passages, as causes of things being what they are. But the exact mode of causation is unclear. The Form of Beauty does not appear to push, generate, or transform a beautiful object in the manner of an efficient cause. Aristotle’s account distributes causal roles more precisely. Form explains what a thing is; matter explains what it is made of and its potentiality; efficient causes explain processes; and final causes explain directedness. A growing organism changes through the action of biological and environmental causes in accordance with its nature. No transcendent Form must enter the organism to account for the alteration.

The third burden concerns the relation between permanence and change. Plato preserves permanence by placing it in the Forms, but the connection between permanence and the changing individual remains external. Aristotle preserves permanence within the changing substance. This enables him to explain not merely why there are stable standards of judgment, but why a single object can undergo alteration without ceasing to be the object it is.

The fourth burden concerns opposite properties. Plato’s insistence that Forms do not admit contraries protects them from contradiction, but it also risks making the Forms too remote from the phenomena they are meant to explain. If the Form of Tallness is never itself altered, then it does not directly explain the transition by which a person becomes taller. The particular’s changing participation in Forms becomes the unexplained intermediary. Aristotle’s classification of opposites makes the intermediary explicit: a substance receives contrary accidents, and the transition is the actualization of a potential.

Yet Aristotle’s solution introduces its own burden: potentiality can appear merely to rename the problem. To say that a child is potentially taller does not by itself explain the biological mechanisms of growth. A potential may seem like a latent property invoked whenever an actual property appears. Aristotle’s answer is that potentiality is not an arbitrary hidden actuality but a structured capacity grounded in the nature of the subject. The child’s organism has powers ordered toward development, and those powers operate under appropriate conditions. Still, the metaphysical status of potentiality remains difficult. Is it a real feature of things, a modal truth about what they can do, or a conceptual description of an ordered process?

There is also a difficulty concerning the underlying substratum. If matter is pure potentiality, it may seem too indeterminate to explain anything. If it is already determinate, then the theory must explain how it can underlie different substantial forms. Aristotle’s hylomorphic account addresses this by distinguishing kinds of matter and levels of form, but the relation between matter and form is not simple. In particular, the *Categories* does not itself provide the complete apparatus for explaining substantial generation and destruction. The more robust account depends on Aristotle’s later works.

Nor does Aristotle entirely escape the issue of universals. His forms are immanent rather than transcendent, but the intelligibility of a common nature shared by many individuals still requires explanation. What makes Socrates and Callias both human? Aristotle’s answer appeals to a common form or essence instantiated in each, but the relation between one universal nature and many individuals remains philosophically challenging. Aristotle avoids Plato’s separate Forms, yet he does not make the problem of universality disappear.

Finally, Aristotle’s distinction between substance and accident may be clearer for ordinary alteration than for all cases of change. A growing organism changes not only in an accidental dimension but through an internally organized development that affects its capacities and structures. The simple model of a fixed substance receiving changing predicates can therefore be inadequate if taken too rigidly. Aristotle’s broader biological and metaphysical writings complicate the picture by treating the living organism as a dynamic unity whose development expresses its form.

## Comparative Assessment

The comparison is clearest if Plato and Aristotle are seen as answering somewhat different philosophical needs. Plato’s theory of Forms responds powerfully to the instability of sensible experience. It explains why knowledge seeks universal and unchanging objects, why sensible things can be judged imperfect, and why predicates such as beauty, equality, and justice appear to have objective significance. Recollection dramatizes the distance between sensory encounters and intelligible understanding. Participation expresses the dependence of particulars upon stable intelligible realities.

But these achievements come at a cost. Plato’s Forms are so stable that they cannot themselves undergo the changes needed to explain the changing world. The theory must therefore posit a shifting relation between Forms and particulars. Yet participation is not sufficiently analyzed to show how that relation works. The individual’s transition from one state to another is described as acquiring or losing participation, but the theory does not clearly identify the subject that persists or the process that produces the new state. Recollection explains how the soul recognizes Forms, not how bodies change.

Aristotle’s account is more modest in one respect and more systematic in another. He does not begin by separating intelligible essences into a transcendent realm. Instead, he analyzes the ontological structure of the things we encounter and the ways in which predicates belong to them. Primary substances are the fundamental subjects; accidents depend on them; qualities can be contrary; relations can generate apparently opposite descriptions; privations explain the loss of capacities; and contradictories must be distinguished from contrary positive states.

This classification directly improves the analysis of the growing individual. The same person is the primary substance. Height is an accidental determination. The person is potentially taller and later actually taller. The contrary states do not belong to the same subject simultaneously in the same respect, but they can succeed one another in the same subject. The explanation is therefore neither that Tallness itself changes nor that the person mysteriously shifts between separate transcendent Forms. It is that an enduring substance actualizes a capacity for a new accidental state.

Aristotle’s account is especially strong because it distinguishes different questions that Plato’s framework tends to combine. One question is what makes a predicate intelligible and objective. Plato’s Forms address this directly. Another is what entity bears the predicate. Aristotle’s substance-accident ontology addresses it. A third is what process connects an earlier state with a later state. Aristotle’s potentiality-actuality distinction addresses it. A fourth is what kind of opposition is involved. Aristotle’s classification addresses that as well.

This does not mean that Plato’s theory is simply refuted. Aristotle inherits the Platonic concern that knowledge requires stable structures and that things are intelligible in virtue of what they are. His immanent forms and essences preserve part of this insight. Nor does Aristotle’s account fully explain every aspect of development merely by naming potentiality. His system depends on a rich conception of nature, form, matter, causality, and teleology. The *Categories* alone is not a complete theory of change. Its account of substance as the receiver of contraries is a foundation, not the whole architecture.

Still, on the specific question of how one particular can transition between opposite states while remaining numerically one, Aristotle is clearly more cogent. Plato protects the unity and immutability of Forms but leaves the unity of the changing particular insufficiently grounded. Aristotle makes the particular substance itself the bearer of change and uses the distinctions between accident, contrary, privation, potentiality, and actuality to explain how the transition occurs. He can therefore preserve both sides of the problem: the subject remains one, and the predicate genuinely changes.

## Conclusion

The *Phaedo* and the *Categories* offer two fundamentally different pictures of reality. Plato places the ultimate grounds of intelligibility in unchanging Forms. Sensible things are intelligible because they participate in these Forms, and the soul can recognize them through recollection. This framework powerfully explains the objectivity of concepts such as equality, beauty, and justice. It also prevents contradiction at the level of Forms by insisting that a Form does not admit its opposite.

However, Plato’s theory is less successful in explaining the mechanics and ontology of change in particulars. Participation does not clearly identify the underlying subject, and recollection concerns knowledge rather than physical or ontological transition. When a particular grows taller, becomes colder, or moves from health to sickness, the language of acquiring or losing participation in Forms describes the result without fully explaining the process. The Forms remain stable, but the changing particular is left between a transcendent standard and an insufficiently analyzed relation to it.

Aristotle’s ontology provides a more precise account. Primary substance is the enduring subject; accidents are properties that exist in and are predicated of that subject; contraries can belong to one substance successively; relatives and contradictories must be distinguished from genuine contraries; and privation explains the loss of a capacity or condition. Potentiality and actuality then explain change as the realization of a determinate capacity in an enduring subject. The individual who grows taller remains numerically one because the same substance persists while its accidental determination is altered.

Aristotle does not remove all metaphysical burdens. Potentiality requires a substantial theory of nature and may appear obscure if detached from the broader Aristotelian system. Matter and form raise difficult questions about substantial change, and immanent universals remain philosophically demanding. Nevertheless, Aristotle avoids the most serious weaknesses of Plato’s treatment of opposite properties. He does not need an equivocal notion of participation to connect changing things with immutable entities, and he does not confuse the stability of an explanatory standard with the persistence of the changing subject itself.

The decisive advantage of Aristotle is therefore methodological as much as doctrinal. He does not treat “opposite” as a single undifferentiated relation. He distinguishes contraries from contradictories, relations from qualities, and privations from positive states. He also distinguishes what a thing is capable of being from what it is actually being. Through these distinctions, Aristotle explains how change is possible without making identity illusory and how predication can vary without collapsing into contradiction. Plato shows why changeable things require stable intelligibility; Aristotle more successfully explains how those changeable things themselves can remain the same while undergoing change.
