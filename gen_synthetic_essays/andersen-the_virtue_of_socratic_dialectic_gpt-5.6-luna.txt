# Socratic Dialectic, the Improvement of the Soul, and the Limits of Definition

The claim that Socratic dialectic is a virtuous activity, and that it improves those who practice it, appears at once plausible and problematic. It is plausible because Socrates presents philosophical conversation as a cure for intellectual arrogance, a discipline of self-examination, and a shared search for truth. Unlike sophistic display, dialectic does not merely seek victory, applause, or the appearance of wisdom. It aims to alter the condition of the soul. Yet the claim is problematic because dialectical conversations often end in aporia rather than knowledge. Socrates’ insistence upon strict definitions can expose confusion without replacing it with a final conceptual account. Moreover, the form of questioning itself does not guarantee moral improvement. A person may use refutation competitively, enjoy exposing another’s ignorance, or imitate Socratic questioning while retaining the very vanity Socrates opposes. The ethical value of dialectic therefore cannot be separated from the intentions, dispositions, and relationships that govern its practice.

This tension is especially clear when one distinguishes two functions of Socratic dialectic: *elenchus* and *psychagogia*. Elenchus is the testing or refutation of claims. It examines whether a person’s beliefs are mutually consistent and whether the person can give an adequate account of what he claims to know. Psychagogia, literally the “leading” or “guiding” of the soul, is the broader educational and ethical activity through which questioning turns the soul away from complacency and toward truth. Elenchus is often the instrument by which psychagogia operates, but the two are not identical. Elenchus may produce embarrassment or aporia; psychagogia seeks to make such experiences beneficial by directing them toward intellectual humility, friendship, and continued inquiry.

The *Euthydemus* dramatizes this distinction by contrasting Socratic conversation with the sophistic eristic performances of Euthydemus and Dionysodorus. The sophists manipulate ambiguity, exploit grammatical and logical equivocation, and treat argument as a contest in which victory matters more than understanding. Socrates’ dialectic, by contrast, requires shared meanings, responsiveness to the interlocutor, recognition of ignorance, and a genuine willingness to learn. Nevertheless, the dialogue also warns that the mere presence of questioning or argument is not enough to make an activity philosophical. Dialectic is virtuous only when its formal practices are integrated with ethical aims. It has an internal tendency toward improvement because it makes contradiction and ignorance visible, but its full normative status depends upon the motivation and character of those who participate in it.

## I. Elenchus and the Moral Meaning of Refutation

The Socratic *elenchus* is commonly understood as a method of refuting an interlocutor’s claim. Socrates asks a person to state what he believes, elicits further commitments from him, and then demonstrates that these commitments conflict with the original claim or with one another. In this formal sense, the elenchus is an examination of consistency. But its importance in Plato is not merely logical. Socrates treats contradiction as a condition of the soul. A person who claims to know courage, justice, piety, or virtue while being unable to explain these concepts is not simply mistaken in an abstract proposition. He is living under the influence of an unexamined self-conception.

Socratic refutation is therefore directed at the difference between seeming to know and actually knowing. The interlocutor often begins with confidence. He believes that he possesses an answer and that the question can be settled quickly. Socrates’ questioning reveals that the answer depends upon assumptions the interlocutor has never considered. The resulting aporia is painful because it is not merely the discovery that one proposition is false. It is the collapse of an image of oneself as knowledgeable. The person discovers that he has been using words whose meaning he cannot adequately articulate.

This is why Socrates can describe refutation as beneficial. The unexamined person is unable to learn because he mistakes his opinions for knowledge. As long as he thinks he already understands justice or courage, he has no reason to investigate them. Refutation removes this obstacle. It is negative in its immediate operation, but potentially positive in its educational effect. By exposing a false confidence, the elenchus creates the intellectual space in which genuine learning becomes possible.

The ethical value of this process is clearest in Socrates’ insistence that it is worse to possess an unjust or corrupt soul than to suffer external harm. Socrates’ commitment to examination is grounded in the conviction that the condition of the soul matters more than reputation, wealth, political success, or bodily comfort. To be refuted may be humiliating, but to remain confused about the principles by which one lives is more dangerous. The elenchus is thus a form of moral therapy. It diagnoses a disorder that ordinary social success tends to conceal.

Yet this therapeutic account must be handled carefully. Refutation does not automatically cure. A medical treatment can be applied with skill or cruelty, and the same is true of dialectical questioning. If the questioner seeks only to dominate, the elenchus becomes an instrument of humiliation. If the interlocutor responds with resentment, he may protect his pride rather than examine his beliefs. Socrates’ method has a transformative potential, but potential is not achievement. The act of being refuted can make someone more humble, or it can make him more defensive and hostile.

This ambiguity is important because the *Euthydemus* presents forms of argument that resemble refutation while lacking its ethical purpose. Euthydemus and Dionysodorus repeatedly force their interlocutors into verbal traps. They ask questions whose terms shift meaning, or whose alternatives are constructed so that every answer can be turned against the respondent. The result may look like victory, but it does not test the interlocutor’s considered beliefs. It tests whether he can escape an artificial verbal snare. Such performances produce defeat without insight. They do not improve the soul because they do not disclose a genuine inconsistency in the person’s understanding. They merely exploit the instability of language.

Socratic elenchus, by contrast, attempts to make the interlocutor say what he really believes. Its force depends upon a kind of consent. Socrates does not merely impose a conclusion from outside; he derives consequences from the interlocutor’s own commitments. This is why the elenchus can be personally revealing. The interlocutor discovers that his own beliefs, when followed carefully, lead somewhere he did not intend. The questioner’s authority is not ultimately the source of the refutation. The source is the conflict within the interlocutor’s own thought.

The elenchus is therefore both logical and ethical. Logically, it seeks consistency and clarity. Ethically, it challenges vanity and invites responsibility for one’s own beliefs. It does not improve a person simply by supplying information. It improves him, when it does, by changing his relation to his own opinions. A person who can no longer treat inherited or fashionable claims as self-evident has acquired a more reflective form of agency.

## II. Psychagogia: Leading the Soul Rather Than Merely Defeating an Opponent

If elenchus is the testing function of Socratic dialectic, psychagogia names its formative direction. The term is associated especially with Plato’s account in the *Phaedrus*, where genuine rhetoric is described as the art of leading souls through speech. Philosophical dialectic can be understood as a distinct kind of psychagogia because it does not merely move an audience emotionally or persuade it to adopt a conclusion. It attempts to lead the soul toward an ordered understanding of what is true and good.

The difference between elenchus and psychagogia may be expressed as the difference between clearing away an obstacle and directing a journey. Elenchus removes false confidence, contradiction, and conceptual confusion. Psychagogia asks what the soul should become once these obstacles have been exposed. It includes the cultivation of intellectual desire, patience, courage, and friendship. It is not satisfied with showing that a person does not know. It seeks to awaken a desire to know and to shape the manner in which inquiry is pursued.

This broader function helps explain why Socrates often presents conversation as a shared activity. In a genuinely dialectical exchange, the questioner is not simply a judge who stands outside the inquiry. Socrates frequently insists that he too is searching, or at least that the truth matters more than his personal prestige. Even when this claim is partly ironic, it captures a central feature of the Socratic posture: the conversation is oriented toward an object that exceeds the participants. The interlocutors are companions in relation to the question.

Friendship is therefore not an incidental emotional benefit of dialectic. It is connected to the structure of inquiry itself. A person cannot genuinely investigate a question with another if he treats the other merely as an enemy to defeat. Shared inquiry requires a willingness to listen, to restate another’s position fairly, and to allow one’s own commitments to be examined. The conversational partner is not simply the target of a test but a participant in the pursuit of a common good.

The *Euthydemus* contrasts this relational dimension with the sophists’ combative style. Euthydemus and Dionysodorus do not appear interested in what their interlocutors mean or in whether a conclusion contributes to wisdom. Their arguments are designed as spectacles. The audience is invited to admire their ability to make any answer appear foolish. Their victory consists in controlling the exchange. The interlocutor’s confusion is not a shared problem but evidence of the sophists’ superiority.

In such a context, words cease to function as instruments of mutual understanding. They become weapons. The sophists exploit the fact that a single word may carry several meanings, or that an ordinary expression may be interpreted in a technical way. Their method depends upon refusing the stable, context-sensitive meanings that ordinary conversation requires. If someone asks whether a person who learns becomes different, the sophist may shift between senses of “becoming” or “learning” in order to construct an absurd conclusion. The point is not to clarify the concept but to demonstrate mastery over verbal ambiguity.

Psychagogia excludes this orientation because it treats the soul as something to be educated rather than conquered. Education is not simply the production of assent. A person can be persuaded without understanding and defeated without learning. To lead a soul well is to bring it into an active relation with reasons, distinctions, and the good. The person must come to see why a claim is true or why an opinion is inadequate. Persuasion that bypasses this activity may alter behavior temporarily, but it does not constitute philosophical formation.

The distinction also clarifies why Socrates’ ironic manner should not be confused with sophistic mockery. Socratic irony may expose pretension, but its deeper purpose is to loosen the interlocutor’s attachment to a false self-image. Sophistic mockery seeks the audience’s laughter and the respondent’s humiliation. Socratic irony, at its best, opens a path toward self-knowledge. It can be severe, but its severity is justified by concern for the soul. The relevant question is not whether the exchange contains wit or embarrassment, but what relation to truth the exchange establishes.

## III. The Demand for Strict Definitions and the Boundaries of Meaning

Socrates’ insistence that concepts require strict definitions is one of the most recognizable features of his practice. He does not accept a list of examples when he asks what justice or courage is. He does not accept an account that describes what something does without explaining what it is. He presses the interlocutor to identify the common feature that makes all instances instances of the same thing.

This demand has a double significance. On one level, it is a search for conceptual precision. On another, it reveals the boundaries of meaning. To ask for a definition is to ask what a word includes and excludes, what it can coherently be predicated of, and what distinctions it presupposes. The demand for definition therefore does not merely seek a dictionary formula. It tests whether a concept has determinate content.

A person may use the word “courage” successfully in ordinary circumstances while lacking a stable conception of courage. He may call a soldier courageous because the soldier stands his ground, but also call a reckless person courageous because he acts without fear. He may then claim that courage is knowledge, or that it is endurance, without examining whether these accounts apply to both cases or whether they conflict with other judgments. Socratic questioning reveals that the word is not self-interpreting. Its ordinary use contains tensions that require reflection.

The boundaries of meaning become visible through counterexamples and distinctions. If courage is simply endurance, then foolish endurance may count as courage. If justice is helping friends and harming enemies, one must ask whether harming anyone can be just, or whether one’s friends are always correctly identified. If piety is what the gods love, one must determine whether the gods love something because it is pious or whether it is pious because they love it. Each question exposes a limit in the proposed account. The point is not arbitrary skepticism. It is to show that the meaning of a concept cannot be secured by a vague association or a single familiar case.

Nevertheless, Socrates’ demand for definition does not guarantee that a final definition will be achieved. Indeed, many Socratic conversations end with the interlocutors unable to state what they had initially presumed to know. The inquiry appears to move from confidence to confusion. This is not necessarily a failure of the method. Aporia can be the proper result when the available conceptual resources are inadequate. The conversation may establish what a concept cannot mean without yet establishing what it does mean.

This point is philosophically significant. There is a temptation to judge the Socratic dialogues by a model of inquiry according to which the only successful conclusion is a completed definition. On that model, a dialogue that ends in aporia has failed to deliver knowledge. But Socratic inquiry may have a different standard of success. It can succeed by transforming an unexamined certainty into a conscious problem. The transition from false knowledge to recognized ignorance is not knowledge in the full sense, but it is an epistemic and ethical improvement.

Aporia is therefore not simply an empty state. It has structure. Through questioning, the interlocutor may learn that certain proposed definitions are too broad, too narrow, circular, inconsistent, or dependent upon an unexamined distinction. He may discover that the concept is connected to other concepts in ways he had not noticed. The final definition is absent, but the field of possible definitions has been narrowed. The limits of meaning have become more visible.

This is why the Socratic conversation can be productive without being conclusive. It produces self-knowledge about the condition of one’s understanding. The person learns not merely that he lacks an answer, but why his former answers were inadequate. He also learns what would be required of a better answer: universality rather than example, coherence rather than assertion, and explanatory power rather than verbal familiarity.

The absence of final definitions can also be understood as a safeguard against premature closure. If Socrates accepted the first plausible formulation, he would turn inquiry into dogmatism. The continued demand for “what it is” preserves the openness of the question. A concept such as virtue may be too fundamental to define independently of an account of knowledge, desire, the good, and the soul. To demand a definition can reveal that a seemingly simple question implicates an entire philosophical structure.

Still, one should not romanticize aporia. Endless dissatisfaction with definitions can become an evasion of knowledge. If every account is rejected merely because it is imperfect, dialectic can become sterile. Socrates’ practice is defensible only if aporia is a stage within an orientation toward truth, not the final celebration of uncertainty. The point of discovering the boundaries of meaning is to make better inquiry possible. Aporia is educational when it awakens the need for further thought; it is merely negative when it becomes a performance of superiority.

## IV. The Sophistry of the *Euthydemus*

The *Euthydemus* offers one of Plato’s most vivid representations of sophistic eristic. Euthydemus and Dionysodorus present themselves as teachers of excellence, but their actual practice consists in defeating anyone who speaks with them. They claim to be able to make a person wise, and they treat their capacity to produce verbal paradoxes as evidence of intellectual power. Their activity is not governed by the question of what is true or what benefits the soul. It is governed by the question of how to win.

The difference between eristic and dialectic is not merely that one is bad and the other good while both employ arguments. The difference concerns the purpose and structure of argument. Eristic treats the interlocutor as an opponent whose defeat is the goal. Dialectic treats him as a partner whose understanding matters. Eristic exploits the instability of language in order to make the respondent appear inconsistent. Dialectic examines language in order to determine whether the respondent’s beliefs can stand together. Eristic seeks the audience’s admiration. Dialectic seeks a shared orientation toward truth.

The sophists’ manipulation of equivocation illustrates this contrast. They may use a word in one sense in the question and another sense in the conclusion, relying on the respondent’s ordinary understanding to generate a contradiction. In a genuine philosophical examination, ambiguity is a problem to be clarified. In eristic, ambiguity is a resource to be concealed and exploited. The sophist does not want the interlocutor to notice that the meaning has shifted, because the shift is what creates the appearance of victory.

This practice undermines the possibility of learning. Learning requires that the student understand what is being asserted and why it is being asserted. But if terms are continually shifted, no stable object of understanding remains. The respondent may be silenced, but he has not been educated. The sophists’ arguments produce the experience of defeat without the acquisition of a reasoned insight.

The issue of friendship further separates sophistry from dialectic. In the *Euthydemus*, the sophists’ performances threaten the social bond between interlocutors because they turn conversation into rivalry. The respondent is not encouraged to trust that his partner is helping him investigate a common issue. He must instead guard against traps. Such an environment makes intellectual generosity irrational. To interpret an argument charitably is to risk being defeated by one’s own charity.

Socratic conversation requires a different kind of trust. This does not mean that Socrates avoids conflict or always speaks gently. He may challenge, embarrass, and persist. But his persistence is directed toward the issue rather than toward the destruction of the person. The questioner and respondent must be able to distinguish the refutation of a claim from the condemnation of the claimant. Their friendship is compatible with disagreement because the argument is understood as a shared instrument.

The recognition of ignorance is another decisive difference. Socrates’ avowal of ignorance is not a claim that he knows nothing whatsoever. It is a refusal to pretend to possess the wisdom he lacks. This humility makes learning possible. The person who knows that he does not know can ask, listen, revise, and continue. By contrast, the sophists represent themselves as experts in the production of victory. Their authority depends upon never appearing uncertain. The public exposure of ignorance would damage their status, so they transform dialogue into a form in which uncertainty can be imposed upon others but never acknowledged by themselves.

The genuine intent to learn is therefore central. Socrates asks questions because he takes the answer to matter more than his reputation. The sophists ask questions because the question itself is a device for producing defeat. Of course, Socrates’ conversations can contain irony, and Plato’s depiction of the sophists is satirical. But the philosophical distinction remains: dialectic is answerable to the possibility that the questioner may need to change, whereas eristic is structured to ensure that the performer remains victorious.

The *Euthydemus* also suggests that sophistry can imitate dialectic closely enough to deceive observers. Both practices may involve rapid questioning, contradiction, and verbal precision. The difference is not visible merely in the outer form. One must examine the relation to truth, the treatment of the interlocutor, and the ultimate effect on the soul. A person who learns to ask questions without learning humility, charity, or concern for truth has acquired a technique but not dialectical virtue.

## V. Does Dialectic Inherently Make People Better?

The central normative question can now be stated more precisely. Does participation in dialectic itself transform people into better individuals, or does the value of dialectic depend entirely upon external ethical motivations? Neither extreme is satisfactory.

If dialectic inherently made people better, then anyone skilled in refutation would necessarily become just, moderate, and wise. But Plato’s dialogues give no reason to accept this strong claim. Intelligence and argumentative skill can be used for domination. The sophists demonstrate that the ability to manipulate arguments may increase vanity rather than diminish it. Even a person who regularly exposes others’ inconsistencies may become addicted to the role of critic. He may use dialectic to avoid examining his own life. The capacity to ask penetrating questions does not by itself establish the desire to be questioned.

Nor does dialectic guarantee friendship. Conversation can be formally dialogical while remaining psychologically adversarial. A person may engage in inquiry in order to display superiority, recruit followers, or secure political influence. If the participant’s motive is competitive, dialectic can intensify the vice of competitiveness by giving it an intellectual form. The mere fact that an argument is structured as questions and answers does not make it ethical.

On the other hand, it would be too strong to say that the normative value of dialectic depends entirely upon motivations external to its practice. The structure of genuine dialectic exerts pressure toward certain virtues. To participate seriously, one must allow claims to be tested, distinguish appearance from reason, tolerate uncertainty, and attend to what another person actually says. These practices cultivate habits that are ethically significant. Dialectic does not simply receive its moral value from an intention imposed from outside; it contains internal norms that shape the character of anyone who consistently honors them.

For example, intellectual honesty is built into the elenchus. A participant must report his own commitments accurately rather than alter them whenever a difficulty appears. Responsiveness is also internal to the practice. One must answer the question asked rather than replace it with a more convenient question. Patience is required because conceptual clarification often proceeds slowly. Humility follows from the repeated discovery that one’s first formulations are inadequate. These are not external decorations attached to argument. They are conditions of dialectical success.

Yet internal pressure is not compulsion. The norms can be acknowledged hypocritically or obeyed selectively. A sophist may imitate responsiveness while designing questions to trap the respondent. A debater may concede minor points only to create a stronger rhetorical position. Someone may profess ignorance as a pose while privately remaining certain of his superiority. The practices of dialectic can be mimicked without their ethical spirit.

The best conclusion is therefore that dialectic has an intrinsic *educative tendency* but not an automatic moral effect. It is normatively structured toward improvement, but actual improvement depends upon the participant’s willingness to inhabit its norms. External motivations matter, but “external” should not mean arbitrary or unrelated. The relevant motivations are precisely those that dialectic itself invites: concern for truth, care for the soul, willingness to acknowledge ignorance, and respect for the interlocutor as a fellow seeker.

This position also clarifies the relation between method and virtue. Dialectic is not a virtue in the same straightforward sense as justice or courage. It is a practice that can cultivate virtues when guided by the right conception of its purpose. Its moral status resembles that of education, rhetoric, or political deliberation. These activities possess characteristic goods and standards, but they can be corrupted. The ability to speak persuasively may serve truth or manipulation; the ability to reason may serve wisdom or vanity. What matters is not only what the activity can produce but what kind of agent it forms through repeated use.

Socratic dialectic nevertheless differs from sophistry because its central aspiration is self-transcendence. The dialectician must remain open to the possibility that the inquiry will transform his own view. The sophist’s activity is self-protective: it preserves the performer’s superiority by ensuring that every exchange ends in the other person’s defeat. Dialectic exposes the self to the risk of being wrong. That risk is ethically important because it loosens the attachment to honor and power that prevents moral learning.

## VI. Aporia, Continued Inquiry, and the Possibility of Moral Progress

The fact that Socratic conversations often end without final definitions might seem to weaken the claim that they improve participants. If the conversation does not establish what justice or virtue is, how can it make anyone more just or virtuous? The answer is that moral improvement may begin with a transformation in the conditions of judgment rather than with the possession of a complete theory.

A person who has undergone elenchus may no longer trust his spontaneous reactions. He may recognize that he has used moral language without understanding the principles that justify it. This recognition can produce a more careful relation to action. He may hesitate before condemning another, examine the grounds of his praise, or ask whether an apparent benefit is genuinely good. Such changes do not constitute complete virtue, but they are movements away from moral complacency.

Socratic moral psychology often treats ignorance as a central source of wrongdoing. If people do what seems good to them, then distorted judgments about the good lead them into harmful action. Aporia interrupts this distortion by making the inadequacy of one’s judgment visible. It may not yet provide the correct judgment, but it weakens the confidence with which the false judgment was held.

There is also a social dimension to this moral progress. The recognition of ignorance can make cooperation possible. People who assume that they already know what is best are unlikely to deliberate seriously with others. People who recognize the limits of their understanding can listen and revise. In this sense, aporia is not merely an individual psychological state. It can support a form of civic friendship in which participants seek common understanding rather than impose private certainty.

Still, aporia must be directed toward inquiry. A person who concludes that nothing can be known has not achieved Socratic wisdom. Socrates’ ignorance is active and disciplined. He does not use the absence of final definitions as an excuse to abandon questions. He continues to ask what virtue is, how knowledge relates to action, and what kind of life is worth living. His ignorance is a commitment to intellectual responsibility, not a retreat from it.

Psychagogia names this direction. The soul is not merely emptied of false beliefs; it is turned toward a different object of desire. The person begins to want understanding more than victory. He learns to value the process by which beliefs are tested. He may also come to see that the good of the conversation is not exhausted by obtaining a conclusion. The participants are being trained in how to think and how to live together.

This helps explain why friendship is philosophically significant. Friendship in dialectic is not simply pleasant companionship. It expresses a shared orientation toward the good. The friends agree that neither personal prestige nor immediate victory is the highest value. They are willing to let the question govern them. In this sense, dialectical friendship is a political and moral alternative to the competitive ethos embodied by the sophists.

## VII. Final Assessment

Socratic dialectic should be regarded as a virtuous activity, but only under a qualified interpretation of “virtuous.” It is not virtuous because every instance of questioning automatically improves its participants. Nor is it virtuous merely because it sometimes produces correct conclusions. Its virtue lies in the orientation and discipline it embodies: the testing of beliefs, the refusal of false certainty, the search for stable meanings, the willingness to expose oneself to refutation, and the treatment of the interlocutor as a companion in inquiry.

Elenchus performs the critical work. It reveals contradictions, tests definitions, and brings hidden assumptions into view. Its immediate result is often aporia, but aporia can be an achievement when it replaces imaginary knowledge with conscious ignorance. The demand for strict definitions shows that ordinary language is not transparent to itself. Concepts have boundaries, implications, and relationships that must be examined. Socrates may not produce final definitions because the inquiry often reaches the limits of the interlocutors’ conceptual resources. Yet he produces something philosophically valuable: a clearer awareness of what has not been understood and what a genuine account would require.

Psychagogia gives this critical process its ethical direction. It leads the soul away from vanity and toward truth, from rivalry toward friendship, and from the desire to appear wise toward the willingness to learn. Without psychagogia, elenchus can become merely destructive. Without elenchus, psychagogia can become vague persuasion or moral exhortation without intellectual accountability. The two functions belong together, but they should not be collapsed. Refutation is not yet education; education requires that refutation be integrated into a transformation of desire and character.

The *Euthydemus* makes this distinction especially vivid. Euthydemus and Dionysodorus possess argumentative ingenuity, but their eristic does not cultivate friendship, recognize their own ignorance, or seek understanding. Their manipulation of equivocation demonstrates that verbal victory can coexist with intellectual and moral emptiness. Socratic dialectic differs not simply by being more logically valid, but by being answerable to a shared search in which the questioner and respondent can both be changed.

The final resolution, then, is neither that dialectic inherently makes every practitioner better nor that its value depends entirely upon arbitrary external intentions. Dialectic has an internal ethical orientation and a built-in capacity to cultivate intellectual virtues. It disciplines participants to give reasons, face contradiction, and recognize the limits of their understanding. But this capacity must be activated by the right motivations. A person who uses dialectic to humiliate, dominate, or display superiority can reproduce sophistry under a Socratic vocabulary. The practice improves people only when they accept its deepest demand: that truth matters more than victory and that the condition of one’s soul matters more than the appearance of wisdom.

Socratic dialectic is therefore best understood as a morally promising but not morally automatic practice. It is virtuous because it makes self-correction possible, encourages friendship through shared inquiry, and turns the recognition of ignorance into a reason to continue learning. Its practitioners become better not simply by arguing, but by allowing argument to govern them. The decisive transformation occurs when the participant ceases to regard dialectic as a weapon possessed by the self and begins to experience it as a discipline through which the self can be examined, reordered, and led toward the good.
