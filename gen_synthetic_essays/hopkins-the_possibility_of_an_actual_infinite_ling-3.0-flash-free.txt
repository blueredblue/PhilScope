**The Limits of Intuition: Evaluating William Lane Craig’s Arguments Against the Actual Infinite and the Viability of LDS Eternalism**

The question of whether the actual infinite can exist in reality has profound implications for our understanding of time, cosmology, and the nature of existence. William Lane Craig, a prominent analytic philosopher and Christian theologian, has built a significant portion of his philosophical career around the argument that the actual infinite is metaphysically impossible. Central to his case is the Kalam Cosmological Argument, which posits that the universe began to exist because an actual infinite cannot exist in reality. Craig employs a series of *reductio ad absurdum* arguments—most notably involving Hilbert’s Hotel, the Principle of Correspondence, and the Infinite Library—to demonstrate that the actual infinite generates logical absurdities. If Craig succeeds in proving the logical impossibility of the actual infinite, then beginningless time and eternalist cosmologies, such as Latter-day Saint (LDS) eternalism, would be rendered philosophically untenable. This essay evaluates whether Craig’s arguments successfully demonstrate a genuine logical impossibility or merely reflect the counter-intuitive nature of Cantorian set theory, and it examines the capacity of LDS eternalism to accommodate an actual infinite despite objections regarding arithmetic operations and successive addition.

**I. Craig’s Triad of Absurdities: The Reductio Ad Absurdum Against the Actual Infinite**

Craig’s philosophical assault on the actual infinite rests on the premise that if a concept entails logical absurdities or contradictions, it cannot refer to anything real. He deploys three primary thought experiments to illustrate the bizarre consequences of granting ontological status to the actual infinite.

The first argument centers on the **Principle of Correspondence**, which Craig uses to highlight the Galilean paradox and Cantor’s transfinite set theory. In finite arithmetic, the whole is strictly greater than its part; a proper subset of a set always contains fewer elements than the set itself. However, Cantor demonstrated that for an actual infinite set, a proper subset can be put into a one-to-one correspondence with the set itself. Craig points to this as a metaphysical absurdity, arguing that if an infinite hotel is fully occupied, the set of all occupied rooms is identical in cardinality to the set of even-numbered rooms. In the finite realm, a part cannot equal the whole; to assert otherwise violates the fundamental axioms of finite quantity. For Craig, the fact that Cantorian set theory accepts this "part-whole identity" as a mathematical truth reveals a fatal flaw in the concept of the actual infinite when applied to reality.

The second argument is the famous **Hilbert’s Hotel**. Imagine a hotel with an infinite number of rooms, all of which are occupied. If a new guest arrives, the hotel manager can accommodate them by shifting the guest in room 1 to room 2, the guest in room 2 to room 3, and so on, ad infinitum. The hotel remains fully occupied despite having accommodated an additional guest. Furthermore, if an infinite number of new guests arrive, the manager can shift each current guest from room *n* to room *2n*, leaving all the odd-numbered rooms—which constitute an infinite set—vacant. Craig argues that these scenarios are metaphysically absurd. An actually infinite number of objects cannot be fully accommodated, nor can the addition of finite or infinite quantities to an already complete infinite set result in a set of the same size. The absurdity lies in the violation of the finite arithmetic intuition that addition increases quantity and that a fully occupied space cannot accept more occupants.

The third argument involves the **Infinite Library**. Consider a library containing an infinite number of books. If all the even-numbered books are removed, the library still contains an infinite number of books. If one book is removed, the library still contains an infinite number of books. Craig uses this to highlight the paradoxical nature of arithmetic operations on actual infinites. In finite arithmetic, subtraction yields a definite, smaller number. But if we subtract an infinite number of books from an infinite library, we get the same infinity; if we subtract one book from an infinite library, we get the same infinity. This, Craig argues, renders arithmetic operations on actual infinites meaningless and absurd. If the actual infinite existed in reality, the very act of counting or subtracting objects would yield contradictory, indeterminate results, thereby destroying the coherence of the physical world.

**II. Logical Impossibility or Counter-Intuitive Set Theory?**

The critical question is whether Craig’s *reductio ad absurdum* arguments successfully demonstrate a genuine logical impossibility, or whether they merely reflect the counter-intuitive nature of Cantorian set theory. To answer this, we must distinguish between logical impossibility (a contradiction in terms, such as a square circle) and epistemic or psychological impossibility (something that conflicts with finite human intuition).

Craig’s arguments implicitly rely on the latter. The "absurdities" he identifies—such as a part being equal to the whole, or an occupied hotel accepting more guests—are only absurd if one insists that the laws of finite arithmetic apply universally to all ontological domains. However, Cantorian set theory is not a logically incoherent system; it is a rigorous, axiomatically defined mathematical framework (Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with the Axiom of Choice). Within this formal system, the Principle of Correspondence is not an absurdity but a defining feature of infinite sets. The fact that a proper subset can map one-to-one onto the whole set is not a contradiction; it is the very definition of what it means to be an infinite set (Dedekind-infinity). 

By treating the axioms of finite arithmetic as necessary metaphysical truths rather than contingent conventions for finite sets, Craig commits what might be called the "fallacy of extension." He assumes that because finite arithmetic breaks down when applied to actual infinites, the actual infinite must be metaphysically impossible. Yet, mathematics frequently extends beyond the bounds of finite intuition. The square root of negative numbers is "absurd" to a finite mind, yet it is logically consistent within the complex number system. Similarly, Cantorian transfinite arithmetic is logically consistent. The supposed absurdities of Hilbert’s Hotel are not logical contradictions; they are the counter-intuitive consequences of applying finite intuitions to transfinite mathematics. Because the absurdity is generated by the clash between finite intuition and infinite reality, rather than by an internal contradiction in the concept of the actual infinite, Craig’s *reductio ad absurdum* fails to establish logical impossibility.

**III. The Tension Between Finite Intuition and the Actual Infinite**

The tension between finite human intuition and the properties of an actual infinite is the crux of the philosophical debate. Human beings are finite creatures whose cognitive faculties evolved to navigate a macroscopic, finite world. Our intuitions about quantity, space, and time are inherently finitary. We intuitively understand that if I add one apple to a pile of ten apples, I have eleven apples. We intuitively understand that a part is smaller than the whole. 

Craig leverages this cognitive limitation to argue that the actual infinite is impossible in reality. However, this argument is vulnerable to a profound counterpoint: the fact that a concept violates our finite intuitions does not entail that it violates the laws of logic. If the actual infinite *were* to exist in reality, we should expect it to defy our finite intuitions, precisely because it is infinite. The demand that the actual infinite behave according to the rules of finite arithmetic is akin to demanding that a three-dimensional object behave according to the rules of two-dimensional geometry. It is a category mistake. 

Furthermore, if Craig’s argument is that an actual infinite *in reality* would produce paradoxes, we must ask: paradoxes for whom? The paradoxes of Hilbert’s Hotel are only paradoxical to an observer operating within a finite, localized frame of reference. From a God’s-eye view—or from the perspective of an omniscient being—the operations of shifting guests or removing books are perfectly coherent. The hotel is never "overfull" because the set of guests and the set of rooms share the same cardinality, which is an infinite cardinality. The hotel is simply infinite. Craig’s *reductio ad absurdum* ultimately collapses into an appeal to human cognitive limitations, revealing that the actual infinite is counter-intuitive, not logically impossible.

**IV. LDS Eternalism and the Accommodation of the Actual Infinite**

LDS eternalism posits that the universe—and the intelligences that inhabit it—have no temporal beginning. In LDS cosmology, as articulated in the King Follett discourse and the Book of Abraham, God did not create the universe *ex nihilo*; rather, He organized existing, uncreated matter and eternal intelligences into the worlds we know. Time, in this framework, is beginningless and endless. This stands in direct opposition to the Kalam Cosmological Argument’s conclusion that the universe must have a temporal beginning, and it places LDS eternalism squarely in the crosshairs of Craig’s objections to the actual infinite.

Craig’s objections to beginningless time generally rely on the impossibility of traversing an actual infinite series of past events (the "traversal problem") and the incoherence of performing arithmetic operations on an infinite past. However, when examined closely, LDS eternalism can consistently accommodate an actual infinite, provided one reorients from an arithmetic ontology to a structural and relational one.

**Successive Addition and the Traversal Problem**

One of the primary objections to beginningless time is the traversal problem: how could an infinite series of past events be "completed" or traversed to reach the present moment? If the past is beginningless, there is no first event; the series of past events is an actual infinite. Yet, we find ourselves at the present, seemingly having "traversed" this infinite series. Craig argues that an actual infinite cannot be formed by successive addition, and since the past is the result of successive addition (one moment after another), the past cannot be actually infinite.

LDS eternalism, however, aligns naturally with the B-theory of time (eternalism), which posits that past, present, and future are equally real. In an eternalist block universe, the past is not a series of events that were sequentially added to reach the present; rather, the entire temporal block exists simultaneously in its eternal constitution. The "traversal" of time is an illusion of consciousness moving through a pre-existing, infinite temporal continuum. Because the past is not a process of accumulation but an ontological given, the successive addition objection dissolves. There is no need to "complete" an infinite series to reach the present; the present is simply one coordinate within an eternal, beginningless manifold. 

**Arithmetic Operations on an Infinite Past**

Craig’s objections regarding arithmetic operations on an actual infinite also fail to pose an insurmountable threat to LDS eternalism. Craig argues that if the past is infinite, subtracting a finite number of past events should yield a different infinity, yet intuitively, an infinite past minus a finite number of years remains an infinite past. This, he claims, makes arithmetic on an infinite past incoherent.

LDS theology, however, does not conceptualize time as a mathematical set of discrete, countable events subject to subtraction. In LDS cosmology, time is the dimension in which God’s creations and the progression of intelligences occur. The "infinity" of the past is not a mathematical quantity (an aleph-null or a transfinite cardinal) that can be manipulated by arithmetic; it is an ontological reality of uncreated, continuous existence. The intelligences that inhabit the universe are co-eternal with God; they have always existed, and they will always exist. Their existence is not a number to be added or subtracted, but an eternal, unbroken continuity of being. 

Moreover, LDS doctrine explicitly accommodates the infinite in its understanding of God and the eternities. God is infinite in His intelligence, His power, and His duration. The "absurdities" Craig identifies in Hilbert’s Hotel—such as an infinite hotel being able to accommodate more guests—only seem absurd if we treat the hotel as a bounded physical object governed by finite spatial constraints. LDS cosmology, with its expansive view of eternal worlds and the limitless progression of intelligences, does not conceive of reality as a finite structure trapped in a mathematical paradox. Instead, it envisions a universe where the infinite is sustained by the eternal power of God, not bound by the rigid, discrete structures of finite set theory.

**The Coherence of the Uncreated**

Ultimately, Craig’s arguments assume that the actual infinite must be a completed, discrete totality that can be subjected to the operations of finite arithmetic. But LDS eternalism posits an actual infinite that is continuous, dynamic, and sustained by divine will. The uncreated intelligences and the uncreated matter of the universe constitute an actual infinite that is not a "collection" in the sense of a set of discrete objects, but an eternal, flowing reality. In this framework, the "paradoxes" of the infinite library or the infinite hotel are resolved because the infinite is not a static, countable set, but an eternally unfolding, living continuum. 

**Conclusion**

William Lane Craig’s philosophical arguments against the actual infinite—while rhetorically compelling and intuitively forceful—ultimately fail to demonstrate the logical impossibility of beginningless time or LDS eternalism. His *reductio ad absurdum* arguments involving the Principle of Correspondence, Hilbert’s Hotel, and the Infinite Library rely on a conflation of logical impossibility with counter-intuitiveness. The "absurdities" Craig identifies are not contradictions in logic; they are the necessary consequences of applying finite arithmetic axioms to transfinite ontologies. Cantorian set theory demonstrates that the actual infinite is mathematically coherent, and Craig’s reliance on finite intuition to judge metaphysical possibility commits the genetic fallacy.

LDS eternalism, with its commitment to beginningless time, co-eternal intelligences, and the organization of uncreated matter, can consistently accommodate an actual infinite. By rejecting the assumption that the past must be traversed by successive addition and by conceptualizing the infinite not as a countable set subject to finite arithmetic but as an ontological continuum sustained by divine power, LDS cosmology sidesteps Craig’s objections. The tension between finite human intuition and the properties of an actual infinite is precisely what we should expect when finite minds attempt to grasp the infinite. Craig’s arguments prove that the actual infinite is *counter-intuitive*, but they do not prove that it is *impossible*. In the eternal, uncreated reality posited by LDS doctrine, the actual infinite is not a logical absurdity, but the very ground of unending existence.
