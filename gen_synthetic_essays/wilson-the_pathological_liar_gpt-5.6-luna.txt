# Pathology, Exclusion, and the Liar’s Paradox

Richard Kirkham’s discussion of the liar’s paradox is valuable because it places the paradox within a broader methodological question: what should count as an acceptable solution to a philosophical problem about truth? It is not enough merely to devise a formal system in which the liar does not generate contradiction. A proposed solution must also explain why the paradox arises, preserve as much of ordinary reasoning as possible, and avoid replacing one difficulty with another. Kirkham therefore evaluates solutions by reference to several criteria, including completeness, consistency, specificity, non-ad hocness, and the avoidance of overkill. These criteria are plausible when applied to an ordinary semantic theory. Yet they become considerably less decisive once the liar is classified as a pathological construction of natural language rather than as an ordinary, truth-valued sentence whose truth conditions have somehow been overlooked.

The central thesis of this essay is that the liar should be treated as a pathological case. It is structurally analogous, not identical, to division by zero in arithmetic or to the pathological behavior of the Weierstrass function in analysis. In each case, a familiar operation or concept is extended beyond the domain in which it functions coherently. The resulting expression is not an ordinary counterexample to the theory governing ordinary cases. Rather, it marks a boundary of applicability. Once this diagnosis is accepted, two of Kirkham’s criteria—specificity and the avoidance of ad hoc postulations—must be qualified. A solution that simply excludes self-referential contradictions may appear insufficiently specific because it does not provide a complete semantic analysis of every syntactically well-formed sentence. It may also appear ad hoc because it introduces a restriction precisely where the paradox occurs. But those objections lose much of their force if the restriction is understood as a principled domain restriction rather than as an arbitrary patch.

An exclusionary approach, on this view, denies that certain self-referential contradictions receive truth values at all. It does not assign them the value false, and it does not assign them both truth and falsity. Instead, it treats them as semantically defective or outside the proper domain of truth attribution. This approach can satisfy completeness in the relevant philosophical sense, avoid overkill, and preserve the bivalent intuitions that govern ordinary discourse. It does not require a comprehensive formal system capable of assigning a semantic status to every possible expression of natural language. The aim of a theory of truth need not be to domesticate every pathological construction. It may instead be to articulate the conditions under which the ordinary concept of truth legitimately applies.

## Kirkham’s criteria and the problem of pathology

A theory of truth is assessed not only by whether it blocks a contradiction but also by how well it performs several competing theoretical tasks. The criteria at issue can be stated in the following way.

First, a solution should be **consistent**. It should not reproduce the contradiction it was designed to resolve, nor should it make truth and falsity collapse into one another. A theory that entails both that a sentence is true and that it is false has not solved the liar merely by redescribing the problem.

Second, it should be **complete**, at least in an appropriate sense. It should tell us what semantic status belongs to the relevant sentences and should not leave large portions of the language unexplained. A solution that handles only one carefully selected version of the liar may fail to explain related cases involving strengthened liars, truth-tellers, falsity-tellers, or semantic predicates embedded in more complex contexts.

Third, the theory should be **specific**. It should identify exactly which sentences are affected and why. “Some sentences are exceptional” is not enough. A satisfactory account should distinguish pathological constructions from merely unusual but meaningful sentences.

Fourth, it should avoid **ad hoc postulations**. The theory should not introduce a special rule solely to save itself from a single counterexample. Restrictions must be motivated by general semantic or logical principles rather than by the accidental shape of one paradox.

Finally, it should avoid **overkill**. A solution should not eliminate more semantic phenomena than necessary. In particular, it should not sacrifice ordinary truth, falsity, inference, and communication simply because certain self-referential constructions cause trouble.

These criteria pull in different directions. Completeness may favor assigning some status to every sentence, whereas avoidance of overkill favors preserving ordinary bivalence. Specificity may demand a precise boundary between acceptable and pathological cases, whereas non-ad hocness may resist a boundary that seems constructed solely in response to the liar. A theory that assigns a third value to all sentences involving semantic vocabulary may be specific and formally elegant, but it may overgenerate semantic indeterminacy. A theory that retains classical bivalence everywhere may preserve ordinary intuitions, but it may be inconsistent in the presence of the liar. The philosophical question is therefore not simply which criterion is correct. It is how the criteria should be interpreted once the liar is regarded as a failure of applicability rather than as an ordinary semantic datum.

## The liar as a pathological case

The liar sentence is commonly represented as:

> This sentence is false.

If the sentence is true, then what it says is the case, and it is false. If it is false, then what it says is not the case, and it is true. The familiar contradiction arises from the interaction of self-reference, negation, and a semantic predicate. The sentence does not merely happen to be difficult to evaluate. Its evaluation is part of the mechanism by which it refers to itself.

The pathological diagnosis begins by distinguishing grammatical well-formedness from semantic legitimacy. Natural language permits the construction of expressions that are syntactically intelligible but semantically defective. This distinction is not unusual. A sentence can be grammatical while failing to express a determinate proposition, as in “The present king of France is bald” under certain theories of presupposition, or “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously,” which is syntactically well formed but semantically anomalous. The liar is more extreme: it is grammatically meaningful enough to generate an apparent argument, but its semantic role defeats the conditions necessary for truth attribution.

The mathematical analogies clarify this point. Division by zero is not ordinarily treated as a counterexample to arithmetic. Arithmetic does not fail because the expression \(1/0\) lacks an ordinary value. Rather, division is defined only under conditions that exclude a zero divisor. The expression is syntactically formable in some notation, but the operation is not defined there. One may construct alternative mathematical systems in which additional objects—such as points at infinity—are introduced, but these extensions do not show that ordinary division was defective. They show that a new theory has been created with new purposes and new rules.

Similarly, the Weierstrass function is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere. It is “pathological” relative to the intuitive picture that continuous functions should generally possess tangents or behave smoothly. But the function is not a refutation of the definition of continuity. It reveals that an intuitive association between continuity and differentiability was never a theorem. The pathological example marks the limits of an informal expectation.

The liar plays a comparable role in semantics. Ordinary truth attribution presupposes that a sentence has a determinate content whose truth does not depend on a viciously circular evaluation of that very sentence. The liar violates this presupposition. It should therefore not automatically be treated as an ordinary falsehood, an ordinary truth, or even an ordinary proposition with an unusual truth condition. Its pathology consists precisely in the fact that the truth predicate cannot be applied to it without generating a contradiction.

This diagnosis does not establish, by itself, that exclusion is the only possible solution. Mathematical pathologies can sometimes be accommodated by extending a domain, and semantic theories can introduce partial, many-valued, or hierarchical truth predicates. But the analogy establishes a methodological possibility: not every expression produced by a permissive grammar must fall within the intended domain of a semantic operation. The demand that every syntactically generated expression receive a truth value may be as misguided as the demand that every division expression receive a numerical result.

## Why specificity loses its force

The criterion of specificity initially appears to pose a serious problem for exclusion. If one says merely that “liar-like sentences lack truth values,” one must explain what counts as liar-like. Otherwise, the theory risks being vague. Does the restriction apply only to the sentence “This sentence is false”? Does it apply to all self-referential sentences? What about indirect self-reference, semantic cycles involving two sentences, or sentences that refer to their own truth through several layers of quotation and negation?

These are legitimate questions. An exclusionary theory should not simply use “pathology” as a label for whatever cases embarrass it. It must offer a principled account of the defective class. Nevertheless, the demand for maximal specificity cannot be treated as absolute. The existence of borderline cases does not entail that there is no genuine distinction between normal and pathological expressions.

Consider division by zero. The rule excluding zero denominators is precise in ordinary arithmetic, but the underlying philosophical point does not depend on a comprehensive classification of every possible generalized division operation. Mathematics can distinguish ordinary division from extensions involving projective points, complex infinities, or partial algebras. No single theory must settle every possible use of the symbol “/” across all mathematical contexts. Likewise, a semantic theory can identify a core pathological structure—self-reference through a negated truth predicate—without classifying every imaginable semantic construction in natural language.

The demand for complete specificity may also be self-defeating in the context of natural language. Natural language is not a formal calculus with a fixed and exhaustively stated grammar. It contains indexicals, quotation, metalinguistic uses, context sensitivity, implicit reference, pragmatic enrichment, and indefinitely many ways of embedding one expression within another. A proposal that promises to classify every possible self-referential construction may thereby commit itself to a formal reconstruction of natural language far more ambitious than the original problem requires.

The relevant kind of specificity is therefore not exhaustive enumeration but structural specification. An exclusionary account should say what feature makes a sentence pathological: for example, a sentence is excluded when its truth evaluation requires the sentence, directly or through a semantic cycle, to receive a truth value that would make the evaluation fail. This will not settle every edge case immediately, but it identifies a general mechanism. It is comparable to saying that division is undefined when its denominator is zero or that a function is pathological relative to a property because it satisfies one formal condition while violating an associated intuitive expectation.

There is also an important difference between semantic indeterminacy and theoretical ignorance. A theory can fail to classify a sentence because the theorist has not yet done enough work, or because the sentence has no truth value. Exclusion claims the latter in the pathological cases. It does not leave the liar unresolved in the same way that an incomplete scientific theory leaves an empirical phenomenon unexplained. It explains the liar by denying that the relevant semantic question has a legitimate answer.

Thus, specificity should require a principled characterization of the excluded domain, not a complete catalogue of all expressions that fall within it. Once pathological constructions are recognized as boundary cases, the absence of a total classification is not automatically a defect. The theory’s task is to identify the semantic condition under which truth attribution breaks down, not to provide a truth value for every expression that language users can mechanically construct.

## Why non-ad hocness must be understood comparatively

The objection from ad hocness is closely related. Exclusion can seem like a maneuver designed after the fact: the liar produces a contradiction, so the theorist declares it meaningless or truth-valueless. Such a response may appear no better than changing the rules whenever a counterexample arises.

The force of this objection depends on what counts as an ad hoc postulation. A restriction is ad hoc when it lacks independent motivation and is tailored narrowly to preserve a favored conclusion. But not every restriction introduced in response to a problem is ad hoc. Scientific and mathematical theories often discover that their concepts have domains of application. The clarification may be prompted by a troublesome case without being arbitrary.

The exclusion of division by zero is not ad hoc because it follows from the structure of multiplication and inverses. If \(a/b\) is understood as the number \(c\) such that \(b \cdot c=a\), then division by zero cannot operate ordinarily: \(0 \cdot c=0\) for every \(c\), so no unique \(c\) solves \(0\cdot c=a\) when \(a\neq 0\). The restriction is a consequence of the role division is supposed to play. It is not an arbitrary exception to protect arithmetic.

An analogous motivation is available in the semantic case. The truth predicate is ordinarily used to endorse or reject propositions. It presupposes a determinate proposition and a distinction between the proposition’s being true and its being false. A sentence that says of itself that it is false undermines both roles. If it is treated as true, its content defeats that assignment; if treated as false, its content reinstates it. The exclusion is therefore motivated by the function of truth attribution itself. We do not exclude the liar merely because it is inconvenient; we exclude it because applying the truth predicate to it destroys the contrast that gives the predicate its point.

Moreover, exclusion may be less ad hoc than many positive alternatives. A three-valued theory, for example, assigns the liar a third status such as indeterminate. This can be elegant, but it requires a substantive account of what the third value means, how it behaves under negation and conjunction, and whether it is preserved under semantic embedding. A paraconsistent theory may assign both truth and falsity, but then it must explain why contradiction does not trivialize inference and why ordinary speakers should regard the liar as both true and false. A hierarchy of languages may block self-reference by restricting the truth predicate to lower levels, but it introduces a considerable formal architecture that may not reflect the actual organization of natural language.

These theories are not necessarily ad hoc. They can be independently motivated by logical or semantic aims. The point is comparative: exclusion is not uniquely guilty of stipulation. Every solution must impose some constraint on the relation between language and truth. The question is whether the constraint follows from a general account of semantic applicability. If truth is a partial operation, then refusing to apply it to a self-referential contradiction is no more ad hoc than refusing to divide by zero.

The exclusionary theorist should, however, avoid pretending that no theoretical choice is involved. The precise boundary between acceptable and pathological self-reference may require refinement. The important claim is not that exclusion is effortless or final. It is that the need for refinement does not make it arbitrary. A theory can be principled without being fully formalized.

## Completeness without universal truth valuation

The criterion of completeness is often interpreted as requiring a truth value for every sentence. On that interpretation, exclusion appears to fail by definition. If the liar is syntactically a sentence but receives neither truth nor falsity, then the theory is incomplete.

This interpretation should be rejected or at least distinguished from a more defensible one. There are two notions of completeness relevant here. **Absolute semantic completeness** would require that every grammatically well-formed expression receive a truth value or some fully specified semantic status. **Domain-relative completeness** requires that the theory completely account for every sentence within the domain to which its central concepts properly apply. An exclusionary theory can satisfy the second while rejecting the first.

The distinction is familiar elsewhere. Ordinary arithmetic is complete with respect to its intended operations without assigning a natural number to every string of symbols. The expression \(1/0\) is not a missing answer within arithmetic; it lies outside the operation’s ordinary domain. A theory of differentiation can be complete with respect to differentiable functions without assigning a derivative to every continuous function. The Weierstrass function does not expose an incompleteness in the definition of derivative. It shows that continuity does not guarantee differentiability.

Likewise, a theory of truth can be complete with respect to ordinary truth-apt sentences while excluding pathological semantic constructions. It explains which normal sentences are true, which are false, and why the liar is not a legitimate candidate for either status. This is not semantic silence. It is a classification.

One might object that the liar is not like \(1/0\), since it is a sentence of natural language and appears to express a proposition. But syntactic form alone cannot settle semantic domain membership. Natural language allows category mistakes and defective presuppositions. A sentence can be grammatically complete without being truth-apt. The exclusionary account treats the liar as a semantic failure generated by the interaction of otherwise legitimate resources. Its grammatical status does not force its inclusion in the domain of truth.

There is also a practical reason not to demand absolute completeness. Natural language is open-ended, and semantic phenomena can be embedded without limit. If every expression must receive a formally defined status, then a theory of truth must become a comprehensive theory of quotation, reference, context, indexicals, vagueness, modality, presupposition, and recursion. That is an enormous project. The liar does not show that such a project is required. It shows only that unrestricted semantic self-reference creates a special problem.

Completeness, then, should be assessed relative to explanatory purpose. The exclusionary approach is complete if it gives a general reason why ordinary bivalent truth applies where it does and why it fails at the pathological boundary. It need not construct a total semantic algebra for every possible string in natural language.

## Avoiding overkill

The exclusionary approach is particularly strong with respect to the criterion of avoiding overkill. Many proposed solutions to the liar respond to a local pathology by altering the semantic behavior of a very broad range of sentences.

A global three-valued logic, for instance, may assign a third value not only to the liar but also to sentences that contain or depend upon it. Depending on the system, conjunctions, disjunctions, conditionals, and quantificational constructions can inherit indeterminacy. This may be formally manageable, but it can produce a large departure from ordinary semantic practice. Sentences that appear plainly true or false may become indeterminate because of remote connections to a pathological expression.

A hierarchical theory of truth can avoid contradiction by distinguishing object-language truth from metalanguage truth, but the price may be a proliferation of levels. Natural language, however, routinely uses “true” across different contexts without explicitly marking a hierarchy. The theory may preserve consistency by idealizing away from the semantic flexibility that makes natural language useful.

A paraconsistent approach avoids triviality by allowing contradictions, but it risks overkill in another direction. It changes the inferential status of contradiction throughout the logical system. The liar becomes both true and false, and the theory must then explain why ordinary contradictions do not behave in the classical way. That may be an acceptable revisionist project, but it does not preserve the ordinary conceptual distinction between truth and falsity as directly as exclusion does.

Exclusion alters less. It leaves ordinary sentences bivalent: a statement such as “Snow is white” is true if things are as it says; “Snow is not white” is false in the relevant circumstances. It leaves ordinary negation, conjunction, implication, and inference intact. It does not make every sentence containing a semantic term suspect. Only those constructions whose semantic evaluation is circular in the relevant pathological manner are denied truth values.

This is why exclusion is not the same as a general endorsement of semantic gaps. It does not hold that natural language is broadly non-bivalent. It holds that bivalence applies within the ordinary domain, while certain constructions fail to enter that domain. The distinction resembles the difference between saying that arithmetic has no numbers and saying that a particular operation is undefined for a particular input. The latter is a local restriction, not a wholesale abandonment of the theory.

Overkill can also arise from attempts to eliminate every possible version of the liar by imposing restrictions on all self-reference. But exclusion need not prohibit self-reference as such. “This sentence is written in English” may be self-referential but harmless. “This sentence is true” may generate a truth-teller that is stable under truth assignment. The relevant defect is not self-reference alone but self-reference combined with a contradictory semantic condition. A properly formulated exclusionary view can therefore preserve benign forms of self-reference while blocking the pathological form.

## Preserving bivalent intuitions

The strongest intuitive motivation for exclusion is that ordinary truth appears bivalent. Statements are generally treated as true or false, not as possessing a third semantic value. This intuition is not infallible. Vagueness, future contingents, presupposition failure, and fictional discourse may all complicate it. Nevertheless, bivalence remains a central organizing ideal of ordinary assertion and classical reasoning.

Exclusion preserves this ideal more successfully than many alternatives. It says that for every ordinary truth-apt proposition, exactly one of truth and falsity applies. The liar is not a counterexample to bivalence because it is not an ordinary truth-apt proposition. This is a familiar strategy in philosophy: preserve a principle within its proper domain by denying that a purported counterexample belongs to that domain.

The distinction between “false” and “not true” is crucial. If the liar is excluded, then it is not false in the ordinary sense. Nor is it true. The theory must resist the temptation to infer that anything lacking truth is therefore false. That inference is valid only under the assumption that all relevant expressions are truth-apt and bivalent. The exclusionary account denies that assumption for the pathological class while preserving it elsewhere.

This may appear to introduce a third status after all. But a semantic gap is not necessarily a third truth value. A third value is typically an element of the theory’s valuation space, with specified logical behavior. A gap is a failure of the valuation function to apply. The difference matters. If the liar is assigned an indeterminate value, then indeterminacy becomes part of what the sentence semantically expresses or possesses. If the liar is excluded, there is no additional kind of truth-status; there is only the failure of the truth predicate to find a legitimate object of application.

The exclusionary position therefore preserves bivalent intuitions without claiming that every linguistic item must be either true or false. It preserves them by limiting their scope. This is not an evasion but a standard philosophical strategy for reconciling a powerful principle with exceptional cases.

## The objection that exclusion is too easy

A significant objection remains. If the theorist may simply declare troublesome sentences outside the domain of truth, then perhaps every paradox can be “solved” by exclusion. The method might be too easy to be explanatory. It could be used to avoid any counterexample to any theory.

The response is that exclusion is legitimate only when the excluded case exhibits a principled failure of the conditions required by the concept under analysis. One cannot exclude any sentence that conflicts with a theory. The liar is special because its semantic evaluation is constitutively self-defeating. A sentence about an ordinary object that conflicts with a theory of truth cannot be excluded merely because it is inconvenient. It is a normal truth-apt sentence and must be evaluated. The pathological status must be established through structural features of reference and semantic predication, not through mere theoretical unpleasantness.

This also shows why the mathematical analogies are useful but limited. Division by zero is excluded because the operation’s defining conditions fail. The Weierstrass function is pathological relative to differentiability because it satisfies continuity while defeating a commonly assumed implication. In both cases, the exclusion or classification is based on independently intelligible structures. The liar likewise requires an account of why self-referential negation prevents ordinary evaluation.

The method is therefore not “exclude whatever is difficult.” It is “recognize when the operation being applied has ceased to be defined.” That recognition can be philosophically substantive even if it does not yield a complete formal calculus.

## Natural language and the limits of formalization

A further advantage of the exclusionary approach is that it does not require a comprehensive formal system for natural language. Formal systems are indispensable for clarifying particular mechanisms, and a formal model of partial truth may be useful. But the philosophical diagnosis need not wait upon a total formalization of natural language.

Natural language is not simply an untyped formal language with a recursively specified semantic interpretation. It contains speakers, contexts, intentions, quotation practices, and flexible shifts between use and mention. The liar exploits precisely the resources that make natural language expressive: it allows a sentence to refer to linguistic items, to use semantic vocabulary, and to embed such expressions within themselves. A demand for a complete formal solution may therefore be misplaced. It risks treating the openness of natural language as a defect rather than as a fact about the subject matter.

The exclusionary account can be developed at several levels. At the intuitive level, it says that the liar is semantically defective because it attempts to assign a truth value to itself in a way that makes either assignment fail. At a more formal level, one can use partial valuations, fixed-point constructions, or restricted truth predicates to model the distinction between ordinary truth and pathological self-reference. But no particular formalism is required for the basic philosophical claim.

Indeed, a comprehensive formal system might obscure the central point. If the system assigns a carefully engineered status to every expression, one may mistake formal totality for semantic insight. The question is not whether a mathematician can construct an algebra in which every token has a value. The question is whether ordinary truth is correctly represented. A formal extension that treats the liar as a third value, as both values, or as a hierarchy-dependent object may be theoretically fruitful, but it should not be assumed to describe the ordinary concept of truth simply because it is total.

## Conclusion

Kirkham’s five criteria provide a valuable framework for evaluating solutions to the liar’s paradox. Consistency is indispensable, completeness is a real explanatory virtue, specificity guards against vague diagnosis, non-ad hocness protects theory from arbitrary exceptions, and avoidance of overkill preserves the ordinary phenomena with which a theory began. Yet these criteria cannot be applied without first deciding what kind of case the liar represents.

If the liar is treated as an ordinary truth-apt sentence, then exclusion seems to fail. It does not assign a value, it appears insufficiently specific, and its restriction may look like an ad hoc response. But if the liar is classified as a pathological construction, analogous to division by zero or to a function that violates an intuitive but nonnecessary association in analysis, the assessment changes. The liar is not an ordinary sentence that reveals a missing truth value. It is a construction that falls outside the conditions under which truth attribution functions coherently.

On this diagnosis, specificity requires a principled account of the pathological structure, not an exhaustive inventory of every possible expression in natural language. Non-ad hocness requires independent motivation for the domain restriction, not the absence of all theoretical decisions. Exclusion is motivated by the role of truth itself: a truth predicate must distinguish truth from falsity, and self-referential contradictions prevent either status from being stably assigned. Completeness can be understood relative to the proper domain of truth-apt discourse, while overkill is avoided because ordinary sentences remain bivalent and ordinary reasoning remains intact.

The exclusionary approach therefore denies truth values to self-referential contradictions without abandoning bivalence. It does not say that all language is semantically indeterminate, nor does it introduce a new universal truth value. It says that some constructions are malformed with respect to truth, just as division by zero is malformed with respect to ordinary division. This approach may not provide a comprehensive formal system for every sentence natural language can generate. But that is not a decisive defect. A philosophical theory of truth need not assign a value to every artifact of an unrestricted grammar. It must instead explain the domain in which truth and falsity apply, preserve the structure of ordinary semantic practice, and identify the limits at which those concepts cease to function. The liar is best understood not as a devastating counterexample to bivalence, but as a pathological boundary case revealing where bivalence properly begins and ends.
