### The Sovereign's Dilemma: Beneficence, Right, and the Limits of Kantian State Coercion

Immanuel Kant’s moral philosophy presents a seemingly insurmountable paradox for political thought. On one hand, the Categorical Imperative commands us to treat humanity in ourselves and others always as an end, never merely as a means, suggesting a profound obligation to aid those in need. On the other hand, Kant’s political philosophy, as articulated in the *Metaphysics of Morals*, grounds the state’s legitimacy not in the promotion of happiness or welfare, but in the strict protection of external freedom—the independence of each individual from the arbitrary will of another. This tension erupts into a full-blown dilemma when we consider the state’s coercive power to tax citizens to support the needy. Does the state possess an independent moral duty of beneficence, a juridical obligation to redistribute resources to secure the material conditions of its citizens? Or does such coercion fundamentally violate the very principles of freedom that justify the state’s existence?

This essay will evaluate this dilemma by examining the tension between Allen D. Rosen’s egalitarian interpretation of Kantian justice and the alternative framework defending individual rights, often associated with libertarian readings of Kant. I will argue that while Rosen’s synthesis is philosophically ingenious, it ultimately conflates the *Doctrine of Virtue* (individual moral duty) with the *Doctrine of Right* (juridical external law). A rigorous application of the Categorical Imperative and Kant’s principles of freedom—specifically liberty and equality—reveals that the state does **not** possess an independent moral duty of beneficence. Coercive taxation for the needy cannot be derived as a categorical requirement of Right. However, this does not render all welfare measures illegitimate. Rather, the state may *permissibly* institute welfare programs, but only as a contingent, prudential measure necessary to preserve the juridical order itself, not as a fulfillment of a moral duty to promote the happiness of others. The resolution of this dilemma rests on a correct understanding of the Kantian “united will,” which is not an empirical majority consent but an *a priori* idea of reason that authorizes only laws preserving external freedom. Consequently, the state’s legitimate actions are strictly constrained; welfare is a political choice, not a moral imperative.

#### I. The Architecture of Kant’s Moral and Legal Philosophy

To navigate this problem, one must first grasp the fundamental architecture of Kant’s practical philosophy. The Categorical Imperative (CI) is the supreme principle of morality, tested through its various formulations. The Formula of Universal Law (FUL) demands that we act only on maxims that we can will to become universal laws. The Formula of Humanity (FH) commands us to act so that we treat humanity, whether in our own person or in the person of another, always as an end and never merely as a means. These formulations apply to the *internal* realm of moral motivation—the realm of *Ethics* or the *Doctrine of Virtue*. In this realm, we have perfect and imperfect duties to ourselves and others. Among these is the imperfect duty of beneficence: the duty to promote the happiness of others, not because we are commanded to do so by external law, but because it is a maxim of our own rational will. This duty is inherently non-coercive; one cannot be forced to be virtuous, as virtue requires a free adoption of the moral law as one’s own incentive.

However, Kant meticulously separates this internal realm from the external realm of *Right* (or *Recht*), articulated in the *Doctrine of Right*. Right concerns the external relationship of persons to one another, specifically the compatibility of their free choices under a universal law of external freedom. The Universal Principle of Right (UPR) states: “So act externally that the free use of your choice can coexist with the freedom of everyone according to a universal law.” Crucially, Right does not concern itself with the *ends* that individuals pursue—their happiness, welfare, or perfection—but only with the *form* of their external actions. Coercion is justified in the Doctrine of Right solely to hinder a hindrance to freedom. If A interferes with B’s external freedom, then the state, as the agent of Right, may coerce A to cease such interference. This coercion is not a violation of A’s freedom but a *hinderance to a hinderance of freedom*, thereby upholding the principle of reciprocal freedom.

From this foundation, Kant derives the innate right to freedom: “Freedom (independence from being constrained by another's choice), insofar as it can coexist with the freedom of every other in accordance with a universal law, is the only original right belonging to every man by virtue of his humanity.” This innate right yields two corollaries: *liberty* (the right to pursue one’s own ends without interference, provided they do not violate the rights of others) and *equality* (the right to be independent of being bound by others to more than one can in turn bind them). These principles are not aspirational; they are the *a priori* conditions of any legitimate legal order. The state, therefore, is not a vehicle for moral improvement or material redistribution. Its sole *raison d'être* is to secure the external conditions under which each individual’s freedom can coexist with the freedom of all.

#### II. Rosen’s Egalitarian Kantianism: The State as a Condition of Freedom

Allen D. Rosen, in his influential work *Kant’s Theory of Justice*, challenges this strictly libertarian reading. Rosen argues that the state possesses a positive duty to secure the material conditions necessary for the exercise of external freedom. He contends that Kant’s own principles, when fully developed, entail a right to welfare. Rosen’s argument rests on a specific interpretation of what constitutes “independence.” He argues that a person who lacks basic material resources—food, shelter, healthcare—is not truly independent. Instead, they are subject to the arbitrary will of others, particularly those who control the means of subsistence. A desperate worker who must accept any wage offered, or a starving individual who must beg for charity, is in a state of *de facto* dependence. This dependence undermines their innate right to freedom, as they are effectively coerced by their circumstances into serving the ends of others.

Rosen connects this to the Categorical Imperative, specifically the Formula of Humanity. He argues that if the state fails to provide for the needy, it treats them merely as means to the economic ends of the wealthy, denying them their status as ends in themselves. Furthermore, Rosen invokes Kant’s discussion of the “right of necessity” (*Notrecht*). Kant famously argues that there is no right to steal bread to survive, as this would violate the property rights of another. However, Rosen interprets Kant’s broader framework as suggesting that the state must preemptively address such extreme need to maintain the coherence of the legal order. If a citizen is reduced to a state where they have no legal recourse to sustain their own existence, they are effectively outside the civil condition, which undermines the state’s legitimacy.

Rosen’s most potent argument, however, lies in his reinterpretation of the “united will.” He argues that the united will is not merely a procedural device for legitimizing laws that protect property but is a substantive moral ideal. Since every rational being has a duty of beneficence (from the Doctrine of Virtue), the united will—as the collective will of all rational citizens—must incorporate this duty into the legal framework. Therefore, the state, as the representative of the united will, has a juridical duty to enact laws that provide for the poor. For Rosen, the state’s coercive taxation for welfare is not a violation of freedom but the very condition of freedom for all. It secures the material independence that allows every citizen to participate in the juridical order as an equal. Without this, the poor are relegated to a state of servitude, rendering their nominal legal freedom a mere fiction. Rosen’s interpretation is compelling because it seeks to reconcile the CI’s demand for beneficence with the UPR’s demand for external freedom, suggesting that the latter is impossible without the former.

#### III. The Libertarian Counter-Argument: The Priority of Negative Liberty

Against Rosen, a strict libertarian interpretation of Kant—championed by thinkers like Hans-Hermann Hoppe, Tibor Machan, and certain contemporary Kantian legal scholars—insists that the state’s sole legitimate function is the protection of negative liberty. This view argues that coercive taxation for welfare is a fundamental violation of the UPR and the CI. The libertarian begins with the same premise: the state is justified only to hinder hindrances to freedom. The acquisition of property, through original acquisition and contract, is an exercise of external freedom. When the state taxes one citizen to provide for another, it is forcibly taking the property of A and transferring it to B. This is not a hinderance to a hinderance of freedom; it is a direct interference with A’s legitimate exercise of freedom.

The libertarian also appeals to the CI, but specifically to the Formula of Universal Law. Can the maxim “I will coerce others to provide for the needy” be universalized? If everyone were to adopt this maxim, the distinction between property owner and recipient becomes arbitrary and unstable. The principle would undermine the very concept of property rights, as no one could be secure in their possessions if they were subject to the discretionary claims of others. More importantly, it violates the Formula of Humanity. When the state taxes the wealthy, it uses them merely as a means to the ends of the poor. The wealthy citizen is not acting from a voluntary maxim of beneficence; they are being compelled to serve the purposes of another. This instrumentalization is precisely what the FH prohibits. The wealthy person’s property is an extension of their external freedom, and to seize it for another’s benefit is to treat their personhood as a resource.

Furthermore, the libertarian argues that Rosen conflates *dependence* with *coercion*. A person may be economically dependent on an employer, but this is a result of voluntary contractual relations, not arbitrary state coercion. The state’s role is to enforce the terms of such contracts, not to rewrite them based on subjective assessments of need. The libertarian concedes that a person in extreme poverty is indeed unfortunate, but argues that their misfortune does not create a right against others. Rights protect freedom, not outcomes. To claim a right to welfare is to claim a right to the labor and property of others, which is a violation of their equal right to freedom. The libertarian concludes that the state has no duty of beneficence whatsoever. Individual citizens have a moral duty to help the needy (from the Doctrine of Virtue), but this duty is internal and cannot be externally coerced by the state. The state that attempts to enforce beneficence is not a republic of free equals but a paternalistic despotism.

#### IV. The Constraints of the Categorical Imperative and the Principles of Freedom

The central question, then, is which interpretation more faithfully adheres to Kant’s own texts and the logical structure of his philosophy. To answer this, we must scrutinize how the CI and the principles of equality and liberty constrain state action. The CI, in its juridical application, is not directly a test for state policies but is mediated through the UPR. The UPR does not ask whether a law promotes happiness; it asks whether the law’s *external form* is compatible with the freedom of all. A law that mandates taxation for welfare has a specific external form: it compels A to transfer resources to B. Does this form coexist with the freedom of A?

The principle of *liberty* (independence from another’s choice) is directly violated. A is not free to choose how to dispose of their property; they are subject to the state’s choice regarding its allocation. However, Kant recognizes that all laws restrict liberty to some extent—for example, laws against theft restrict the liberty to steal. The difference lies in the *reciprocity* of the restriction. A law against theft restricts everyone equally; no one may steal. A welfare tax, however, imposes a *non-reciprocal* burden. A is compelled to give, while B is compelled to receive. This is not a universal restriction of freedom but a specific transfer of freedom from one party to another. The principle of *equality*—the right not to be bound by others to more than one can bind them—is thus violated. A is bound to support B, but B is not bound to support A (unless B also falls into poverty, but this is a contingent fact, not a universal law). The law creates a hierarchical relationship between giver and receiver, which undermines the juridical equality of citizens.

Rosen might respond that this inequality is justified because B is in a state of need, and without the transfer, B would be subject to the arbitrary will of A (or other wealthy individuals). However, this response conflates *private dependence* with *legal subjection*. In the state of nature, B’s dependence on A is a matter of private contract or charity. The state can and should regulate such contracts to prevent fraud or coercion. But to argue that the state must *preemptively* redistribute wealth to prevent dependence is to assume that economic inequality itself is a violation of freedom. Kant does not make this assumption. He famously argues that the division of property and the resulting inequality of wealth are permissible as long as they arise from lawful acquisition and exchange. The state’s role is to uphold the legal framework, not to correct the outcomes of that framework.

Moreover, the CI’s Formula of Humanity, when applied to the state, does not generate a duty of beneficence. The FH prohibits treating persons merely as means. Does the state treat the wealthy merely as a means when it taxes them for welfare? If the state’s end is to promote the welfare of the poor, then yes, the wealthy are being used as instruments for that end. However, if the state’s end is to preserve the juridical order itself—to prevent the poor from being forced into a state of nature where they have no rights—then the wealthy are not being used merely as means. They are being subjected to a law that applies to all citizens as a condition of civil society. The question becomes whether the preservation of the juridical order *requires* welfare. This is an empirical and prudential question, not a purely moral one. It is conceivable that a society could maintain its legal order without a welfare state, relying instead on private charity, family support, or economic growth. Therefore, the justification for welfare cannot be grounded in the CI as a categorical duty; it can only be a contingent political judgment.

#### V. The United Will vs. Majority Consent: The Source of Legitimate Law

The resolution of this dilemma hinges on the correct interpretation of the Kantian “united will.” Kant distinguishes sharply between the *volonté générale* (the general will) and the *volonté de tous* (the will of all). The united will is not an empirical aggregation of individual preferences, nor is it the outcome of a democratic vote. It is an *a priori* idea of reason—the idea of a will that legislates only laws that could be consented to by all rational beings under conditions of universal freedom. In the *Doctrine of Right*, Kant states that the civil condition is established by a “united will” that is the ground of all public law. This united will is not something that is *created* by a historical contract; rather, it is a normative standard against which all positive laws are measured. A law is legitimate if it *could* have arisen from the united will, regardless of whether a majority actually voted for it.

This has profound implications for the welfare debate. Rosen argues that the united will *could* consent to welfare laws because every rational being recognizes the duty of beneficence and the need for material security. But this argument is flawed. The united will is not a moral agent that has duties of virtue; it is a juridical agent that has the sole duty of securing external freedom. The united will cannot will a law that violates the external freedom of some of its members, even if that law promotes the welfare of others. If the united will were to consent to a coercive transfer of property, it would be contradicting its own foundational principle, which is the reciprocal protection of freedom. Therefore, the united will cannot authorize a law that treats some citizens as means to the ends of others.

By contrast, popular majority consent is a contingent, empirical mechanism for approximating the united will. A majority may vote for a welfare state, but this does not make the law legitimate. The law is legitimate only if it conforms to the *a priori* principles of Right. If it does not, it is a mere act of force, a violation of the rights of the minority. This is why Kant insists on the separation of the legislative, executive, and judicial powers, and why he argues that the sovereign cannot enact laws that degrade citizens to the status of subjects. A welfare law that compels A to support B is not a law of freedom; it is a law of servitude, regardless of how many people voted for it. The united will is a rational ideal, not a democratic procedure. It authorizes laws that are *universalizable* in the juridical sense—laws that restrict freedom only to the extent necessary to secure freedom for all.
