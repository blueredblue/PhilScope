## The Educated Heart: Reconciling Humean Sentiment with Aristotelian Character

**Abstract:** This essay evaluates David Hume's sentimentalist moral philosophy against the potent objection that sympathy, as its foundational mechanism, lacks internal value judgments and fails to generate universal obligations. I argue that Hume’s account, while correctly identifying affect as the indispensable spring of moral action, leaves morality dangerously unmoored from rational constraint, thereby risking the validation of harmful inclinations such as misguided sympathy toward vengeance. The essay proposes that Aristotle’s concept of habituated character and practical wisdom (*phronesis*) offers a robust corrective. By integrating reason *into* the emotional disposition itself, Aristotle provides the internal normative standard and universalizability that Hume’s pure sentiment lacks, while crucially preserving the motivational force of feeling. The synthesis presented here demonstrates that a hybrid model—sentimentalist motivation disciplined by rational habituation—resolves the vulnerabilities of emotion-based ethics without collapsing into either sterile rationalism or subjective emotivism.

---

### I. Introduction: The Tyranny of the Heart and the Silence of Reason

The history of moral philosophy is often framed as a dialectical struggle between the head and the heart. Rationalists, from Plato to Kant, have argued that moral truth is discovered through the exercise of pure reason, independent of contingent desires or passions. Sentimentalists, most famously David Hume, have countered that reason is inert, incapable of moving us to action, and that morality is ultimately grounded in our emotional responses. Hume’s project is profoundly appealing because it acknowledges the undeniable psychological reality that moral judgments feel like something—they are accompanied by warmth, approbation, or revulsion. Yet, this very appeal conceals a critical vulnerability. If morality is reducible to the sentiments we happen to have, what prevents a society from collectively approving of atrocity? What binds the individual to a moral law they do not feel? And crucially, what stops sympathy—that great engine of moral sentiment—from being hijacked by malicious passions, such as the intoxicating thrill of vengeance?

This essay will argue that Hume’s sentimentalism, for all its psychological insight, fails to provide an internal normative standard. It lacks the capacity to issue value judgments that are *true* or *false*, and it cannot generate obligations that are *universally binding*. The consequence is a moral theory that is dangerously susceptible to validating harmful inclinations. However, this critique does not necessitate a retreat to cold rationalism. Instead, I will propose that Aristotle’s virtue ethics, with its emphasis on habituated character and the intellectual virtue of *phronesis* (practical wisdom), offers the missing rational constraint. Aristotle does not reject the motivational power of emotion; rather, he trains it, shapes it, and integrates it with reason so that the virtuous person *feels* the right way for the right reasons. By incorporating Aristotle’s framework, we can salvage Hume’s crucial insight—that feelings are indispensable for moral action—while securing the objectivity and universality that pure sentiment lacks.

### II. Hume’s Sentimentalist Architecture: Reason as Slave, Sympathy as Conduit

To evaluate the objection, one must first understand the precise mechanics of Hume’s moral psychology. In *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Hume famously declares that “reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions.” This is not a cynical dismissal of reason but a precise division of labor. Reason, for Hume, is the faculty of discovering truth and falsehood—it deals with relations of ideas (mathematics, logic) and matters of fact (empirical causation). Its function is instrumental: it calculates means to ends, but it cannot, by itself, prescribe ends. The ends of human action are set by the passions—desires, aversions, hopes, and fears. A passion is an original existence, a “impression” that propels us toward or away from objects. Without a passion, no rational argument can motivate action. If I desire to drink water, reason can tell me how to get it; but if I have no desire, reason is silent.

Morality, for Hume, is a species of this passionate motivation. Moral distinctions are not derived from reason because they are not discovered by comparing ideas or tracing causal chains. Instead, moral judgments are the product of a particular kind of sentiment—a sentiment of approbation or disapprobation that we feel when contemplating an action or character trait. When we observe an act of benevolence, we feel a warm glow of approval; when we observe an act of cruelty, we feel a cold shudder of disapproval. These feelings are not arbitrary; they are rooted in our human nature, specifically in the mechanism of **sympathy**.

Sympathy, in Hume’s system, is the psychological process by which we communicate the passions of others to ourselves. It is a kind of emotional contagion. When we see another person in pain, we feel a faint echo of that pain. When we see another person joyful, we feel a corresponding pleasure. This capacity for emotional resonance is the bedrock of all social and moral life. It is what allows us to take an interest in the welfare of others, even when our own immediate interests are not at stake. Hume argues that we approve of those character traits that are useful or agreeable to the person themselves or to others, and we disapprove of those that are harmful. Sympathy is the mechanism that makes us sensitive to this utility or harm. We feel pleasure at the thought of a trait that benefits society because we sympathetically share in that benefit.

Hume is aware of the obvious problem of partiality. Our sympathy is naturally strongest for those closest to us—family, friends, neighbors—and weaker for distant strangers. If morality were merely the expression of this natural sympathy, we would approve of our own family’s vices and condemn a stranger’s virtues. To remedy this, Hume introduces the “general point of view.” He argues that moral judgments require us to correct for our natural biases by adopting a stable, impartial perspective. When making a moral judgment, we must consider the influence of a character trait on “those who have intercourse with the person,” not on ourselves. We must set aside our personal connection and sympathize with the *general* sentiments of mankind. This correction, Hume insists, is similar to the way we correct our visual perception of a table that appears smaller at a distance; we learn to judge by its real size, not its apparent size. Likewise, we learn to judge by the general utility of a trait, not its utility to us.

This is a sophisticated account. It grounds morality in human feeling, explains why we care about others, and offers a mechanism for impartiality. Yet, it is precisely at this point that the objection begins to bite. For what is the “general point of view” but *another* sentiment? It is not a rational principle that dictates what *ought* to be approved; it is merely a *calibrated* sentiment—a broader, more inclusive sympathy. The correction is not a logical deduction but a psychological adjustment. And this adjustment is contingent upon our natural disposition to feel sympathy in the first place. If a person lacks sympathy entirely (a psychopath), they will not be moved by the general point of view. If a person’s sympathy is malformed, they may approve of things that are objectively harmful.

### III. The Fatal Flaw: The Absence of Internal Value Judgments and the Specter of Malicious Sympathy

The objection that sympathy lacks internal value judgments strikes at the heart of Hume’s project. A sentiment is a brute fact of experience. It is a feeling, not a proposition. When I feel approval for an act of charity, I am not asserting a judgment that “charity is good” in the sense of corresponding to an objective moral truth. I am merely reporting my psychological state. The sentiment itself contains no standard by which it can be evaluated. It is simply there.

This leads to the famous “is-ought” problem, which Hume himself articulated but which ultimately undermines his own moral theory. From the fact that I *feel* approval, it does not follow that I *ought* to feel approval, nor that anyone else *ought* to feel it. The feeling is not self-justifying. If I feel approval for cruelty, my feeling is just as real and just as psychologically compelling as the feeling of approval for benevolence. The only difference is the object of the feeling. But if morality is reducible to feeling, then the difference between benevolence and cruelty is merely a difference in what people happen to feel. There is no external, rational standard to adjudicate between conflicting sentiments.

Hume might respond that human nature is uniform—that all people, in similar circumstances, will feel similar sentiments. But this is an empirical claim, and it is demonstrably false. People disagree about moral matters, and these disagreements are not always resolvable by appealing to a deeper sentiment. Moreover, the uniformity of human nature does not guarantee the *correctness* of the sentiment. A society that has habituated its members to take pleasure in the suffering of an outgroup will have uniform sentiments of approval for persecution. Sympathy, in this case, would not extend to the outgroup; it would be confined to the ingroup, reinforcing the cruelty.

This brings us to the most devastating consequence of Hume’s account: the validation of harmful inclinations, specifically misguided sympathy toward vengeance. Consider a scenario where a community has been wronged—perhaps a family member has been murdered. The community feels a powerful passion for vengeance. They sympathize with the victim’s family’s rage and grief. They share in the desire for retribution. In Hume’s system, this sympathy would generate approbation for acts of revenge. The avenger who kills the perpetrator would be praised as a hero, a restorer of balance. The sentiment of approval is strong, it is shared, and it is rooted in a natural passion (the desire for retaliation).

Does Hume have any resources to condemn this? He might appeal to utility—perhaps vengeance is socially disruptive and leads to cycles of violence. But this appeal is itself sentimental. The *disutility* of vengeance is felt as a sentiment of disapproval. If the community’s desire for revenge is stronger than their desire for social stability, the sentiment of approval will win out. Hume cannot say that the community is *wrong* in a rational sense; he can only say that they have different sentiments. But this is a weak response. It fails to capture the intuitive truth that vengeance is morally wrong regardless of how strongly it is felt. The objection, therefore, is not merely that Hume fails to provide universal obligations (which he does), but that his theory actively risks *endorsing* malice when the prevailing sentiment is malicious.

Furthermore, Hume’s “general point of view” does not save him. The general point of view is designed to correct for partiality to ourselves, but it does not correct for partiality to our *group*. A community united in vengeance adopts a general point of view—they consider the impact of the act on their own community, and they approve of it. The correction is internal to the group; it does not extend to the perpetrator or to universal humanity. The general point of view is a *psychological* mechanism, not a *normative* one. It tells us to be impartial among the objects of our sympathy, but it does not tell us *which* objects deserve sympathy. It assumes that our sympathy is naturally directed toward the good, but this is precisely what is at issue.

In essence, Hume’s sentimentalism is a form of moral subjectivism—or at best, a form of cultural relativism. It describes how we do make moral judgments, but it fails to justify why we should make them in a particular way. It lacks the normative force of a genuine value judgment. A judgment, in the logical sense, is an assertion that can be true or false. A sentiment is neither. To say “I approve of this” is not the same as saying “This is worthy of approval.” The latter requires a standard that transcends my subjective feeling. Hume’s system collapses the distinction, leaving us with a morality that is descriptive rather than prescriptive.

### IV. The Aristotelian Corrective: Habituation, Phronesis, and the Educated Emotions

If Hume’s sentimentalism is to be salvaged, it must be supplemented with a rational constraint that is *internal* to the moral judgment itself. This is precisely what Aristotle offers. Unlike Hume, Aristotle does not see reason and emotion as separate faculties locked in a zero-sum struggle. Instead, he sees the moral life as the cultivation of a unified character in which reason informs emotion and emotion enriches reason. The key concepts are *hexis* (habituated state) and *phronesis* (practical wisdom).

For Aristotle, virtue (*arete*) is not a feeling that happens to us; it is a settled disposition of the soul to act and feel in accordance with the mean. In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, he argues that moral virtue is acquired through habituation (*ethismos*). We become just by performing just acts, courageous by performing courageous acts, temperate by performing temperate acts. This is not mere behavioral conditioning, however. The habituation is not just about outward behavior; it is about training our emotional responses. A child is taught to take pleasure in noble actions and pain in base ones. Through repetition and guidance, the child’s appetitive and spirited parts of the soul are shaped to respond correctly. The goal is not to suppress emotion but to educate it, so that we *want* to do the right thing.

This habituation is guided by reason, specifically by the intellectual virtue of *phronesis*. *Phronesis* is the ability to deliberate well about what is good and beneficial for a human being in a particular situation. It is not abstract theoretical knowledge (*episteme*) but practical discernment. The *phronimos*—the person of practical wisdom—is able to perceive the morally relevant features of a situation and determine the appropriate course of action. Crucially, *phronesis* is not a detached calculator that overrides emotion. It is the perfected state of the rational part of the soul that works *in conjunction with* the habituated emotions. The virtuous person does not have to fight their desires; their desires are aligned with their reason. They feel anger at the right things, in the right amount, for the right duration, toward the right people.

This integration of reason and emotion resolves the first objection—the lack of internal value judgments. In Aristotle’s framework, the moral judgment is not a bare feeling. It is a complex state that includes a rational appraisal of the situation. The virtuous person’s approval of a courageous act is not just a warm glow; it is a recognition that the act is *truly* courageous, that it hits the mean between cowardice and recklessness. The judgment “this is courageous” is a proposition that can be true or false, and its truth is determined by its correspondence to the reality of the situation, as discerned by *phronesis*. The feeling is the motivational component, but the rational appraisal is the cognitive component. Together, they form a unified moral judgment that has genuine normative content.

Furthermore, Aristotle’s account provides a basis for universal obligation. While Aristotle is not a Kantian—he does not posit a categorical imperative that applies to all rational beings regardless of their desires—he does ground obligation in the human *telos* (function or purpose). Humans, he argues, have a natural function: to live a life of rational activity. The good life (*eudaimonia*) consists in flourishing, which is achieved through the exercise of virtue. Because all humans share this common nature, the virtues are universally applicable. What is good for one human is good for another, assuming they are functioning properly. The obligation to be virtuous is not an external command but an internal necessity of our own nature. We *ought* to be virtuous because that is what it means to be a fully realized human being.

This grounds moral judgments in an objective standard—human flourishing—rather than in subjective sentiment. The *phronimos* is the standard of correctness, not because they are arbitrary, but because they have perfectly developed the rational and emotional capacities that all humans possess. Their judgments are not merely their own feelings; they are the correct application of the universal norms of human excellence.

### V. Resolving the Vengeance Problem: The Role of Character

Let us now return to the problem of vengeance. How does Aristotle’s framework handle the community that approves of retributive murder? The answer lies in the concept of habituated character. A community that has been properly habituated will not feel a strong, unqualified passion for vengeance. They will feel anger, to be sure, but their anger will be *informed* by reason. The *phronimos* would recognize that the murder of the perpetrator is not a virtuous act; it is an act of excessive retaliation. It does not hit the mean. The mean between cowardice and recklessness is courage; the mean between servility and arrogance is proper pride; the mean between pity and spite is righteous indignation.

Righteous indignation is the virtuous response to injustice. It is a feeling of anger at a wrong that has been committed, but it is calibrated to the severity of the wrong and directed toward the restoration of justice, not toward the infliction of pain. The properly habituated person would not sympathize with the avenger’s desire to kill; they would sympathize with the victim’s suffering, but they would also recognize that the response must be proportionate and guided by law and reason. They would feel a sentiment of disapprobation for the act of revenge because it violates the virtue of justice.

This is a crucial difference. Hume’s sympathy is a raw, unreflective mechanism. It transmits the passion of the avenger to the observer, who then feels approbation. Aristotle’s *phronesis* intervenes. It evaluates the passion of the avenger. Is this anger justified? Is it proportionate? Does it promote flourishing? The *phronimos* would see that the avenger’s anger, while natural, is corrupted by excess. It is not the virtuous mean of righteous indignation; it is the vice of spite or malice. Therefore, the *phronimos* would not feel approbation for the act. Their sympathy is *educated*—it is selective and discerning. It does not simply resonate with any strong passion; it resonates only with those passions that are aligned with virtue.

Thus, Aristotle resolves the vulnerability of pure sentiment by making the sentiment itself rational. The value judgment is not external to the feeling; it is constitutive of the feeling. The virtuous person’s approval of justice and disapproval of vengeance are not two separate acts—one cognitive and one affective. They are one unified act of the whole soul. The feeling is the judgment’s motivational force; the judgment is the feeling’s normative content.

---

### VI. Synthesis: Feelings as Indispensable Motivation, Reason as Internal Constraint

The final question is whether Aristotle’s corrective preserves the indispensable motivational component of moral action. Does the integration of reason and emotion sacrifice the motivational power that Hume rightly champions? The answer is a resounding no. In fact, Aristotle’s account *strengthens* motivation.

Hume argued that reason alone cannot motivate action; we need passion. This is true. But Aristotle shows that passion alone cannot guide action; we need reason. The virtuous person is not a passionless automaton. They are intensely passionate. They love justice, they hate cruelty, they feel compassion for the suffering, and they feel righteous anger at the oppressor. These passions are not mere adjuncts to their moral reasoning; they are the very fuel that drives them to act. The difference is that these passions have been *shaped* by reason.

Consider the difference between a person who acts charitably because they feel a fleeting impulse of pity and a person who acts charitably because they have cultivated the virtue of generosity. The former may act inconsistently, may be swayed by countervailing impulses, and may resent the cost. The latter acts reliably, joyfully, and with full conviction. The latter’s feeling is not weaker; it is stronger and more stable because it is integrated with a rational understanding of why generosity is good. Aristotle says that the virtuous person takes pleasure in virtuous action. This pleasure is the motivational component, but it is a *rational* pleasure. It is the pleasure that comes from doing what is truly good, not just what feels good.

Therefore, the hybrid model I am proposing—Humean sentiment constrained by Aristotelian habituation—does not abandon the sentimentalist insight. It refines it. We retain the claim that moral judgments must be felt to be effective. We retain the claim that sympathy is the root of our concern for others. But we add the crucial claim that sympathy must be *educated*. It must be trained by reason to respond to the right objects, in the right way, to the right degree.

This resolves the problem of universal obligation. The obligation is not derived from a feeling but from the rational understanding of human flourishing. Yet, the obligation is *internalized* through feeling. The virtuous person feels the pull of duty because their character has been formed to align their desires with their rational understanding of the good. They do not need an external law to compel them; they are internally compelled by their own well-ordered soul.

---

### VII. Conclusion: The Heart Trained by Reason

Hume’s sentimentalism offers a profound insight: morality is not a cold calculation but a warm engagement of the heart. His emphasis on sympathy captures the deeply social and affective nature of moral life. However, as the objection demonstrates, this insight is incomplete and dangerous. Without an internal rational constraint, sentiment becomes a chameleon—it changes color with whatever passion happens to dominate. It cannot distinguish between the approbation we feel for genuine virtue and the approbation we feel for a satisfying act of vengeance. It cannot issue a value judgment that is true or false, and it cannot bind us to obligations that transcend our contingent feelings.

Aristotle’s virtue ethics provides the missing piece. By focusing on the habituation of character and the cultivation of *phronesis*, Aristotle shows that reason does not have to be an external censor imposed on emotion. Instead, reason can become the internal structure of emotion itself. The virtuous person’s feelings are not irrational; they are finely tuned instruments of moral perception. They feel the right way because they understand the good. This integration resolves the vulnerabilities of pure sentiment: it provides an internal standard for value judgments (the mean determined by *phronesis*), and it grounds obligations in the universal human *telos* of flourishing.

Crucially, this synthesis retains the motivational power of feeling. We do not become moral by suppressing our emotions but by educating them. The heart is not silenced; it is trained. The result is a moral agent who is not torn between reason and passion but who acts with the full force of a unified soul—feeling deeply, reasoning clearly, and acting justly. In this sense, Aristotle completes Hume. He takes the raw material of sympathy and shapes it into the gold of virtue. He takes the passion of the heart and gives it the discipline of the mind. The moral life, therefore, is not a choice between the head and the heart; it is the art of teaching the heart to listen to reason, and reason to speak the language of the heart.
