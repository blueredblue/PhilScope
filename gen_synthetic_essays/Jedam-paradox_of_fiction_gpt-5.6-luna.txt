# Fiction, Emotion, and the Grammar of Belief: Wittgenstein and the Paradox of Fiction

Colin Radford’s paradox of fiction begins with an apparently compelling set of claims. First, when we engage with a fictional narrative, we know that its characters and events do not exist in the ordinary sense. Second, we nevertheless experience genuine emotional responses to those characters and events: we fear Michael Myers, grieve over the death of a fictional character, feel anger toward a villain, or pity someone whose circumstances we know to be invented. Third, emotional responses seem to require belief in the existence of their objects. It would appear irrational, for example, to fear a person one believes does not exist. These claims generate the paradox: how can we be emotionally moved by fictional objects if we do not believe that those objects exist?

One way of diagnosing the paradox is to argue that it rests upon an untenable metaphysical distinction between mind-dependent fictional states of affairs and mind-independent reality. According to this diagnosis, Radford implicitly treats fictional objects as ontologically inferior or unreal while assuming that genuine emotions must be directed toward objects belonging to an independently existing reality. The paradox would then arise only because Radford imposes a narrow theory of emotion and a rigid picture of existence upon practices that do not conform to them. If the distinction between fictional and real objects is metaphysically overburdened, then the problem may seem to disappear.

There is an important insight in this criticism, but it must be formulated carefully. The distinction between fictional and mind-independent reality is not simply incoherent. We ordinarily distinguish between Michael Myers and an actual murderer, between pitying a fictional character and helping an actual person, and between a fictional event and a historical event. The distinction performs indispensable functions in our language-games. What is untenable is not necessarily the distinction itself, but the assumption that it determines whether an emotional response is genuine. Wittgenstein’s use-theory of meaning and his conception of language-games help to dissolve this assumption. They show that words such as “fear,” “pity,” “anger,” “belief,” and “existence” derive their meaning from their roles in particular practices rather than from a single hidden psychological or metaphysical essence. On this account, emotional responses to fictional characters need not involve belief in their literal existence because emotional expressions do not invariably function as theoretical judgments about objects.

Wittgensteinian quietism therefore offers not so much a new theory explaining how we manage to have emotions toward non-existent entities as a dissolution of the demand for such an explanation. To say that a viewer is afraid of Michael Myers in a horror film is ordinarily to describe a rule-governed response within the language-game of fiction, spectatorship, and emotional appraisal. It is not necessarily to attribute to the viewer an irrational belief that Michael Myers is stalking them in the actual world. The response may be real without its object being real in the same sense as an ordinary person. The paradox arises when the grammar of one practice is illicitly projected onto another.

## Radford’s paradox and its presuppositions

Radford’s argument can be reconstructed in a simple form. When someone watches a horror film, that person may become frightened by the monster or killer represented on screen. Yet the viewer knows that the character is fictional. If fear requires belief that its object exists, then the viewer should not be afraid. But the viewer is afraid. Hence either the fear is not genuine, the viewer does believe in the character’s existence despite appearances, or the ordinary theory of emotion is mistaken.

The power of this argument depends on the third premise: that genuine emotional responses require belief in the existence of their objects. In ordinary cases, this premise has considerable plausibility. If I tell you that there is no dog behind you, and you fully believe me, your fear of the dog should disappear. If I am angry with someone, it is natural to assume that I believe that person exists and has done something blameworthy. If I mourn the death of a friend, my grief ordinarily presupposes that the friend was a real person. Emotions seem to be intentional states: they are about or directed toward something. This intentionality appears to create an existential commitment.

Yet the inference from intentionality to existential belief is not straightforward. We can fear a possible catastrophe, regret an event that never occurred, feel shame over an imagined scenario, or become anxious about a fictional possibility. One can be afraid of ghosts while being uncertain whether ghosts exist. A child can fear a monster under the bed without possessing a settled metaphysical commitment about monsters. Even in ordinary cases, emotions are not always based on explicit beliefs. They may be responses to appearances, possibilities, remembered events, bodily sensations, or socially presented situations. The object of an emotion is therefore not necessarily an object affirmed as existing in the same way that a scientific hypothesis is affirmed.

Radford’s paradox depends upon treating emotional intentionality as though it were a form of theoretical representation. On this model, to fear Michael Myers is to stand in a mental relation to Michael Myers, and that relation requires the proposition “Michael Myers exists” to be believed. But emotional concepts do not always work propositionally. “I am afraid,” “That is terrifying,” and “I cannot bear to watch this” may express a reaction rather than report an inner judgment. Even when an emotion is directed toward an object, the object need not be represented under the category of an independently existing entity. The grammar of emotional language is more varied than Radford’s model allows.

This does not mean that fictional emotions are somehow fake. It means that the genuineness of an emotion is not fixed by the ontological status of its object. A viewer can genuinely tremble, avert their eyes, experience an elevated heart rate, feel suspense, and later describe the film as frightening. Those are not counterfeit responses merely because the viewer would not call the fictional killer into the room or seek protection from him in everyday life. The response belongs to a practice in which the fictional representation is treated as a source of fear within the relevant context.

## Is the distinction between fiction and reality metaphysically untenable?

The criticism of Radford often focuses on his apparent division between mind-dependent fictional entities and mind-independent real entities. Fictional characters seem to depend upon human practices: authors create texts, audiences interpret them, and communities preserve the conventions by which characters such as Michael Myers are identified. A fictional character does not exist independently of all such practices. By contrast, actual people and physical events are ordinarily regarded as existing independently of our imagining them. The objection is that if fictional states of affairs are mind-dependent, they are somehow dismissed as less real, even though they can have real effects on our bodies, conduct, and emotional lives.

There is a legitimate concern here. The word “real” has several uses, and the contrast between fictional and real can obscure rather than clarify these uses. Fictional objects are not nothing. They occupy a role within stories, cultural traditions, legal disputes, educational practices, and ordinary conversation. Michael Myers can be recognized, compared with other horror villains, discussed as a cultural icon, and used to explore ideas about violence, trauma, or the nature of evil. A fictional character may influence fashion, film production, audience behavior, and public discourse. In these respects, fictional entities possess a kind of cultural and institutional reality.

Moreover, fictional states of affairs are not simply private images in an individual mind. A character is often constituted by publicly available rules and conventions. What Michael Myers does in a particular film is constrained by the film’s narrative, visual presentation, and genre. Different viewers may disagree about the character, but their disagreements occur against a shared background. Fiction is therefore not reducible to an isolated mental representation. It is a socially organized practice.

Nevertheless, it would be a mistake to conclude that there is no meaningful distinction between fictional and non-fictional existence. The fact that a fictional character is culturally real does not entail that he exists as a human murderer in the world represented by ordinary empirical descriptions. We can ask whether a fictional character appears in a film, but not whether he has a current physical location in the same sense as an actual person. We can say that Michael Myers kills Laurie Strode in the fiction, but we cannot infer that anyone was murdered by him outside the fictional framework. The distinction is not necessarily a metaphysical hierarchy between the “truly real” and the “merely mental.” It is a distinction between different systems of criteria for identification, truth, reference, and verification.

The problem with Radford, then, is not that he recognizes a difference between fiction and reality. The problem is that he treats this difference as though it settled the question of emotional legitimacy. The ontological status of an object and the grammatical conditions under which an emotion is expressed are separate matters. An actual person may be the object of an emotion in one language-game, while a fictional character may be the object of a different but still genuine response in another. To say that Michael Myers is fictional is not to say that fear of him is meaningless. It is to specify the context in which the fear occurs.

A further complication is that “mind-dependent” and “mind-independent” are themselves unstable categories. Human institutions, promises, laws, money, and works of art are dependent upon collective practices, yet they are not therefore unreal or arbitrary. Conversely, our access to mind-independent reality is always mediated by human forms of description and judgment. Wittgenstein would resist the urge to transform these observations into a general metaphysical theory. He would instead ask how these words are used. What do we do when we say that something is fictional? What distinction does that remark draw in a particular situation? What difference does it make whether we say that Michael Myers exists, exists in the film, is portrayed by an actor, or is a character in a franchise? These questions direct attention away from an abstract hierarchy of realities and toward the practical grammar of our expressions.

## Wittgenstein’s use-theory of meaning

Wittgenstein’s later philosophy begins from the idea that the meaning of a word is closely connected with its use in language. This does not mean that words have no stable meanings or that anything can mean anything. It means that meaning is not ordinarily secured by attaching a word to a private mental image or by identifying an abstract essence behind all its uses. The meaning of “game,” “pain,” “belief,” “fear,” or “fiction” is manifested in the practices in which these expressions have a role.

This approach is particularly relevant to the paradox of fiction because the paradox assumes that “fear” has one essential logical structure. If fear is defined as a mental state that represents a dangerous object as existing, then fear of a fictional character becomes impossible or defective. But the word “fear” is used in many contexts. It may describe a survival-oriented reaction to a physical threat, an anxious concern about the future, a response to a disturbing image, a theatrical performance, a childhood fantasy, or a fictional narrative. These uses are related, but they need not share a single set of necessary and sufficient conditions.

The use-theory does not merely multiply definitions. It encourages us to examine what counts as an appropriate application of the word in each situation. In the cinema, if an audience member grips the seat, covers their eyes, and says “Michael Myers is terrifying,” the statement is not ordinarily treated as an empirical assertion that a murderer is present in the theatre. It functions as an expression of the spectator’s response to the film. If the same person runs from the theatre shouting that Michael Myers is behind them, the context changes. We may now ask whether the person is joking, acting, hallucinating, or genuinely confused. The same name and the same word “fear” can participate in different language-games, and the criteria for correctness differ accordingly.

This point allows us to challenge the existential requirement built into Radford’s argument. In some contexts, fear does require a belief in the existence of the feared object. If I claim to fear a dangerous animal in my room, others may reasonably ask whether the animal is actually there. But in the language-game of horror spectatorship, the relevant criteria are different. The viewer’s response is appropriately described as fear because the film presents a threatening character within a structured imaginative engagement. The viewer need not believe that the character exists outside the film. What matters is that the fictional representation plays the role of a threat in the practice.

Wittgenstein’s account thus separates two questions that Radford conflates. The first is whether a character exists independently in the actual world. The second is whether the viewer’s response can properly be called fear. The answer to the first may be negative while the answer to the second remains affirmative. There is no contradiction because the criteria for applying “fear” do not always include the criterion “the object exists independently of the relevant representation.”

## Language-games and the fictional object

The concept of a language-game makes this account more precise. Language is not a single activity with one uniform purpose. It includes describing, questioning, commanding, promising, joking, praying, calculating, telling stories, giving orders, expressing pain, and many other activities. A language-game consists not only of words but also of actions, expectations, rules, social roles, and circumstances.

Engaging with a horror film is a complex language-game. It includes buying a ticket, entering a theatre, recognizing a genre, interpreting images as part of a narrative, temporarily attending to represented events, responding vocally or bodily, discussing the film afterward, and distinguishing what happens in the story from what happens in the theatre. The fictional character is not an object presented in exactly the same way as a person standing in front of us. The character’s presence is constituted by a network of representational practices. Yet within those practices, the character can perform the role of an aggressor, victim, antagonist, or object of sympathy.

The rules of the game do not require the spectator to believe that the represented object exists independently of the game. Indeed, participants understand that a film is a film. The audience’s knowledge that the actor is not actually being murdered does not normally prevent them from responding with shock or sorrow. The film establishes a context in which certain expressions are licensed. “Michael Myers is coming” can be true in the fiction without being true of the theatre. “He is about to kill her” may guide the audience’s attention and emotional response even though it is not a report of an actual event. The viewer’s practical and emotional orientation is therefore governed by fictional rules rather than by an empirical belief in the character’s presence.

This is not the same as pretending in every respect that the fictional character is real. Spectators do not normally attempt to call the police, physically attack the screen, or search the streets for Michael Myers after the film. Their behavior reflects a layered practice: they respond to the fictional danger while remaining aware of the actual situation. The language-game permits emotional involvement without requiring total cognitive deception. It is precisely because the rules are understood that the response can be sustained without ordinary belief.

Here, Wittgenstein helps replace the model of an isolated mind confronting an impossible object with a model of participation in a public practice. The viewer’s emotion is not a mysterious inner event that must be explained by postulating an irrational belief. It is part of an activity with established conventions. The fictional character is emotionally significant because the narrative gives the character a role within that activity.

## Emotional expressions are not always reports of beliefs

A central advantage of the Wittgensteinian approach is that it challenges the assumption that emotional utterances primarily report internal states. Consider the difference between saying “I believe there is a dangerous man in this room” and saying “That scene is terrifying.” The first sentence can function as an empirical claim. It invites investigation and may justify practical action. The second may function as an expression, evaluation, or invitation to attend to the scene in a particular way. It is not normally tested by searching the theatre for Michael Myers.

Wittgenstein’s discussions of pain are instructive here. A cry of pain is not ordinarily a description of a private object called “pain” discovered through introspection. It is part of a human practice that includes seeking help, responding to injury, offering comfort, and learning to use pain language. Emotional expressions have similarly varied roles. Crying at a film, gasping at a jump scare, or saying “I hate him” may manifest an attitude and participate in a social interaction without asserting a metaphysical proposition about the object.

This does not eliminate the distinction between expression and description. People can make false or insincere emotional claims. A person may pretend to be frightened for comic effect, or exaggerate their anger to influence others. But the possibility of insincerity does not show that every genuine emotional expression is a report of a belief. Nor does it follow that an expression has no cognitive content. A viewer who says that Michael Myers is terrifying may be expressing an evaluative response shaped by the character’s actions, the film’s music, camera work, and narrative conventions. The response is meaningful precisely because it is embedded in a practice of interpretation.

The distinction between expression and belief also clarifies why emotional responses to fiction can be genuine even when behavior is constrained. A person who is afraid of Michael Myers in a film may not act as they would if an actual attacker were present. Fear does not always produce avoidance of the same kind or degree. The viewer may remain seated, continue watching, or even purchase a ticket for another horror film. This does not prove that the fear is unreal. It shows that the fear is situated within a context where the practical consequences of the emotion are limited and partly regulated by the rules of spectatorship.

A spectator can therefore have what might be called a genuine but context-bound fear. The fear is genuine because it affects perception, attention, bodily reaction, mood, and speech. It is context-bound because the spectator knows how to use the response within the fictional game and does not treat it as a demand for emergency action in the actual world.

## Quietism and the dissolution of the paradox

Wittgensteinian quietism is often misunderstood as a doctrine that denies philosophy any positive contribution. More accurately, quietism seeks to dissolve philosophical problems produced by misleading pictures of language. Instead of answering the question “How can I fear something I believe does not exist?” by proposing a special mental state, it asks whether the question has been constructed by imposing the wrong grammar upon the case.

The paradox of fiction may be a paradigmatic example. The philosophical picture says that emotions are inner states with propositional contents; that emotional objects must exist; that fictional characters do not exist; and that spectators nevertheless have the same emotions toward them. Once these assumptions are combined, a contradiction appears. But each assumption may be a philosophical generalization from only one portion of ordinary language.

The quietist response is not that there is no difference between a real murderer and a fictional murderer. Nor is it that the spectator secretly believes the fictional murderer exists. It is that the phrase “fear of Michael Myers” has different conditions of use depending on whether it occurs in conversation about a film, a report of a real-world threat, a psychological diagnosis, a theatrical performance, or a joke. The question “But do you really believe he exists?” can be appropriate in some contexts and confused in others. If someone claims to have seen Michael Myers outside the cinema, we may ask for evidence. If someone says that the film made them afraid of him, demanding evidence of his actual existence misunderstands the utterance.

Quietism therefore shifts the burden of explanation. It does not ask how an impossible object enters the mind as the cause of a real emotion. It asks what role the fictional character plays in the circumstances in which the emotion is expressed. Michael Myers is not required to be an independently existing object in order to function as a threatening figure within a horror film. The film’s representational structure, the audience’s knowledge of genre, and the bodily and linguistic responses of spectators jointly establish the relevant emotional practice.

This dissolution is philosophically attractive because it avoids two unsatisfactory alternatives. The first is to deny that the response is real, reducing fear of fiction to a mere “as-if” or quasi-emotion. The second is to posit that viewers somehow believe in the existence of fictional entities while simultaneously knowing that they do not. The Wittgensteinian view preserves the reality of the response without introducing an irrational contradiction in the spectator’s psychology.

## Does use-theory fully solve the problem?

The Wittgensteinian approach is powerful, but it should not be presented as a complete explanation of all aspects of fictional emotion. Its quietism may seem to evade rather than answer certain questions. A philosopher may ask what precisely distinguishes fear of Michael Myers from mere imaginative attention, aesthetic appreciation, or physiological arousal. If “fear” can be used in multiple language-games, does that make the uses too dissimilar to belong to one concept? Are there not psychological mechanisms shared by fear in fiction and fear in life? Does the viewer not, in some sense, represent Michael Myers as a dangerous agent, even while withholding belief in his actual existence?

These questions have force. A complete philosophy of fiction may need cognitive, affective, and phenomenological accounts of how representations generate emotional responses. The fact that an expression is governed by social rules does not by itself explain the bodily intensity of suspense or the imaginative simulation of danger. Wittgensteinian quietism is not a substitute for neuroscience or psychology. It is primarily a critique of conceptual confusion.

Nor does use-theory imply that all linguistic uses are equally legitimate. If a person is terrified by a fictional character because they have lost the ability to distinguish a film from ordinary reality, then it may be appropriate to describe the situation differently. The relevant language-game could involve delusion, hallucination, or psychological distress rather than ordinary spectatorship. Context matters, but context does not make every application correct. Wittgenstein’s appeal to use is normative: language-games have standards of appropriateness, even if those standards are not reducible to metaphysical definitions.

Furthermore, emotional responses to fiction can involve beliefs, even if they need not. A viewer may believe that the fictional character exists within the story, believe that the character’s actions are morally wrong according to the story, and believe that certain events will occur next. Fictional engagement is not free of cognition. What it rejects is the claim that genuine emotion requires the belief that the emotional object exists independently of the fictional framework. The viewer can believe “Michael Myers kills people in this film” without believing “Michael Myers is an actual murderer in the world outside the film.” These are different beliefs governed by different standards of truth.

The best conclusion, then, is not that the paradox has been disproved by showing that emotions never involve beliefs. It is that Radford’s generalization is too strong. Some emotions may conventionally involve existential belief, especially where the emotion is connected with practical action toward an actual object. But the concept of emotion has enough grammatical flexibility to permit genuine responses to objects presented within fiction. Wittgenstein dissolves the necessity of the paradox by showing that its key premise is not universally valid.

## Michael Myers and the grammar of horror

The example of Michael Myers makes the contextual nature of the response especially vivid. The character is not merely a name attached to a mental image. He is constructed through cinematic conventions: the mask, the silence, the slow movement, the apparent persistence, the invasion of domestic spaces, and the recurring narrative association with violence. These features teach the audience how to respond. The viewer learns that a certain musical cue, camera movement, or visual appearance is a reason for apprehension. The response is not arbitrary, but rule-governed.

To say that the audience fears Michael Myers is therefore to say that the character occupies the role of a threat in the horror language-game. The viewer’s fear may involve bodily arousal, anticipation, aversion, identification with victims, and moral condemnation. It may also involve expressive behavior: gasping, screaming, covering the eyes, or warning another viewer. These responses are appropriate within a practice that treats the represented events as significant while preserving the distinction between representation and actuality.

The language-game also explains why the same character can be emotionally inert in one setting and frightening in another. A still image of Michael Myers in a museum exhibit may evoke nostalgia or aesthetic interest. A parody may make him funny. A discussion in a film studies seminar may treat him as an example of genre construction. A person wearing the mask at a Halloween party may be playful rather than terrifying. The object has not undergone a metaphysical transformation. The surrounding practice has changed, and with it the criteria for the relevant emotional response.

This contextual variation supports a Wittgensteinian view. The emotional significance of Michael Myers is not contained wholly within a private mental representation or in the character considered apart from every practice. It emerges from a relation among the representation, the circumstances, the participant’s training, and the norms of response. This does not make the emotion subjective in the sense of being arbitrary. The film can succeed or fail at producing horror; an audience can misread a scene; a reaction can be apt, excessive, feigned, or confused. But these judgments are made within practices rather than by measuring the response against a single metaphysical condition of object existence.

## The limits of the mind-dependent/mind-independent opposition

The original claim that Radford’s paradox rests upon an untenable metaphysical distinction can now be assessed more precisely. If the claim means that fictional objects are not absolutely unreal, it is persuasive. Fictional entities have roles in public practices and can produce real psychological, social, and economic effects. They are not private fantasies in the simplistic sense. Their identity and significance are sustained by communal forms of life.

If the claim means that there is no difference whatsoever between fictional and actual objects, it is unpersuasive. The distinction is built into the ordinary use of words such as “invented,” “imaginary,” “historical,” “present,” and “real.” It regulates behavior and prevents serious confusions. A fictional killing cannot replace a forensic investigation; a fictional victim cannot testify; and fictional danger does not normally warrant the same intervention as an actual threat. Wittgensteinian philosophy does not abolish these distinctions. It asks us to understand their use rather than convert them into a single theory of being.

The distinction becomes untenable only when it is made to perform work it cannot perform—namely, when it is used to establish that emotions directed toward fictional objects are not genuine. There is no contradiction in saying both that Michael Myers is fictional and that a viewer is genuinely afraid of him in the context of a horror film. The first statement identifies the rules governing the character’s existence and truth. The second describes the viewer’s response within a different but related practice. The two statements conflict only if fear is defined as a belief-dependent relation to a mind-independent object.

A more adequate account recognizes several senses of “object.” An emotion may have an actual object, such as a person in the room; a represented object, such as a character on screen; a possible object, such as an anticipated disaster; or an intentional object, whose role is fixed by the emotion even when its existence is uncertain. These categories should not be collapsed, but neither should they be treated as though only the first can support genuine affect. The fictionality of an object changes the grammar of the response, not necessarily its authenticity.

## Conclusion

Radford’s paradox of fiction exposes a real tension in traditional accounts of emotion, but the tension does not require us to choose between denying fictional emotions and attributing contradictory beliefs to spectators. The paradox arises from an overly restrictive conception of emotional intentionality and from a tendency to make the distinction between fiction and reality bear more metaphysical weight than ordinary practices support.

The contrast between mind-dependent fictional states of affairs and mind-independent reality is not wholly untenable. Fiction and actuality differ in important ways, and those differences guide our actions, assertions, and standards of evidence. What is untenable is the assumption that only a mind-independent object can be the target of a genuine emotional response. Fictional characters possess roles within publicly organized practices, and those roles can be sufficient for fear, pity, anger, suspense, or admiration.

Wittgenstein’s use-theory of meaning and his conception of language-games clarify this point by directing attention to the circumstances in which emotional expressions function. “I am afraid of Michael Myers” may, in a horror-film context, express a genuine and embodied response to a fictional threat without asserting that Michael Myers exists as an actual murderer. The viewer’s response is governed by the conventions of cinematic representation and spectatorship. It need not be an irrational psychological state generated by a false belief.

Wittgensteinian quietism goes further by questioning whether the paradox requires a metaphysical solution at all. Once we see that “fear,” “belief,” “existence,” and “fiction” have different uses across different language-games, the demand for a single explanation loses its force. The viewer can know that Michael Myers is fictional, respond fearfully to his representation, and remain perfectly rational. The emotional expression belongs to the grammar of horror; the ontological disclaimer belongs to the grammar of ordinary description. Their coexistence is not a contradiction but a feature of the plural and context-sensitive character of language.

The philosophical lesson is therefore not that fiction and reality are indistinguishable. It is that emotional reality should not be measured solely by the independent existence of the object toward which an emotion is directed. A response can be genuine because of the role an object plays in a form of life, not because the object satisfies one privileged metaphysical model of existence. Fiction does not have to deceive us in order to move us, and fear does not have to be irrational merely because its object belongs to a story.
