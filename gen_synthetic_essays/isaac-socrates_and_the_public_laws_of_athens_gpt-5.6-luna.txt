# Socrates, Law, and the Divine Mission: Reconciling the *Apology* and the *Crito*

The *Apology* and the *Crito* present two of the most influential pictures of Socrates’ relation to political authority. In the *Apology*, Socrates declares that he must obey the god rather than the city whenever the two commands conflict. His divine mission to examine himself and others is not negotiable: even if the Athenians were to release him on the condition that he cease philosophizing, he would refuse. In the *Crito*, however, Socrates rejects an opportunity to escape from an unjust conviction and impending execution. He speaks through the personified Laws of Athens, which insist that citizens must either persuade the city to alter its decisions or obey them. The Laws also argue that escaping would injure the city, violate agreements, and return wrong for wrong.

At first sight, these positions seem inconsistent. The *Apology* appears to establish the priority of divine command over human law, while the *Crito* appears to establish an unconditional obligation to obey legal verdicts. If Socrates may disobey the city when his divine mission requires it, why may he not disobey the court’s sentence when he believes it unjust? Conversely, if he is bound to accept the verdict simply because he has lived under Athens’ laws and benefited from its institutions, why did he resist the Thirty Tyrants when they ordered him to participate in the arrest of Leon of Salamis?

The contradiction is real only if the dialogues are read as asserting a single, undifferentiated duty of obedience. Socrates does not, in fact, treat every command of every regime as equally binding. His moral framework is better understood as establishing several distinctions: between divine and human authority, between laws and the arbitrary commands of rulers, between suffering injustice and committing it, between accepting a legal penalty and endorsing the justice of the verdict, and between persuasion within a political order and violent or evasive evasion of that order. The *Crito* does not show that Socrates owes an unconditional duty to obey every legal command. Rather, it dramatizes a demanding but qualified obligation arising from justice, civic reciprocity, and his own settled relationship to Athens. His refusal to obey the Thirty is consistent with this framework because their command was not an ordinary exercise of lawful judgment but an attempt to instrumentalize him in an unlawful killing. Nevertheless, the reconciliation is not costless. Plato leaves a genuine tension between principled civil disobedience and the strong language of obedience used by the Laws.

## The divine mandate in the *Apology*

Socrates’ defense in the *Apology* is not merely a denial of the charges against him. It is a defense of a way of life. He presents himself as having been assigned a divine task: to examine the claims to wisdom made by politicians, poets, and craftsmen, and to expose the limits of their knowledge. The famous comparison to a gadfly captures the political character of this mission. Athens is a large and powerful city, but it has become sluggish and complacent; Socrates’ questioning irritates it into moral awareness.

This mission is not represented as a private preference. Socrates does not say simply that he enjoys arguing or that philosophy is his chosen career. He claims that the god has directed him toward it. The divine sign, or *daimonion*, also functions as a source of moral restraint, warning him against certain actions. His philosophical activity is thus part of a religious vocation. To abandon it merely to save his life would be to betray the authority that gives his life its meaning.

The most uncompromising statement comes when Socrates imagines that the jury might acquit him only if he promised to stop philosophizing. He replies that he would obey the god rather than the jury. He would continue to question people about virtue, wisdom, and the care of the soul, even at the cost of death. This seems to establish a clear principle: divine obligation takes priority over political obligation. The city may command, threaten, or punish, but it cannot rightfully require Socrates to abandon what the god has commanded him to do.

Yet the scope of this principle must be specified. Socrates does not claim that every action he wants to perform is divinely authorized, nor that anyone who invokes conscience may ignore the law. The divine mandate concerns a particular form of activity: examining himself and others, speaking philosophically, and encouraging the care of the soul. It does not give him a general license to evade legal consequences, seize property, harm others, or overthrow the political order. Indeed, the content of his divine mission presupposes moral limits. Socrates repeatedly argues that one must never commit injustice, even in response to injustice. Divine obedience cannot mean arbitrary disobedience; it must be consistent with the most basic moral principle that injustice is never permissible.

The *Apology* therefore establishes a hierarchy, but not an unlimited one. God’s command is superior to a human command when the human command requires Socrates to abandon his moral and philosophical vocation. The relevant conflict is between an order to stop doing what is just and an obligation to continue doing it. The dialogue does not directly address whether a person may disregard an otherwise legitimate legal punishment imposed for disobeying that order. That question is left for the *Crito*.

## The argument from harm

The first major argument in the *Crito* is that escaping would harm Socrates, the city, and the Laws. Crito’s initial argument is practical and personal. Socrates’ death will make his friends look cowardly or miserly, and his children will suffer if they are deprived of their father. Socrates replies that the opinions of the many should not govern moral action. What matters is not what most people think but what the person who understands justice thinks. The relevant question is therefore not whether escape would be socially embarrassing or emotionally painful, but whether it would be just.

Socrates then states the central moral premise: one must never willingly do wrong. Nor may one return wrong for wrong, even when one has been wronged. The court’s verdict was unjust, but that fact does not automatically make escape just. If escaping would itself be an injustice, then the injustice of the conviction cannot justify it.

The Laws develop this thought by presenting escape as an injury to the political order. If Socrates leaves without the city’s permission, he would undermine the laws that make courts, judgments, contracts, and civic life possible. His private act would have public consequences. The Laws ask whether a city can exist if individual citizens disregard its judgments whenever they believe those judgments mistaken. They suggest that the authority of legal decisions depends upon their being treated as binding, not continually re-litigated by those subject to them.

This argument from harm is powerful but must be interpreted carefully. It does not establish that every disobedience to law is wrong simply because it may cause some social disruption. If that were the argument, no unjust law could ever be resisted, since resistance ordinarily creates disorder and may weaken respect for legal authority. The argument instead concerns a particular kind of harm: the destruction of the institutional framework through which citizens resolve disputes. Socrates’ escape would not merely express disagreement with the verdict; it would unilaterally cancel its practical effect. He would claim the right to decide for himself whether the judgment should govern him.

There is an important difference between criticizing a law, seeking its reform, refusing to perform an immoral act, and fleeing after conviction. Socrates’ conduct in the *Apology* belongs primarily to the first two categories. He criticizes Athens’ moral priorities and refuses to abandon philosophy. The proposed escape in the *Crito* belongs to the fourth. It would involve rejecting the authority of the court after the legal process had concluded. The Laws contend that these forms of resistance are not equivalent.

Still, the argument from harm cannot by itself yield an unconditional duty of obedience. Legal institutions can themselves become sources of profound harm. A legal order that systematically condemns the innocent, authorizes oppression, or suppresses all avenues of criticism may be more destructive when obeyed than when resisted. Moreover, the mere fact that disobedience threatens institutional stability does not show that disobedience is unjust. Stability is a condition of political life, but it is not the highest moral value in Socrates’ philosophy. The care of the soul and the avoidance of injustice remain more fundamental.

The force of the argument therefore depends on an implicit judgment about the character of Athens. Socrates treats the city as sufficiently lawful that accepting one unjust verdict will not amount to surrendering all moral agency. He can continue to speak, criticize, and philosophize, even if he cannot avoid the sentence. His death will be a grave injustice committed against him, but his escape would not be the only available form of resistance, nor would staying necessarily constitute cooperation in ongoing wrongdoing. The argument from harm is thus contextual rather than absolute.

## The argument from just agreements

The second major argument is contractual. The Laws claim that Socrates has entered into an agreement with Athens. He was born there, educated there, lived there for seventy years, raised children there, and never chose exile when he had the opportunity. By remaining, he implicitly accepted the city’s laws and the procedures through which they operated. The Laws express the agreement in a formula: a citizen must either persuade the city to change its laws or obey its decisions. Socrates has enjoyed the benefits of the political order; he cannot accept its advantages while rejecting its burdens.

This is one of the earliest and most famous versions of the social-contract argument. It attempts to ground political obligation not merely in force or utility but in fairness. If Socrates has voluntarily participated in a system, benefited from it, and accepted its general rules, then it may be unjust for him to abandon the system at the precise moment when it imposes an unwelcome cost.

The argument is strengthened by the Laws’ claim that Socrates could have left Athens. If he had found the city’s institutions intolerable, he could have emigrated. Instead, he remained and continued to live according to Athenian law. This conduct is interpreted as tacit consent. The Laws also claim a quasi-parental authority: they gave Socrates life, education, and the conditions in which he could develop. To injure them would be comparable to injuring one’s parents.

The contractual argument, however, faces several objections. First, Socrates never explicitly signed an agreement promising to accept every future verdict. Tacit consent is difficult to establish, especially where the alternatives are limited and where political membership is inherited rather than chosen. Second, the analogy between laws and parents may be emotionally powerful but morally ambiguous. Parents can be resisted when they abuse their authority, and a city does not automatically acquire unlimited power merely by providing education and security. Third, the ability to leave may not be enough to make consent voluntary. Exile can involve the loss of property, friendships, family relationships, and cultural identity. The choice between remaining under law and becoming an outsider may be highly coercive.

These objections show why the argument from just agreements cannot establish an unconditional duty. Consent, even when genuine, is normally limited by the content of the agreement and by the justice of the authority exercising it. A person who agrees to obey a government has not thereby agreed to commit murder, renounce conscience, or accept every arbitrary action by officials. Nor does participation in a society imply that every legal decision is substantively just.

The *Crito* itself contains resources for this qualification. The Laws do not simply say “obey whatever the rulers command.” They speak of persuading the city or obeying it. Persuasion presupposes that citizens may criticize, argue, and seek reform. Political obligation is therefore not identical to silence or passive submission. Socrates’ entire life of philosophical examination can be understood as an effort to persuade the city, even when his persuasion is unwelcome.

Moreover, the Laws’ demand is framed as a demand of justice. They ask Socrates whether he can honestly deny that he has agreed to abide by the city’s judgments. The issue is not whether every verdict is correct but whether he may destroy the legal relationship whenever it produces an unfavorable result. His acceptance of the sentence may therefore reflect a limited promise: he will use legal and argumentative means to challenge the city, but he will not unilaterally withdraw from the system after losing a case.

This is still a severe obligation. It requires Socrates to distinguish between being wronged and being permitted to do wrong in response. But it need not imply that the laws are always morally right or that every command must be performed. The agreement is best understood as an agreement to participate in a lawful political order and to bear the consequences of that participation, subject to the overriding prohibition against doing injustice.

## Does the *Crito* invalidate Socrates’ disobedience?

The apparent contradiction becomes sharpest if the death sentence is understood as a legal verdict and the divine mission as a command to disobey it. Socrates was convicted for allegedly corrupting the young and not believing in the city’s gods. If he is willing to disregard the city’s demand that he cease philosophizing, why does he not disregard the city’s later demand that he die?

One possible answer is that the two cases involve different kinds of commands. The hypothetical order in the *Apology* would prohibit Socrates from performing his divine duty. It would require him to commit a moral wrong by abandoning the care of the soul. The death sentence, by contrast, does not require him to commit an injustice. It threatens him with an injustice, but it does not command him to perform one. Socrates can continue to philosophize until his execution and can face death without betraying his mission. The distinction is between being forbidden to do what one must do and being punished for doing it.

This distinction is morally significant. A person may have a duty to resist an order that requires participation in wrongdoing, while having no duty to evade a punishment imposed for refusing that order. The first concerns the substance of one’s conduct; the second concerns whether one may use force or escape to avoid the consequences of a principled refusal. Socrates’ decision to remain does not mean that the verdict was just. It means that he refuses to add a further injustice—an unauthorized escape—to the injustice already committed against him.

The distinction also protects the priority of the divine mission. Socrates is not obeying the court by ceasing to philosophize. He continues to explain and defend his life before the jury. After conviction, he remains committed to the same activity until the end. His acceptance of execution is not a renunciation of philosophy but a refusal to preserve his life by violating his moral principles.

A second answer is that the *Apology* presents Socrates as willing to disobey only when directly commanded to act against the god, whereas the *Crito* concerns a verdict issued through a political procedure. This distinction cannot be pushed too far, since Socrates believed the verdict itself was unjust. Yet the procedure matters to his political theory. He does not claim that personal conviction automatically nullifies public judgment. He may oppose the content of a decision while recognizing the authority of the process by which the decision was made.

This position is not entirely satisfying from a modern perspective. An unjust verdict can be as oppressive as an immoral command. If a state imprisons or kills a person for exercising conscience, accepting the punishment may allow injustice to triumph. Civil disobedience often involves not only refusing to perform an evil act but also resisting the legal penalties attached to that refusal. Socrates’ refusal to escape therefore reflects a more conservative commitment to civic order than many later theories of conscientious resistance.

Nevertheless, it is consistent with his own priorities. Socrates is not trying to maximize his political influence or preserve his life for future philosophical work. He believes that the essential question is whether his soul remains just. Escape would be permissible only if it were just; the fact that it would enable him to continue philosophizing is not sufficient. Philosophy is not a license to violate the principles it teaches.

## The Thirty Tyrants

The most serious challenge comes from Socrates’ conduct under the Thirty Tyrants. In the *Apology*, he recounts how, when the oligarchic regime was in power, the Thirty summoned him and four others to the Hall and ordered them to bring Leon of Salamis for execution. The regime had not charged Leon with a publicly adjudicated offense; it wished to implicate respectable citizens in its crimes. Socrates alone refused to participate. He went home, fully aware that disobedience might cost him his life.

This episode appears to show Socrates practicing precisely the kind of disobedience that the *Crito* seems to prohibit. The Thirty were the de facto government of Athens. Their order was an official command. Socrates disobeyed it because he regarded it as wrong. Why, then, could he not disobey the democratic court’s wrong verdict?

The answer depends on distinguishing “law” from the will of whoever happens to control the state. The Thirty were rulers, but their command was not necessarily a law in the normative sense invoked by the Laws in the *Crito*. It was an order to participate in an extrajudicial killing, issued by a tyrannical regime for the purpose of spreading guilt. Socrates’ refusal did not amount to repudiating legal judgment as such. It was a refusal to become an agent of murder.

The contrast with the death sentence is therefore substantial. In the case of Leon, Socrates was asked to perform an unjust act against another person. In his own case, he was the object of an unjust act. His principle that one must never do injustice, even in response to injustice, directly explains the former refusal. He would not kill Leon merely because powerful men commanded it. In the latter case, he could suffer injustice without committing it.

This distinction also clarifies Socrates’ relationship to political authority. He does not obey the Thirty because they are rulers. He obeys the law insofar as law represents a legitimate moral order. The Thirty’s rule is deficient not merely because it is oligarchic rather than democratic but because it is lawless and criminal. Their commands reveal the difference between political power and political right. A government can possess the power to issue orders without possessing the moral authority to command every action.

One might object that the *Crito* does not make this distinction clearly enough. The personified Laws tell Socrates that a citizen must either persuade or obey. They do not explicitly limit their claim to commands consistent with justice. If the Laws’ words are taken literally and universally, Socrates’ refusal to assist the Thirty seems disobedient in precisely the forbidden sense. This suggests that the dialogues may preserve a genuine tension rather than a perfectly systematic theory.

Yet the context of the *Crito* indicates that the Laws are defending a relationship with a lawful city, not every act of every regime. Their argument presupposes institutions that legislate, adjudicate, educate, and sustain civic life. The Thirty’s order against Leon was an attempt to corrupt those institutions, not an ordinary application of them. Their command was lawless because it sought to bypass public accountability and make Socrates complicit in political murder.

Socrates’ own account in the *Apology* supports this interpretation. He says that he demonstrated, not through words but through conduct, that he cared more about justice than about death. He refused the order because obeying it would be wrong. The same principle leads him to remain in prison: escaping would be wrong if it violated his commitments to the city and injured the legal order. In both cases, the governing question is not “Who commands?” but “What would my action make me do?”

## Law, verdict, and the limits of civil disobedience

The reconciliation between the dialogues therefore depends upon a layered conception of obligation.

At the highest level is the prohibition against injustice and the obligation to care for the soul. This is expressed through the divine mission in the *Apology*. No human authority can rightly compel Socrates to abandon this duty or to commit an immoral act.

At the political level is an obligation to respect the legal order in which one has chosen to live. This obligation is grounded in reciprocity, reliance, and fairness. Socrates has benefited from Athens, participated in its institutions, and had opportunities to leave or persuade. He therefore cannot treat the city’s verdict as though it were merely an external inconvenience. His membership has generated responsibilities.

At the level of particular commands, however, political authority is limited by justice. A command to arrest Leon for execution without lawful process is not binding merely because it comes from rulers. Socrates may refuse to perform an unjust act. The *Crito* does not require him to do otherwise if its arguments are interpreted in the light of the broader moral commitments of the *Apology*.

This framework permits a distinction between resistance and evasion. Socrates resists the Thirty by refusing cooperation in an unjust killing. He does not attempt to use his refusal as a reason to abolish all political authority. He also resists the democratic city by continuing to philosophize despite threats. But he does not evade the court’s final sentence, because doing so would, in his judgment, injure the laws and violate his settled civic relationship.

Whether this distinction is persuasive depends partly on how one evaluates the value of legal institutions. Socrates takes institutional continuity seriously. He fears that if each citizen obeys only those verdicts he personally approves, the city will cease to be a city. His concern is not that the court is infallible. It is that law cannot function if its judgments have no finality. The citizen who loses a case must have some obligation to accept the result, even while arguing that the result was mistaken.

But legal finality is not the same as moral correctness. The *Crito* is strongest when it says that Socrates should not commit a new injustice in order to escape an old one. It is weaker when it suggests that disobedience itself necessarily destroys the city. A political order that leaves no room for resistance may deserve to be weakened. The challenge for any theory of political obligation is to identify when resistance preserves justice rather than merely expressing self-interest.

Socrates solves this problem, though imperfectly, by imposing an exceptionally demanding standard on himself. He does not escape because his motives are mixed, because his friends are distressed, or because his life would be useful to his children. He refuses to treat his own case as an exception. The consistency of his decision depends on his willingness to accept the same principles when they work against him. He would not tell another person that injustice is wrong only until the law commits it against that person. His refusal to return wrong for wrong is therefore not passive submission but a form of moral discipline.

## Is Socrates’ framework defensible?

Socrates’ framework has considerable philosophical strength. It avoids two opposite errors. On one side, it rejects the idea that legal authority is unlimited. The divine mission and the episode of Leon demonstrate that conscience and justice can override commands. On the other side, it rejects the idea that every citizen may disobey whenever a law seems personally mistaken. Civic life requires stable procedures, and membership in a political community can create real obligations even when particular decisions are unjust.

The weakness lies in the ambiguity of “persuade or obey.” If persuasion is permitted only before a final verdict, then a citizen who fails to persuade must submit forever, even under a deeply corrupt regime. If persuasion includes public resistance, civil disobedience, and refusal to accept penalties, then Socrates’ acceptance of death is one possible strategy rather than a universal rule. The dialogue does not settle the matter.

Nor does tacit consent provide a fully adequate foundation. Socrates’ long residence in Athens is evidence that he accepted much of its legal order, but it does not prove that he consented to every future misuse of power. His agreement should be understood as limited by the moral purposes of law. He accepted the city as a framework for shared life, not as an infallible substitute for judgment.

The best interpretation is therefore neither that Socrates owes unconditional obedience nor that he owes no political obedience at all. He is committed to a conditional but exceptionally serious duty: obey lawful decisions, honor the institutions in which he has voluntarily participated, and seek change through persuasion rather than private force; but never perform injustice, never abandon the divine task of moral examination, and never confuse the commands of a tyrant with the authority of law.

On this interpretation, the verdict against Socrates does not require him to stop philosophizing, because he never does. It requires him to accept the consequences of having philosophized in a particular city under its procedures. His obedience is not an acknowledgment that the verdict was correct. It is an acknowledgment that he will not preserve his life by violating justice as he understands it. The Thirty’s command, by contrast, required him to become an instrument of injustice. Refusing it was not a rejection of law but fidelity to the moral condition that makes law worthy of obedience.

## Conclusion

The *Apology* and the *Crito* do not present a simple contradiction, although they do expose a serious tension in Socratic political morality. The *Apology* affirms that divine obligation can override human commands, especially when the city demands that Socrates abandon philosophy or participate in injustice. The *Crito* affirms that citizens cannot treat legal verdicts as optional whenever they regard them as mistaken. These claims become compatible once political obligation is distinguished from blind obedience.

The argument from harm shows why Socrates thinks escape would damage the legal order and the conditions of civic life. The argument from just agreements shows why his long participation in Athens creates responsibilities that cannot be discarded at the moment of danger. Neither argument, however, establishes an unconditional duty to obey every legal command. Both depend upon a background conception of justice, lawful authority, and reciprocal civic membership.

Socrates’ refusal to obey the Thirty Tyrants confirms rather than refutes this interpretation. He did not submit to an order to assist in an unlawful killing because the command was incompatible with justice. His acceptance of his own sentence involved a different kind of action: not performing injustice, but refusing to evade the consequences of his relationship with the city. He could suffer an unjust judgment without returning injustice for injustice.

The resulting position is demanding and perhaps too demanding. It leaves little room for successful resistance to tyranny and treats institutional stability as a profound moral concern. Yet it is not incoherent. Socrates’ fundamental principle remains constant: one must never commit injustice, and one must obey the highest moral authority available to reason and conscience. The city deserves obedience where it embodies a lawful order, but it cannot claim the soul’s allegiance when it commands wrongdoing or demands abandonment of truth. Socrates’ death is thus neither simple submission nor straightforward rebellion. It is an attempt to remain faithful simultaneously to the divine mission, to the prohibition against injustice, and to the responsibilities generated by civic life.
