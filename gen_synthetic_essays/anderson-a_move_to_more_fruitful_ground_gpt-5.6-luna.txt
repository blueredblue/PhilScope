# Compromise Platonism and the Conflict of Mathematical Evidence

## Introduction

Philosophical realism about mathematics is often presented as a dispute between two fundamentally different explanations of mathematical knowledge. On one side stands a Gödelian picture according to which mathematicians possess some form of direct intellectual access to an objective mathematical reality. Mathematical intuition, in this view, plays a role analogous to sense perception: it gives us prima facie knowledge of mathematical objects and principles that are not reducible to empirical observation or merely formal manipulation. On the other side stands the Quine–Putnam indispensability tradition. According to this approach, our reasons for believing in mathematical entities are continuous with our reasons for believing in theoretical entities in science. If mathematics is indispensable to our best scientific theories, then, given a general realism about science, we should be realist about mathematics as well.

Penelope Maddy’s work is important because it attempts to take both kinds of justification seriously. Mathematical belief, she argues, is not sustained by a single epistemic source. Some axioms appear compelling because they express basic features of the mathematical universe or follow from deeply entrenched mathematical conceptions. Others gain support from their consequences: their capacity to unify mathematics, generate fruitful theories, simplify proofs, or contribute to the success of mathematics as used in science. This position may be called *compromise Platonism*: mathematical entities and truths are objective, but our access to them is mediated by more than one type of evidence. Mathematical intuition supplies intrinsic justification, while mathematical practice and scientific application supply extrinsic justification.

The attraction of this position is evident. A purely Gödelian Platonism risks making mathematical knowledge mysterious and elitist. A purely Quinean or Putnamian Platonism risks making mathematical ontology dependent on contingent scientific practice and leaves unclear why mathematics that is not scientifically indispensable should be believed. Compromise Platonism promises to preserve objectivity while acknowledging the actual diversity of mathematical reasons.

Its central difficulty, however, appears when intrinsic and extrinsic evidence point in opposite directions. If intuition supports one axiom while mathematical or scientific practice supports its negation, what should a realist say? The Axiom of Choice and the parallel postulate provide particularly illuminating cases. The Axiom of Choice has often attracted intrinsic suspicion because of its nonconstructive character and counterintuitive consequences, while receiving substantial extrinsic support from its extraordinary mathematical fruitfulness. The parallel postulate, by contrast, may appear intrinsically supported by ordinary spatial intuition, while extrinsic developments in mathematics and physics have vindicated non-Euclidean alternatives.

These cases reveal that merely adding intrinsic and extrinsic justification does not by itself resolve conflicts between them. A compromise Platonist must explain whether one form of evidence has priority, whether the conflict is only apparent, or whether mathematical truth must be understood in a less traditional way. Each route has a cost. Siding with intrinsic evidence revives the epistemological problems of Gödelian Platonism; siding with extrinsic evidence risks the conventionalism and scientistic dependence associated with indispensability arguments; abandoning bivalent truth threatens the realist conception of mathematical objectivity altogether. Nevertheless, a carefully developed realist framework can avoid a crude choice among these options by distinguishing levels and roles of justification. The result will not be a simple compromise, but a more qualified form of realism in which evidence can guide belief without functioning as an infallible detector of truth.

## I. Intrinsic and Extrinsic Justification

The distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic justification is not perfectly sharp, but it captures two genuine dimensions of mathematical reasoning.

Intrinsic justification concerns what an axiom says and how it fits with basic mathematical conceptions. An axiom may seem intrinsically justified because it expresses the iterative conception of set, follows from the idea of a geometrical space, accords with the structural role of an object, or appears unavoidable once the subject matter is properly understood. The justification is internal to mathematics and to the concepts used in formulating the axiom. It does not depend primarily on the axiom’s applications or on the number of theorems it makes possible.

Extrinsic justification, by contrast, concerns what an axiom does. An axiom may be supported because it yields a rich and coherent theory, settles previously open questions, simplifies proofs, connects otherwise distant areas, or is indispensable to successful science. Its justification is therefore relational. The axiom earns credibility through its position within a larger mathematical or scientific practice.

Gödelian Platonism gives intrinsic justification a central role. Gödel claimed that mathematical intuition enables us to apprehend mathematical objects and axioms, even though such intuition is not sensory perception. On this model, axioms can be justified in something like the way basic perceptual beliefs are justified: not by deduction from more primitive premises, but by direct intellectual apprehension. Such apprehension remains fallible, but it is meant to connect the mathematician with an objective realm of mathematical facts.

The Quine–Putnam argument gives extrinsic justification a central role. Quine’s indispensability argument begins with a general commitment to scientific realism. We should believe in the entities quantified over by our best scientific theories, provided those theories are indispensable to explanation and prediction. Mathematics is indispensable to modern science; therefore, we should believe in mathematical entities. Putnam emphasizes that mathematics is not merely a convenient language that can be removed without loss. Mathematical structures often enter into the formulation of physical laws and the explanation of physical phenomena. Mathematical realism is consequently part of a broader realist attitude toward successful science.

Maddy’s contribution lies partly in refusing to regard these as mutually exclusive explanations. Mathematicians do in fact appeal to both kinds of considerations. They regard some axioms as obvious or natural, but they also evaluate axioms in terms of their consequences and their power to organize mathematics. In set theory, for example, the status of an axiom may depend on whether it fits the iterative conception of sets, whether it preserves desirable closure principles, whether it yields a robust theory of cardinal arithmetic, and whether it clarifies the structure of the set-theoretic universe. Axioms are not accepted solely because they are “seen” by intuition, nor solely because they occur in physics.

This pluralism is a strength, but it creates an epistemological problem. If intrinsic and extrinsic evidence are independent routes to truth, what happens when they disagree? A theory that invokes multiple evidential sources must say how they are to be balanced. Otherwise, the appeal to pluralism is merely descriptive: it tells us that mathematicians use different considerations, but not which beliefs are rational when those considerations conflict.

## II. The Axiom of Choice as a Conflict Case

The Axiom of Choice is an ideal test case because it has both strong intrinsic objections and powerful extrinsic credentials.

The axiom says, roughly, that for any collection of nonempty sets, there is a function choosing one member from each set. In its general form, it extends beyond finite or explicitly describable collections. The axiom has consequences that have often seemed philosophically troubling. It implies, for example, the existence of nonmeasurable sets and supports the Banach–Tarski theorem, according to which a solid ball can be decomposed and reassembled into two balls of the same size. It also licenses existence claims without providing explicit means of constructing the objects in question.

From a constructivist or finitistic perspective, these features undermine intrinsic support for the axiom. If mathematical existence should be tied to construction, proof, or effective specification, the Axiom of Choice appears extravagant. It asserts the existence of choice functions even when no general method for producing them is available. Its consequences can conflict with ordinary intuitions about volume, decomposition, and the relation between existence and construction.

Even from a classical Platonist perspective, the axiom may seem less intrinsically secure than the axioms of elementary arithmetic or finite set theory. It is not obvious that the totality of sets must possess choice functions for arbitrary families. The iterative conception of set, which is often used to support standard set theory, may establish that certain sets exist at successive stages, but it does not straightforwardly imply that every family of nonempty sets has a choice set. The axiom can therefore look like an additional principle imposed upon the hierarchy rather than a direct expression of its underlying conception.

Yet extrinsically, the Axiom of Choice is extraordinarily well supported. It is equivalent, over suitable background theories, to numerous important principles: Zorn’s Lemma, the Well-Ordering Theorem, and various results in algebra, analysis, topology, and functional analysis. It enables general existence proofs throughout mathematics. Many central theorems—such as the assertion that every vector space has a basis—are naturally formulated using it or principles closely related to it. Mathematical practice has repeatedly found the axiom useful, powerful, and structurally illuminating.

Its usefulness is not merely pragmatic in the narrow sense. The axiom reveals common patterns across different mathematical domains. Zorn’s Lemma, for example, provides a general method for proving the existence of maximal objects. It unifies arguments that might otherwise appear unrelated. The value of the axiom therefore lies not simply in producing more theorems but in exposing a deep architecture of mathematical reasoning.

The conflict can now be stated sharply. Suppose intrinsic considerations favor a weaker theory, perhaps one rejecting the Axiom of Choice or accepting only restricted choice principles. Suppose extrinsic considerations favor full Choice because of its mathematical fruitfulness and indispensability. What should the realist conclude?

One possible response is to side with intrinsic support. The Platonist might say that mathematical truth is determined by the structure of the mathematical universe, not by the fertility of an axiom. If the Axiom of Choice does not follow from the fundamental nature of sets, its extrinsic usefulness cannot make it true. Mathematical practice may explain why mathematicians adopt it, but not whether it correctly describes the universe of sets.

This response preserves a robust notion of truth, but it inherits the familiar problems of Gödelian Platonism. How are we to determine what the fundamental nature of sets requires? If the answer is “through intuition,” then the account needs an explanation of how intuition works and why it should be trusted in controversial cases. The fact that the Axiom of Choice seems less obvious than other axioms may indicate either that it is false, that it concerns a more remote region of the mathematical universe, or simply that our intuitive capacities are limited. Without an independent criterion, intrinsic appeal cannot decide among these possibilities.

The opposing response is to side with extrinsic support. The Platonist might say that the strength, coherence, and indispensability of the Axiom of Choice justify belief in it, even if it initially conflicts with informal intuition. Mathematical success is evidence that the axiom captures a genuine structural feature of mathematical reality. In this way, extrinsic justification can correct or educate intuition, just as scientific theory can correct ordinary perceptual expectations.

This move is plausible, but it risks collapsing Platonism into a more sophisticated form of Quinean naturalism. If usefulness and indispensability determine which axioms we should accept, then mathematical truth appears hostage to changing mathematical interests and scientific needs. A different mathematical community, with different goals, might find a weaker choice principle more useful. Does usefulness reveal truth, or merely select a convenient framework? Moreover, the indispensability argument supports the existence of mathematical entities quantified over in successful science, but it does not obviously settle questions internal to pure set theory. The fact that mathematics is useful in physics does not by itself establish that every strong set-theoretic axiom is true.

The Axiom of Choice thus exposes a fundamental distinction between *justification for adoption* and *evidence for truth*. Extrinsic considerations can rationally explain why an axiom should be included in a theory, without proving that the axiom is literally true of an independently existing mathematical universe. A Platonist must maintain that the former is evidence for the latter, not merely evidence of usefulness. That bridge is possible, but it cannot be taken for granted.

## III. The Parallel Postulate and the Reversal of Intuition

The parallel postulate presents the opposite pattern. In Euclid’s geometry, the postulate asserts, in one standard formulation, that through a point not on a given line, exactly one line can be drawn parallel to the given line. For centuries, it appeared less self-evident than Euclid’s other postulates. Nevertheless, ordinary spatial intuition strongly favors a Euclidean picture. The geometry of familiar physical space seems to conform to the postulate, at least at ordinary scales.

Intrinsic support for the parallel postulate therefore has two sources. First, it appears geometrically natural within the system of Euclidean constructions. Second, it accords with the intuitive conception of flat space. The other axioms seem to describe basic possibilities of spatial construction, while the parallel postulate appears to articulate the structure of the space in which those constructions occur.

The emergence of non-Euclidean geometries undermined the claim that the parallel postulate is necessary. Lobachevskian and hyperbolic geometries reject uniqueness of parallels, while elliptic geometries reject the existence of parallels altogether. These systems are internally coherent and mathematically fertile. The discovery of such geometries demonstrated that the denial of the parallel postulate need not generate contradiction.

The extrinsic evidence became even more significant with the development of modern physics. General relativity represents gravitation through the geometry of spacetime, and the relevant geometry need not be Euclidean. Physical theory therefore supports the thought that the geometry of actual space is not determined by ordinary intuition. In this case, extrinsic evidence appears to favor the negation of the parallel postulate, or at least to favor a non-Euclidean geometry as a more accurate representation of physical reality.

The case is philosophically complex because the parallel postulate is not simply true or false without qualification. Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries describe different structures. If the question concerns the geometry of a mathematical structure, then the postulate is true in some structures and false in others. If the question concerns physical space, empirical theory becomes decisive. If the question concerns an abstract mathematical universe, the issue is one of which structure is being studied.

This complication is precisely what makes the parallel postulate relevant to compromise Platonism. It suggests that an apparent conflict between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence may result from conflating different domains. Intuition may support Euclidean geometry as a model of ordinary local space, while physics may support a non-Euclidean model of spacetime at a larger scale. There need be no direct contradiction if the two forms of evidence concern different structures or different interpretations.

But this reply does not eliminate every conflict. Suppose intrinsic intuition is taken to support a particular mathematical axiom as universally true, while extrinsic science supports its negation as a description of the actual world. The realist must then decide whether intuition concerns mathematical reality or merely a useful representation of experience. If it concerns mathematical reality, scientific evidence cannot straightforwardly refute it, because physical space is not identical with the abstract structure studied in geometry. If it concerns physical space, then it is defeasible empirical intuition rather than a priori mathematical insight.

The parallel postulate thus reveals an ambiguity in the idea of intrinsic support. Some intuitions are genuinely mathematical: they concern what follows from a concept or what a structure must be like. Other intuitions are quasi-empirical: they reflect our experience of physical objects and spatial environments. A robust compromise Platonism must distinguish these. Otherwise, it will treat a psychologically compelling image of space as evidence about an abstract mathematical universe.

## IV. Three Responses to Conflict

The conflict between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence generates three broad options: prioritize intrinsic support, prioritize extrinsic support, or reject classical bivalence. Each option has serious consequences.

### 1. Prioritizing intrinsic evidence

The first option assigns ultimate authority to intrinsic justification. Mathematical axioms are true because they correctly express the nature of mathematical objects, and intrinsic intuition is our primary access to that nature. Extrinsic success can increase confidence but cannot overturn a genuine mathematical insight.

This approach most clearly preserves traditional Platonism. Mathematical truth is independent of human practices, physical theories, and instrumental usefulness. The mathematical universe has a determinate structure, and the task of the mathematician is to discover it.

The danger is epistemic obscurity. The Gödelian appeal to intuition is not necessarily irrational, but it is difficult to distinguish genuine intuition from familiarity, conceptual habit, or aesthetic preference. The history of mathematics contains many examples in which an apparently self-evident principle was rejected, restricted, or reinterpreted. Intuition supported Euclidean geometry as the geometry of space, yet that support proved unreliable once the scope of the claim became clear. Intuition opposed certain consequences of the Axiom of Choice, yet mathematical practice came to regard the axiom as central.

A pure appeal to intrinsic justification also struggles with independence. If neither an axiom nor its negation follows from accepted principles, what does intuition tell us? Different mathematicians may experience different degrees of obviousness. The appeal to mathematical perception begins to resemble an unexplained faculty whose deliverances are accepted when they fit prior commitments. This is the classic epistemological challenge to Gödelian Platonism: how can finite, causally embedded human beings acquire knowledge of an abstract, non-spatiotemporal realm?

Compromise Platonism does not necessarily succumb entirely to this objection, because it can treat intuition as only one source of evidence. But if it gives intrinsic support priority whenever evidence conflicts, then the extrinsic half of the theory becomes secondary and potentially dispensable. The view approaches pure Gödelian Platonism with pragmatic supplements.

### 2. Prioritizing extrinsic evidence

The second option gives priority to extrinsic evidence. Axioms are justified by their role in successful mathematics and science. If an axiom conflicts with initial intuition but generates an extraordinarily coherent and fruitful theory, that is a reason to revise intuition. The mathematical community’s long-term practice, rather than individual intellectual seeming, becomes the primary guide.

This position has important virtues. It is fallibilist, historically sensitive, and responsive to the actual methods of mathematicians. It explains why controversial axioms can become accepted over time. The Axiom of Choice is not simply imposed by metaphysical preference; its adoption is supported by a vast network of results and practices. Similarly, non-Euclidean geometry gained legitimacy through internal mathematical development and, later, through physical theory.

Nevertheless, prioritizing extrinsic support threatens to reduce truth to success. A theory can be fruitful without being literally true. Mathematicians routinely study structures because they are interesting, useful, or formally elegant, not because they believe those structures describe an independently existing realm. Scientific applications can support the reality of certain structures without settling every question about the totality of mathematical objects.

The indispensability argument itself does not provide a complete foundation for mathematical realism. It relies on scientific realism, on the claim that indispensability is evidential rather than merely pragmatic, and on the inability to reformulate science without the relevant mathematics. Each premise is disputable. Nominalist programs attempt to eliminate or reduce mathematical commitments in science, and even if they fail globally, their partial success may weaken the inference from use to truth.

If extrinsic evidence is made decisive, compromise Platonism risks becoming a form of Quine–Putnam Platonism with an acknowledgment that mathematicians sometimes have intuitions. The realist’s ontology would be determined primarily by the mathematics that survives theoretical selection. This could make the truth of mathematical claims dependent on contingent facts about our scientific world. It would also leave pure mathematics, especially strong set theory, without adequate justification unless it can be connected to science or to broad mathematical utility.

### 3. Abandoning bivalent truth

The third option is to reject the idea that every mathematical statement has a determinate truth value. This response is associated, in different ways, with intuitionism, formalism, structuralism, and certain forms of pluralism. If intrinsic and extrinsic evidence conflict, perhaps neither side has discovered a pre-existing fact. The truth of an axiom may depend on the structure selected, the proof system adopted, or the mathematical context in which the statement is evaluated.

This option can make sense of the parallel postulate. Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries are not rival answers to one univocal question but distinct legitimate theories. The postulate is true in one structure and false in another. Likewise, different set-theoretic universes may satisfy different principles concerning Choice, large cardinals, or determinacy. Mathematical discourse may be pluralistic rather than governed by one all-encompassing universe.

The cost is that this view departs from traditional Platonism. A realist typically wants mathematical objects and truths to exist independently of our choices. If there is no fact of the matter whether the Axiom of Choice is true in the intended universe of sets, then the realist has surrendered the central idea that mathematical claims describe a mind-independent reality. One may retain realism about structures—there are objectively many structures—but not necessarily realism about which structure constitutes the fundamental universe of mathematics.

Abandoning bivalence also threatens the explanatory role of evidence. If evidence helps determine which principles to adopt but does not reveal truth, then mathematical justification becomes closer to conceptual engineering or theory selection. That may be an attractive philosophy of mathematics, but it is not a compromise between Gödelian and Quinean Platonism. It is a departure from Platonism.

## V. Can Realism Resolve the Conflict?

The preceding alternatives suggest that compromise Platonism faces a dilemma. If intrinsic support is authoritative, it resembles Gödelian Platonism; if extrinsic support is authoritative, it resembles Quinean Platonism; if neither is authoritative because truth is non-bivalent, realism is abandoned. Is compromise therefore unstable?

The answer depends on whether a realist framework requires evidential sources to function as independent and infallible truth-makers. It need not. A more sophisticated realism can maintain that both intrinsic and extrinsic evidence are fallible indicators of objective truth, with neither possessing automatic priority. Their conflict may be resolved not by a universal weighting rule but by examining what each form of evidence actually concerns.

First, intrinsic and extrinsic justification may operate at different levels. Intrinsic considerations may support the legitimacy of a concept or the naturalness of a structure, while extrinsic considerations support the adoption of a theory for particular purposes. In the case of geometry, intuition may justify the study of Euclidean space as a mathematically fundamental structure without showing that physical space is Euclidean. Physics may select a non-Euclidean geometry for describing the universe without showing that Euclidean geometry is mathematically illegitimate. In the case of set theory, intrinsic considerations may support the iterative conception and certain axioms derived from it, while extrinsic considerations may support extending the theory with Choice because doing so yields a stable and fruitful framework.

Second, realism can distinguish *truth* from *epistemic entitlement*. An axiom may be rationally accepted even when its truth is not conclusively established. Mathematical practice often operates under such conditions. The Axiom of Choice may be entitled to acceptance because of its immense theoretical role, while the realist remains agnostic about whether it is true in the ultimate universe of sets. This is not necessarily a retreat to anti-realism. Scientific realists often distinguish justified commitment from certainty; they can believe that a theory is approximately or structurally true while recognizing that future evidence may lead to revision.

Third, a realist can treat mathematical reality as structurally plural rather than as a single, fully determinate domain represented by one theory. On this view, there are objective mathematical structures, and mathematical theories describe them. Some mathematical statements are unambiguously true within a structure, while questions about “the” universe of sets may remain underdetermined by our current conception. This preserves bivalence relative to structures while weakening the demand for a unique absolute domain. It is a form of structural realism rather than classical, object-based Platonism.

Whether this position deserves the name Platonism depends on how much independence it requires. It retains objective structures, objective relations, and truth independent of individual mathematicians. But it may reject the idea that every well-formed set-theoretic sentence has a determinate truth value in one privileged universe. The result is a restrained realism, not the robust Platonism defended by Gödel.

A further possibility is that intrinsic and extrinsic evidence are not genuinely independent. Mathematical intuition itself may be shaped by long mathematical practice, while extrinsic success may reveal structural features that eventually become incorporated into mathematical understanding. The apparent conflict between intuition and application may therefore be a conflict between an immature and a more developed conception of the subject matter. Euclidean intuition was not simply false; it was appropriate to a limited conception of spatial geometry. The initial resistance to Choice may reflect constructive expectations that remain valid in some contexts, even if classical Choice is accepted in others.

On this interpretation, extrinsic evidence can educate intrinsic understanding without replacing it. Conversely, intrinsic analysis can limit the significance of extrinsic success by showing that a fruitful principle belongs to one structural framework rather than to mathematics as such. The two forms of justification interact dialectically. Neither is a mechanical criterion of truth, but each can expose the limitations of the other.

## VI. Maddy’s Compromise and Its Limits

Maddy’s attempt to reconcile intrinsic and extrinsic justification is most convincing as an account of mathematical rationality rather than as a complete theory of mathematical truth. It accurately reflects the fact that mathematicians assess axioms by multiple standards. They care about intuitive fit, conceptual naturalness, consistency, explanatory power, unification, fruitfulness, and applicability. No single standard governs all mathematical decisions.

This pluralism also provides a better account of mathematical change than either pure intuitionism or simple indispensability. Mathematical communities can rationally revise their beliefs when new structures, proofs, or applications reveal limitations in previous assumptions. The eventual acceptance of non-Euclidean geometry illustrates how mathematical intuition can be transformed by theoretical understanding. The role of Choice illustrates how an initially controversial principle can become embedded in a wide network of mathematical results.

However, an account of how mathematicians should choose axioms is not automatically an account of what makes axioms true. Extrinsic success may provide evidence of truth, but its connection to truth requires a metaphysical explanation. Why should a theory’s fruitfulness indicate that it describes a mind-independent mathematical reality rather than that it is a particularly effective formal system? Similarly, intrinsic obviousness may provide evidence of truth, but its connection to truth requires an epistemology of mathematical intuition.

Maddy’s compromise therefore faces a residual underdetermination problem. Different mathematical theories can be internally coherent and externally fruitful. Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries are both mathematically rich. Set theories with and without Choice support extensive mathematics. Large cardinal axioms and determinacy principles may each organize important regions of set theory while making incompatible claims. In such cases, methodological virtues may not select a single theory.

A realist can respond that underdetermination is not unique to mathematics. Physical theories can also be empirically equivalent or compatible with the available evidence. Scientific realism does not require that evidence always uniquely identify the true theory. It requires only that there be a fact of the matter and that evidence can rationally support belief about it. Yet the analogy is incomplete. In science, empirical constraints are at least in principle available. In foundational mathematics, the relevant structures may not be subject to independent observation. The realist’s faith in eventual convergence may therefore appear less grounded.

The best version of compromise Platonism should consequently be modest about what justification achieves. Intrinsic and extrinsic evidence can confer rational warrant without guaranteeing truth. The realist can maintain that mathematical statements are objectively true or false while conceding that our methods may leave some statements unresolved. The possibility of unresolved truth is compatible with bivalence. What must be rejected is not bivalence but the assumption that every truth is currently accessible through a decisive evidential procedure.

This response avoids the charge that compromise Platonism collapses into either pure Gödelian or pure Quinean Platonism. It does so by denying that priority must be assigned globally. Intrinsic evidence has special authority concerning conceptual coherence and structural naturalness; extrinsic evidence has special authority concerning mathematical integration and scientific representation. When they conflict, the conflict may indicate that the question has been formulated too broadly, that the two forms of evidence concern different domains, or that our present evidence is insufficient.

Still, this is a qualified victory. If the conflict remains direct after all distinctions are made—if intuition says that an axiom is false of the one intended mathematical universe and extrinsic practice provides equally strong evidence that it is true—then compromise Platonism has no simple algorithm for resolution. It must rely on reflective equilibrium, revision of concepts, and comparative assessment of theories. That may be the best available epistemology, but it lacks the decisive metaphysical authority often associated with Platonism.

## Conclusion

The challenge of compromise Platonism is not that it recognizes too many kinds of evidence. Its difficulty is that it must explain how those kinds of evidence bear on objective truth when they diverge. The Axiom of Choice and the parallel postulate show that mathematical intuition and extrinsic success can pull in different directions. Choice appears intrinsically suspect but extrinsically indispensable to much of modern mathematics. Euclidean geometry appears intrinsically natural as an account of space, while non-Euclidean geometry receives powerful mathematical and physical support.

Three straightforward responses are unstable. To prioritize intrinsic support is to revive the mysterious epistemology of Gödelian Platonism. To prioritize extrinsic support is to risk reducing mathematical truth to theoretical success and scientific indispensability. To abandon bivalent truth values avoids conflict only by relinquishing the traditional Platonist commitment to a determinate mathematical reality.

A more defensible realist position refuses to treat either intrinsic or extrinsic evidence as an infallible tribunal. Intrinsic justification can reveal conceptual and structural naturalness; extrinsic justification can reveal coherence, fruitfulness, and applicability. Their apparent conflicts may sometimes be dissolved by distinguishing mathematical structures from physical systems, or by recognizing that an axiom’s rational acceptability need not amount to certainty about its ultimate truth. In other cases, the realist must acknowledge genuine underdetermination and epistemic limitation.

Maddy’s compromise is therefore best understood not as a final synthesis that mechanically reconciles all evidence, but as a fallibilist realism about mathematical practice. It preserves the thought that mathematics concerns objective structures while allowing that access to those structures is mediated through intuition, theory construction, proof, application, and communal practice. Its success depends on accepting that realism does not require a single privileged evidential route. Nor does it require that every objective truth be presently decidable.

The resulting framework is less confident than pure Platonism and less reductive than indispensability naturalism. It does not wholly escape the traditional criticisms of either. Rather, it distributes and moderates them. The epistemological mystery of intuition remains, but it is constrained by mathematical practice. The threat of conventionalism remains, but it is constrained by the realist distinction between usefulness and truth. Compromise Platonism consequently survives only as a modest realism: a view on which mathematical reality is independent of us, while our evidence for it is plural, revisable, and sometimes permanently inconclusive.
