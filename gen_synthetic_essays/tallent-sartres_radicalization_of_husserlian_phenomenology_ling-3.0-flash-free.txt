The history of twentieth-century continental philosophy is, in many ways, the history of a profound filial rebellion. Jean-Paul Sartre stands as the most formidable critic of his mentor, Edmund Husserl, having turned the foundational principles of phenomenology against their founder. The central battleground of this intellectual rupture is the ontological status of consciousness itself. Is consciousness a "something"—a substantial entity, a hidden metaphysical realm, or a Cartesian res cogitans—or is it an "empty nothingness," a void that merely illuminates the world without adding any material or mental substance to it? Sartre’s critique of Husserl’s phenomenology, focused specifically on the rejection of the hyle (the sensuous matter of experience) and the transcendental ego, is the engine of this transformation. This essay argues that Sartre’s rejection of these concepts is a genuine radicalization of Husserl’s own project of eliminating hidden metaphysical realms, successfully establishing consciousness as an empty nothingness rather than a substantial entity. Furthermore, consciousness does not add a mental substance to reality; it merely functions as the pre-reflective self-consciousness of an existent's appearances.

To understand the radical nature of Sartre’s critique, one must first grasp the phenomenological architecture Husserl constructs in *Cartesian Meditations* and *Ideas I*. Husserl’s phenomenological reduction aims to bracket the natural attitude—the naive assumption of an independent external world—to uncover the absolute ground of all experience. In *Cartesian Meditations*, Husserl argues that the transcendental ego is the absolute "principle of all principles," the unconditioned ground of constitution. This ego is not the empirical, psychological self, but the pure transcendental subject that unifies the manifold of experience. Alongside this ego, Husserl posits the hyle—the sensuous manifold, the "stuff" of lived experience that is given in intuition. In *Ideas I*, Husserl elaborates on this, describing how the noesis (the act of consciousness) and noema (the object-as-intended) are correlated, with the hyle serving as the immanent, sensuous content that makes intentional acts possible. 

Crucially, Husserl insists that this transcendental ego and its hyle do not constitute a hidden metaphysical realm in the traditional sense. By bracketing the natural world, Husserl eliminates the Kantian noumenon and the metaphysical hiddenness of external objects. The transcendental ego and its immanent hyle are the only realities left. However, the very act of grounding experience in an absolute "something"—the transcendental ego—and a "stuff"—the hyle—creates a new metaphysical residue. Even if Husserl denies that the transcendental ego is a Cartesian soul-substance, structurally, it functions as an absolute, hidden ground behind the phenomena. The hyle, similarly, becomes the hidden, immanent "matter" that fills the consciousness. While Husserl claims to have eliminated hidden metaphysical realms by reducing the world to immanence, Sartre argues he has merely replaced the external metaphysical world with an internal one: the transcendental ego and its hyle.

Sartre’s critique, articulated most forcefully in *The Transcendence of the Ego* and *Being and Nothingness*, targets this very foundation. Sartre’s rejection of the hyle is rooted in his ontological commitment to the nothingness of consciousness. If consciousness possesses hyle—if it contains a "stuff" or material component—then consciousness risks becoming a substantial entity, a container that holds mental images and sensations. For Sartre, this is a reification that smuggles a new materialism into phenomenology. Consciousness, Sartre argues, cannot be a "something" that contains "somethings." If consciousness had hyle, it would be a substantial entity with properties, duration, and mass, essentially becoming a new form of Cartesian res extensa merged with res cogitans. By rejecting hyle as the material of consciousness, Sartre ensures that consciousness remains an empty nothingness. It is not a substance that possesses contents; it is a pure relation, a nihilation that posits the world without being constituted by it.

Similarly, Sartre’s rejection of the transcendental ego operates on the same logic. In *The Transcendence of the Ego*, Sartre demonstrates that the ego is not the absolute ground of consciousness but an object *within* consciousness. Consciousness is always intentional; it is always consciousness *of* something else. The ego is merely what consciousness makes of itself when it reflects upon itself. By making the transcendental ego the absolute ground of experience, Husserl reifies consciousness, turning it into a hidden metaphysical realm—a "thing" that stands behind the phenomena as their unconditioned source. Sartre argues that this transcendental ego functions as a new hidden metaphysical realm, a "thing-in-itself" of immanence that is inaccessible to the very phenomenological method that claims to describe immanence directly. By eliminating the transcendental ego, Sartre eliminates the last hidden metaphysical realm, ensuring that consciousness remains an empty nothingness rather than a substantial entity.

This brings us to the dialectical tension between Sartre’s phenomenological ontology in *Being and Nothingness* and Husserl’s formulations in *Cartesian Meditations* and *Ideas*. Is Sartre’s reading of the transcendental ego and hyletic data an unfair misinterpretation, or a genuine radicalization of Husserl’s original elimination of hidden metaphysical realms? 

On the surface, Sartre’s reading appears to be an unfair misinterpretation. Husserl explicitly distinguishes the transcendental ego from the empirical ego and the Cartesian soul-substance. For Husserl, the transcendental ego is not a "thing" but a structural, functional moment of constitution. It is the absolute "pole" of experience, not a hidden metaphysical substance lurking behind the scenes. Furthermore, Husserl’s hyle is not metaphysical matter in the Aristotelian or Cartesian sense; it is the "sensuous manifold" given in intuition, the immanent correlate of noetic acts. Husserl’s entire project is predicated on the idea that phenomenology eliminates all hidden metaphysical realms by restricting itself to what is given in immanence. To accuse Husserl of reintroducing a hidden metaphysical realm via the transcendental ego and hyle is to misunderstand the phenomenological reduction. The transcendental ego and hyle are not hidden behind the phenomena; they are the phenomena themselves, purified of the natural attitude. Thus, Sartre seems to unfairly conflate Husserl’s structural, functional transcendental ego with a substantial, hidden metaphysical ground.

However, this surface-level critique misses the deeper, dialectical logic of Sartre’s project. Sartre’s reading is a genuine radicalization of Husserl’s own elimination of hidden metaphysical realms. Husserl’s elimination of the external world is incomplete because he replaces it with an absolute internal ground. The transcendental ego, even as a structural moment, functions as an absolute "something" that grounds all experience. Any "something," however structural, risks becoming a hidden metaphysical realm if it stands as the unconditioned foundation of all that appears. Sartre radicalizes Husserl's anti-metaphysical impulse by pushing the reduction to its absolute limit. If phenomenology is to truly eliminate hidden metaphysical realms, it must eliminate the transcendental ego as an absolute ground. Sartre sees that Husserl’s transcendental ego is the last vestige of a metaphysics of presence—a "hidden" absolute that remains untouched by the phenomenological reduction. By rejecting the hyle and the transcendental ego, Sartre completes the phenomenological project Husserl began but could not finish. Consciousness is stripped of all substantiality, all "hidden" metaphysical foundations, and is revealed as a pure, empty nothingness.

This radicalization allows us to defend the specific position that consciousness does not add a mental substance to reality, but rather merely functions as the pre-reflective self-consciousness of an existent's appearances. In *Being and Nothingness*, Sartre develops a rigorous ontology of the for-itself (pour-soi) and the in-itself (en-soi). The in-itself is the brute, substantial presence of existence—thick, opaque, and self-identical. The for-itself is consciousness, which is defined by its nothingness. Consciousness is the nothingness that negates the brute facticity of the in-itself, allowing it to appear as an appear-ance. 

When consciousness encounters an existent—say, a tree—it does not add a mental substance to the tree. It does not posit a "mental tree" alongside a "physical tree" or overlay the tree with a subjective, mental image. Rather, consciousness is the empty nothingness that illuminates the tree as an appearance. The tree appears to consciousness, but consciousness adds nothing to the tree's being; it merely provides the void through which the tree is given. This is the essence of Sartre’s pre-reflective self-consciousness. When I am simply perceiving a tree, I am not reflecting on my own consciousness of the tree; I am absorbed in the tree itself. My consciousness is the pre-reflective self-consciousness of the tree's appearance. It is the empty nothingness that is aware of the tree, but it is not a "thing" that possesses the tree.
