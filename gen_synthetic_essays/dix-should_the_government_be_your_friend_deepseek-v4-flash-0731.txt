### The Hollow Polis: The State, Perfect Friendship, and the Locus of the Good Life

Aristotle famously declares in the *Politics* that “man is by nature a political animal.” This declaration is not merely a descriptive observation about human sociability; it is a normative claim about the very essence of human flourishing. The *polis*, or political community, is not a mere convenience for mutual protection but the highest form of community, existing for the sake of the “good life” (*eu zēn*). Yet, this Aristotelian vision confronts a profound and enduring tension. The state, by its very structural nature, is tasked with providing the necessary material and legal conditions for life—security, justice, and economic stability—but it is inherently incapable of fostering the highest and most complete form of human connection: perfect friendship (*teleia philia*). This essay will argue that while the Aristotelian state is a necessary and indispensable foundation for the good life, it is fundamentally insufficient for its completion. The requirements of perfect friendship—loving another as “another self” based on a shared essence of virtue—are irreconcilably opposed to the impersonal, pluralistic, and bureaucratic character of modern political regimes. Consequently, while the state cannot be entirely superseded in its instrumental role, the ultimate actualization of happiness and virtue must be sought in the intimate, pre-political spheres of the family and close peers, which serve as the true, complete arena for the realization of *eudaimonia*.

#### Part I: The State as the Necessary Condition for Eudaimonia

To evaluate the state's capacity to aid in the attainment of the good life, one must first clarify what Aristotle means by the good life. In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle posits that *eudaimonia*—usually translated as happiness or flourishing—is the highest good and the ultimate end of human action. It is not a passive state of pleasure but an “activity of the soul in accordance with perfect virtue” (*NE* 1102a5). This activity requires the cultivation of moral virtues (such as courage, temperance, and justice) and intellectual virtues (such as wisdom and understanding). Crucially, Aristotle insists that virtue is not innate; it is acquired through habituation. We become just by performing just acts, and courageous by performing courageous acts. This habituation requires a structured environment that rewards virtue and punishes vice.

Herein lies the state's primary and indispensable function. The state, through its laws and institutions, provides the *structural necessity* for moral development. As Aristotle argues in the *Politics*, the state comes into being “for the sake of life, but exists for the sake of the good life” (*Pol* 1252b29). The state is the *sine qua non* of human flourishing. It provides the material conditions necessary for leisure and contemplation, which are prerequisites for the exercise of virtue. Without a stable political community that ensures peace, defends against external threats, and regulates internal disputes, individuals would be consumed by the struggle for mere survival. The state's provision of a legal framework, a system of justice, and a common defense creates the stable environment in which individuals can develop their moral capacities without the constant fear of violence or deprivation.

Furthermore, the state plays a crucial pedagogical role. Aristotle famously states in the final book of the *Nicomachean Ethics* that “legislation is a matter of great importance” because it is through laws that citizens are habituated into virtue. The state's laws are not merely prohibitive; they are formative. They shape the character of citizens by rewarding certain behaviors and punishing others. A well-ordered state, such as the one Aristotle sketches in the *Politics*, will institute a system of public education designed to instill the virtues in its youth. The state, therefore, is the architect of the moral landscape. It provides the gymnasium, the school, the marketplace, and the courts—all of which are the training grounds for virtue. In this sense, the state is not merely a necessary evil but a positive good, the very condition of possibility for the moral life.

However, this structural necessity reveals a crucial limitation. The state's role is primarily *instrumental*. It provides the scaffolding, the external framework, within which individuals must actively pursue their own virtue. The state can make a person law-abiding, but it cannot make a person good in the deepest sense. It can prevent injustice through the threat of punishment, but it cannot inspire the internal disposition of justice. The state can create the conditions for leisure, but it cannot dictate the content of one's contemplative life. The state is, in a sense, a *deficient* community. It is too large, too abstract, and too impersonal to engage the individual soul at the level required for the highest forms of human excellence. This deficiency becomes most apparent when we turn to the concept of perfect friendship, which Aristotle considers the pinnacle of the social virtues and the very glue of the good life.

#### Part II: The Nature of Perfect Friendship and the “Another Self”

In Books VIII and IX of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle undertakes a detailed analysis of friendship (*philia*), which he elevates to a status that modern political theory often overlooks. He argues that friendship is not merely a pleasant addition to life but a necessity. “No one would choose to live without friends, even if he had all other goods” (*NE* 1155a5). Aristotle distinguishes between three types of friendship, differentiated by their object of love: friendships of utility, friendships of pleasure, and friendships of virtue.

Friendships of utility are based on mutual usefulness. We befriend people because they are beneficial to us—a business partner, a colleague, a political ally. These friendships are easily dissolved when the utility ceases. Friendships of pleasure are based on the enjoyment we derive from the other person—a companion for drinking, a witty conversationalist, a sports partner. Like friendships of utility, these are transient and contingent on the pleasure continuing. Both of these types are grounded in what the other person can do for us, not in who they are.

The third type, perfect friendship (*teleia philia*), is fundamentally different. It is based on mutual admiration of each other's virtue. It arises between people who are “good, and alike in virtue” (*NE* 1156b7). In this friendship, each person loves the other for the other's own sake, because of their character, not because of any utility or pleasure they provide. This is the highest and most complete form of friendship. It is rare, requires time and intimacy to develop, and is the most stable and enduring of all human bonds.

The defining feature of perfect friendship is the concept of the friend as “another self” (*allos autos*). Aristotle writes, “The friend is another self” (*NE* 1166a31). This is not a mere metaphor. It signifies a profound ontological and psychological identification. In perfect friendship, the boundaries between the self and the other become porous. One wishes good things for the friend for the friend's own sake, and one experiences the friend's joys and sorrows as one's own. The friend's flourishing is constitutive of one's own flourishing. This requires a deep, mutual recognition of each other's essence—the core of their character, their values, their virtues, and their aspirations. It requires that both individuals share a common conception of the good life and are committed to helping each other achieve it.

For this “shared essence” to be realized, certain conditions must be met. First, there must be *intimacy*. Perfect friendship requires “living together” (*suzēn*), sharing in conversation, thought, and activity. This cannot be achieved through occasional contact or formal interaction; it demands sustained, face-to-face presence. Second, there must be *equality*. While friendships can exist between unequals (parent and child, ruler and subject), perfect friendship presupposes a rough equality of virtue and status. Third, there must be *mutual recognition*. Each friend must see the other as a virtuous agent, and must be seen in turn. This recognition is not abstract or legalistic; it is a personal, particular, and loving acknowledgment of the other's unique goodness.

Perfect friendship is thus the crucible of the good life. It is within such friendships that virtue is most fully exercised and actualized. We become more virtuous by loving and being loved by the virtuous. We gain self-knowledge by seeing ourselves reflected in the eyes of a friend. We achieve a kind of immortality by extending our own being into the life of another. Aristotle goes so far as to say that the happy person needs friends because happiness is an activity, and it is more pleasant and more continuous when done in the company of friends. The state, with all its laws and institutions, cannot possibly provide this. It is structurally incapable of fostering perfect friendship.

#### Part III: The Incompatibility of Perfect Friendship and the Modern State

The modern political regime—whether liberal democratic, social democratic, or otherwise—differs radically from the Aristotelian *polis* in its scale, its principles, and its administrative logic. These differences render the state fundamentally hostile to the conditions required for perfect friendship.

First, consider the *impersonality* of the modern state. The state operates on the principle of formal equality. The law is blind; it applies to all citizens regardless of their particular character, virtue, or personal relationships. This is a monumental achievement of modern civilization, ensuring that no one is above the law and that all are treated with equal dignity. However, this very impersonality is antithetical to perfect friendship. Perfect friendship is radically personal. It is a relationship between *this* particular individual and *that* particular individual, based on their unique, shared essence. The state, by contrast, deals with its citizens as abstract bearers of rights. It sees a taxpayer, a voter, a litigant, a claimant—not a unique soul. The state's gaze is cold and universalizing. It cannot love a citizen for their virtue because it cannot see their virtue; it can only see their compliance with the law. The state's impersonality reduces the citizen to a role, whereas perfect friendship requires the full, unrepeatable person.

Second, the modern state is *pluralistic*. Unlike the relatively homogeneous Greek *polis*, which was small enough to share a common religion, a common set of heroes, and a common conception of the good life, the modern nation-state encompasses a vast diversity of cultures, religions, ethnicities, and moral worldviews. As John Rawls famously argued, modern societies are characterized by a “reasonable pluralism” of comprehensive doctrines. Citizens hold deeply conflicting views about what constitutes the good life. The state, therefore, cannot promote a single substantive conception of virtue without violating the freedom of its citizens. It must remain neutral between competing conceptions of the good. This neutrality is a cornerstone of liberal legitimacy. However, this very neutrality precludes the state from fostering the “shared essence” necessary for perfect friendship. Perfect friendship requires a robust, substantive agreement on what is good, what is virtuous, and what is worthy of love. The state cannot provide this because it cannot take a stance on these questions without betraying its pluralistic mandate. The modern state can only ensure that individuals are free to pursue their own conception of the good, but it cannot supply the content of that good. It leaves the individual alone to find their own companions, their own community of virtue.

Third, the modern state is *bureaucratic*. Max Weber famously described the modern state as an “iron cage” of rational-legal authority, governed by impersonal rules, hierarchical structures, and specialized expertise. The bureaucratic logic of the state is one of efficiency, calculation, and administration. It treats human beings as cases to be managed, problems to be solved, and resources to be allocated. The state's interactions with its citizens are mediated through forms, databases, and standardized procedures. This rationalization of life is profoundly dehumanizing in the context of perfect friendship. Perfect friendship is characterized by spontaneity, emotional depth, and uncalculating generosity. It is a relationship of love, not administration. The bureaucratic state cannot foster such relationships because its very logic is instrumental. It sees relationships as means to social outcomes (e.g., social cohesion, economic productivity) rather than as ends in themselves. The state may fund community centers or promote “civic engagement,” but these are bureaucratic interventions that cannot manufacture the deep, personal bond of *teleia philia*. They can only create the *appearance* of community.

Fourth, the sheer *scale* of the modern state makes perfect friendship impossible. Aristotle's *polis* was small enough that citizens could know each other by name. The modern nation-state, by contrast, contains millions or hundreds of millions of people. The state is a “community of strangers.” It is impossible to have a perfect friendship with someone you have never met, and it is impossible to meet, let alone know, the vast majority of your fellow citizens. The state's scale necessitates its abstract and bureaucratic nature. It cannot be intimate because intimacy requires proximity and shared time, which are impossible at the national level. The state can provide a framework of justice for strangers, but it cannot provide a framework of love for friends.

Thus, the modern state is not merely failing to foster perfect friendship; it is structurally incapable of doing so. The state's impersonal, pluralistic, bureaucratic, and large-scale nature are all features that make it an effective administrator of a complex society, but they are all features that preclude it from being a locus of perfect friendship. The state can ensure that I am not robbed by my neighbor, but it cannot make my neighbor my friend. It can guarantee my right to associate with whom I choose, but it cannot give me the community of virtue I crave.

#### Part IV: The Supersession of the State by Family and Close Peers

Given the state's inherent incapacity to foster perfect friendship, we must ask: where can the individual find the “another self” necessary for the completion of the good life? The answer, I argue, lies in the smaller, more intimate societal groupings that precede and exceed the state: the family and the circle of close peers. These are the true loci of perfect friendship and the ultimate arenas of human flourishing.

The *family* is the first and most natural community. Aristotle himself acknowledges that the household (*oikos*) is the primary association, existing to meet the daily needs of life. But the family is far more than an economic unit. It is the primordial school of virtue and the first experience of unconditional love. The parent-child relationship, while not a friendship of equals in the strict Aristotelian sense, provides the foundational experience of loving another as an extension of oneself. A parent sees their child as “another self” in the most literal sense—a part of their own being, a continuation of their own life. The parent's love is not contingent on the child's utility or pleasure; it is unconditional, based on the sheer fact of shared kinship and essence. This familial love provides the initial habituation into virtue. Within the family, the child learns the meaning of justice, courage, and temperance through the loving guidance of parents. The family is the crucible where the child's character is first formed.

However, the family alone is insufficient for perfect friendship because the parent-child relationship is inherently unequal. Perfect friendship, as we have seen, requires equality of virtue. The child must grow and mature to become a peer of the parent. The true completion of the good life, therefore, is found in the circle of *close peers*—the friends we choose. These are the individuals with whom we share a common commitment to virtue, with whom we can achieve the full reciprocity and mutual recognition that perfect friendship demands. In the company of close peers, we find the “another self” who is our equal, who understands our deepest aspirations, and who helps us become the best version of ourselves.

This is where the argument for *supersession* gains its force. If the state is structurally incapable of fostering perfect friendship, and if perfect friendship is essential for the good life, then the state cannot complete the individual's pursuit of happiness and virtue. The state provides the necessary conditions—peace, security, material goods—but it cannot provide the sufficient conditions. The sufficient conditions are found in the intimate sphere. Therefore, in the hierarchy of ends, the family and close peers must *supersede* the state. They are not merely subordinate parts of the state; they are the *telos* for which the state exists. The state is a means to an end, and that end is the flourishing of the individual within their intimate communities.

Consider the practical implications of this argument. A citizen of a modern democracy may enjoy all the rights and protections of the state—free speech, fair trial, social security—and yet still lead a profoundly unhappy and unfulfilled life if they lack a loving family and true friends. Conversely, an individual who is deprived of certain political rights but enjoys a rich network of intimate relationships may still achieve a high degree of *eudaimonia*. This is not to say that political rights are unimportant; they are crucial for the stability and justice that allow intimate communities to flourish. But it is to say that the *content* of the good life is not political. The good life is lived in the home, in the garden, in the shared meal, in the late-night conversation with a friend. The state is the stage, but the drama of life is played out in the smaller circles.

Furthermore, the state's attempt to intervene in these intimate spheres is often counterproductive. When the state tries to mold citizens into a particular vision of virtue, it risks becoming totalitarian. When it tries to provide “social services” to replace the functions of the family, it can erode the organic bonds of mutual care. The modern welfare state, for all its benefits, can inadvertently weaken the family by taking over its caring functions. The state, in its bureaucratic impersonality, cannot provide the love that a parent provides. It can provide a check, but it cannot provide a hug. Thus, the state must recognize its own limits. It must provide the framework of justice but step back from the attempt to be the primary source of meaning and virtue.

#### Part V: A Dialectical Resolution – Complementarity over Absolute Supersession

One might object that my argument for supersession leads to an untenable atomism or a retreat from civic responsibility. If the state is merely a means and the family is the end, why should we care about politics at all? Why not simply retreat into our private enclaves and ignore the public realm?

This objection rests on a misunderstanding of the relationship between the state and the intimate sphere. It is not a question of *either/or* but of *both/and*, with a clear hierarchy of final ends. The state is not superseded in the sense of being abolished or rendered irrelevant; it is superseded in the sense of being *transcended* as the ultimate locus of meaning. The state remains an absolutely necessary condition for the good life. Without the state's provision of peace, security, and material prosperity, the family and close friends cannot thrive. A family cannot cultivate virtue in a state of war or anarchy. A circle of friends cannot engage in philosophical contemplation if they are starving or fearing for their lives. The state is the *foundation* upon which the edifice of the good life is built.

However, a foundation is not the building itself. The state provides the *conditions of possibility*, but it does not provide the *content* of flourishing. The content is found in the intimate sphere. This is a dialectical relationship: the state enables the private sphere, and the private sphere gives the state its ultimate purpose. The state is not an end in itself; it is for the sake of the household and the friend. As Aristotle writes, the state is prior to the individual in time, but the individual's end is prior to the state. The state is the *material cause* of the good life, but the family and friends are the *final cause*.

This resolution has profound implications for how we understand citizenship. A good citizen is not merely someone who obeys the law or participates in elections. A good citizen is someone who cultivates virtue in their own soul and in their intimate communities, and who recognizes that the state is a necessary but insufficient vehicle for that cultivation. The state's role is to protect the *space* for these intimate communities to flourish. It must guarantee the freedom of association, the sanctity of the home, and the right to raise one's children according to one's own values (within reasonable limits). The state should be a *facilitator* of the good life, not a *substitute* for it.

Moreover, this perspective offers a powerful critique of modern political ideologies that seek to make the state the central source of identity and meaning. Whether it is nationalism, socialism, or a technocratic liberalism, the attempt to make the state the primary locus of human flourishing is doomed to fail because it cannot provide the love and intimacy of perfect friendship. The state can inspire a sense of solidarity or patriotic pride, but this is a poor substitute for the deep, personal bond of a friend who loves you for your virtue. The state can provide a sense of belonging to a large, abstract community, but it cannot provide the “another self” that completes the soul.

#### Conclusion

In conclusion, the Aristotelian state is a magnificent but hollow structure. It is a necessary and indispensable institution, providing the material, legal, and educational scaffolding upon which the good life must be built. Without the state, we would be reduced to a state of nature, incapable of developing the virtues that define human flourishing. Yet, the state is fundamentally incapable of fostering the highest form of human connection: perfect friendship. The requirements of perfect friendship—loving another as “another self” based on a shared essence of virtue—require intimacy, equality, mutual recognition, and shared time. These requirements are irreconcilably opposed to the impersonal, pluralistic, bureaucratic, and large-scale nature of modern political regimes. The state sees us as abstract citizens, not as unique souls.

Therefore, the individual's pursuit of happiness and virtue cannot be completed by the state. It must be completed in the smaller, more intimate societal groupings of the family and close peers. These groups are the true loci of perfect friendship, the arenas where we find the “another self” who helps us become who we are meant to be. While the state cannot be entirely superseded—it remains the necessary foundation—the family and close peers must be recognized as the *final* and *complete* arena of human flourishing. The state is the stage, but the drama of life is played out in the home and among friends.

The modern individual, living in a vast and often alienating political community, must remember Aristotle's profound insight: we are political animals, but we are also, and more fundamentally, loving animals. The state can give us laws, but only our family and friends can give us love. The state can provide us with security, but only our intimate circles can provide us with meaning. The good life is not found in the halls of government or the corridors of bureaucracy; it is found in the shared laughter of a family dinner, in the quiet wisdom of a trusted friend, and in the deep, mutual recognition of two souls who see each other as they truly are. The state is the necessary condition for this flourishing, but it is the family and the friend who are its ultimate completion. We must, therefore, look to the state for the conditions of life, but look to the intimate sphere for the good life itself.
