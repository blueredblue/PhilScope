In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle presents a nuanced and multifaceted account of personal friendship, one that challenges traditional notions of altruism and egoism. At its core, Aristotle's conception of friendship appears to embody a paradoxical blend of self-referential altruism, where the pursuit of personal eudaimonia (happiness or flourishing) is inextricably linked with a genuine concern for the well-being of others. This essay will delve into the complexities of Aristotle's theory, examining the tension between achieving personal eudaimonia and maintaining genuine concern for friends, while also analyzing the role of self-concern in the origin and development of friendships.

Aristotle's concept of friendship is rooted in the idea that friends are, in a sense, "another self" (heteros autos). This notion suggests that friends are an extension of oneself, a mirror image that reflects one's own values, desires, and aspirations. The friend, in this context, is not merely an external entity, but an integral part of one's own identity. This idea is reinforced by Aristotle's claim that "what is most one's own is most dear" (NE 1168b35-1169a1), implying that the friend is, in a way, an aspect of oneself. This intimate connection between self and friend raises important questions about the nature of altruism and egoism in friendships.

On one hand, the idea that friends are "another self" implies a deep-seated concern for their well-being, one that is not solely driven by self-interest. Aristotle argues that true friends wish for the good of their friends for the friends' own sake, rather than merely for their own benefit (NE 1155b31-1156a5). This emphasis on the friend's own sake suggests a genuine altruistic concern, one that prioritizes the friend's eudaimonia over one's own. However, this altruistic impulse is also deeply rooted in self-concern, as the friend's well-being is, in a sense, an extension of one's own. The friend's happiness and flourishing are, in this view, essential to one's own eudaimonia, as they contribute to a sense of completeness and wholeness.

This interplay between self-concern and altruism is at the heart of Aristotle's theory of friendship. While it may seem counterintuitive to suggest that self-concern can be the foundation of altruistic relationships, Aristotle argues that it is precisely this self-referential aspect that enables us to genuinely care for others. By recognizing the friend as "another self," we are able to extend our own self-concern to include the friend's well-being, thereby creating a sense of shared identity and community. This synthesis of self-concern and altruism allows Aristotle to overcome the traditional dichotomy between pure egoism and pure altruism, presenting a more nuanced and realistic account of human relationships.

However, this synthesis is not without its limitations and challenges. One of the primary concerns is that Aristotle's theory restricts altruistic concern to close associates and family, implying that our moral obligations are primarily directed towards those with whom we have a personal connection. This raises questions about the scope and universality of moral concern, as it seems to prioritize the well-being of those closest to us over the greater good. While Aristotle acknowledges the importance of universal moral principles, such as justice and fairness, his emphasis on personal relationships and friendships may be seen as undermining the unselfish nature of good friendship.

Another challenge to Aristotle's theory is the persistent presence of self-seeking elements in friendships. Even if we prioritize the friend's well-being for their own sake, there is always a risk that our actions may be motivated, at least in part, by self-interest. For example, our desire to help a friend in need may be driven by a sense of obligation, guilt, or a desire for reciprocal benefits, rather than purely altruistic concerns. This ambiguity highlights the complexity of human motivations and the difficulties of distinguishing between selfless and selfish actions.

In response to these challenges, Aristotle would likely argue that the presence of self-seeking elements in friendships does not necessarily undermine their altruistic nature. Rather, he would suggest that true friendships are characterized by a dynamic interplay between self-concern and altruism, where the two are inextricably linked. In this view, the pursuit of personal eudaimonia is not mutually exclusive with a genuine concern for the well-being of others; rather, the two are interconnected and interdependent. By recognizing the friend as "another self," we are able to integrate our own self-concern with a deep-seated concern for the friend's well-being, thereby creating a rich and multifaceted relationship that is greater than the sum of its parts.

Ultimately, Aristotle's theory of friendship presents a compelling and nuanced account of personal relationships, one that challenges traditional notions of altruism and egoism. While the synthesis of self-concern and altruism may be imperfect, it offers a more realistic and humane understanding of human relationships, one that acknowledges the complexities and ambiguities of human motivations. By recognizing the friend as "another self," we are able to create a sense of shared identity and community, one that is rooted in a deep-seated concern for the well-being of others. This, in turn, enables us to achieve a deeper understanding of ourselves and our place in the world, as we come to recognize that our own eudaimonia is inextricably linked with the eudaimonia of those around us.

In conclusion, Aristotle's concept of personal friendship in the *Nicomachean Ethics* constitutes a unique and fascinating blend of self-referential altruism, where the pursuit of personal eudaimonia is intertwined with a genuine concern for the well-being of others. While this synthesis is not without its limitations and challenges, it offers a more nuanced and realistic account of human relationships, one that acknowledges the complexities and ambiguities of human motivations. By examining the role of self-concern in the origin and development of friendships, we are able to gain a deeper understanding of the nature of altruism and egoism, and the ways in which these two seemingly opposing forces can be integrated and reconciled. As we strive to cultivate good friendships and achieve personal eudaimonia, we would do well to remember Aristotle's wisdom, that "the friend is another self," and that our own well-being is inextricably linked with the well-being of those around us.
