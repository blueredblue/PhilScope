## The Eternal Present: Divine Foreknowledge, Libertarian Freedom, and the Illusion of Altering the Past

The classical problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom presents one of the most intractable dilemmas in philosophical theology. If God is omniscient, then He infallibly knows, from all eternity, every action a human agent will perform. If God’s knowledge is infallible, then it is a necessary truth that whatever He knows will occur, will occur. Yet if an agent acts freely in the libertarian sense—that is, if she possesses the genuine power to do otherwise, to choose among alternative possibilities—then it seems she must have the power to render false the proposition that she will perform a given action. But if God infallibly knew that she would perform that action, then she lacks the power to make God’s belief false. The agent is thus trapped in a web of logical necessity, and libertarian freedom appears to be an illusion.

This essay will argue that this apparent incompatibility is not a genuine logical contradiction but rather a category error born of conflating human temporal intuitions with the divine mode of existence. Specifically, I will examine the sophisticated incompatibilist argument advanced by Blake Ostler, which contends that human freedom requires the power to bring it about that God held different past beliefs. Ostler’s argument, I will contend, rests on a flawed ontological assumption about the nature of time and the fixity of the past—an assumption rooted in the A-theory of time. By contrast, when we separate human intuitions about time, memory, and causal efficacy from the divine perspective—a perspective that is more adequately captured by the B-theory of time or a timeless eternity—we find that the alleged power to “change God’s past beliefs” involves no logical impossibility whatsoever. The apparent dilemma dissolves once we recognize that God does not have a “past” in the human sense, and that human agency does not require altering an immutable past, but rather actualizing one of several tenselessly true propositions.

### I. The Dilemma of Foreknowledge and Libertarian Freedom

To properly assess the compatibility of infallible divine foreknowledge (IDF) and human libertarian freedom (LF), we must first define our terms with precision. Libertarian freedom, as understood in the contemporary philosophical literature, typically involves two core components. First, the *principle of alternative possibilities* (PAP): an agent S acts freely at time *t* only if S could have done otherwise at *t*. Second, the *sourcehood condition*: the agent must be the ultimate originator of her action, such that her choice is not determined by factors external to her own agency—whether those be causal laws, prior states of the world, or divine decrees. For the libertarian, free will is a power of self-determination that breaks the chain of deterministic causation.

Infallible divine foreknowledge, on the other hand, is a property of an omniscient God. It entails that for any proposition *p* about a future free action, God knows *p* infallibly. This knowledge is not merely probabilistic or conjectural; it is certain and necessarily true. The classic statement of the problem is straightforward. Let *S* be an agent, and let *A* be an action that *S* will perform at time *t₂*. Suppose God, at an earlier time *t₁*, infallibly believes that *S* will do *A* at *t₂*. Given the infallibility of God’s belief, it is necessarily true that *S* will do *A* at *t₂*. If *S* were to do otherwise—say, action *B*—then God’s belief at *t₁* would have been false, which is impossible. Therefore, *S* cannot do otherwise than *A* at *t₂*. Hence, *S* lacks PAP and, consequently, lacks libertarian freedom.

This argument, in its simplest form, relies on a principle of the *fixity of the past*. The past is what it is; it cannot be changed. God’s belief at *t₁* is a fact about the past. Since the past is immutable, *S* has no power to make it the case that God held a different belief. Thus, *S* is constrained by the past in a way that is incompatible with libertarian freedom.

### II. Blake Ostler’s Incompatibilist Argument

Blake Ostler, a philosopher of religion within the Latter-day Saint tradition, has articulated a particularly forceful version of this incompatibilist argument. Ostler’s argument is notable for its explicit focus on the *power* of the agent to alter the past, rather than merely the logical contradiction involved. He argues that if God has infallible foreknowledge, then the content of God’s past beliefs is a *hard fact* about the world—a fact that is entirely independent of any human action. Consequently, for an agent to have libertarian freedom, she must possess the power to bring it about that God held different past beliefs. Since the past is inalterable, this power is impossible, and therefore libertarian freedom is impossible.

Ostler’s argument can be formalized as follows:

1.  If an agent *S* has libertarian freedom with respect to an action *A* at *t₂*, then *S* has the power to bring it about that God’s belief at *t₁* (that *S* will do *A* at *t₂*) is different.
2.  *S* does not have the power to bring it about that God’s belief at *t₁* is different, because the past is inalterable.
3.  Therefore, *S* does not have libertarian freedom with respect to *A*.

The crucial premise here is the first one. Ostler contends that the very meaning of libertarian freedom entails the ability to make a difference in the world, including a difference in the truth-value of past propositions. If God infallibly believed that *S* would do *A*, and *S* instead does *B*, then the proposition “God believed that *S* would do *A*” would be false. But for *S* to make that proposition false, *S* would have to act in a way that renders God’s past belief false. This, Ostler argues, is a power to alter the past. Since we intuitively recognize that no one can alter the past, and since God’s beliefs are part of the past, *S* lacks the requisite power.

Ostler’s argument is powerful because it shifts the focus from the abstract logical necessity of propositions to the concrete power of the agent. He insists that the libertarian cannot merely say, “Well, *S* does *A*, and God knew it all along.” The libertarian must also explain what it is that *S* has the power to do. If *S* has the power to do otherwise, then *S* has the power to actualize a state of affairs that is incompatible with the past. But the past is fixed, so *S* lacks that power. The only way out, Ostler suggests, is to deny the fixity of the past, which seems absurd, or to deny that God’s beliefs are part of the past, which he argues is ad hoc.

### III. The Ontology of Time: A-Theory vs. B-Theory

The linchpin of Ostler’s argument is the assumption that the past is inalterable in a specific sense—namely, that the past is a fixed, determinate, and ontologically privileged region of time. This assumption is characteristic of the A-theory of time. The A-theory, which includes presentism and the growing block theory, holds that time is tensed. There is a fundamental distinction between past, present, and future. The present is ontologically privileged; it is real in a way that the past and future are not (presentism), or the past and present are real while the future is not (growing block). On the A-theory, the past is “closed” or “fixed” because it has already happened. Events in the past have a determinate character that cannot be changed. We cannot causally interact with the past; we can only remember it. This is the intuitive, common-sense view of time that underlies Ostler’s argument.

The B-theory of time, by contrast, holds that time is tenseless. All times—past, present, and future—are equally real. The distinctions between past, present, and future are merely subjective features of human consciousness, not objective features of reality. On the B-theory, an event is not “past” or “future” *simpliciter*; it is merely earlier than or later than another event. The universe is a four-dimensional block of spacetime, and all events within that block are ontologically on par. There is no privileged “now” that flows from the past into the future.

The choice between these theories has profound implications for the problem of foreknowledge. If we adopt the A-theory, the past is fixed in a robust sense. God’s belief at *t₁* is a past event, and it is immutable. Ostler’s argument gains traction because it leverages this intuition. However, if we adopt the B-theory, the past is not “fixed” in the dynamic sense of being closed off. Rather, the past is simply a tenselessly existing region of spacetime. The fact that an event occurs at *t₁* does not make it more or less real than an event at *t₂*. The notion of “altering the past” becomes incoherent on the B-theory because there is no single, flowing present that could be altered; all events simply exist tenselessly.

Yet, the B-theory alone does not immediately solve the problem of foreknowledge. Even on the B-theory, God’s belief at *t₁* is a fact. If God is a temporal being, then He has beliefs at various times, and those beliefs are part of the tenseless manifold. The proposition “God believes at *t₁* that *S* does *A* at *t₂*” is a tenseless truth. If *S* does *B* at *t₂*, then that proposition is false. The question remains: does *S* have the power to make that proposition false? The B-theory does not, by itself, answer this question. It merely removes the dynamic, flowing character of time.

### IV. The Divine Perspective and the Problem of Memory

Herein lies the crux of my argument. Ostler’s incompatibilism fails because it imports a distinctly human, A-theoretic intuition about time into the divine mode of existence. Specifically, it treats God as a temporal being who has a “past” that is analogous to the human past, complete with memories and a fixed history. But classical theism—and indeed, the most philosophically coherent account of omniscience—holds that God is not a temporal being at all. God is eternal, existing outside of time altogether. This is not a mere ad hoc escape; it is a deeply rooted theological and philosophical commitment. God, as the creator of time itself, cannot be subject to time. His knowledge is not a sequence of beliefs that change over time; it is a single, timeless, and all-encompassing intuition of reality.

Once we adopt this divine perspective, the phrase “God’s past beliefs” becomes a category error. God does not have past beliefs, because God does not have a past. He does not remember events, because memory presupposes a temporal gap between the remembered event and the act of remembering. God’s knowledge is not a memory of what has happened; it is a direct, simultaneous, and tenseless apprehension of all events—past, present, and future—as a single, unified whole. This is often described as God’s *simultaneous* knowledge, or His *eternal present*.

This distinction is crucial for evaluating Ostler’s argument. Ostler claims that for *S* to have libertarian freedom, *S* must have the power to bring it about that God held different past beliefs. But if God does not have past beliefs, then this requirement is nonsensical. The correct formulation is that *S* must have the power to bring it about that God’s *eternal* knowledge has a certain content. And this is not a power to alter the past; it is a power to *determine* the content of an eternal truth.

Consider the following analogy. Imagine a mathematical equation, *x* + 2 = 5. The equation is timeless; it has always been true and will always be true. However, the value of *x* is not fixed independently of the equation’s solution. If we say that *x* = 3, the equation is true. If we say that *x* = 4, the equation is false. The equation does not “change” when we plug in a different value; rather, the truth-value of the equation is *determined by* the value we assign to *x*. Similarly, God’s eternal knowledge is like a timeless equation. The proposition “God knows that *S* does *A* at *t₂*” is tenselessly true. But the *content* of that knowledge is not independent of *S*’s action. Rather, *S*’s action at *t₂* is the truthmaker for God’s eternal knowledge. If *S* freely chooses to do *A*, then God eternally knows that *S* does *A*. If *S* freely chooses to do *B*, then God eternally knows that *S* does *B*. In either case, God’s knowledge is infallible and unchanging. The difference is not in God’s knowledge—which is eternally fixed—but in *S*’s choice, which is genuinely free.

The problem of memory further illuminates this point. Human beings experience the past as fixed because we have memories of it, and our memories are causally connected to past events. We cannot change our memories because the events they represent have already occurred. But God does not have memories in this sense. God’s knowledge is not causally dependent on the events He knows; it is a direct, intuitive, and timeless apprehension. Therefore, the fixity that we associate with memory—the sense that the past is “over and done with”—does not apply to God. God’s knowledge is not a record of a completed past; it is a timeless vision of the whole of reality.

### V. Causal Loops and Logical Impossibility

One might object that even if we grant God’s timelessness, the problem of causal loops remains. If *S*’s action at *t₂* is the truthmaker for God’s knowledge, then it seems that *S*’s action *causes* God to have a certain belief. But God’s knowledge is eternal, existing before *t₂* in a tenseless sense. This would entail a backward causal loop: *S*’s action at *t₂* causes God’s knowledge at *t₁*, which in turn causes *S*’s action at *t₂*. This is a logical impossibility, as it would require an event to be the cause of itself.

This objection, however, misunderstands the nature of the relationship between *S*’s action and God’s knowledge. The relationship is not causal; it is *logical* or *constitutive*. *S*’s action does not *cause* God to know something; rather, *S*’s action *constitutes* the content of God’s knowledge. There is no temporal directionality involved. In a timeless framework, there is no “before” or “after” between God and the event. God’s knowledge is not located at *t₁*; it is located in eternity, which is not a point on the timeline. Therefore, the concept of a causal loop—which requires a temporal sequence of cause and effect—is inapplicable.

To illustrate, consider the proposition “2 + 2 = 4.” This proposition is timelessly true. Does the fact that I write “2 + 2 = 4” on a piece of paper *cause* the proposition to be true? No. The proposition is true independently of my writing it. My writing merely *instantiates* the truth. Similarly, *S*’s action at *t₂* does not cause God’s eternal knowledge to be true; it *instantiates* the truth that God eternally knows. The relationship is one of logical entailment, not efficient causation.

Furthermore, the alleged logical impossibility of changing the past is itself a product of human temporal intuition. The phrase “changing the past” presupposes a temporal sequence in which the past exists at one time and is then altered at a later time. But in a timeless framework, there is no “later time” from which one could alter the past. The past, the present, and the future are all equally real and tenselessly fixed. The question is not whether *S* can change the past, but whether *S* can *determine* which of several tenselessly true propositions obtains. And this is precisely what libertarian freedom requires. *S* has the power to actualize a state of affairs that makes one set of eternal truths true rather than another. This is not a power to change the past; it is a power to *create* the past, to determine what the eternal facts are.

### VI. Assessing the Claim of Logical Impossibility

Let us now directly assess Ostler’s central claim: that human freedom requires the power to bring it about that God held different past beliefs. I have argued that this claim is false because it rests on a mistaken conception of God as a temporal being with a past. However, let us grant, for the sake of argument, that God is temporal and that His beliefs are located in the past. Even then, Ostler’s argument fails to establish a logical impossibility.

The key is to distinguish between the *necessity of the consequence* and the *necessity of the consequent*. It is necessarily true that if God infallibly believes that *S* will do *A* at *t₂*, then *S* will do *A* at *t₂*. This is a logical truth. However, it does not follow that *S*’s doing *A* is itself necessary. The necessity of the implication does not transfer to the consequent. In modal logic, this is the distinction between □(P → Q) and P → □Q. The former is true, but the latter is false. *S* is not forced to do *A*; rather, it is necessarily true that *if* God believes *S* will do *A*, *then* *S* will do *A*. But *S* has the power to do *B*, and if *S* were to do *B*, then God would have believed that *S* would do *B*.
