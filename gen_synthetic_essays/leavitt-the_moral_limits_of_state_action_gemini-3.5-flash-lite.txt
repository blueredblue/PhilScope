# The Architecture of Coercion: Kant, Rosen, and the Moral Limits of State Beneficence

## Introduction

In contemporary political philosophy, the modern welfare state occupies an ambiguous, heavily contested moral terrain. On one hand, it is routinely defended as the institutional realization of collective beneficence—a mechanism through which a society ameliorates structural inequalities, prevents destitution, and expresses a duty of care toward its most vulnerable members. On the other hand, this beneficence is rarely voluntary; it is extracted through the inescapable apparatus of coercive taxation. 

This raises a foundational question that strikes at the heart of liberal political theory: Does the state possess an independent moral duty of beneficence, or is such a mandate fundamentally incompatible with the principles of individual freedom, equality, and rightful autonomy?

To evaluate this problem, one must turn to the rigorous framework of Immanuel Kant’s political philosophy, particularly as articulated in the *Metaphysics of Morals*. Kant’s legal and political thought has inspired deeply divergent interpretations. Prominent among contemporary engagements is Allen D. Rosen’s reading in *Kantian Justice*, which attempts to reconcile state-enforced welfare with Kantian principles of right. Rosen’s account contends that a just state must secure the conditions of rightful honor and ensure that no citizen falls into a state of absolute dependence that compromises their moral agency. 

However, this reading confronts a formidable alternative framework grounded strictly in the principles of individual rights, external freedom, and the strict limits of rightful coercion. This framework insists that Kantian justice is juridical, not ethical; it protects the boundaries of external freedom and categorically rejects the state’s usurpation of moral duties that belong properly to the individual’s internal capacity for virtue.

This essay evaluates whether the state possesses an independent moral duty of beneficence by analyzing the tension between Rosen’s interpretation of Kantian justice and the rights-based framework. It investigates how the Categorical Imperative and the foundational principles of freedom—specifically liberty and equality—constrain legitimate state action, particularly regarding the taxation of citizens to support the needy. 

Furthermore, it examines the crucial distinction between the "united will" (*vereinigt Willkür*) and mere popular majority consent in the authorization of public law. Through this analysis, I will resolve the persistent dilemma of whether positive state welfare measures inherently violate individual freedom under Kant’s system, arguing that while the state cannot possess a *moral* duty of beneficence in the Kantian sense, it does possess a foundational *juridical* necessity to secure the civil union through the provision of a system that prevents citizens from sinking into conditions of absolute dependence.

## 1. The Kantian Foundation: Right, Virtue, and the Split Legal Order

To understand the debate over state beneficence, one must first delineate the architecture of Kant’s moral and political philosophy. Kant sharply distinguishes between ethics (*Tugendlehre*) and right (*Rechtslehre*). This distinction is not merely administrative; it is ontological, rooted in the structure of human agency, autonomy, and the nature of the will.

Ethics commands the cultivation of virtue, requiring agents to act from the *duty* of beneficence. As Kant argues in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* and the Doctrine of Virtue, the maxim of helping others in distress is a universalizable duty of love (*officium caritatis*). Every rational agent possesses an internal, imperfect duty to promote the happiness of others, precisely because we are ourselves vulnerable beings who inevitably require the assistance of our fellows. 

Crucially, however, ethical duties are characterized by their latitude and their dependence on internal incentives. An ethical duty cannot be externally coerced without destroying its moral worth. If an agent gives to the poor merely out of fear of state imprisonment or police enforcement, the action lacks moral content; it is a mechanical performance rather than a manifestation of a good will acting from duty.

Right (*Recht*), by contrast, governs the external relations of free agents. As Kant famously defines it in the *Metaphysics of Morals*: 

> "Right is therefore the sum of the conditions under which the choice of one can be united with the choice of another in accordance with a universal law of freedom."

The domain of Right is entirely indifferent to the internal motives of agents. It concerns exclusively the external collision of liberties. Because external freedom of action can be constrained by physical impediments, Kant argues that the prevention of such impediments—specifically, the prevention of hindrances to freedom—is itself consistent with freedom. Consequently, Right authorizes coercion. Coercion that counters a hindrance to freedom is, in Kant’s formulation, "a hindrance to or resistance against freedom" and is therefore "consistent with freedom in accordance with universal laws."

This distinction establishes a structural wall between the juridical and the ethical. The state belongs entirely to the domain of Right, not ethics. The state is an association of human beings under laws of coercion, designed to secure the external freedom of each against the arbitrary will of others. If the state attempts to enforce ethical duties—such as the duty of beneficence—it risks committing a category mistake. It treats internal moral character as an object of public legislation, thereby violating the fundamental autonomy of the rational agent.

Yet, this tidy division is precisely what is tested when we confront poverty, destitution, and the structural inequalities of civil society. Can a state founded purely on the juridical protection of external freedom remain indifferent to the material survival of its citizens? To answer this, we must examine the specific interpretation advanced by Allen D. Rosen.

## 2. Allen D. Rosen’s Interpretation of Kantian Justice

In *Kantian Justice*, Allen D. Rosen undertakes the formidable task of defending a robust conception of distributive justice and state-mandated welfare from within a Kantian framework. Rosen argues that standard libertarian readings of Kant—which restrict the state to a minimalist "night-watchman" role focused exclusively on contract enforcement and physical protection—misunderstand the deep moral architecture of Kant’s concept of right.

Rosen’s thesis hinges on the idea that Kantian justice is not merely formal or procedural, but substantive. For Rosen, the rightful condition (*rechtlicher Zustand*) is not merely a legal mechanism for resolving disputes among property owners; it is a moral prerequisite for the realization of human freedom. A person cannot exercise their external freedom or meaningfully participate as an equal citizen in a commonwealth if they are reduced to a state of absolute destitution or dependence upon the arbitrary grace of others.

To support this reading, Rosen draws attention to Kant’s explicit remarks in the *Metaphysics of Morals* regarding the sovereign’s power to tax the people to maintain the poor:

> "The general will of the people has united itself into a society in order to perpetually maintain this society; and for this it has submitted itself to the internal authority of the state for the sake of maintaining those members of the society who are unable to maintain themselves. For the sovereign has authorized the people to contribute from their own property what is necessary to maintain those who cannot provide for themselves."

According to Rosen, this passage reveals that the state’s provision for the poor is not an act of discretionary charity, nor is it an optional ethical policy. Rather, it is a structural requirement of the civil union itself. Rosen contends that in entering the civil state, individuals do not merely surrender their executive power to punish; they enter into a reciprocal pact of survival. 

The state, acting as the embodiment of the general will, has an independent mandate to ensure that the civil constitution does not reduce any citizen to a subhuman or rightless status where their survival depends on the private beneficence of the wealthy. Such dependence violates the core Kantian principle of independence (*Selbständigkeit*), which Kant identifies as a requisite for full citizenship.

In Rosen’s reading, the state’s duty of beneficence (or what might more accurately be termed its duty of distributive provision) is derived from the very concept of the rightful condition. Without it, the state fails to secure the conditions under which the freedom of *all* citizens can be universally reconciled. If the commonwealth permits some members to starve or sink into total destitution, the legal order ceases to be an association of free and equal beings and instead becomes an instrument of oppression, where the property of the rich is protected by state coercion while the very survival of the poor is rendered precarious.

Thus, Rosen bridges the gap between right and welfare by arguing that material security is a *precondition* for external freedom. Coercive taxation for the needy is not a violation of property rights; it is the realization of the rightful condition, ensuring that property itself remains a legitimate institution grounded in a universal system of reciprocal freedom.

## 3. The Rights-Based Counter-Framework and the Critique of State Welfare

Despite the elegance and moral appeal of Rosen’s interpretation, it encounters fierce resistance from defenders of the strict rights-based framework. This alternative reading—championed by various libertarian and classical liberal Kantians—insists that Rosen smuggles ethical teleology into a juridical theory that has no room for it.

The core objection of the rights-based framework centers on the inviolability of private property and the nature of external freedom. For Kant, property is not a grant bestowed by the state; it is an intelligible possession acquired prior to the state in the "state of nature" (as a postulate of practical reason regarding the usability of objects). While private property in the state of nature is provisional and lacks the definitive security of public law, its underlying moral title originates in the agent’s capacity to put an object under their will.

When the state institutes coercive taxation to fund welfare programs, it seizes a portion of the citizen’s legitimately acquired property without their direct consent. Under the rights-based reading, this constitutes an infringement upon external freedom. External freedom, as Kant defines it, is the independence from being constrained by another’s choice, except through universal laws that apply symmetrically to all. 

If the state taxes A to support B not because A has committed a tort against B, or because A has broken a contract, but simply because B is poor, the state is treating A as a mere means to B’s happiness. This directly violates the formula of the Categorical Imperative that commands humanity—in one's own person as well as in the person of every other—never to be used merely as a means, but always simultaneously as an end.

Critics within this framework argue that Rosen conflates *poverty* with *rights violations*. While poverty is an undeniable human tragedy and an appropriate object for ethical action, it is not, in itself, an injustice perpetrated by the wealthy or by the state. Unless the poor person’s destitution is the result of state-sponsored coercion, legal monopoly, or rights violations, their condition is a misfortune, not an injustice. 

To use the coercive apparatus of the state to correct misfortune is to confuse the distinct spheres of Right and Virtue. It transforms the state from an impartial arbiter of external freedom into a moralizing agency that enforces compulsory charity.

Furthermore, the rights-based framework warns against the slippery slope of state-managed beneficence. Once the state is granted an independent moral duty of beneficence, there is no principled limit to the expansion of state power. Every social inequality, every disparity in talent, luck, or ambition, can be framed as a "hindrance to freedom" or a failure of the rightful condition. 

The state assumes the role of an overarching paternalistic guardian, dictating how the fruits of citizens' labor are distributed and eroding the very domain of individual moral responsibility. If individuals are legally relieved of the duty to care for the poor through state taxation, the moral muscle of society atrophies. Beneficence ceases to be a virtuous expression of an autonomous will and becomes a tax code compliance issue.

## 4. The Categorical Imperative, Liberty, and Equality as Constraints on Legitimate Action

To navigate the tension between Rosen’s substantive justice and the rights-based framework, we must examine how the Categorical Imperative and the foundational principles of freedom—specifically liberty and equality—constrain legitimate state action.

Kant’s political philosophy is famously anchored in three *a priori* principles of a rightful commonwealth:
1. The freedom of every member of society as a human being.
2. The equality of every member with every other as a subject.
3. The independence of every member of a commonwealth as a citizen.

These principles serve as the ultimate litmus test for the legitimacy of any public law or state action. Let us analyze how they apply to the specific question of coercive taxation for the needy.

### Liberty

Liberty, in Kant’s juridical sense, means that no one can compel me to be happy in accordance with their conception of the welfare of others. Kant is explicit on this point in *On the Common Saying: "That May Be Correct in Theory, But It Is No Use in Practice"*:

> "A paternal government (19:*imperium paternum*), where the subjects are treated as minor children who cannot distinguish what is truly useful or harmful to them, is the greatest despotism thinkable."

If the state undertakes welfare measures out of a paternalistic desire to make citizens happy, or to enforce a particular vision of social justice, it violates the liberty of the citizens. 

However, Kant distinguishes between paternalistic legislation and legislation required to maintain the civil union itself. If taxation for the poor is framed not as paternalistic care for their happiness, but as a juridical requirement to prevent the dissolution of the state and the reversion of citizens to a state of nature, the constraint of liberty is justified. 

Why? Because in entering the civil state, individuals alienate their wild, lawless freedom in exchange for lawful freedom. A civil union in which a significant portion of the population is left to perish or sink into absolute destitution is inherently unstable and self-contradictory. It fails the test of universalizability because a rational agent could not will a universal law of a commonwealth that permits the systematic abandonment of its members, since any agent could fall into such a state of destitution through misfortune.

### Equality

Equality in the Kantian commonwealth is strictly civil equality: the principle that no subject can have a hierarchical, legally privileged status over another, and that all are equally subject to the coercive authority of the law. Crucially, Kantian equality is entirely compatible with wide disparities in economic wealth. 

A Kantian state is not tasked with achieving distributive economic egalitarianism. It does not demand that the wealth of citizens be equalized. Indeed, Kant defends the right of individuals to acquire unequal amounts of property through their labor, talent, and enterprise.

Yet, this civil equality contains a paradox. If economic inequality becomes so extreme that one class of citizens is entirely dependent on the arbitrary will of another class for their very survival, civil equality is hollowed out. A person who is entirely dependent on a wealthy benefactor for food and shelter is not an independent citizen; they are structurally reduced to the status of a dependent—akin to a domestic servant, minor, or dependant—who lacks the independence required to be an autonomous co-legislator in the commonwealth. 

Therefore, equality as a foundational constraint requires that the state prevent the creation of a permanent, rightless underclass whose survival depends on private charity.

### Independence (*Selbständigkeit*)

Independence is the linchpin of Kant’s political theory. Kant defines an independent citizen as one who owes their existence and support not to the arbitrary choice of another person, but to their own rights, property, and skill. 

This leads to Kant’s notorious (and deeply problematic) exclusion of domestic servants, day laborers, women, and apprentices from active citizenship and the right to vote. Kant argues that these individuals lack civil independence because their livelihood depends on the will of others.

While Kant’s historical application of this principle reflects the class and gender prejudices of late 18th-century Prussia, the philosophical principle itself is profound. If the state’s legitimacy rests upon the idea that laws are authorized by a united will of independent citizens, then a state that allows its citizens to be structurally stripped of their independence undermines its own foundational premise. 

Thus, coercive taxation to support the needy is constrained by the very logic of independence: it must be designed not to create a permanent class of passive welfare recipients dependent on state bureaucracy, but to secure the baseline conditions of independence that allow individuals to function as autonomous co-legislators.

## 5. The United Will Versus Popular Majority Consent

Having established the normative constraints of liberty, equality, and independence, we must address the procedural question of how public laws—including tax-funded welfare measures—are legitimately authorized. Herein lies one of the most sophisticated and demanding components of Kant’s political philosophy: the distinction between the "united will" (*vereinigt Willkür*) and mere "popular majority consent."

In contemporary democratic theory, legitimacy is typically proceduralized through aggregation: a majority of citizens (or their representatives) vote in favor of a policy, and the law is thereby enacted. Kant rejects this majoritarian reductionism. 

For Kant, the legitimacy of a law does not depend on whether a majority of the population actually approves of it, votes for it, or likes it. Popular majority consent is empirical, contingent, and susceptible to factionalism, tyranny of the majority, and self-interested bargaining.

Instead, Kant introduces the foundational standard of the *united will* (or the original contract as an idea of reason). A law is just if and only if it could have arisen from the united will of *an entire people*, such that every single citizen, acting as a rational co-legislator, could or must logically assent to it. 

This is a counterfactual test of universal rational acceptability, not an empirical poll. As Kant writes in *Theory and Practice*:

> "This is a mere test of the rightfulness of every public law. For if the law is such that a whole people *could not* possibly give its consent to it (e.g., that a certain class of subjects should be hereditary nobles), it is unjust; but if it is at least *possible* that a people should agree to it, it is a duty to consider the law just..."

This distinction is crucial for evaluating state beneficence and taxation. If the state institutes a welfare tax, does it pass the test of the united will? 

Critics of state welfare argue that a wealthy taxpayer could never rationally consent to having their hard-earned property seized to support someone else’s lifestyle or misfortune. They argue that the united will test fails because no rational agent would agree to a system that institutionalizes the partial expropriation of their assets.

However, defenders like Rosen, building upon a deeper reading of Kant’s social contract, argue precisely the opposite. A rational agent behind a Kantian equivalent of the veil of ignorance—or simply reasoning from the perspective of an essential member of a civil union—*must* consent to a constitutional arrangement that guarantees survival and basic independence against the contingencies of nature and fortune. 

Why? Because in the state of nature, property and life are constantly insecure. To exit the state of nature and enter the civil state, rational agents commit to maintaining that state. If a subset of citizens falls into absolute destitution, the social bond is ruptured, and the state loses its moral title to enforce compliance. 

Therefore, a rational citizen *must* consent to a system of taxation that prevents the collapse of the commonwealth into Hobbesian chaos or rightless destitution. 

Crucially, this united will test distinguishes between *redistribution for social engineering* and *provision for civil preservation*. 

*   A tax designed to equalize wealth, engineer social outcomes, or reward political constituencies fails the united will test because no rational citizen would consent to being a mere instrument for the social engineering projects of others.
*   A tax designed to maintain the integrity of the civil union by ensuring that no citizen sinks into absolute dependence and rightlessness passes the test, because it is a necessary condition for the very existence of the legal order that protects the property and freedom of all.

## 6. Resolving the Dilemma: Do Positive State Welfare Measures Inherently Violate Freedom?

We are now equipped to resolve the central dilemma of this essay: Do positive state welfare measures inherently violate individual freedom under Kant’s *Metaphysics of Morals*?

The resolution requires rejecting both the uncritical endorsement of the modern welfare state as an exercise in collective virtue and the uncompromising libertarian rejection of all public welfare as a violation of rights. 

### 1. The State Cannot Have an Ethical Duty of Beneficence
From a strictly Kantian perspective, the state cannot possess an independent *moral* or *ethical* duty of beneficence. Virtue, benevolence, and love are characteristics of individual moral agents acting from internal maxims of duty. A state is an artificial juridical person; it possesses external coercive power, but it has no soul, no internal will, and no capacity for moral virtue. 

When politicians or bureaucrats invoke the "moral duty of the state to care for the poor," they commit a category error. If the state engages in welfare distribution out of a paternalistic desire to be morally "good" or benevolent, it oversteps its rightful boundaries and degenerates into despotism.

### 2. The State Possesses a Juridical Necessity for Preservation
Nevertheless, the state *does* possess an indispensable juridical mandate to maintain the civil union and secure the external freedom of all its members. As we saw in Kant’s own text, the sovereign is authorized to tax the people to maintain those who cannot provide for themselves. 

This authorization is not grounded in the ethics of compassion, but in the logic of Right. The civil condition is an association designed to secure right. If the state permits its members to starve, it fails in its foundational purpose of maintaining the commonwealth. 

### 3. The Structural Nature of Welfare in Kantian Justice
To avoid violating individual freedom, state welfare measures must be strictly structured in accordance with Kantian principles:
*   **Non-Paternalism:** Welfare must not be administered as a paternalistic gift that reduces citizens to dependent minors, but as a juridical entitlement grounded in citizenship and the preservation of the rightful condition.
*   **Threshold of Independence:** The benchmark for state provision is not economic equality or the maximization of aggregate utility, but the *prevention of absolute dependence*. Welfare must aim to restore or secure the individual’s capacity for independence (*Selbständigkeit*), equipping them to act as free and equal co-legislators.
*   **Universalized Consent:** The taxation that funds these measures must be justifiable not through majoritarian whim, but through the counterfactual standard of the united will—as a necessary condition for a legal order that protects the freedom of every citizen against arbitrary encroachment.

When welfare is structured in this manner, it does not violate individual freedom. On the contrary, it *secures* it. External freedom is not a formal abstraction enjoyed in a vacuum; it requires a material baseline of survival and legal recognition. Without this baseline, property rights become a cruel fiction for the dispossessed, and the state becomes merely an armed camp protecting the privileges of the fortunate few against the desperate many.

---

## Conclusion

The philosophical evaluation of whether the state possesses an independent moral duty of beneficence reveals a profound truth about the architecture of Kantian political thought. Allen D. Rosen’s interpretation correctly identifies that Kantian justice cannot be reduced to a minimalist framework indifferent to human destitution, yet it must be carefully insulated from the error of converting the state into an agency of moral virtue.

The rights-based framework rightly insists on the strict boundaries of external freedom, the inviolability of legitimately acquired property, and the danger of paternalistic state overreach. Yet, its insistence on a completely passive state ignores the foundational imperative of the civil condition: a commonwealth cannot survive, nor can it legitimately claim to embody a united will of free and equal citizens, if it permits its members to sink into conditions of absolute dependence and rightlessness.

Ultimately, the state does not possess a moral duty of beneficence, for morality belongs exclusively to the autonomous human agent. But the state *does* possess a stringent juridical necessity of preservation. By constraining state action through the rigorous standards of liberty, civil equality, independence, and the united will of the people, Kantian philosophy provides a brilliant middle path. 

It permits—indeed, it mandates—coercive public provision for the needy, not as an act of discretionary charity or ethical benevolence, but as the indispensable structural bedrock required to make the realm of Right a reality for all.
