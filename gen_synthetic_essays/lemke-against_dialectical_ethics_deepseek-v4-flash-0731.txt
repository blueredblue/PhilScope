**Title: The Unburied Feminine: Sacrifice, Totality, and the Possibility of Dialogical Ethics in Hegel’s Wake**

**Introduction**

The philosophical ambition of G.W.F. Hegel is nothing less than the self-comprehension of the Absolute. In the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, he charts a rigorous odyssey of consciousness, moving from immediate sense-certainty through the fraught terrains of self-consciousness and reason, culminating in the speculative heights of Absolute Knowing—a state in which Spirit recognizes the entirety of reality as its own conceptual manifestation. In the *Philosophy of Right*, this metaphysical ambition is given a concrete, institutional form, where the ethical life (*Sittlichkeit*) of the modern state is presented as the rational actualization of freedom. Yet, embedded within this monumental edifice lies a troubling structural fault line: the treatment of the family and, more specifically, the gendered assignment of women to the realm of “Divine Law” and the unconscious rites of burial. Contemporary feminist philosopher Kelly Oliver has identified this not as a mere historical prejudice but as a constitutive, structural necessity within Hegel’s dialectic. She argues that the transition from the immediate, familial sphere to the self-conscious universality of civil society and the state requires the *sacrifice* of the feminine—a violent sublation that leaves an opaque, unassimilable residue at the heart of a system that claims total conceptual transparency.

This essay will evaluate Oliver’s critique, arguing that it successfully exposes a fatal flaw in Hegel’s project. If the dialectical movement requires a gendered, unconscious foundation that is *itself* never brought fully into the light of the Concept, then Hegel’s overarching claim to have made all reality fully conceptualizable collapses under the weight of its own repressed other. In contrast to Oliver’s deconstructive reading, Shari Neller Starrett offers a defense of what she terms “Hegelian Critical Relations,” proposing that the dynamic, dialogical tension between the family and the community need not culminate in a violent synthesis. Starrett suggests that this ongoing, unresolved negotiation constitutes a more empowering ethics, one that resists the totalizing impulse inherent in traditional sublation. However, as this essay will contend, while Starrett’s model is ethically compelling and philosophically sophisticated, it ultimately succeeds only by abandoning the very core of Hegel’s systematic project—the teleological drive toward Absolute Knowing. In the final analysis, the preservation of an empowering ethics demands the refusal of Hegel’s sacrificial logic, embracing instead the tragic, unresolved tension that Starrett articulates, but doing so requires a post-Hegelian admission that reality is not, and cannot be, fully conceptualizable.

**Part I: The Architecture of Ethical Life and the Gendered Division of Labor**

To understand Oliver’s critique, one must first grasp the precise function of the family within Hegel’s ethical universe. In the *Phenomenology*, the transition from the abstract realms of Reason to the concrete realm of Spirit introduces the concept of the ethical order—a world composed of two interpenetrating but distinct laws. The first is Human Law, the law of the community, the state, and the conscious pursuit of the universal good. The second is Divine Law, the law of the family, the household, and the unconscious, subterranean bonds of kinship and blood. For Hegel, the family is the “immediate substance” of Spirit. It is the natural, unreflective ethical unit where individuals exist not as autonomous persons but as members of a whole, bound by feeling and love.

Crucially, Hegel assigns these two spheres along strict gendered lines. The man, as citizen, leaves the immediate confines of the household to engage in the universal struggles of civil society and the state. His consciousness is oriented outward, toward the recognition and mastery of the objective world. The woman, conversely, remains the guardian of the household and the Penates—the household gods. She is the priestess of Divine Law, responsible for the particular, the emotional, and the biological continuity of the family. This division is not merely social but ontological. In the *Philosophy of Right*, Hegel states that women are “the irony of the community” and that their vocation is the “piety” of the family. Their ethical life is not one of self-conscious reflection but of immediate, natural feeling.

The pivotal act within this feminine sphere is the burial rite. In the *Phenomenology*, Hegel argues that the family’s supreme duty is to perform the funeral rites for its deceased members. This act is of immense metaphysical importance. The dead individual, having been a member of the family, is a particular being. Upon death, they are reduced to a mere natural object, a corpse. Without the intervention of the family, the deceased would simply rot and dissolve into the meaningless flux of nature. The burial rite, performed by the woman, elevates the dead from their natural particularity to a spiritual universality. It transforms the corpse into an ancestor, a memory, a member of the ethical substance. As Hegel writes, the family “makes the dead individual a member of the community of Spirit.” The woman, through this ritual, ensures that the individual’s essence is not lost to nature but is preserved in the memory of the community. She is the guardian of the unconscious past, the link between the biological and the spiritual.

This role is absolutely necessary for the dialectical progression. The community, or Human Law, relies on the family to produce and sustain its citizens. The citizen must have a stable, particular identity rooted in a family before he can abstract himself into the universal. The burial rite is the final act of this particularization—it consolidates the individual’s identity as a member of the family, providing the grounding from which the individual’s conscious life can emerge. Yet, this very act is one of *unconscious* service. The woman performs her ethical duty not out of self-conscious comprehension of the universal, but out of immediate, natural piety. She is the vanishing mediator: the necessary condition for the emergence of conscious universality, yet herself excluded from that consciousness.

**Part II: Kelly Oliver and the Structural Sacrifice of the Feminine**

Kelly Oliver’s critique, articulated across her works on Hegel, Antigone, and the ethics of witnessing, seizes upon this structural arrangement. She argues that Hegel’s system is predicated on a fundamental disavowal. The transition from the family (Divine Law) to the community (Human Law) is not a simple, harmonious progression. Rather, it is a violent sublation (*Aufhebung*) that requires the *sacrifice* of the feminine sphere. To understand this, we must examine the tragic collision between the two laws, famously dramatized by Sophocles in *Antigone*.

For Hegel, the tragedy of Antigone is not a simple moral conflict between good and evil. It is the necessary collision between two equally valid ethical claims. Antigone, representing Divine Law, insists on burying her brother Polynices against the edict of Creon, the representative of Human Law. Creon insists on the primacy of the state’s law. Hegel reads this as a tragic but necessary moment in the development of Spirit. The community, to achieve self-consciousness, must negate the immediate, particular claims of the family. The state must establish its authority over the blood ties of kinship. In this dialectical clash, the feminine principle (Divine Law) is necessarily defeated. Antigone dies, and Creon’s state is established.

Oliver’s insight is to point out that this is not merely a tragic necessity but a *constitutive* one. The community does not simply overcome the family; it requires the family’s continued existence as its unconscious foundation. The man (the citizen) can only be a self-conscious universal subject because the woman (the guardian of the family) remains in the unconscious realm. The woman is the “sacrifice” that grounds the dialectic. She is the one who must remain unself-conscious so that the man can become self-conscious. Her labor—the burial rites, the emotional maintenance of the household—is the hidden, unacknowledged condition of possibility for the public, rational sphere.

This is where Oliver’s critique becomes devastating. She argues that the feminine is not simply *excluded* from the dialectic; it is *consumed* by it. The woman is the opaque, material ground upon which the transparent edifice of the Concept is built. She is the “unconscious” of Spirit. In psychoanalytic terms, she is the repressed other who is necessary for the formation of the subject but whose own subjectivity is denied. The dialectic of recognition, which Hegel outlines in the master-slave struggle, is predicated on a mutual recognition between self-conscious subjects. However, the woman is denied this recognition. She is not a full subject who enters into the struggle for recognition; she is the object, the vessel, the unconscious medium through which male subjectivity is realized.

The sacrifice is structural because it is built into the very logic of the transition. For the community to emerge as a self-conscious universal, it must sublate the family. But sublation, in this context, does not merely mean “preserve and elevate.” It means a violent cancellation of the feminine’s *own* claim to self-consciousness. The woman is allowed to have an ethical role, but only an unconscious one. She is the guardian of the dead, but she cannot herself be a fully living, self-conscious citizen. Her ethical action is necessary, yet it is an action that prevents her from becoming a subject. She is the “beautiful soul” who, in her pure particularity, cannot survive the encounter with the universal.

Oliver’s key claim is that this sacrifice is not incidental to Hegel’s system but is its very engine. The dialectic cannot move forward without the feminine as its unacknowledged, unconscious motor. The man’s journey from the family to the state is a journey *away from* the mother, the wife, the sister—a journey that requires the repression of that maternal, feminine origin. The state is founded on the burial of the feminine, both literally (in the burial rites) and figuratively (in the exclusion of women from the public sphere). This is not a flaw that can be corrected by simply granting women equal rights within Hegel’s framework, because the framework itself is built upon the gendered division of conscious and unconscious, universal and particular.

**Part III: The Undermining of Absolute Knowing**

The critical question now arises: does this structural necessity undermine Hegel’s overarching project of making all reality fully conceptualizable? The answer, following Oliver’s logic, is an unequivocal yes.

Hegel’s ultimate goal is the achievement of Absolute Knowing, a state in which the distinction between subject and object, consciousness and its world, is dissolved. In this state, Spirit recognizes that the entire history of its development is the unfolding of the Concept (*Begriff*). Reality is fully rational, and rationality is fully real. There is no opaque remainder, no “thing-in-itself” that escapes the grasp of thought. The system is a closed circle, a totality that comprehends its own genesis.

However, if the dialectic requires the sacrifice of the feminine as its unconscious foundation, then the system is *not* self-grounding. It depends on a condition that it cannot fully absorb into the Concept. The feminine, as the guardian of Divine Law, is the “unconscious” of Spirit. Spirit is defined by its self-consciousness, its ability to know itself. Yet, the process by which Spirit achieves this self-consciousness relies on a sphere that remains fundamentally unconscious. The woman, in her role as the priestess of the Penates, performs a function that is necessary for the emergence of the universal, but she does so without comprehending that universality. Her action is a blind, natural necessity.

This creates a paradox at the heart of Hegel’s system. The Absolute claims to be the totality of all that is. Yet, the Absolute’s own genesis requires a moment of non-totality, a moment of opaque, unconscious particularity that is never fully sublated. The feminine is the “remainder” that cannot be digested by the Concept. She is the material, bodily, natural ground that the Concept must transcend, but which it can never fully eliminate. The system is haunted by its own repressed other.

This is not merely a matter of epistemic limitation. It is an ontological flaw. If reality is truly conceptualizable, then the feminine sphere must be fully expressible in the terms of the Concept. But the feminine is defined precisely by its resistance to conceptualization. It is the realm of feeling, immediacy, and the unconscious. To bring it fully into the light of the Concept would be to destroy its essential nature. Thus, the system is caught in a double bind. It must include the feminine to complete itself, but it cannot include the feminine without destroying its own claim to completeness. The sacrifice of the feminine is the price Hegel pays for his totalizing ambition, and it is a price that reveals the ambition to be fundamentally flawed.

Furthermore, this critique has profound ethical and political implications. If the Hegelian state is built upon the unacknowledged sacrifice of women, then the state’s claim to universal freedom is a lie. The “universal” subject of the state is a specifically masculine subject, one who has been constituted by the repression of the feminine. The state’s consciousness is a partial consciousness that mistakes its own partiality for totality. This is what Oliver means when she argues that Hegel’s system is a form of “masculine” metaphysics. It is a system that universalizes the male perspective while disavowing its dependence on the female other. The project of Absolute Knowing is thus not a neutral, universal project; it is a gendered project that serves to legitimate a particular form of patriarchal domination.

**Part IV: Starrett’s Defense of Critical Relations**

In the face of Oliver’s devastating critique, Shari Neller Starrett offers a compelling re-reading of Hegel that seeks to salvage the dialectical method while jettisoning its totalizing conclusions. Starrett’s concept of “Hegelian Critical Relations” proposes that the relationship between the family and the community is not a linear progression toward a final synthesis but an ongoing, dynamic, and unresolved tension. She argues that Hegel’s own text, particularly the *Phenomenology*, contains the resources for a more dialogical and less violent reading of the ethical life.

Starrett’s key move is to reinterpret the tragedy of Antigone. Oliver, following a more orthodox reading, sees Antigone’s defeat as the necessary sacrifice of the feminine. Starrett, however, argues that the tragedy is not a one-sided victory of Human Law over Divine Law. Instead, it is a mutual failure of both spheres. Creon, the representative of the state, is not the triumphant hero of the dialectic. He is a tragic figure who has overreached by denying the legitimate claims of the family. His state is founded on a repression that will ultimately destroy him. Similarly, Antigone is a tragic figure who has overreached by prioritizing the particular claims of the family over the universal claims of the state. The tragedy lies in the fact that neither side can fully absorb the other. The ethical life is not achieved by the victory of one law over the other, but by the recognition of their irreducible tension.

For Starrett, the ethical substance is not a final, static synthesis. It is the *relation itself*—the ongoing, critical negotiation between the particular and the universal, the family and the community. She argues that the family is not a stepping stone to be left behind but a permanent pole of ethical life. The family provides the emotional, particular grounding that prevents the community from becoming a cold, abstract, and tyrannical universal. The community, in turn, provides the universal framework that prevents the family from becoming narcissistic, insular, and regressive. The two spheres are mutually critical. They hold each other in a state of productive tension.

This model is profoundly empowering for feminist ethics. It validates the feminine sphere as a site of critical resistance, not just a subordinate step in a masculine progression. The woman’s role as the guardian of Divine Law is not a sign of her inferiority but a source of her ethical power. She represents the particular, the embodied, the emotional—all those aspects of human experience that the abstract universal tends to repress. By insisting on the ongoing validity of this sphere, Starrett allows for a form of ethical critique that can challenge the injustices of the state. The family becomes a refuge from the alienating demands of civil society, a space where alternative values can be nurtured.

Starrett’s model of “Critical Relations” avoids the violent sublation that Oliver criticizes. It does not require the sacrifice of the feminine to achieve the universal. Instead, it insists that the universal is only ever realized *through* its ongoing relation to the particular. The ethical life is a dynamic process of mutual recognition and critique, not a final state of achieved harmony. This is a dialogical ethics, one that resists closure and embraces the messy, unresolved nature of human existence.

**Part V: Can Dialogical Tension Survive Without Sublation?**

The central question, however, is whether Starrett’s model can truly preserve an empowering ethics without collapsing back into the traditional Hegelian sublation she seeks to avoid. To answer this, we must carefully distinguish between different meanings of sublation (*Aufhebung*).

In its traditional Hegelian sense, sublation is a triple movement of negation, preservation, and elevation. The lower term is negated in its immediacy, preserved as a moment within the higher term, and elevated to a new level of comprehension. For the family, this would mean that the family’s particularity is negated, its ethical content is preserved in the state, and it is elevated to a higher form of ethical life. Starrett explicitly rejects this linear, teleological model. She argues that the family is not merely a moment to be superseded but a permanent, irreducible pole of ethical life.

However, can a relation be truly permanent and unresolved? If the tension between family and community is never resolved, does that not constitute a perpetual state of crisis? Starrett would argue that this crisis is not a failure but the very essence of ethical life. The ethical is not a destination but a journey. It is the ongoing, never-ending work of negotiating between our particular attachments and our universal obligations. This is a deeply appealing vision, and it aligns with many contemporary feminist and post-structuralist critiques of totality.

Yet, this vision comes at a significant cost. To maintain the dialogical tension, Starrett must abandon the core of Hegel’s project: the claim to Absolute Knowing. If the tension is truly unresolvable, then reality is not fully conceptualizable. There is an irreducible gap between the Concept and the world, a gap that is occupied by the feminine, the particular, the unconscious. This is precisely Oliver’s point. The system cannot close itself off. It is haunted by its own other.

So, does Starrett’s model collapse into traditional sublation? The answer is no, but only if we are willing to redefine what it means to be “Hegelian.” Starrett is not defending Hegel the systematizer. She is defending Hegel the dialectician—Hegel the thinker of negativity, contradiction, and process. She is offering a post-Hegelian Hegel, one who has been read through the lens of Kierkegaard, Adorno, and Derrida. This Hegel is not the prophet of Absolute Knowing but the philosopher of the “unhappy consciousness,” the one who recognizes that the infinite is only ever approached through the finite, that the universal is only ever realized through the particular.

This redefinition is both the strength and the weakness of Starrett’s position. Its strength is that it allows us to preserve a powerful method of critical analysis without endorsing the totalizing, and patriarchal, conclusions of the system. It provides a framework for an empowering ethics that validates the feminine sphere as a site of resistance. Its weakness is that it is, strictly speaking, a betrayal of Hegel’s own explicit intentions. Hegel himself would never have accepted a perpetual, unresolved tension. For him, the dialectic must eventually come to rest in the Absolute. A dialectic without a telos is, for Hegel, a contradiction in terms.

Ultimately, the choice is stark. We can have Hegel’s system, with its claim to totality, but we must accept the sacrifice of the feminine. Or we can have an empowering ethics, grounded in dialogical tension, but we must abandon the Hegelian dream of Absolute Knowing. Oliver’s critique forces us to see that these two options are mutually exclusive. The sacrifice of the feminine is not an unfortunate byproduct of the system; it is the structural condition of its possibility.

**Conclusion: The Refusal of Sacrifice**

In conclusion, Kelly Oliver’s critique of Hegel’s treatment of the family is both incisive and compelling. She demonstrates that the assignment of women to the unconscious realm of Divine Law is not a mere historical oversight but a structural necessity that underpins the entire dialectical progression. The transition from the family to civil society requires the sacrifice of the feminine—a sacrifice that leaves an opaque, unassimilable residue at the heart of a system that claims total conceptual transparency. This structural necessity fundamentally undermines Hegel’s overarching project. The Absolute cannot be fully self-conscious if its own genesis depends on an unconscious, gendered other. The system is not a closed totality but a haunted edifice, built upon the buried corpse of the feminine.

Shari Neller Starrett’s defense of Hegelian Critical Relations offers a tantalizing alternative. By reinterpreting the tragedy of Antigone as a mutual failure rather than a one-sided victory, she opens the door to a reading of Hegel that prioritizes ongoing, dialogical tension over violent synthesis. This model preserves an empowering ethics, validating the feminine sphere as a site of critical resistance. However, this model can only succeed if we are willing to abandon the teleological core of Hegel’s project. The dialogical tension must remain unresolved, which means accepting that reality is not fully conceptualizable.

The ultimate answer to our question is that an ongoing dialogical tension *can* preserve an empowering ethics, but it cannot do so while remaining faithful to traditional Hegelian sublation. Starrett’s model is a post-Hegelian model. It uses Hegel’s method of negation and critique but refuses his conclusion of Absolute Knowing. This refusal is not a failure but a liberation. It frees us from the demand to sacrifice the particular for the universal, the feminine for the masculine, the unconscious for the conscious. It allows us to dwell in the unresolved tension, to live in the tragic space where the family and the community, the particular and the universal, are locked in a perpetual, productive embrace.

In the end, we must side with Oliver’s diagnosis. The Hegelian system, in its totality, is a monument to patriarchy, built upon the unacknowledged sacrificial labor of women. But we must also embrace Starrett’s prescription. The way forward is not to abandon the dialectic but to radicalize it, to push it beyond its own totalizing ambitions. We must refuse the sacrifice. We must insist that the feminine, the particular, the unconscious are not obstacles to be overcome but essential, irreplaceable poles of ethical life. This means embracing a form of thought that is never fully at home with itself, a form of thought that is always in process, always in tension. It means abandoning the dream of Absolute Knowing and accepting the infinite, unfinished task of ethical negotiation. This is the price of an empowering ethics, and it is a price we must be
