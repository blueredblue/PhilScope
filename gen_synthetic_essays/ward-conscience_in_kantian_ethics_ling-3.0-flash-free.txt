# Conscience, the Categorical Imperative, and the Willie Case: Kant on Subjective Moral Judgment and the Need for an Ultimate Judge

## I. Introduction: The Dual Architecture of Kantian Morality

Immanuel Kant's moral philosophy is built upon a striking and deliberate duality. On the one hand, he posits a universal, objective principle of morality—the categorical imperative—that governs all rational agents regardless of their particular circumstances, inclinations, or psychological states. On the other hand, he insists that morality must be subjectively realized within the agent's own consciousness, not merely recognized as an external standard to which one conforms. This subjective realization takes the form of conscience, which Kant treats not as a mere feeling of guilt or a sentimental reaction to wrongdoing, but as the practical faculty through which the rational will applies the moral law to itself. The relationship between the objective categorical imperative and the subjective conscience is not one of simple correspondence; it is a complex, dynamic relation that raises profound questions about the nature of moral accountability, the possibility of self-condemnation, and whether the immanent structure of moral reason is sufficient to ground the full weight of moral judgment without recourse to a divine judge.

This essay evaluates Kant's framing of conscience as a subjective condition of morality that parallels the objective categorical imperative, with particular attention to the mechanism of ex post facto conscience—the retrospective judgment an agent passes upon their own actions after those actions have been performed. To test the adequacy of Kant's framework, I will examine a specific dilemma: the case of Willie, who faces competing duties and must choose between them. The question at the heart of this examination is whether moral accountability, understood as the capacity of an agent to genuinely hold themselves responsible for their actions, requires the positing of God as an ultimate judge, or whether Kant's purely rational and immanent account of conscience can avoid reducing inner self-condemnation to a pointless contradiction. The stakes of this question are not merely academic; they touch upon the deepest concerns of moral psychology, the philosophy of religion, and the foundations of practical reason itself.

## II. The Categorical Imperative as the Objective Standard of Morality

Kant's categorical imperative, as articulated in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), is the supreme principle of morality. It is "categorical" in the sense that it commands unconditionally, without reference to any particular end or desire of the agent. Unlike hypothetical imperatives, which command only on the condition that one desires the means to some end, the categorical imperative commands absolutely, because it is grounded in the very structure of rational agency. Kant formulates it in several ways, but the most fundamental is the formula of universal law: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law" (*Grundlegung*, 4:421). This formulation demands that the agent test their proposed maxim— the subjective principle of their action—against the requirement that it be consistent with itself as a universal law. If the maxim cannot be willed as a universal law without contradiction, then the action is morally impermissible.

The categorical imperative is objective in a precise and philosophically significant sense. It is not contingent upon any particular agent's desires, cultural conventions, or empirical circumstances. It applies equally to all rational beings, and its validity is derived from the concept of rationality itself. When an agent acts morally, they do not merely happen to act in accordance with a rule that others happen to approve of; they act in accordance with a law that they recognize, through the exercise of pure practical reason, as binding upon them necessarily. This is what Kant means by the autonomy of the will: the will gives itself the law, and the law it gives itself is the categorical imperative. In this sense, morality is not externally imposed but self-legislated, and the categorical imperative is the form that this self-legislation takes.

The objectivity of the categorical imperative ensures that moral judgments, properly grounded, are not merely expressions of individual preference or subjective sentiment. When Kant says that an action is morally wrong, he means that it cannot be rationally willed as a universal law, and this judgment is, in principle, accessible to any rational being who engages in the relevant deliberation. Moral objectivity, for Kant, is thus not a metaphysical claim about a transcendent realm of moral facts, but a rational claim about the necessary structure of practical reason itself. The categorical imperative is the objective condition of morality: it is the standard against which all actions, maxims, and wills are to be measured.

## III. Conscience as the Subjective Parallel

If the categorical imperative is the objective condition of morality, then conscience is its subjective counterpart. In the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), Kant describes conscience as "the practical judge within us" (*KpV*, 5:71), the faculty by which the rational will applies the moral law to itself in concrete, particular situations. Conscience is not, for Kant, a separate faculty or a distinct moral principle; it is the subjective realization of the categorical imperative within the agent's own consciousness. It is the voice of the moral law as it speaks from within, not as an external command but as a self-imposed obligation.

This is a crucial point in Kant's account. Conscience is not a feeling, an emotion, or an inclination, though it may be accompanied by feelings of guilt, remorse, or moral satisfaction. It is a rational judgment—a judgment of the will about its own maxims in light of the moral law. When conscience condemns an action, it does so not because the action violates some external rule or offends some divine authority, but because the agent's own rational will recognizes that the maxim of the action cannot be universalized without contradiction. The subjective necessity of conscience is thus grounded in the same rational structure that grounds the objective necessity of the categorical imperative. Conscience is, in effect, the categorical imperative as experienced from the inside.

However, this parallel between the objective categorical imperative and the subjective conscience is not without tension. The categorical imperative is a principle of universal validity—it applies to all rational beings in all circumstances. Conscience, by contrast, is a subjective condition—it belongs to the individual agent and is shaped by the agent's particular epistemic situation, psychological disposition, and moral development. Kant acknowledges that conscience can be "counterfeit"—that is, it can be mistaken, misguided, or corrupted by self-deception, cultural conditioning, or irrational passion (*KpV*, 5:71-72). A counterfeit conscience is one that sincerely believes itself to be obeying the moral law when it is in fact serving some hidden inclination or interest. This raises a deep and difficult question: if conscience is merely subjective, how can we distinguish genuine conscience from counterfeit conscience? How can we be confident that our retrospective moral judgments—the judgments we pass upon ourselves after the fact—are correct?

Kant's answer to this question is, in essence, that we cannot be fully confident, and that this uncertainty is not a defect of the moral framework but a reflection of the limitations of finite rational beings. The categorical imperative provides the objective standard, but the subjective application of that standard requires practical judgment, which is fallible. Conscience, as a subjective condition of morality, is thus both necessary and imperfect. It is necessary because without it, the moral law would remain an abstract principle with no bearing on the agent's actual conduct; it is imperfect because the agent's capacity to apply that principle correctly is always subject to error.

## IV. Ex Post Facto Conscience: Retrospective Moral Judgment

The mechanism of ex post facto conscience is particularly instructive for understanding the relationship between subjective moral judgment and objective moral standards. Ex post facto conscience refers to the retrospective judgment that an agent passes upon their own actions after those actions have been performed. Unlike the anticipatory conscience that guides action before it is taken—the conscience that warns or encourages in the moment of decision—ex post facto conscience looks backward, evaluating actions that are already done and cannot be undone.

This retrospective dimension of conscience raises a distinctive philosophical problem. When an agent reflects on a past action and condemns it, what exactly is happening? Is the agent simply experiencing a painful emotion—a feeling of guilt or regret? Or is the agent making a rational judgment about the moral quality of their action, a judgment that can be true or false, correct or mistaken? Kant's answer is that ex post facto conscience is, or at least ought to be, a rational judgment. It is the agent's practical reason retrospectively assessing whether the maxim of their past action was consistent with the categorical imperative. The feeling of guilt that often accompanies this judgment is a secondary phenomenon—a psychological response to the recognition of moral failure—not the judgment itself.

But the retrospective nature of ex post facto conscience introduces a complication that anticipatory conscience does not face. When an agent acts in the moment, they may be uncertain about the moral permissibility of their action, and their anticipatory conscience may be unclear or conflicted. After the action is taken, however, the agent has the benefit of hindsight. They can see the consequences of their action, they can reflect more carefully on the maxim that guided them, and they can assess their action against the moral law with greater clarity—or at least, this is what one would hope. Yet hindsight does not guarantee clarity. The agent may rationalize their past action, finding post hoc justifications that they did not consider at the time. They may engage in self-deception, convincing themselves that their action was morally permissible when it was not. The very fact that ex post facto conscience is retrospective means that it is vulnerable to distortions that anticipatory conscience, by its very nature, is less susceptible to.

This vulnerability of ex post facto conscience is not merely a psychological curiosity; it has profound implications for the question of moral accountability. If an agent's retrospective judgment about their own moral worth is unreliable—if ex post facto conscience can be corrupted by self-deception, rationalization, or cognitive bias—then can we say that the agent is genuinely accountable for their actions? Accountability, in the moral sense, requires that the agent be capable of making a correct judgment about their own conduct. If the mechanism by which they make this judgment is inherently fallible, then accountability seems to be in tension with the very subjective condition that is supposed to ground it.

## V. The Willie Case: A Dilemma of Competing Duties

To test the adequacy of Kant's framework in the face of these challenges, consider the case of Willie. Willie is a young man who works as a clerk in a government office. One day, he receives a request from a colleague to falsify a document that would protect the colleague from disciplinary action for a serious error. Willie knows that falsifying government documents is a violation of his duty of truthfulness and integrity. However, he also knows that the colleague is a single parent with young children, and that losing the colleague's job would cause severe hardship for the family. Willie thus faces a conflict between two duties: the duty not to lie or falsify documents, and the duty of compassion or beneficence toward someone in desperate need.

Willie decides to falsify the document. He tells himself that the harm of losing the colleague's job outweighs the harm of a minor breach of official integrity, and that he is acting out of compassion rather than dishonesty. The action is taken, and Willie moves on with his life.

Now consider Willie's ex post facto conscience. Some weeks later, Willie finds himself unable to shake a persistent sense of unease. He begins to question his decision. Was he truly acting out of compassion, or was he rationalizing a cowardly decision? Was the falsification of the document really justified by the consequences, or was he simply making an exception to a moral principle that he ought to have upheld absolutely? Willie's ex post facto conscience is in conflict: one part of him feels that he acted rightly, given the circumstances; another part of him feels that he has compromised his moral integrity and that no amount of good intentions can justify a breach of duty.

This is the Willie case dilemma: a conflict of competing duties that produces a corresponding conflict in ex post facto conscience. The dilemma is not merely about which duty is stronger—it is about whether the very framework of competing duties is coherent within Kantian moral theory. Kant's categorical imperative, properly understood, does not admit of genuine conflicts of duty. If two duties conflict, it is because one of the apparent duties is not a genuine duty at all, or because the agent has misapplied the moral principle. In Kant's framework, the duty of truthfulness is absolute and cannot be overridden by considerations of compassion or beneficence. To falsify a document is to violate a categorical duty, regardless of the consequences.

But this raises a troubling question for ex post facto conscience. If Willie's ex post facto conscience tells him that he acted wrongly, is this conscience correct? If it is correct, then Willie's retrospective self-condemnation is justified, and he bears full moral responsibility for his action. But if Willie's ex post facto conscience tells him that he acted rightly—that the competing duty of compassion justified the breach of truthfulness—then this conscience is counterfeit, and Willie is engaged in self-deception. The problem is that Willie cannot, from the inside, definitively determine which voice of conscience is genuine and which is counterfeit. The subjective condition of morality, which is conscience, provides the mechanism by which the agent judges their own actions, but it does not guarantee the correctness of that judgment.

This is the core difficulty of ex post facto conscience within Kant's framework: it is a subjective mechanism that is supposed to reflect an objective moral standard, but it is fallible, and its fallibility is not something the agent can simply overcome through rational reflection. The more Willie agonizes over his decision, the more he may find himself constructing elaborate rationalizations that feel morally compelling but are, in fact, distortions of the categorical imperative. Ex post facto conscience, in other words, can become a closed loop: the agent judges their own action, the judgment feels subjectively necessary, and the agent accepts it as correct, even if it is objectively wrong.

## VI. The Problem of Moral Accountability Without God

This brings us to the deepest question raised by Kant's framework: does moral accountability require the positing of God as an ultimate judge? If conscience is merely subjective—if it is a fallible, internal mechanism for applying the moral law—then can the agent genuinely hold themselves accountable for their actions? And if the agent cannot genuinely hold themselves accountable, does the entire structure of Kantian morality collapse into a form of moral subjectivism that lacks the binding force Kant intended?

Kant himself grappled with this question in *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* (1793). There, he argues that God is not a theoretical postulate—something we must believe in on the basis of evidence or rational demonstration—but a practical postulate, something we must posit as a condition of the possibility of moral accountability in the widest sense. The argument is roughly this: if we are to be fully accountable for our actions, we must be able to trust that our moral judgments are correct, that our conscience is not merely a subjective illusion. But finite rational beings, embedded in the empirical world, are prone to self-deception, rationalization, and error. Without an objective, divine judge to confirm or correct our moral judgments, our accountability remains uncertain. God, as the guarantor of the moral order, provides the objective standpoint from which our subjective moral judgments can be ultimately validated.

Kant's argument is subtle and carefully constructed. He does not claim that God is necessary for morality in the sense that without God there would be no moral law. The categorical imperative is grounded in reason alone, and its validity does not depend on any theological assumption. What Kant claims is that God is necessary for the full realization of moral accountability—the assurance that our moral efforts are not in vain, that our striving for moral worth is recognized and rewarded in the highest sense. In the absence of God, the moral life would be a kind of Sisyphean labor: the agent strives to obey the moral law, but can never be certain whether their striving is genuine or merely a sophisticated form of self-deception. The inner self-condemnation that Willie experiences in our case would be, in this scenario, a pointless contradiction: an internal judgment that cannot be validated by any external authority, and therefore cannot bear the full weight of moral accountability.

This is a powerful argument, and it reveals a genuine limitation in Kant's otherwise rigorous framework. The categorical imperative provides an objective standard of morality, and conscience provides the subjective mechanism by which the agent applies that standard. But the connection between the objective standard and the subjective mechanism is not guaranteed. Conscience can be wrong, and the agent cannot, from the inside, definitively distinguish genuine conscience from counterfeit conscience. If moral accountability requires that the agent's self-judgment be correct—if the agent must be able to trust their own moral faculty—then the subjective condition of morality, as Kant frames it, is insufficient. Something beyond the immanent structure of practical reason is needed to guarantee the reliability of conscience.

## VII. Is Inner Self-Condemnation a Pointless Contradiction?

The question, then, is whether inner self-condemnation—the experience of guilt, remorse, and moral self-reproach that Willie undergoes in our case—is a pointless contradiction if it is not grounded in an objective, divine judgment. A pointless contradiction, in this context, would be a judgment that purports to be morally significant but cannot, in the end, be justified by any objective standard. If Willie condemns himself for falsifying the document, but he cannot be certain that this condemnation is correct—because his ex post facto conscience is fallible and he has no external authority to confirm it—then his self-condemnation seems to float free of any rational foundation. It is a feeling, an emotion, a psychological state that masquerades as a moral judgment. It is, in the deepest sense, pointless: it does not actually accomplish anything morally, because it cannot be grounded in anything morally certain.

This is the reductio ad absurdum that critics of Kant's framework have long pressed against his account of conscience. If conscience is merely subjective—if it is a psychological mechanism rather than a rational faculty—then it cannot bear the weight of moral accountability. The agent who condemns themselves in their own conscience is doing nothing more than experiencing a painful emotion, and that emotion has no more moral significance than any other feeling. If this is right, then Kant's entire edifice of moral philosophy rests on a contradiction: it posits an objective moral law and then relies on a subjective faculty to apply it, but the subjective faculty cannot be trusted to apply it correctly, and therefore the moral law is never truly applied at all.

Kant's own response to this objection is, I believe, more sophisticated than it is often credited. He does not claim that conscience is infallible or that the agent can always trust their own moral judgments. He acknowledges that conscience can be counterfeit and that the agent is often uncertain about the moral quality of their actions. What Kant insists is that the very fact that the agent experiences conscience—that they feel the moral law pressing upon them from within—is itself evidence of the objective validity of the moral law. Conscience is not merely a subjective feeling; it is the subjective realization of an objective rational principle. The fact that Willie feels guilty, that he is unable to rest easy after falsifying the document, is not a meaningless psychological event. It is the subjective manifestation of the categorical imperative, the voice of reason speaking within him, even if he cannot fully articulate or trust that voice.

This response has considerable force, but it also has a significant limitation. The limitation is that it conflates the experience of moral unease with the correctness of moral judgment. Just because Willie feels guilty does not mean that his guilt is justified. The categorical imperative may be objectively valid, but Willie's subjective realization of it may be distorted, confused, or self-serving. The fact that conscience is the subjective parallel of the categorical imperative does not mean that every instance of conscience is correct. The parallel is structural, not epistemic: conscience mirrors the form of the moral law, but it does not guarantee the content of the judgment.

This is where the question of God becomes unavoidable. If the agent's ex post facto conscience is fallible—if it can be corrupted, distorted, or self-deceived—then the agent needs some external guarantee that their moral judgments are correct. Without such a guarantee, the agent's self-condemnation remains uncertain, and their moral accountability remains incomplete. God, as Kant argues, provides this guarantee. God is the ultimate judge who confirms or corrects the agent's moral self-assessment, who ensures that the subjective realization of the moral law corresponds to its objective validity. Without God, the agent is left alone with their conscience, and conscience, however powerful it may feel, cannot provide the certainty that moral accountability requires.

But this argument, for all its force, also raises a further difficulty. If God is necessary for moral accountability, then Kant's entire framework of moral autonomy is undermined. Autonomy, for Kant, is the capacity of the rational will to give itself the moral law without appeal to any external authority. If moral accountability requires God, then autonomy is not self-sufficient; it depends upon a theological assumption that lies beyond the scope of practical reason itself. The agent who condemns themselves in their own conscience is not, in the final analysis, accountable to themselves alone; they are accountable to God, and their conscience is merely the subjective anticipation of a divine judgment. This seems to introduce a heteronomous element into what Kant intended to be a purely autonomous moral framework.

Kant tries to navigate this difficulty by arguing that the postulation of God is not a heteronomous appeal to external authority but a necessary condition of the practical use of reason. The agent does not appeal to God as an external judge who stands above the moral law; rather, the agent posits God as the condition under which the moral law can be fully realized and the agent's moral striving can be brought to its proper conclusion. God is not an alternative to autonomy but a complement to it: autonomy provides the principle of morality, and God provides the guarantee that the moral life is not ultimately meaningless. In this sense, Kant's account remains consistent with the spirit of autonomy, even if it stretches the boundaries of what pure practical reason can establish on its own.

## VIII. Conclusion: The Limits of Immanent Conscience and the Necessity of the Transcendent Postulate

The evaluation of Kant's framework through the lens of the Willie case and the mechanism of ex post facto conscience reveals both the strengths and the limitations of his approach. Kant's account of conscience as the subjective parallel of the categorical imperative is elegant and philosophically rigorous. It captures the essential insight that morality is not merely a matter of external compliance with rules but requires the internalization of the moral law within the agent's own rational will. Conscience, as the subjective realization of the categorical imperative, is the mechanism by which the agent applies the moral law to their own actions, and it is this internal application that gives moral life its personal and immediate character.

However, the Willie case demonstrates that the mechanism of ex post facto conscience is insufficient, on its own, to ground full moral accountability. When Willie faces competing duties and must choose between them, his ex post facto conscience is conflicted, and he cannot definitively determine whether his self-condemnation is justified. The subjective condition of morality, which is conscience, cannot provide the certainty that moral accountability requires, because conscience is fallible and the agent is vulnerable to self-deception and rationalization. Without an external guarantee—without some objective standpoint from which the agent's moral self-assessment can be confirmed or corrected—inner self-condemnation risks becoming a pointless contradiction: a psychological state that masquerades as moral judgment but lacks rational justification.

This is why Kant's positing of God as a practical postulate is, I believe, philosophically necessary, even if it exceeds the bounds of pure practical reason. God is not a theoretical assumption about the existence of a supernatural being; God is a necessary condition of the possibility of moral accountability in its fullest sense. Without God, the agent's moral life would be an island of subjective judgment in a sea of rational uncertainty, and the self-condemnation that Willie experiences would be, at bottom, an unsupported feeling rather than a justified moral judgment. With God, by contrast, the subjective realization of the moral law is guaranteed by the objective reality of a moral order that transcends the finite agent's limited perspective. The agent's conscience is not the final word on their moral worth; it is the beginning of a moral dialogue with a divine judge who confirms or corrects the agent's self-assessment and ensures that the moral life is not ultimately in vain.

This conclusion does not, I think, undermine Kant's achievement. On the contrary, it deepens it. Kant showed that morality does not require a leap of faith in God as its foundation; the categorical imperative is sufficient to ground the principle of morality, and conscience is sufficient to make that principle subjectively real for each agent. But Kant also showed, through the very structure of his argument, that the immanent framework of morality has limits—limits that can only be transcended by the postulation of a reality that lies beyond the reach of pure practical reason. The Willie case, with its agonizing conflict of duties and its uncertain ex post facto conscience, is not an anomaly in Kant's framework; it is a revelation of the framework's deepest structure, the point at which the subjective and the objective, the immanent and the transcendent, converge and diverge in ways that no purely rational analysis can fully resolve.

Moral accountability, then, is not a simple matter of the agent judging themselves by an objective standard and finding themselves wanting or worthy. It is a complex, layered, and ultimately transcendent phenomenon that requires both the immanent structure of practical reason and the postulation of a divine judge who gives that structure its fullest meaning. Kant's genius lies not in resolving this tension but in making it visible—to showing us, with unparalleled clarity, the precise point at which the subjective condition of morality meets its limits and the demand for an ultimate judge begins. In this respect, Kant's philosophy of conscience is not a closed system but an open invitation, a provocation to think more deeply about the nature of moral responsibility, the limits of self-judgment, and the role of the transcendent in the moral life. The Willie case, with its unresolved tensions and its haunting ex post facto conscience, remains a powerful illustration of why this invitation must be accepted.
