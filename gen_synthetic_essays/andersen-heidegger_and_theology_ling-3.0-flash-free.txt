The question of the relationship between philosophy and theology has long been a crucible for some of the most profound intellectual tensions in Western thought. At the center of this debate stands Martin Heidegger, whose reorientation of philosophy toward the question of Being fundamentally altered the landscape of both disciplines. Heidegger famously asserted that "philosophy remains atheism" and that theology must ground its conceptual understanding in pre-theological ontology. This claim demands that theology relinquish its traditional attempt to ground itself in divine revelation or theological dogma, instead submitting its conceptual apparatus to the rigorous analysis of fundamental ontology. Yet, as Nathan Andersen has compellingly argued, this demand creates an irreconcilable tension. Heidegger’s atheistic ontology, which reduces human existence to the structures of thrown projection and essential "Being-guilty," cannot serve as the pre-theological foundation for theology without stripping theology of its necessary relation to faithful existence. This essay evaluates Andersen’s critique by examining whether an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis of Dasein can account for theological phenomena such as man’s guilt before God and the creative existence of the Word, ultimately resolving the question of whether theology can achieve conceptual understanding through philosophy while remaining within faith.

Heidegger’s project of fundamental ontology rests on a decisive distinction between the ontic and the ontological. Ontology is the study of Being as such—the meaning of Being that makes the being of entities possible. Ontic science, by contrast, investigates specific beings within the world. Theology, for Heidegger, is an ontic science: it is the science of a particular being, namely God, and of the human being’s relation to that divine entity. However, Heidegger argues that ontic sciences are fundamentally limited because they forget the ontological question of Being in their preoccupation with specific entities. Theology, therefore, is doubly implicated in this forgetfulness: it investigates the being of God while neglecting the Being that makes both God and human beings intelligible in the first place.

To remedy this, Heidegger insists that theology must ground its conceptual understanding in pre-theological ontology. Before theology can speak meaningfully about God, it must first understand the Being for which God is God—namely, human existence, or *Dasein*. *Dasein*—the being for whom its own Being is an issue—is the site where the question of Being can be awakened. Heidegger’s analysis of *Dasein* reveals it as thrown projection (*Geworfenheit* and *Entwurf*). *Dasein* is "thrown" into a world it did not choose, situated in a specific historical and cultural context, yet it is also "projective," constantly reaching out toward future possibilities and defining itself through the choices it makes. This dual structure constitutes the fundamental ontology of human existence.

Crucially, Heidegger’s analysis of *Dasein* yields the concept of essential "Being-guilty" (*wesensmäßige Schuldhaftigkeit*). Guilt, in Heidegger’s sense, is not merely a moral failing or a legal transgression; it is an ontological structure. Because *Dasein* is thrown into a world it did not choose, it is always already in a state of indebtedness to its facticity. Yet, because *Dasein* is also projective, it must take up this thrownness and actualize its possibilities. In doing so, *Dasein* inevitably fails—its projections fall short of the full meaning of Being, and it succumbs to the "They" (*das Man*), losing itself in the anonymous publicness of everyday existence. This failure is the essential guilt of *Dasein*. It is the inescapable condition of finite human existence.

For Heidegger, this ontological analysis provides the necessary corrective to theology. Theology, as an ontic science, treats God as a being alongside other beings, often falling into the metaphysical trap of equating God with the highest being. By submitting to pre-theological ontology, theology can be purged of its ontic absolutism and learn to think about God in relation to the question of Being. Heidegger famously declared that "philosophy remains atheism," meaning that philosophy must not presuppose the existence of God or any specific theological entity in order to ask the question of Being. The ontological corrective requires an atheistic stance—not necessarily a militant denial of God, but an methodological refusal to let the ontic concept of God bypass the ontological question of Being.

It is precisely at this juncture that Nathan Andersen mounts his powerful critique. Andersen argues that Heidegger’s insistence on atheism and pre-theological ontology fundamentally misunderstands the nature of theology. Theology, unlike other ontic sciences, does not begin and end in theoretical speculation about a divine being; it begins and ends in faith. Faith is not merely an additional datum added to an otherwise neutral ontological investigation; it is the horizon within which theological discourse is intelligible. To demand that theology ground itself in a pre-theological ontology that is explicitly atheistic is to demand that theology abandon its own identity. 

Andersen’s critique centers on the tension between Heidegger’s atheistic ontology and the theological phenomenon of faithful existence. Heidegger’s analysis of *Dasein* as thrown projection is explicitly non-faithful. It operates by bracketing out the question of God, reducing human existence to its structural encounter with Being. But if theology utilizes Heidegger’s analysis, it must adopt this atheistic framework. The moment theology adopts the atheistic ontology of thrown projection, it loses its necessary relation to faithful existence. The "faith" that constitutes theology is not merely a subjective feeling or a belief added to an objective ontological structure; it is the mode of being in which *Dasein* relates to the divine. By severing theology from the ontological ground of faith and replacing it with the ontological ground of atheistic thrown projection, Heidegger effectively secularizes theology, transforming it into a secularized anthropology.

This secularization becomes glaringly apparent when we examine whether an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis of Dasein as thrown projection can account for the theological phenomenon of man’s guilt before God. For Heidegger, essential Being-guilty is the inescapable structure of *Dasein*'s finitude. It is the guilt of having to be, of failing to fully actualize the possibilities of Being, and of falling into the They. It is a guilt that is ontological, not personal or soteriological. 

Theological guilt, however, is categorically different. Man’s guilt before God is not merely the result of failing to actualize one's thrown possibilities or losing oneself in the They; it is a relational reality, a breach in the covenant between the Creator and the creature. Theological guilt is inextricably linked to sin, which requires a moral and relational subject who stands before a personal God. Heidegger’s ontological guilt lacks the transcendent Thou; it is a guilt of being-toward-death and Being-toward-the-nothing, but it is not a guilt of standing in the presence of a righteous God who demands justice and offers grace. 

If theology were to adopt Heidegger’s analysis, the "guilt before God" would be reduced to the "guilt of thrown projection." The rich, soteriological texture of sin and redemption would be flattened into the bare ontological structure of *Dasein*. The theological claim that guilt is absolved through the creative Word of God—through the event of revelation and justification—would be rendered unintelligible within an atheistic ontological framework. In Heideggerian ontology, there is no place for a Word that creates *ex nihilo* and reconciles the guilty. There is only Being, which discloses itself through the clearing of *Dasein*, and the silent call of the Call of Conscience, which is not a divine voice but the voice of Being itself calling *Dasein* back from its forgetfulness. 

The second theological phenomenon under scrutiny is the creative existence of the Word, or the *Logos*. In Christian theology, the Word is not merely a human projection or a linguistic structure; it is the divine utterance through which the world is brought into being, and through which salvation is accomplished. The creative Word is the self-revelation of God, an act of grace that precedes and enables human understanding. It is an act of sheer freedom, unbound by the structural necessities of thrown projection.

Heidegger’s analysis of *Dasein* as thrown projection, however, cannot accommodate the creative existence of the Word. In Heidegger’s framework, language is the house of Being, and *Dasein* speaks because it is thrown into a world and must project its possibilities. Language is an ontological condition of *Dasein*, but it is not a divine act of creation. The Word, for Heidegger, is a manifestation of the truth of Being, not a personal divine speech that creates and redeems. To insist that theology ground its understanding of the Word in the pre-theological ontology of thrown projection is to strip the Word of its creative, revelatory power. The theological Word becomes merely another ontic entity, a human linguistic expression shaped by the historicality of *Dasein*. 

Andersen’s critique thus exposes the fundamental impossibility of theology achieving conceptual understanding through Heidegger’s atheistic philosophy while remaining within faith. If theology begins and ends in faith, it cannot adopt a philosophical method that is explicitly atheistic and non-faithful. The moment theology utilizes Heidegger’s analysis of Dasein, it implicitly concedes that faith is not the ground of its conceptual understanding, but rather a secondary, derivative phenomenon that must be explained by the prior, neutral structures of thrown projection. Theology becomes a mere application of ontological categories to a specific ontic entity (God), rather than a living mode of existence rooted in the divine.

The counter-argument might suggest that Heidegger’s atheism is merely methodological—that he is simply asking the question of Being without presupposing God, and that theology can use this methodology as a tool without being compromised by it. This is the position of theological hermeneutics that attempts to "baptize" Heideggerian ontology. However, Andersen and other critics rightly point out that a tool shapes the hand that wields it. The ontological difference that Heidegger establishes—between Being and beings—inevitably subordinates the divine to the category of "being." God becomes a being, albeit the highest being, rather than the Lord of Being. The ontological corrective that Heidegger offers to theology is, from the theological perspective, a theological distortion. It forces theology to think God within the horizon of ontic beings, rather than allowing theology to think God as the cause of Being itself.

Furthermore, the concept of "thrown projection" fundamentally misconstrues the theological nature of human existence. In theology, human existence is not merely thrown into a meaningless world of contingency; it is created by a Word and sustained by a divine presence. The "thrownness" of *Dasein* is not merely a brute facticity, but a condition of creatureliness—a relation of dependence on the Creator. The "projection" of *Dasein* is not a secular reaching toward possibilities, but an eschatological orientation toward the Kingdom of God, a future that is promised and sustained by divine grace. When theology adopts Heidegger’s atheistic thrown projection, it loses the relational, covenantal, and grace-filled dimensions of human existence. It reduces the *fides qua*—the faith by which one believes—to an ontological structure of resoluteness, stripping it of its personal and dialogical character.

So, can theology achieve conceptual understanding through philosophy while remaining within faith? The answer, in light of Andersen’s critique and the structural analysis of Heidegger’s ontology, is a resounding negative—at least if philosophy is understood as Heideggerian fundamental ontology. Theology cannot utilize an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis of Dasein as thrown projection to account for theological phenomena like guilt before God and the creative Word without losing its necessary relation to faithful existence. The conceptual understanding that theology achieves cannot be grounded in a pre-theological ontology that is structurally atheistic, because such an ontology removes the very conditions of intelligibility for theological discourse. 

If theology is to remain within faith, its conceptual understanding must be grounded in faith itself. This does not mean theology can or should abandon philosophy. Theology has always utilized philosophy to articulate its doctrines, to clarify its concepts, and to engage in dialogue with the broader intellectual culture. However, the relationship between theology and philosophy must be reconfigured. Philosophy cannot be the *ground* of theology; it can only be a *clarifier* or a *dialogical partner*. Theology must not submit its fundamental concepts to the judgment of atheistic ontology, but must instead allow its faith to judge the adequacy of philosophical concepts. 

In this reconfigured relationship, philosophy does not provide the pre-theological foundation upon which theology builds; rather, philosophy operates within the horizon opened by theological claims. For instance, the analysis of human existence can be enriched by theological insights about creatureliness and sin, rather than being imposed upon theology as a neutral, atheistic framework. The "Being-guilty" of *Dasein* can be understood as a secular echo of the theological reality of sin, but it cannot be the foundation from which the theological reality of sin is derived. 

Ultimately, Heidegger’s demand that theology ground itself in pre-theological ontology represents a category mistake. Theology is not an ontic science that simply needs an ontological correction; it is a mode of existence that is constitutively shaped by the encounter with the divine. Nathan Andersen’s critique successfully demonstrates that an atheistic ontology cannot serve as the pre-theological foundation for a theology that begins and ends in faith. The non-faithful analysis of Dasein as thrown projection fails to account for the theological phenomena of guilt before God and the creative Word, because it severs the essential relation between human existence and the divine Word. Theology may engage philosophy, but it must do so as a faith that understands, never as an understanding that precedes and grounds faith.
