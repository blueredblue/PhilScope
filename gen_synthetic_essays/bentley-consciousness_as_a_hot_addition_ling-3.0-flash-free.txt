# Object Awareness, Fact Awareness, and the Nature of State Consciousness: A Critical Evaluation of the Higher-Order Thought Thesis

## I. Introduction

What makes a mental state conscious? This question lies at the heart of contemporary philosophy of mind and has generated a rich and contentious literature. One influential answer holds that a mental state achieves intransitive consciousness—that is, its being a conscious state in itself—only when it satisfies two conditions simultaneously: the subject must be directly acquainted with the object of the state (object awareness), and the subject must possess a higher-order thought representing that very state of affairs (fact awareness). On this view, consciousness is fundamentally a higher-order phenomenon, a kind of cognitive addition to what would otherwise be an unconscious mental occurrence.

This essay evaluates the claim that state consciousness requires both object awareness and fact awareness. It examines whether a higher-order thought (HOT), understood as fact awareness, is strictly necessary alongside direct acquaintance to confer intransitive consciousness upon a mental state. Drawing on Derek Browne's objection—which invokes Fred Dretske's important distinction between object awareness and fact awareness—the essay argues that Browne's case against the HOT addition thesis is powerful but ultimately insufficient. By critically assessing the counterexamples of blindsight and unconscious driving, the essay defends a nuanced version of the HOT thesis while acknowledging genuine tensions that any theory of consciousness must confront.

## II. The Architecture of State Consciousness: Object Awareness and Fact Awareness

To understand the claim under evaluation, one must first appreciate the conceptual architecture within which it operates. The basic intuition behind many higher-order theories of consciousness is deceptively simple: a mental state becomes conscious when the subject thinks about that state, or, more precisely, when the subject has a thought that represents the state as being in a certain condition. Rosenthal's version of HOT theory, for instance, holds that a mental state is conscious just in case there is a higher-order representation that takes that state as its object. The first-order state—say, a visual perception of a red apple—does not, by itself, constitute consciousness; it becomes conscious when a suitable higher-order thought represents the subject as being in that perceptual state.

The framework introduced here distinguishes two components that, on the composite view, jointly constitute state consciousness. The first is **object awareness**: the subject's direct, first-order acquaintance with the object or content of the mental state. When one sees a red apple, one is directly aware of the apple and its redness. This is a relational, causal, or representational connection between the subject and the object of experience. The second is **fact awareness**: awareness of the fact that one is in this mental state, or equivalently, awareness of the fact that one is aware of the object. Fact awareness is the higher-order component; it is the subject's representation of their own first-order mental condition.

The claim that state consciousness requires both object awareness and fact awareness can thus be stated as follows: a mental state M is conscious if and only if the subject is directly acquainted with the object of M and the subject has a higher-order thought that represents M as being in the relevant condition. Neither component alone is sufficient. Object awareness without fact awareness yields an unconscious mental state—a mere occurrence in the mind that lacks the "phenomenal character" or qualitative feel that we associate with conscious experience. Fact awareness without object awareness would be a peculiar case, perhaps akin to a higher-order thought about a non-existent or absent object, which most theorists would regard as either impossible or not genuinely conscious.

This composite view has several attractive features. It provides a clear criterion for distinguishing conscious from unconscious mental states, it avoids the notorious difficulties that plague pure first-order theories of consciousness (such as the problem of explaining why certain neural processes feel like something), and it coheres with our ordinary introspective practices, in which we become conscious of our mental states precisely by thinking about them or attending to them. Moreover, it aligns with the phenomenology of self-conscious experience, in which there seems to be an irreducible "what-it-is-like" quality that arises from the combination of a mental content and awareness of having that content.

## III. Dretske's Distinction and Browne's Objection

Fred Dretske's distinction between object awareness and fact awareness provides a crucial conceptual framework for evaluating higher-order theories of consciousness. Dretske, a prominent externalist and representationalist about mental content, drew a sharp line between a subject's awareness of an object—the target of a mental state—and a subject's awareness of facts about their own mental condition. Object awareness, for Dretske, is the direct, first-order relation between a subject and the world; it is what happens when perceptual experience succeeds in representing the environment. Fact awareness, by contrast, involves awareness of the fact that one has a particular mental state, or more broadly, awareness of the representational content of one's own mind.

Dretske himself did not necessarily endorse a HOT theory of consciousness, but his distinction has been fruitfully deployed by those who wish to challenge such theories. Derek Browne, in his critical engagement with higher-order accounts, draws on Dretske's framework to argue that object awareness is sufficient for consciousness and that fact awareness—the higher-order thought—is unnecessary. Browne's objection runs as follows: if we accept Dretske's distinction, then the first-order relation between a subject and an object of perception already contains all the information needed for consciousness. The subject is directly aware of the object; this direct awareness constitutes the phenomenal character of experience. To add a higher-order thought—that one is aware of the object—is to introduce a redundant cognitive layer that does nothing to generate or explain the qualitative feel of experience.

Browne's argument can be reconstructed in several ways. One version appeals to parsimony: if object awareness alone can account for the phenomenology of consciousness, then the additional postulation of fact awareness violates Ockham's razor. Another version appeals to the phenomenology of simple perceptual experiences: when one sees a red apple, one does not seem to be having a higher-order thought about one's perception; one is simply seeing. The consciousness of the experience seems to be located entirely in the first-order relation, not in any reflective or self-representational cognitive act. A third version invokes the problem of the " HOT theorist's dilemma": if the higher-order thought is itself a mental state, then it too would require consciousness, leading to an infinite regress; if it is not conscious, then it cannot confer consciousness on the first-order state.

Browne's objection is not merely a repetition of the familiar criticism that HOT theory is too intellectualistic or cognitive in its conception of consciousness. Rather, it is a sustained argument that draws on the representationalist framework to show that the object-awareness component already does the heavy lifting required for a theory of consciousness. If the representational content of a first-order state is rich enough to individuate its object and to guide behavior, then the state is conscious in virtue of that content alone. The HOT is, at most, an epiphenomenon or an introspective accompaniment—not a constitutive condition of consciousness.

## IV. Blindsight and the Limits of Object Awareness

To assess Browne's objection, one must examine whether there are cases in which object awareness seems to be present without fact awareness, yet the subject does not experience the relevant mental state as conscious. Blindsight is perhaps the most powerful and well-known of these cases.

Blindsight occurs in patients who have suffered damage to the primary visual cortex (V1) and thereby lose the capacity for conscious visual perception in certain regions of their visual field. Despite this blindness, these patients can sometimes respond to stimuli in their blind fields with above-chance accuracy—they can "guess" the direction of motion, the location of an object, or the shape of a stimulus—without having any conscious visual experience of the stimulus. The patient reports seeing nothing; yet their behavior indicates that some visual processing is occurring, some representation of the object is being constructed in the visual system.

The implications of blindsight for the object-awareness versus fact-awareness debate are profound. If object awareness alone were sufficient for consciousness, then the blindsight patient's visual system, which processes information about objects in the blind field, should produce conscious visual experiences. But it does not. The patient can discriminate the stimulus without seeing it. This suggests that object awareness—the first-order representation of an object—is not sufficient for consciousness; something else is needed to transform mere information processing into phenomenal experience.

The HOT theorist can exploit blindsight as evidence for the necessity of fact awareness. In the blindsight case, the first-order visual system represents the stimulus (object awareness is present), but there is no higher-order thought that takes this representation as its object (fact awareness is absent). The absence of the HOT correlates with the absence of consciousness. Therefore, the HOT is a necessary condition for consciousness. Browne might reply that blindsight reveals a deficit in the *quality* of object awareness rather than the absence of fact awareness; perhaps the visual representation in blindsight is degraded or impoverished in a way that precludes consciousness, and this degradation has nothing to do with the presence or absence of higher-order thoughts. But this reply is unsatisfying because it merely relocates the explanatory burden without providing a positive account of why degraded object awareness fails to produce consciousness.

The unconscious driving phenomenon provides another instructive case. Many drivers report that, during long and monotonous stretches of highway driving, they can continue to drive safely—maintaining lane position, adjusting speed, responding to traffic signals—while their conscious attention drifts. Upon "coming to," they have little or no memory of the intervening period. The question is whether their driving-related mental states during this period were conscious or unconscious.

If object awareness alone sufficed for consciousness, then the driver's perceptual and motor representations of the road, the lane markings, and the other vehicles would constitute conscious experiences. The driver would be consciously seeing the road and consciously controlling the car, even if they were not reflecting on these activities. But this seems counterintuitive. The driver's experience during the "zoning out" period is characterized precisely by a lack of conscious awareness of the driving activity. The mental states involved—the visual representations of the road, the motor commands steering the wheel—are, intuitively, unconscious. They occur without phenomenal character.

The HOT theorist can interpret this as follows: during unconscious driving, the first-order representations are active (object awareness is present), but there is no higher-order thought that takes these representations as its object (fact awareness is absent). The absence of the HOT corresponds to the absence of consciousness. This interpretation is strengthened by the fact that the driver can be brought back to conscious awareness of their driving simply by reflecting on it—that is, by generating a HOT about the driving-related mental states. The arrival of the HOT seems to transform unconscious processes into conscious experience.

Browne might respond by arguing that the "zoning out" driver lacks not fact awareness but a certain kind of object awareness—perhaps the object awareness is too diffuse or too automatic to count as genuine direct acquaintance with the objects of perception. But this response faces difficulties: if the driver's visual system is successfully representing the road and their motor system is successfully controlling the car, then in what sense is object awareness absent? The representational content is there; the causal and informational connections to the objects of perception are intact. It is only the higher-order awareness—the reflective "knowing that one is driving"—that is missing.

## V. Defending the HOT Addition Thesis

Having examined Browne's objection and the relevant counterexamples, the question remains: should we defend or reject the thesis that consciousness is fundamentally a HOT addition? The foregoing analysis suggests that the HOT addition thesis, properly understood, has considerable explanatory power and should be defended in a qualified form.

The defense proceeds in several steps. First, it must be acknowledged that Dretske's distinction between object awareness and fact awareness is a genuine and important conceptual distinction. We do distinguish, in our ordinary mental lives, between simply seeing a tree and knowing that we are seeing a tree, between having a sensation and reflecting on that sensation. This distinction maps onto the first-order/second-order structure that HOT theories exploit, and it provides a vocabulary for articulating why both components might be needed for consciousness.

Second, the counterexamples of blindsight and unconscious driving, far from undermining the HOT thesis, provide positive evidence for it. In both cases, the absence of a higher-order thought correlates with the absence of consciousness, even when first-order representations are functioning. The simplest and most explanatorily powerful hypothesis is that the HOT is what transforms unconscious mental processing into conscious experience. Without it, the mind processes information without the felt quality of consciousness.

Third, one should address Browne's parsimony objection directly. Browne argues that if object awareness alone can do the work of consciousness, then the HOT is an unnecessary addition. But this argument assumes that object awareness *can* do the work of consciousness—which is precisely what the counterexamples call into question. The parsimony argument is only successful against a thesis that has independent grounds for being true; if the thesis is supported by independent evidence (as the counterexamples provide), then the charge of ontological extravagance loses its force.

Fourth, one must clarify what kind of higher-order thought is required. Not every HOT theory posits the same kind of higher-order representation. Some, like Rosenthal's, hold that the HOT must be a thought that is itself unconscious—there is no requirement that the subject be aware of having the HOT. This "weak" HOT theory avoids the regress problem that Browne raises: the HOT need not itself be conscious in order to confer consciousness on the first-order state. It is a background cognitive operation that transforms the first-order state into a conscious one without itself being an object of consciousness.

Fifth, the defense should acknowledge a genuine limitation of the HOT thesis: it does not fully explain *why* the addition of a HOT produces consciousness. The HOT theorist can specify the necessary conditions for consciousness, but the deeper question—why should a thought about a mental state make that state feel like something?—remains open. This is not a fatal objection, because the HOT theory is primarily a theory of the *conditions* of consciousness, not a full metaphysical explanation of phenomenal character. But it is a limitation that any defender of the thesis must acknowledge.

## VI. Addressing Remaining Challenges

A fair evaluation must also consider challenges that remain even after the foregoing defense. One challenge concerns the "hard problem" of consciousness (Chalmers): even if we can specify the functional conditions under which a mental state becomes conscious, this does not explain why those conditions are accompanied by phenomenal experience. The HOT addition thesis is a theory about the functional and representational requirements for consciousness; it does not purport to solve the hard problem. Critics who demand an explanation of qualia may find the HOT thesis unsatisfying regardless of whether it correctly identifies the necessary conditions for consciousness.

A second challenge concerns the variety of conscious experiences. Are all conscious states—perceptual, emotional, memorial, imagistic—alike in requiring a HOT? Some theorists have argued that emotional experiences, for instance, may be conscious in virtue of their intrinsic phenomenal character rather than in virtue of a higher-order thought about them. If this is correct, then the HOT thesis is too narrow to capture the full range of conscious mental states. The defender of the thesis can respond by distinguishing between the *content* of an experience and its *consciousness*: while the content of an emotional experience may be intrinsically qualitative, its being a conscious experience may still require a HOT that represents the subject as being in that emotional state.

A third challenge concerns the temporal dynamics of consciousness. Sometimes we seem to become conscious of a mental state only after the fact—retrospectively. Does this mean that the HOT can follow the first-order state rather than occur simultaneously with it? Rosenthal's theory allows for this possibility: the HOT need not be contemporaneous with the first-order state. But this raises questions about the nature of the conscious experience itself—does it have the same character if it is retroactively made conscious? These questions remain unsettled and represent areas of ongoing research.

## VII. Conclusion

The claim that state consciousness requires both object awareness and fact awareness is a sophisticated and defensible position within the philosophy of mind. The HOT addition thesis—that consciousness is fundamentally a higher-order phenomenon—receives significant support from the analysis of blindsight and unconscious driving, both of which suggest that first-order representations alone are insufficient to produce phenomenal consciousness. Derek Browne's objection, drawing on Dretske's distinction between object awareness and fact awareness, raises important challenges to this thesis by arguing that direct acquaintance with an object already carries the full weight of consciousness. However, the counterexamples demonstrate that object awareness without fact awareness yields unconscious processing rather than conscious experience, and Browne's alternative explanations of these phenomena are less parsimonious and less explanatory than the HOT account.

The thesis that consciousness is fundamentally a HOT addition should therefore be defended, albeit in a qualified form. The HOT is not a mysterious addition that creates consciousness ex nihilo; it is a cognitive representation that transforms otherwise unconscious mental processes into conscious ones. The HOT need not itself be conscious, and it need not be contemporaneous with the first-order state. What it does is provide the subject with a representation of their own mental condition, and this representation is what makes that condition conscious. The HOT addition thesis thus offers a coherent, empirically grounded, and philosophically rigorous account of what it is for a mental state to be conscious—a theory that takes seriously both the representational character of the mind and the irreducibly subjective quality of conscious experience.
