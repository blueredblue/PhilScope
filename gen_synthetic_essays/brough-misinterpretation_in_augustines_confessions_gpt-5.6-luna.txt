# Signs at the Edge of Silence: Augustine, Misinterpretation, and the Impossible Text

Augustine’s reflections on language are marked by a fundamental tension. On the one hand, he insists that God exceeds every finite representation. Divine majesty cannot be contained by words, images, concepts, or even the most elevated forms of theological speech. Human language is temporal, material, conventional, and dependent upon the changing circumstances of bodily and historical life; God is eternal, immaterial, and immutable. No sign can be adequate to what it signifies. On the other hand, Augustine’s theology is inseparable from an extraordinary confidence in signs. Scripture, preaching, sacramental forms, memories, gestures, voices, and textual narratives all mediate divine truth. The Christian faith depends upon communication: God speaks through Scripture, Christ is understood as the divine Word, and the believer is called to interpret signs correctly.

This tension invites a distinctly modern question. If signs are inherently inadequate to divine reality, and if the meaning of a sign depends upon its reception within shifting contexts, does Augustine’s semiotic theory produce an unavoidable vulnerability to misinterpretation? More radically, does the distance between sign and divine reality generate a deconstructive freeplay in which meaning can never be stabilized by authorial intention? Augustine’s *Confessions* supplies striking examples of semantic failure. Alypius misreads a situation at the arena and is transformed by what he sees; Augustine hears the childlike command, “Take up and read,” and receives it as a divine summons, although its immediate semantic content is ambiguous. These episodes suggest that meaning does not simply pass from author or speaker to recipient. It emerges through a context of reception that may redirect, distort, or even exceed the original intention of the sign.

Yet Augustine does not therefore embrace unrestricted interpretive freeplay. His semiotics recognizes instability, but it places that instability within a theological order governed by divine providence, charity, and the distinction between signs and things. Authorial intention may fail to control reception, but meaning is not thereby abandoned to pure arbitrariness. The misinterpretation of a sign can become, retrospectively, the instrument of a higher intention. Augustine’s texts do not deny the possibility of meaning; rather, they make meaning dependent upon a process of conversion in which the inadequacy of signs becomes itself significant. The result is an “impossible text”: a text that attempts to speak of what cannot be adequately spoken, and that performs its own inadequacy as a form of praise. Augustine intentionally deploys semiotic failure as a paradoxical tribute to divine ineffability, but he does so without dissolving meaning into unrestricted play. His signs remain unstable because they are finite, yet they are not meaningless because their instability can direct the reader beyond itself.

## I. Augustine’s Semiotic Framework

Augustine’s account of signs in *On Christian Doctrine* begins from a distinction between things and signs. A thing is not a sign simply by existing; it becomes a sign when it is used to signify something beyond itself. Words are the most familiar signs, but they belong to a broader field that includes bodily gestures, ritual actions, objects, natural phenomena, and historical events. A sign is “a thing which causes us to think of something beyond the impression the thing itself makes upon the senses.” The sign has a double structure: it is materially present in itself, but it directs the mind toward something absent or not immediately given.

This structure already implies both the power and the danger of language. A sign can mediate knowledge precisely because it is not identical with what it signifies. But because it is not identical with its referent, it can also be misunderstood. The sign must be interpreted, and interpretation depends upon convention, habit, context, education, memory, and desire. Augustine distinguishes between natural signs, which signify without deliberate intention, and given signs, which living beings use in order to communicate their thoughts and feelings. Human speech belongs primarily to the latter category. Words do not naturally contain their meanings. Their meanings are established through social usage.

This conventionality does not make language arbitrary in the modern sense, but it does make language vulnerable. A word may be unknown, misheard, taken metaphorically when it is intended literally, or understood according to a different linguistic convention. The sign’s material form does not guarantee the meaning that a speaker intends. Augustine’s own discussions of Scripture repeatedly acknowledge that the reader can misunderstand a passage, especially when the reader lacks linguistic knowledge, historical context, or spiritual maturity.

At the same time, Augustine’s semiotic theory is not merely a theory of communication between human subjects. It is ordered toward the distinction between signs and things, or, more precisely, toward the movement from signs to the realities they signify. In Christian interpretation, the ultimate “thing” is God, although God is not a thing among other things. The created world, Scripture, and the historical life of Christ all function as signs that direct the mind toward divine reality. Signs are therefore necessary, but they are not final. They are instruments of transit.

This distinction also grounds Augustine’s famous difference between using and enjoying. Created realities are to be used insofar as they direct human beings toward God; God alone is to be enjoyed as the final end. The danger of sin is not simply that one may misread a sign, but that one may treat the sign as the final object of desire. One may become attached to the medium rather than moving through it toward its source. A theological semiotics therefore requires a spiritual discipline of detachment. The reader must neither reject signs as useless nor worship them as divine in themselves.

The semiotic problem becomes especially acute when the sign is supposed to communicate God. If signs are finite, conventional, and bodily, how can they represent an infinite and immaterial God? Augustine’s answer is not that signs become adequate to God. Rather, God accommodates himself to human weakness through signs. Scripture speaks in human language; Christ appears in a human body; the sacraments present invisible grace through visible forms. Divine communication is therefore condescension, not containment. God’s truth is present in signs without being exhausted by them.

This distinction is crucial. The inadequacy of language does not entail its uselessness. A finite sign can genuinely communicate what it cannot fully contain. The sign does not reproduce divine reality as an image reproduces a visible object. It directs the mind toward a reality whose fullness exceeds the sign. Augustine’s theory is consequently neither a naïve realism nor a complete skepticism. Signs are true by participation and reference, but their truth is always disproportionate to their referent.

## II. Language, Memory, and the Failure of Representation in the *Confessions*

The *Confessions* dramatizes Augustine’s semiotic theory rather than merely stating it. It is a text about the difficulty of speaking truthfully about a life, a God, and a conversion whose significance becomes clear only retrospectively. Augustine begins by praising God, but the very opening of the work identifies a contradiction: human beings desire to praise God, yet their speech is inadequate to the divine greatness. “Great are you, Lord, and greatly to be praised,” Augustine declares, while immediately recognizing that the human being is finite and unstable. The act of praise thus begins in a confession of insufficiency.

The problem is not only that God exceeds human language. Augustine also finds himself divided from himself. He must remember what he was in order to narrate it, but memory is not a transparent storehouse. In Book X, he describes memory as vast, mysterious, and difficult to comprehend. It contains images, emotions, knowledge, and traces of bodily experience, yet it cannot be fully grasped by the subject who searches it. Augustine is both the speaker and the object of his own investigation. His language attempts to represent a self that is internally divided, historically changing, and known only through recollection.

This produces a peculiar form of textual instability. Augustine’s narrative is written after the fact, and the later Augustine interprets the earlier Augustine. The meaning of past events is not fixed at the moment they occur. Events acquire significance through their later incorporation into a narrative of providence and conversion. The past is not simply reproduced; it is reread. The *Confessions* therefore embodies a hermeneutic in which meaning is generated retrospectively.

That retrospective structure can appear to undermine authorial intention. The younger Augustine often does not understand what is happening to him, while the mature Augustine assigns theological significance to his earlier experiences. The original actors in the narrative do not possess the same interpretation as the narrator. Even Augustine’s own previous intentions are exceeded by the consequences of his actions. The text can thus be read as showing that no speaker fully controls the meaning of his signs. A word, event, or gesture may enter contexts that transform its significance.

Yet this does not mean that Augustine treats interpretation as purely subjective. Retrospection is not arbitrary invention. It is governed by the conviction that God’s providence was at work even when Augustine did not recognize it. The mature narrator does not simply impose a meaning upon meaningless events; he discovers a pattern that was hidden from him. The narrative claims that divine intention can exceed human intention without being reducible to it. The failure of the human subject to control meaning becomes evidence of providence rather than proof of nihilism.

## III. Alypius and the Contextual Production of Meaning

Alypius’s experience at the arena in Book VI provides one of the clearest examples of how reception can redirect the meaning of a sign. Alypius initially resists attending the games because he has rejected the violent spectacle. His friends nevertheless bring him there, and he enters with his eyes closed so that he will not be seduced by what he sees. When the crowd erupts, curiosity leads him to open his eyes. At that instant, he is captivated by the spectacle and becomes, in Augustine’s words, “wounded more deeply in his soul” than by the wound inflicted upon the body of the gladiator.

The episode is a story of failed intention. Alypius intends not to watch. He believes that closing his eyes will protect him from the spectacle. His deliberate act of refusal is transformed by the surrounding context. The roar of the crowd becomes a sign that attracts him, and his attempt to resist the spectacle becomes the condition of his surrender to it. The event’s meaning is not determined by Alypius’s initial intention. The arena’s sounds, the crowd’s emotional force, and his own curiosity create a context in which the sign acquires an unexpected power.

Alypius’s “misinterpretation” is not a misunderstanding of a proposition in the narrow sense. Rather, it is a misreading of the situation. He interprets the spectacle as something he can encounter without being affected by it. He assumes that his own intention will remain sovereign over the sign. But the sign acts upon him before he can master it conceptually. The visual spectacle is not simply an object waiting to be judged; it reorganizes the viewer’s desire. Reception is therefore active and transformative. The sign does not merely transmit an already fixed meaning. It produces an effect in the recipient.

This event complicates any simple notion of authorial intent. The spectacle has no single author in the conventional sense. The organizers intend to display a gladiatorial contest; the crowd interprets it as entertainment; Alypius initially interprets it as a temptation to be avoided. The same event enters several contexts of reception and acquires different meanings in each. The arena’s signification is dispersed among institutional, collective, and individual perspectives.

Augustine’s narrative also suggests that the meaning of the event exceeds all these human intentions. Alypius’s fall is not the final meaning of the story. Later, he returns to a more rational and Christian life, and his conversion is narrated as part of the providential history of Augustine’s circle. The same person who was captured by the spectacle becomes an example through which Augustine reflects upon the mechanisms of desire and the power of signs. The event is reread within a larger narrative.

This rereading does not erase the original misinterpretation. Augustine does not claim that Alypius secretly intended to be seduced or that his initial resistance was false. Rather, the text preserves the gap between intention and effect. Alypius’s will is sincere but insufficient. His misreading becomes the occasion for a more profound lesson: human beings do not fully know how signs operate upon them. The spectacle exposes the vulnerability of the interpreter to the sign’s material and affective force.

In this sense, Alypius anticipates a deconstructive account of signification. The sign does not remain under the control of its sender or its intended recipient. It circulates, enters different contexts, and generates effects that cannot be predicted in advance. Meaning is inseparable from reception. The sign is structurally open to being taken otherwise.

But Augustine’s account also resists the conclusion that this openness amounts to unrestricted freeplay. The episode has a moral and theological direction. Alypius’s reception is judged according to the ordering of his love. The problem is not simply that he interprets differently from the organizer of the games; it is that his desire is drawn toward an object that ought not to command it. Augustine’s semiotics is therefore inseparable from ethics. Signs may be open to multiple receptions, but not every reception is equally truthful or good. Interpretation is evaluated by what it does to the soul and by whether it directs desire toward or away from God.

## IV. The Garden Conversion and the Ambiguity of “Take Up and Read”

The garden conversion in Book VIII appears to provide an even more explicit instance of semantic failure. Augustine is overcome by the conflict between his desire for conversion and his continuing attachment to worldly habits. He withdraws from Alypius into the garden and experiences an intense crisis of will. Weeping beneath a fig tree, he hears a child’s voice from a nearby house repeatedly saying, “Take up and read; take up and read.” Augustine interprets this sound as a divine command. He opens Paul’s Epistle and reads the passage that commands the reader to abandon the works of the flesh and “put on the Lord Jesus Christ.”

Here the sign’s meaning is dramatically unstable. The child’s words, in their immediate context, are not addressed to Augustine. They appear to belong to a children’s game, perhaps a chant or rhyme. The child does not intend to command Augustine to read Scripture. The words have one social meaning within the child’s setting and another meaning for Augustine. Their semantic force is generated through reception. Augustine hears an utterance that was not authored for him and receives it as a personal divine summons.

This event seems to confirm that meaning is not reducible to authorial intention. The child’s intention does not govern Augustine’s interpretation. Nor can the utterance’s meaning be identified with its conventional use. Augustine appropriates it in a new context, linking it to his crisis and to the scriptural text that follows. A casual phrase becomes an oracle. The sign’s significance is produced by the convergence of sound, circumstance, desire, and prior scriptural expectation.

From a modern perspective, one might describe this as a paradigmatic case of textual freeplay. The words are detached from their original context and inserted into another. Their source is indeterminate, and their meaning proliferates. The child’s voice, Augustine’s interpretation, Paul’s epistle, and the later narrative of conversion form a chain of signs in which each element refers beyond itself. No single authorial intention stabilizes the chain. The child does not intend Augustine’s conversion; Paul did not write specifically about Augustine’s garden crisis; Augustine’s later account is itself a reconstruction of the event.

Nevertheless, Augustine does not present the conversion as a random linguistic accident. He explicitly interprets the voice as a divine instrument. The event is meaningful because God uses an ordinary sign in an extraordinary way. The child’s words are not miraculous because their literal content is obscure or because they carry an intrinsic sacred quality. They become significant through providential placement. The ambiguity of the sign is not eliminated; it is appropriated.

The garden scene thus distinguishes between human intention and divine intention. The child does not intend to address Augustine, but God can intend through the child’s utterance what the child does not intend. The event illustrates a layered structure of signification. At the first level, the words belong to a game. At the second, Augustine receives them as a command. At the third, the text presents them as an act of providence. The meaning of the sign is not collapsed into any one of these levels.

This complicates the claim that authorial intention collapses under contextual reception. If “authorial intention” refers to the child’s conscious purpose, then it clearly fails to control the meaning Augustine receives. But if the relevant author is God, Augustine’s account claims that divine intention remains operative even when human intention is absent or contrary. The text therefore relocates, rather than simply abolishes, authorial authority. It moves from human authorship to divine authorship.

There is an important philosophical problem here. If any chance utterance can be retrospectively interpreted as divine communication, what prevents arbitrary projection? Augustine’s narrative does not offer a neutral method for proving that the voice was divine. Its status is established by the convergence between the command and the scriptural passage, by the moral transformation that follows, and by the coherence of the event within a larger Christian narrative. This is not demonstrative certainty in the manner of a syllogism. It is a hermeneutic judgment shaped by faith and by the fruits of interpretation.

The garden scene therefore reveals both the power and risk of Augustine’s semiotics. Signs are not self-interpreting. Their meaning depends upon the interpreter’s situation, desires, memories, and expectations. Yet interpretation can be judged by the transformation it produces. Augustine’s reading is validated, within the narrative, because it turns him away from fragmentation and toward a more unified love of God. Meaning is not only what a sign says; it is also what the sign does within a life.

## V. Divine Ineffability and the Limits of Representation

The theological ground of Augustine’s semiotics is the inadequacy of finite language before divine reality. God is not one object among others that can be represented by a sufficiently accurate description. God is the source of being, truth, and intelligibility. To speak of God is therefore to speak of the condition that makes speech possible. Human language depends upon distinctions, temporal sequence, and finite concepts, while God exceeds all such conditions.

Augustine repeatedly emphasizes this excess. In the opening of the *Confessions*, he asks how human beings can call upon God before they know him and how they can know him without calling upon him. The question reveals a circularity at the heart of religious language. God must already be present as the condition of the search, yet the seeker does not possess God as an object of knowledge. The language of prayer emerges from a relation that it cannot fully explain.

The problem intensifies in Book XI, where Augustine reflects upon time. He can describe past, present, and future, but when he attempts to define time, the object of his inquiry recedes. The past is no longer present, the future is not yet present, and the present seems to vanish as soon as it is isolated. Augustine’s famous formulation—that if no one asks him what time is, he knows, but if asked, he does not know—expresses the instability of conceptual representation. The failure is not simply personal confusion. It shows that human understanding is embedded in the temporal reality it tries to describe.

If finite concepts fail even to capture time, how much more must they fail to capture God? Divine ineffability does not mean that nothing can be said of God. Augustine speaks constantly of divine goodness, eternity, wisdom, and power. But these predicates must be understood analogically and negatively. They do not place God within the conceptual field as an object. They signify by pointing beyond the limits of ordinary finite reference.

This creates what may be called an impossible textual demand. Augustine must write a text whose subject cannot be adequately represented by any text. The *Confessions* must speak of God while acknowledging that God surpasses speech. It must narrate a conversion whose ultimate agent is divine grace, although grace cannot be represented as a visible event in the same way as a physical action. It must describe inward experience while admitting that the interior self is not transparent even to itself.

The text is “impossible” not because it cannot exist, but because its successful completion would require what no finite text can provide: an exhaustive presence of divine reality within language. Augustine’s writing therefore cannot achieve closure in the form of total representation. It ends in praise, prayer, and further interpretation. The text points beyond itself.

This impossibility resembles the deconstructive notion that a sign never arrives at a final, self-present meaning. The sign bears traces of other signs and depends upon differences that prevent it from coinciding with its referent. Augustine’s theology can appear to anticipate this structure. God is always beyond the sign, and the sign’s inadequacy is not accidental but constitutive. The gap between word and reality is what makes signification possible.

Yet Augustine’s conception differs from a purely deconstructive account in one decisive respect. For deconstruction, the endless deferral of meaning may be treated as a structural condition without a final theological destination. For Augustine, the deferral of the sign is oriented toward God. The sign’s failure to contain the divine is not simply an endless postponement; it is the movement by which finite understanding is awakened to transcendence. The sign does not reach God by becoming adequate to God. It reaches God by recognizing its inadequacy and directing desire beyond itself.

## VI. Does Inadequacy Generate Deconstructive Freeplay?

The language of “freeplay” is useful if it names the instability, mobility, and contextual openness of signs. Augustine clearly understands that signs do not carry meaning in a self-sufficient way. Their significance depends upon use, reception, convention, and the spiritual condition of the interpreter. The same words can deceive, instruct, tempt, or convert. A sign’s effect cannot be predicted solely from its form or from the intention of its producer.

Moreover, Augustine’s texts repeatedly displace stable origins. God’s meaning is communicated through human authors who do not exhaust it. Scripture contains obscurities that require interpretation. The reader’s reception becomes part of the text’s life. In the garden, a child’s words become a divine message without the child’s intention. In Alypius’s case, a spectacle becomes a temptation through the viewer’s response. The sign is not a sealed container of meaning; it is an event in which meaning takes shape.

In this limited sense, Augustine’s semiotics does generate a field of interpretive play. The sign can be detached from its first context and reinscribed in another. Its meaning can exceed its origin. The reader can discover significance that no human author consciously intended. A written text can produce effects beyond the circumstances of its composition. Augustine’s own *Confessions* is an example: it is addressed to God, but it also addresses readers who may interpret Augustine’s experiences differently from Augustine’s stated purposes.

However, the term “freeplay” becomes misleading if it suggests that all interpretations are equally valid or that meaning is generated without norms. Augustine’s *On Christian Doctrine* is deeply concerned with rules of interpretation. The reader must consider grammar, rhetoric, historical context, figurative language, and the relation between the parts and whole of Scripture. More importantly, interpretation must be guided by the command to love God and neighbor. Augustine allows that a mistaken interpretation may be acceptable if it leads to charity and does not contradict the faith, but this does not mean that interpretation is unconstrained. The rule of love is a criterion that distinguishes spiritually fruitful readings from those that promote pride, violence, or domination.

This criterion is not merely external to language. It reveals that Augustine understands interpretation as a transformation of desire. The problem of misinterpretation is not only epistemological; it is ethical. A person may understand the words of Scripture literally and still misunderstand their purpose if the reading produces hatred or self-glorification. Conversely, a reader may fail to recover the historical detail of a passage but grasp its spiritual direction. Correct interpretation is therefore connected to the orientation of the interpreter’s will.

Augustine also distinguishes between signs that are used and realities that are enjoyed. Misinterpretation occurs when the reader mistakes the sign for the end. This gives Augustine’s semiotics a built-in account of the danger of freeplay. Signs must move, but they must move toward something. If the movement never reaches a referent or final good, interpretation becomes self-enclosed. Augustine’s concern is not that signs have too much mobility but that the interpreter may stop at the wrong place.

Thus, Augustine’s semiotic world is better described as constrained or teleological play. Meaning is open because signs are finite and mediated; it is constrained because interpretation is ordered toward truth, charity, and God. The openness is real, but it is not directionless. Divine transcendence guarantees that no finite interpretation is final, while divine reality guarantees that interpretation is not merely arbitrary.

## VII. Authorial Intention and Its Partial Collapse

The examples of Alypius and Augustine’s garden conversion challenge the authority of authorial intention in different ways. Alypius’s experience shows that the recipient’s response can overturn the intended function of a sign. A spectacle designed as entertainment becomes a spiritual danger. The garden conversion shows that an utterance can acquire a new meaning when removed from its original context. A child’s game becomes a divine command. In both cases, the sign’s effect exceeds the intentions that seem to govern it.

But “authorial intention” itself must be disaggregated. There is the intention of the immediate speaker, the intention of the institutional producer, the intention of the recipient, and the intention assigned retrospectively by the narrator. Augustine’s semiotics does not deny that these intentions exist; it denies that any one of them can exhaust the sign’s meaning. Meaning is relational and historical. It arises from the interaction of sign, context, interpreter, and reality.

The collapse of authorial intention is therefore partial rather than absolute. Human intention cannot guarantee reception. A speaker may mean one thing and be understood as meaning another. A text may generate consequences its author did not foresee. A sign may become part of a providential pattern unknown to its human agents. But Augustine does not infer that intention is irrelevant. The recognition of misinterpretation presupposes that there was something to be misinterpreted. Alypius’s resistance matters because it was genuinely intended. The child’s utterance has a literal context even though Augustine receives it differently. Scripture’s human authors matter even though the text has a divine depth beyond their consciousness.

Augustine’s theology makes room for multiple levels of intention. Human authorial intention can be exceeded by divine intention, but it is not simply cancelled. This structure resembles the relation between the literal and spiritual senses of Scripture. The literal meaning is not discarded when a spiritual meaning is found. Rather, it becomes the vehicle through which a further meaning is disclosed. The text has more meaning than the human author may have intended, but not less.

This distinction also affects the status of the *Confessions* itself. Augustine writes with a clear purpose: to confess sin, praise grace, instruct readers, and invite them into a shared movement toward God. Yet the narrative contains ambiguities and excesses that cannot be completely controlled by this purpose. Readers may emphasize autobiography, psychology, rhetoric, memory, politics, or theology in ways that Augustine could not have anticipated. The text has a life beyond its author.

Augustine would not necessarily regard this excess as a failure. A text addressed to God is already oriented beyond the finite consciousness of its writer. The writer’s inability to control every reception may be consistent with the text’s participation in a larger truth. The authorial voice is not sovereign; it is itself a sign. Augustine writes as one who has been interpreted by God before he interprets himself. His authority rests not on possessing the final meaning of his life but on confessing his dependence upon a meaning that exceeds him.

## VIII. Semiotic Failure as Praise

The most compelling interpretation of Augustine’s textual practice is that he intentionally deploys semiotic failure as a paradoxical tribute to divine ineffability. His writing repeatedly approaches the limits of speech, displays those limits, and turns them into acts of praise. The inability to say everything about God is not merely a defect to be corrected. It is evidence that the object of discourse is greater than the discourse.

This strategy is visible in the rhetorical form of the *Confessions*. Augustine often asks questions whose answers exceed the capacity of ordinary explanation. He interrupts narrative with prayer. He moves from description to metaphor, from metaphor to paradox, and from paradox to silence. These movements do not simply abandon reason. They show reason discovering that its proper fulfillment lies in recognizing its dependence upon what it cannot master.

The text’s failure to represent God fully can therefore function positively. If Augustine were to present a complete and transparent account of God, he would reduce God to the level of a finite object. Such success would be theological failure. The very incompleteness of the text protects divine transcendence. Augustine must write, but he must write in such a way that the reader is not allowed to mistake the writing for God.

This is why the distinction between sign and thing is indispensable. A sign that makes the referent fully present would cease to function as a sign and would become the thing itself. In the case of God, such a transformation is impossible. The sign remains a sign because God remains beyond it. The text is faithful to God not by abolishing distance but by preserving a distance through which desire can move.

The *Confessions* thus becomes an impossible text in a precise sense. It is impossible as an exhaustive representation, but possible as a performative act of orientation. It does not contain God; it turns the reader toward God. It does not eliminate ambiguity; it teaches the reader how to inhabit ambiguity without confusing it with indifference. It does not produce divine presence as an object; it enacts a relationship of praise, dependence, and seeking.

The same is true of the garden conversion. “Take up and read” is not powerful because the phrase contains an unambiguous command. Its power lies in the way Augustine receives it within a crisis and allows it to generate action. The sign’s inadequacy—its indeterminate origin, its borrowed context, its ambiguous literal meaning—becomes the condition of its theological force. Had the voice been an explicit heavenly proclamation, it might have overwhelmed interpretation. Instead, God’s call arrives through a fragile and ordinary sign, leaving Augustine to discern and respond.

This is an important theological paradox. Divine transcendence does not require God to communicate through signs that are proportionate to divine greatness. On the contrary, God may choose humble, obscure, and inadequate signs precisely because such signs preserve human freedom and expose the interpretive character of faith. The reader must not only receive information but undergo conversion. The sign becomes effective through a response that cannot be mechanically produced.

## IX. The Limits of the Deconstructive Analogy

A comparison with deconstruction clarifies Augustine’s originality but also reveals the limits of assimilating him to a deconstructive thinker. Augustine recognizes that the sign is never self-identical, that meaning depends upon difference and context, and that no finite text can secure a fully present meaning. He also sees that interpretation can expose contradictions within the interpreter and that language often says more—or less—than its speaker intends.

But Augustine does not make undecidability the final truth of language. The fact that signs can be misread does not imply that there is no correct reading. The fact that the divine referent exceeds every sign does not imply that the referent is absent. The impossibility of complete representation is not the impossibility of truth. Augustine’s theology depends upon a strong distinction between God and creation, even though God cannot be captured in created concepts.

In a deconstructive framework, the instability of signs may be treated as intrinsic and irresolvable. In Augustine, instability is a consequence of creaturely finitude and can become a path toward truth. The gap between sign and referent is not simply a linguistic abyss. It is the interval between Creator and creature, and it is traversed by grace. Augustine’s semiotics is therefore not closed within language. Language points beyond itself toward a reality that is not another sign.

This does not make Augustine naïvely foundationalist. God cannot be possessed as a final signified in the same manner as an empirical object. The divine referent is not a stable presence that interpretation can finally seize. It is known through participation, transformation, and love. But this is different from saying that reference fails altogether. For Augustine, the sign’s failure to contain God is the condition under which God can remain God.

The distinction is especially important for the interpretation of the garden conversion. A purely freeplay-oriented reading would emphasize the arbitrary transfer of the child’s words into Augustine’s religious context. Augustine’s own account, however, claims that the transfer is not arbitrary but providential. The sign’s meaning is not fixed by the child’s intention, yet neither is it generated by Augustine alone. It is received as a gift. Theological interpretation introduces an agency that is neither human author nor subjective reader: divine providence.

Whether one accepts that theological agency is, of course, a matter of philosophical and religious commitment. A secular reader may regard Augustine’s providential interpretation as a retrospective construction that imposes coherence upon an ambiguous event. Such a reading is entirely possible and demonstrates the openness of Augustine’s text. Yet even this critical reading should recognize that the *Confessions* itself is designed to display the process by which retrospective meaning is made. Augustine does not hide the interpretive leap; he narrates it. The text invites the reader to examine the conditions under which an ordinary sound becomes a sacred sign.

## X. Conclusion: Meaning Beyond Mastery

Augustine’s semiotic theory does produce an unavoidable vulnerability to misinterpretation. Because signs are finite, conventional, contextual, and dependent upon reception, they cannot guarantee that a speaker’s intention will govern their meaning. Alypius’s encounter with the arena demonstrates that a sign can act upon a recipient in ways that defeat deliberate resistance. The garden conversion demonstrates that a phrase can be detached from its original context and received as a message never intended by its immediate speaker. In both instances, authorial or initial intention fails to determine the sign’s significance.

The inadequacy of language before divine reality intensifies this vulnerability. If God cannot be represented exhaustively, every theological sign remains open, partial, and subject to further interpretation. Augustine’s texts therefore generate something like a deconstructive freeplay of signs: meanings circulate, origins are displaced, and reception becomes constitutive rather than merely derivative. The *Confessions* is itself an impossible text because it speaks about a reality that exceeds speech while making its own failure to represent that reality part of its meaning.

Nevertheless, Augustine does not embrace unrestricted freeplay. He does not conclude that interpretation is arbitrary or that truth is dissolved into endless deferral. Signs are unstable because they are signs, but they remain oriented toward things; language is inadequate, but it can still direct the mind and desire toward God. The criterion of interpretation is not the recovery of a fully transparent authorial intention but participation in truth and charity. Human intention may be exceeded, yet divine intention can operate through that excess. Misinterpretation can become the site of providential disclosure.

The apparent contradiction between divine ineffability and theological speech is therefore resolved not by choosing silence over language or language over silence. Augustine writes at the point where speech acknowledges its own insufficiency. His text does not capture God; it performs the movement of seeking, remembering, confessing, and praising. Its failures are not accidental blemishes upon an otherwise successful representation. They are the formal signs of transcendence.

Augustine intentionally deploys semiotic failure as a tribute to the divine. The inadequacy of the sign becomes an icon of God’s excess. The sign is valuable because it does not imprison its referent. It sends the reader beyond itself, but not into an empty void. It directs the reader toward a reality that cannot be possessed as an object yet can be encountered through conversion, love, and praise. In Augustine, then, the instability of meaning is neither simply a threat nor simply a liberation. It is the condition of a faith that must interpret without mastering, speak without containing, and read its way toward what no finite text can finally say.
