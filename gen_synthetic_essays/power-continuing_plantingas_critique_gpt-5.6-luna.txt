# Evolution, Naturalism, and the Reliability of Probability Judgment

## Introduction

Alvin Plantinga’s evolutionary argument against naturalism is one of the most influential contemporary arguments concerning the relationship between science, metaphysics, and epistemology. Its central claim is not that biological evolution is false, nor that natural selection is incapable of producing organisms with sophisticated cognitive capacities. Rather, Plantinga argues that the conjunction of metaphysical naturalism and evolutionary theory—usually abbreviated “N&E”—provides a reason to doubt the reliability of the very cognitive faculties by which N&E is believed. If a person comes to accept naturalism and evolution, Plantinga contends, that person acquires a defeater for the reliability of human cognition. Since the belief in N&E is itself produced by human cognition, the worldview appears epistemically self-defeating.

Matthew Power develops this argument in a further direction. The issue, on Power’s extension, is not merely whether human faculties are generally reliable with respect to ordinary beliefs. It is also whether they are reliable when making judgments about probabilities, especially judgments concerning the probability that our cognitive faculties are reliable given naturalism and evolution. If unguided evolution selects primarily for behavior conducive to survival and reproduction, rather than for truth as such, then the naturalist may lack grounds for trusting not only ordinary beliefs but also complex probability assessments. This threatens the naturalist’s attempt to respond to Plantinga by assigning a modest or plausible probability to the reliability of cognition under N&E.

The resulting question is highly significant: can a defender of metaphysical naturalism and neo-Darwinian evolution rationally assign even a minimal probability to the truth of that worldview, or to the reliability of cognition given that worldview? If the naturalist cannot trust the faculties that generate probability judgments, then perhaps the naturalist cannot rationally say that the relevant probability is low, moderate, or even nonzero in any meaningful sense. Yet this conclusion raises a further problem. Human beings possess evolved, survival-oriented capacities for rapid judgment—what might be called gut reactions—and these capacities often work reasonably well in ordinary environments. Why should such capacities not support at least some probability judgments? And if they do, does theism really become a necessary condition for epistemically justified probability assessment?

The strongest conclusion is neither that naturalism is immediately incoherent nor that theism is demonstrably required for every probability judgment. Rather, Plantinga’s argument exposes a serious internal tension in N&E. The naturalist can assign a probability to N&E only by relying on some account of cognitive reliability, but the naturalistic account of reliability cannot be assumed without qualification. Power’s extension deepens this difficulty by emphasizing that probabilistic reasoning is itself an epistemic achievement requiring trust in highly structured cognitive processes. Nevertheless, the extension does not establish that all probability judgments are impossible under naturalism, nor does it prove that theism is the only possible basis for epistemic justification. It shows instead that naturalism requires a substantive and non-question-begging explanation of why human probability judgments deserve trust.

## I. Plantinga’s Evolutionary Argument Against Naturalism

Plantinga’s argument depends upon distinguishing three propositions:

1. **N**: metaphysical naturalism is true;
2. **E**: contemporary evolutionary theory is true;
3. **R**: human cognitive faculties are reliable, meaning that they generally produce true beliefs under appropriate conditions.

The relevant conjunction is N&E. Plantinga’s claim is that a person who accepts N&E should assign a low or inscrutable probability to R:

\[
P(R \mid N\&E)
\]

Either this conditional probability is low, or it cannot reasonably be estimated from the naturalist’s position. If that is so, then the person who believes N&E also has a defeater for R. But R is indispensable to rational belief generally. If one has reason to doubt that one’s cognitive faculties are reliable, then one has reason to doubt every belief produced by those faculties, including the belief in N&E. The argument therefore aims to show that N&E is self-defeating.

The force of the argument depends on the distinction between adaptive behavior and true belief. Natural selection directly favors traits that contribute to survival and reproduction. It does not directly favor truth. A false belief can produce adaptive behavior, and a true belief can produce maladaptive behavior. For example, an organism might flee from a harmless shape because it falsely believes the shape to be a predator. If the cost of such false alarms is low and the cost of failing to flee from a genuine predator is high, selection may favor the disposition to flee—even if the associated representation is false.

Plantinga uses this point to argue that there may be many combinations of beliefs and desires that yield adaptive behavior. An organism might possess true beliefs that generate useful actions, but it might also possess false beliefs combined with suitable desires. A creature could, in principle, believe that a predator is approaching when no predator is present, while desiring safety and therefore fleeing. Alternatively, it might have an entirely different internal representation that nevertheless produces the same adaptive action. The relation between truth and adaptive behavior is therefore not one-to-one.

The argument does not require that evolution generally produces false beliefs. It requires only that, given naturalism and evolution, the probability of reliable cognition is not sufficiently high to be taken for granted. The naturalist may reply that creatures with systematically false beliefs would be unable to navigate the world successfully. Yet this reply establishes, at most, a broad correlation between some forms of truth and adaptive success. It does not by itself establish that human cognition is reliable across all domains, especially abstract, theoretical, metaphysical, and probabilistic domains.

This qualification is important. Evolutionary pressures clearly favor accurate perception of some features of the environment. A creature that consistently mistakes cliffs for level ground, or predators for harmless objects, will generally be disadvantaged. But it does not follow that evolution favors the capacity to reason correctly about quantum mechanics, transfinite mathematics, metaphysical naturalism, or the probability of cognitive reliability conditional on an evolutionary worldview. These are not the kinds of tasks that directly shaped the ancestral environment.

Plantinga’s conclusion is therefore epistemological rather than biological. He does not deny that evolution can produce creatures with true beliefs. He argues that the naturalist lacks a sufficient reason to regard the connection between evolutionary adaptation and general cognitive reliability as secure. The naturalist’s problem is not that evolutionary theory predicts universal irrationality. It is that naturalism and evolution appear to undermine the warrant for trusting the faculties that are used to defend them.

## II. The Self-Defeating Character of N&E

The self-defeat structure can be expressed as follows:

1. If N&E is true, then the probability of R is low or inscrutable.
2. If one believes that the probability of R is low or inscrutable, one has a defeater for R.
3. If one has a defeater for R, one has a defeater for any belief produced by one’s cognitive faculties.
4. The belief in N&E is produced by one’s cognitive faculties.
5. Therefore, belief in N&E is defeated.

A defeater need not demonstrate that a belief is false. It is sufficient that it removes or undermines the justification one previously had for the belief. If I learn that my thermometer is unreliable, I need not conclude that its current reading is false. I simply lose my justification for trusting the reading. Likewise, if the naturalist acquires a reason to doubt the reliability of cognition, the naturalist need not conclude that naturalism or evolution is false. The problem is that the naturalist no longer has adequate grounds for believing them.

There is an important distinction between a **rebutting defeater** and an **undercutting defeater**. A rebutting defeater gives reason to believe that a proposition is false. An undercutting defeater attacks the connection between evidence and belief. Plantinga’s argument is primarily an undercutting argument. N&E undercuts the reliability of the cognitive faculties, thereby undercutting the beliefs generated by those faculties.

The naturalist might attempt to avoid self-defeat by saying that the belief in N&E is supported by empirical evidence independently of a general commitment to R. For example, fossils, genetics, comparative anatomy, and observed selection may seem to provide strong evidence for evolution. But the interpretation of evidence depends on cognitive capacities. The naturalist must trust perception, memory, inference, testimony, mathematical reasoning, and the methods by which data are collected and interpreted. If R is in doubt, the empirical evidence cannot simply be treated as an untouched foundation.

Nor can the naturalist easily respond that evolution itself confirms the reliability of cognition. To argue that evolution produces reliable cognition because reliable cognition is necessary for evolutionary success already assumes that the relevant beliefs and inferences are trustworthy. The naturalist would need to establish, without circularity, that the evolutionary process tends to generate accurate representations over the relevant range of cognitive tasks. The mere fact that organisms survive does not settle that question.

Still, the self-defeat argument should not be overstated. Plantinga’s conclusion depends on the probability premise. The naturalist can challenge the claim that \(P(R \mid N\&E)\) is low or inscrutable. One might argue that although truth is not directly selected for, sufficiently accurate cognitive representation is usually required for complex behavior. Organisms that cannot distinguish food from poison, allies from predators, or safe terrain from dangerous terrain will be selected against. As cognitive complexity increases, an internal model of the environment may have to be broadly accurate in order to support flexible planning and cooperation.

This response has force, but it does not entirely defeat Plantinga. It shows that some reliability is adaptive; it does not show that reliability is sufficiently comprehensive. The relevant question is not whether natural selection favors any true beliefs, but whether it makes it probable that human cognitive faculties are reliable with respect to the beliefs that matter in the argument. The crucial domains include abstract reasoning, metaphysics, logic, mathematics, and probability theory. The naturalist must explain why evolutionary success should generate confidence in these capacities rather than merely in practical perception and action.

## III. Power’s Extension: Reliability of Probability Judgment

Matthew Power’s extension presses precisely on this point. Plantinga’s argument already concerns a probability judgment: the probability of R given N&E. But if the naturalist responds by saying that the probability is low, moderate, or high, the naturalist is making a judgment about probability. Power asks whether the evolutionary argument also undermines confidence in this additional cognitive capacity.

The extension can be represented in the following way:

1. N&E provides reason to doubt the reliability of human cognition.
2. Probability judgments are products of human cognition.
3. Therefore, N&E provides reason to doubt the reliability of human probability judgments.
4. The judgment that \(P(R\mid N\&E)\) is low, high, or inscrutable is itself a probability judgment.
5. Therefore, the naturalist cannot confidently use such a judgment to escape the evolutionary argument.

This is more than a verbal complication. A naturalist might reply to Plantinga: “Even if the probability of R given N&E is not high, it is still plausible, perhaps 0.5 or 0.7.” But the naturalist must have some justification for the numerical or comparative assessment. If the reliability of the relevant cognitive processes is itself in question, then the assessment may be epistemically unstable.

Power’s point is especially forceful when one distinguishes simple perceptual or practical judgments from complex probabilistic reasoning. Evolution could plausibly favor an immediate reaction to danger. An animal that rapidly estimates, in a rough and nonconscious way, that a predator is nearby may survive more often than one that pauses to calculate conditional probabilities. Such capacities can be called survival-oriented probability assessments, though they are not necessarily explicit numerical judgments. They involve estimating danger, opportunity, trustworthiness, and uncertainty.

But a sophisticated judgment such as “the probability that human cognition is reliable, conditional on metaphysical naturalism and evolution, is approximately 0.35” is very different. It requires conceptual abstraction, logical consistency, evaluation of competing hypotheses, understanding of conditional probability, and reflection on the reliability of the reasoning process itself. These capacities were not obviously selected because they produced survival advantages in the ancestral environment. They may be byproducts of more basic capacities, products of cultural development, or extensions of practical reasoning. But those possibilities require argument.

The contrast between gut reactions and complex calculations creates a potential objection to Power. If evolved instincts can produce reasonably reliable probability assessments in ordinary cases, why should evolution not also provide the foundation for more elaborate probabilistic reasoning? Human beings may possess a general capacity for pattern detection, comparison, and error correction. Formal mathematics and probability theory could then be cultural elaborations of evolved cognitive tendencies. The reliability of advanced reasoning would not need to have been directly selected. It could arise indirectly from flexible general-purpose intelligence.

This objection is important, but it does not eliminate Power’s challenge. The question is not whether advanced reasoning could have evolved. Clearly it could. The question is whether the naturalist has sufficient epistemic grounds for trusting it once N&E itself is used as the explanatory framework. A capacity can exist without being known to be reliable. Human beings may be capable of accurate mathematics while lacking a non-circular reason to believe that their metaphysical conclusions are accurate. The evolutionary story may explain why we possess the capacity, but explanation is not identical with epistemic vindication.

## IV. Can the Naturalist Assign Even a Minimal Probability?

The central issue can now be stated precisely. Suppose a naturalist wishes to say:

> Even if N&E does not make reliable cognition highly probable, it at least gives cognition a small but nonzero chance of being reliable. Therefore, the naturalist can rationally retain belief in N&E.

This response has intuitive appeal. Rational belief rarely requires certainty. If there is even a modest probability that cognition is reliable, perhaps that is enough to continue using cognition. Moreover, the naturalist might argue that the argument is self-undermining in practice. To deliberate about the probability of R, one must already employ reasoning. Thus, one cannot coherently suspend all trust in cognition while evaluating the argument.

The difficulty is that a minimal probability must itself be justified. If the naturalist says merely that R has a nonzero probability under N&E, this may be an unsupported assertion. The naturalist needs some basis for assigning the probability. Why 0.01 rather than 0.000001? Why “plausible” rather than “merely logically possible”? If all that can be said is that reliable cognition is not ruled out, then the argument has not produced a rational probability assignment. Logical possibility is not the same as epistemic probability.

Power’s extension therefore targets not the possibility of naturalistic reliability but the warrant for probabilistic confidence in it. The naturalist may still be able to entertain the proposition that human cognition is reliable under N&E. But entertaining a proposition is weaker than rationally assigning it a credible probability. The naturalist needs to explain why the relevant probability judgments are trustworthy despite the evolutionary account of cognition.

There are several possible naturalistic responses.

### 1. The reliability of cognition is necessary for successful action

The first response argues that creatures with broadly unreliable cognition would not survive. While exact truth may not be necessary, a significant degree of correspondence with reality is required for action. This makes R probable under E, even if naturalism is added.

The weakness is that “broadly reliable” is ambiguous. It may mean reliable in perception and ordinary practical inference, or it may mean reliable in the full range of beliefs produced by human cognitive faculties. The former is strongly supported by evolutionary considerations. The latter is not. Natural selection might favor a cognitive architecture that is accurate in local, practical contexts while generating systematic distortions in abstract or self-reflective contexts.

### 2. Organisms with flexible intelligence gain an advantage

A second response is that advanced intelligence itself is adaptive. Human beings benefit from planning, social coordination, tool use, language, and cultural transmission. These capacities require general reasoning, not merely fixed instincts. The evolution of intelligence could therefore indirectly support reliable abstract judgment.

Again, the response establishes an evolutionary incentive for cognitive flexibility, but not necessarily for truth across all theoretical domains. Intelligence is an instrument that can be used to generate both true and false theories. A highly intelligent organism may construct elaborate false models that are behaviorally effective. Moreover, the more abstract a belief becomes, the weaker its direct connection to survival.

### 3. Error correction and social science improve reliability

A third response emphasizes that individual cognitive limitations can be corrected through social and scientific practices. Peer criticism, experimentation, replication, statistical methods, and institutional checks can produce reliable conclusions even if unaided human cognition is imperfect.

This is a powerful practical response, but it also relies upon cognitive capacities. Science presupposes that perception, memory, logic, mathematics, testimony, and inferential practices are reliable enough to support correction. Scientific institutions can amplify reliability, but they cannot create epistemic trust from nothing. If the naturalist’s defeater for R is global and undefeated, then scientific methods are also affected.

Still, the argument need not be all-or-nothing. A naturalist could maintain that the evolutionary challenge is local rather than global. Evolution may provide enough reliability for basic reasoning, while cultural practices extend that reliability into more complex domains. This approach weakens the force of the self-defeat argument, although it requires a detailed account of how initial trust in cognition is rationally secured.

### 4. Epistemic bootstrapping

A fourth response appeals to epistemic bootstrapping. Human beings may begin with unavoidable, prima facie trust in their faculties and then test those faculties against experience. Success in prediction and action provides defeasible confirmation of reliability.

The problem is that such testing already presupposes some reliability. One must trust perception to observe the results of experiments, memory to compare predictions with outcomes, and reasoning to draw conclusions from the comparison. Bootstrapping may be legitimate when the initial assumptions are modest and domain-specific, but it does not obviously answer a global skeptical challenge concerning the reliability of all cognition.

For this reason, the naturalist may be able to assign a practical or provisional probability to R, but it remains difficult to show that the assignment is fully rational in the face of Plantinga’s argument. The naturalist can say that the probability is not low, or that it is high enough for practical purposes, but such claims require an account of why evolved cognition is trustworthy in the relevant domains.

## V. Gut-Reaction Probabilities and Complex Calculations

The objection concerning gut reactions deserves separate attention because it reveals an ambiguity in the notion of “probability judgment.” Human beings constantly make rapid assessments of danger and opportunity. These assessments are often shaped by evolution and can be reliable within appropriate environments. A person instantly stepping away from a falling object does not perform an explicit Bayesian calculation, but the response reflects an implicit probability estimate: the object is likely to cause harm, and avoidance is worth the cost.

If such judgments are reliable, perhaps the naturalist can argue that probability assessment is an evolved capacity. Complex calculations would then be merely refinements of an ancient cognitive function. Theistic explanation would not be necessary.

There is some truth in this response. Evolution can favor approximate sensitivity to frequencies, risks, and patterns. Social animals benefit from judging whether another individual is likely to cooperate, whether food is likely to be safe, or whether a sound indicates danger. These capacities may provide the psychological foundation for later probabilistic reasoning.

Nevertheless, the analogy between gut reactions and formal probability judgments is limited. First, gut reactions are often domain-specific. They work best in environments resembling those in which they evolved. They can fail dramatically in modern contexts involving rare risks, statistical independence, base rates, large numbers, or conditional dependencies. Humans are notoriously prone to cognitive biases such as the availability heuristic, confirmation bias, conjunction errors, and neglect of base rates. Evolution may have favored speed and practical usefulness rather than impartial probabilistic accuracy.

Second, a gut reaction usually concerns action under uncertainty, not the abstract probability of a proposition. The evolved system may be asking, implicitly, “Should I flee?” rather than “What is the objective probability that proposition P is true?” Action-oriented cognition can be adaptive even when the associated representation is not strictly accurate. The organism need only respond appropriately often enough.

Third, complex probability judgments require normative standards that gut reactions do not guarantee. Formal probability involves consistency conditions, such as avoiding incompatible assignments and respecting conditional relationships. A person may possess a reliable alarm response without possessing reliable judgments about epistemic probability. The former is a behavioral disposition; the latter is a reflective assessment about degrees of belief or objective chance.

Fourth, the relevant judgment in the evolutionary argument is self-referential. The naturalist is not merely estimating whether a nearby object is dangerous. The naturalist is estimating whether the cognitive system doing the estimating is reliable, given a theory that explains that system in non-teleological terms. This introduces a level of epistemic reflection absent from ordinary adaptive responses.

The naturalist can reply that cultural evolution and education transformed practical reasoning into formal reasoning. This is plausible and may explain the historical emergence of mathematics and science. But once again, an origin story is not a justification. The question is whether these transformations preserve truth-tracking reliability. Cultural selection can favor useful beliefs, prestigious beliefs, group cohesion, or technological success, none of which guarantees metaphysical truth.

Thus, the gut-reaction objection shows that Plantinga and Power should not be interpreted as claiming that evolution produces no reliable cognition. Evolution can produce practically reliable cognitive dispositions. The objection does not show that such dispositions justify confidence in highly abstract probability judgments, especially judgments about the reliability of cognition itself.

## VI. Does Theism Become a Necessary Condition?

Plantinga’s argument is often understood as suggesting that theism provides the missing epistemic foundation. If God created human beings with cognitive faculties aimed at truth, then it is more reasonable to expect those faculties to be reliable. Theism can explain why human cognition is not merely a survival instrument but also a truth-oriented instrument. On this view, theism supplies a probability-raising relationship:

\[
P(R \mid \text{theism and evolution}) > P(R \mid N\&E)
\]

If God is benevolent and intends human beings to know the world, then reliable cognition is unsurprising. Theism may therefore defeat the undercutting defeater generated by N&E.

But does this make theism a necessary condition for justifying probability assessments? The answer depends on what “necessary” means.

If it means **logically necessary**, the answer is no. It is not a contradiction to suppose that naturalism is true and human beings possess reliable cognition. Naturalistic epistemologies can appeal to causal covariation, functional success, inference to the best explanation, coherence, reflective equilibrium, or pragmatic vindication. The existence of a naturalistic explanation for cognition is not logically impossible merely because it is philosophically contested.

If it means **necessary within Plantinga’s dialectical framework**, the answer is closer to yes. Once the naturalist accepts a global defeater for R, the naturalist needs a reason to reinstate trust in cognition. Theism offers a direct explanation: cognitive faculties are designed by a truth-oriented God. Unless the naturalist provides an alternative account, the theist has an advantage. In that limited sense, theism functions as a necessary condition for avoiding the particular defeater only if no naturalistic repair is available.

If it means **the best or most satisfactory condition for epistemic justification**, the issue remains open. Theism may provide a deeper metaphysical explanation of why our faculties are reliable, but theism itself is believed through those faculties. There is therefore a potential circularity: one uses cognition to infer the existence of God, then uses God to justify cognition. Plantinga can respond that this is not necessarily viciously circular if the beliefs are properly basic or mutually reinforcing within a coherent noetic structure. However, the same style of defense may be available to the naturalist.

A further issue concerns theism’s own probability. If the theist says that God makes R probable, the theist must assess the probability of theism and the probability that a God would create reliable cognitive faculties. These too are probability judgments. Theism does not automatically eliminate every epistemic challenge. It offers a metaphysical explanation for reliability, but the explanation must itself be evaluated.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore that theism is not strictly necessary for every justified probability judgment. People can make justified probability assessments within ordinary, local, and empirically constrained contexts without first proving theism. Nor does the evolutionary argument alone prove that naturalism is impossible. However, for a comprehensive, non-question-begging justification of trust in human cognition—including trust in abstract probability judgments—theism offers a powerful resource that N&E lacks or must supplement.

## VII. The Limits of Power’s Extension

Power’s extension strengthens Plantinga’s challenge, but it also faces limits. If the argument claims that no probability judgment can be trusted under N&E, it risks becoming too strong. Any argument for global skepticism tends to undermine itself because it must be formulated and assessed through the very faculties it questions. A skeptical argument can still be rationally considered, but it cannot straightforwardly establish that all reasoning is unreliable without relying on reasoning.

The more modest version is preferable. N&E does not show that probability judgments are false or impossible. It gives the naturalist a reason to be cautious about their reliability, especially when they concern highly abstract and self-referential matters. The result is not total suspension of judgment but a defeater that must be answered.

Moreover, “reliability” may be multidimensional. A faculty can be reliable for one class of propositions and unreliable for another. Evolution may support confidence in perceptual judgments about the immediate environment while failing to support confidence in metaphysical conclusions. Probability judgments may likewise vary by domain. A naturalist could rationally assign probabilities to weather forecasts or medical outcomes while lacking a secure basis for assigning a probability to \(P(R\mid N\&E)\). This domain-specific approach prevents the argument from collapsing into indiscriminate skepticism.

There is also a difference between **epistemic probability** and **credence**. A person may be psychologically compelled to assign a credence to a proposition, even when that credence lacks adequate justification. The naturalist may be unable to avoid thinking that N&E is probably true. But psychological inevitability does not establish rational warrant. Power’s argument concerns the latter.

Finally, probability judgments are not always based on an explicit theory of cognitive reliability. In everyday life, people trust faculties by default. A naturalist might claim that such default trust is rationally innocent until defeated. Plantinga’s argument attempts to provide the defeater. Whether it succeeds depends upon whether the naturalist accepts the relevant probability premise and whether the defeater is strong enough to override default entitlement. The debate is therefore not settled merely by observing that people must reason in order to evaluate the argument.

## Conclusion

Plantinga’s evolutionary argument against naturalism raises a profound epistemological problem. If metaphysical naturalism and neo-Darwinian evolution are true, then the ultimate purpose of human cognitive faculties is not truth but survival and reproduction. Although accurate cognition may often promote adaptive behavior, natural selection does not guarantee general reliability, especially in abstract and self-reflective domains. Consequently, the naturalist appears to acquire an undercutting defeater for R, the reliability of human cognition. Since the belief in N&E is itself produced by those faculties, the worldview threatens to undermine its own justification.

Matthew Power’s extension intensifies this difficulty by focusing on probability judgment. A naturalist cannot simply respond that the probability of R given N&E is low, moderate, or minimally positive without relying on the reliability of the cognitive processes that make such assessments. Evolution may explain the emergence of survival-oriented gut reactions, but these are not equivalent to reliable judgments about formal probability, metaphysical hypotheses, or the conditional probability of cognitive reliability. Complex calculations require abstraction, logical consistency, and self-reflection—capacities whose connection to reproductive fitness is indirect and uncertain.

The naturalist has several possible replies. Accurate representation is often necessary for successful action; flexible intelligence can be adaptive; science and social criticism can correct individual error; and cognitive reliability can be provisionally bootstrapped from practical success. These responses show that N&E need not imply universal irrationality. They may establish local or partial reliability. Yet they do not obviously provide a non-circular justification for trusting human cognition across the full range of beliefs relevant to metaphysics and probability theory.

Theism offers a significant alternative. If human faculties are created by a benevolent and truth-oriented God, then their reliability is more expected than it is under unguided naturalism. Theism can therefore serve as a positive epistemic ground and as a defeater-defeater against the challenge posed by N&E. Nevertheless, theism is not logically necessary for every justified probability assessment, and theism itself must be evaluated through cognitive faculties. Its importance is dialectical and explanatory rather than demonstrably deductive.

The final resolution is thus qualified. A defender of naturalism and evolution can assign a probability to that worldview in the psychological sense, and may construct a naturalistic account of why some cognitive faculties are reliable. But the defender cannot simply assume that a minimal or plausible probability assignment concerning N&E is rationally available. The very act of assigning that probability is part of the epistemic system placed under pressure. Unless naturalism can explain why evolution produces not merely adaptive behavior but sufficiently truth-directed cognition—including reliable probabilistic and metaphysical reasoning—it faces a serious internal defeater.

The argument does not establish that naturalism is impossible, nor that every naturalist probability judgment is irrational. It does show that naturalism bears a justificatory burden often overlooked in appeals to evolution. The question is not only how cognition evolved, but why an unguided evolutionary process should be trusted to generate cognition capable of discovering the truth about its own origins, reliability, and metaphysical significance.
