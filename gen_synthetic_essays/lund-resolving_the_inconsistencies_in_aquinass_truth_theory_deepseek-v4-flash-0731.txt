**The Two Faces of Truth: Aquinas, the Divine Intellect, and the Paradox of Correspondence**

The problem of truth is as old as philosophy itself, yet it remains perpetually unresolved. Is truth a property of fleeting human judgments, subject to the whims of time, culture, and individual perception? Or is truth an eternal, immutable reality that exists independent of our changing minds? The Pre-Socratic philosophers crystallized this dilemma. Heraclitus, with his doctrine of universal flux, suggested that since all things are in constant change, any proposition about them must be transient, true only for a moment. Protagoras radicalized this into a full-blown relativism, declaring that “man is the measure of all things,” thereby reducing truth to the subjective perception of the individual. Against this tide of relativism, Parmenides and his successors posited an unchanging, eternal Being, the only true object of knowledge. This ancient tension—between the changeable world of becoming and the immutable realm of being—found its most sophisticated medieval synthesis in the thought of Thomas Aquinas. In Question 16 of the *Summa Theologiae*, Aquinas develops a theory of truth that attempts to reconcile the finite, changeable nature of human propositions with the eternal, immutable nature of divine truth. He does so by distinguishing between human truth, which is a relation of adequation between the human intellect and created things, and divine truth, which is the very substance of God’s intellect, the measure of all created things. This essay will evaluate whether this distinction successfully resolves the apparent contradictions inherent in Aquinas’s truth theory. I will argue that while the distinction provides a brilliant and internally coherent logical resolution to the conflict between change and eternity, it ultimately fails as a purely philosophical solution. The resolution is achieved only by grounding truth in a theological postulate—the divine intellect—which creates an untenable reliance on theology for a theory that purports to be universally applicable. Furthermore, this grounding necessitates a semantic imprecision in the application of the term “truth,” stretching its meaning to the point where it risks equivocation rather than genuine analogy. Thus, the distinction is a successful *theological* resolution, but an unsuccessful *philosophical* one, leaving the fundamental contradictions of truth theory unresolved for the secular philosopher.

**I. The Architecture of Truth: Correspondence and the Problem of Change**

To understand Aquinas’s solution, one must first grasp the foundational principles of his truth theory as articulated in the *Summa Theologiae*, I, Q. 16. Aquinas famously defines truth as *adaequatio rei et intellectus*—the adequation or correspondence of the thing and the intellect. This is a direct inheritance from Aristotle, but Aquinas refines it with a distinctly metaphysical flavor. In Article 1, he asks whether truth resides in the thing or in the intellect. He concludes that truth resides *primarily* in the intellect, because truth is a perfection of the knowing power. A thing is called true only insofar as it is proportionate to an intellect. However, he makes a crucial distinction: truth is in the intellect *as known* (in the act of judgment), and in things *as they are knowable* (as they have a relation to an intellect). The primary locus of truth is the composing and dividing intellect—the act of judgment where we affirm or deny a predicate of a subject (e.g., “Socrates is sitting”). This is because truth fundamentally involves a relation of conformity between two distinct terms: the intellect and the thing. A simple apprehension of a concept (e.g., “humanity”) is neither true nor false, for it lacks the composition or division necessary for a claim to be made.

This correspondence theory immediately confronts the Pre-Socratic problem. If truth is a relation of conformity, and if the things in the world are subject to change (Heraclitus), then the truth of any proposition must be equally changeable. The proposition “Socrates is sitting” is true when Socrates is sitting, but false the moment he stands. The truth-value of the proposition is contingent upon the mutable state of the external world. Protagoras’s relativism pushes this further: if truth is a relation between an intellect and a thing, and if different intellects perceive the thing differently (e.g., the wind is cold for one person and warm for another), then truth becomes relative to the knower. Aquinas is acutely aware of this threat. He cannot accept a purely relativistic account, for that would undermine the very possibility of science and metaphysics, which demand stable, universal truths. Yet he also cannot deny the obvious fact of change in the physical world.

Aquinas’s initial response to this problem is to distinguish between the *content* of truth and the *principle* of truth. The content of a human proposition is indeed changeable because it is about changeable things. However, the *principle* of truth—the formal definition of truth as adequation—is immutable. The relation of conformity itself, as a logical form, does not change; only the terms of the relation change. But this response is insufficient. If the relation is real, then its truth-value is dependent on the existence and state of its terms. If the terms change, the relation changes, and thus the truth changes. To say that the *form* of truth is immutable is trivial; it does not solve the problem of whether a specific proposition is true or false. The deeper issue is that if all truth is ultimately grounded in the mutable human intellect and the mutable physical world, then there is no absolute standard by which to judge whether a given conformity is the *correct* one. We are left with a mere correlation, not an objective truth.

This is where Aquinas introduces his most radical move: the subordination of human truth to divine truth. He argues that the human intellect is not the ultimate measure of things; rather, it is *measured by* things. The thing is the *mensura* (measure) of the human intellect. But what measures the thing itself? For Aquinas, the thing is true insofar as it conforms to the divine intellect. In Article 5 of Q. 16, he states that God is truth itself. In God, intellect and being are identical. God’s act of understanding is His essence. Therefore, God’s truth is not a relation of adequation between two distinct terms, because in God there is no distinction between the knower and the known. God’s truth is His substance—simple, eternal, and immutable. Created things, on the other hand, are true insofar as they participate in the divine truth. They are true because they conform to the exemplar ideas in the mind of God. Thus, we have two distinct senses of truth: human truth (adequation of human intellect to created things) and divine truth (the creative, substantial truth of God, which is the ultimate measure of all things).

**II. The Distinction as a Logical Resolution: Analogy and Participation**

The distinction between human and divine truth appears to resolve the contradiction between change and eternity by placing them on two different planes of being. Human truth is *epistemological*: it is a relation of conformity between a finite intellect and a finite thing. This relation is contingent, temporal, and changeable because its terms are contingent, temporal, and changeable. Divine truth is *ontological*: it is the very act of existence of the divine intellect, which is the cause of all created being. It is not a relation but an identity. Because these two truths are of different orders, they do not directly contradict each other. The changeable nature of human truth does not impugn the immutable nature of divine truth, just as the fact that a shadow moves does not contradict the fact that the source of light is stationary.

Aquinas employs the doctrine of analogy to explain how the same term “truth” can be applied to both. Truth is not predicated univocally (with exactly the same meaning) of God and creatures, for that would imply that God’s truth is composed of parts and is a relation, which is false. Nor is it predicated purely equivocally (with completely different meanings), for then we could not say that God is the *cause* of created truth. Instead, truth is predicated analogically, specifically by analogy of attribution. In an analogy of attribution, the primary meaning of a term resides in one subject, and secondary meanings are derived from their relation to that primary subject. For example, “healthy” is said primarily of an animal, and secondarily of urine (as a sign of health) and of medicine (as a cause of health). Similarly, truth is said *per essentiam* (by essence) of God, and *per participationem* (by participation) of creatures. Human truths are true because they participate in, or are caused by, the divine truth. The human proposition “Socrates is sitting” is true because it conforms to the actual state of Socrates, but this conformity is itself only possible because the existence of Socrates and the operation of the human intellect are grounded in the divine creative act. The *ratio* of truth—the definition of truth as adequation—is preserved in both, but the *mode* of adequation differs. In God, the adequation is identity (God’s intellect is perfectly adequate to His being). In humans, the adequation is a relation of conformity to an external object.

This distinction successfully resolves the logical contradiction. The contradiction arises when we assume that “truth” must be a univocal property that either changes or does not change. By denying univocity and positing analogy, Aquinas allows for both changeable human truths and immutable divine truth without logical conflict. Human truth changes because its object changes; divine truth does not change because its object is itself. The eternal stability of truth is not located in the content of any particular human proposition, but in the formal principle of truth itself, which is ultimately identical with God. The Pre-Socratic relativist is refuted because human truth is not the ultimate standard; it is answerable to the divine intellect. The Heraclitean flux is contained because the changing things of the world are still measured by an unchanging exemplar. Thus, on a purely logical level, the distinction works.

**III. The Objection of Theological Reliance: A Circular Foundation**

Despite its logical elegance, the distinction faces a formidable objection: it grounds the very definition of truth in the existence and nature of a divine intellect. This creates an untenable reliance on theology, transforming what should be a philosophical theory of truth into a theological doctrine. The objection is not merely that Aquinas invokes God, but that he makes God *necessary* for the coherence of his theory. If one does not accept the existence of a divine intellect, then the entire edifice collapses into the very relativism Aquinas sought to avoid. The question is whether this reliance is philosophically justified or merely an ad hoc appeal to a supernatural postulate.

Aquinas would respond that this reliance is not theological but metaphysical. He argues in the *Summa Contra Gentiles* and the first part of the *Summa Theologiae* that the existence of God, and certain of His attributes, can be demonstrated by natural reason alone, without recourse to revelation. The Five Ways prove the existence of a first mover, a first efficient cause, a necessary being, a supreme perfection, and an intelligent governor of the world. From these, one can deduce that this first cause is pure act, simple, and ultimately identical with its own act of understanding. Therefore, the divine intellect is not a matter of faith but of rational demonstration. The grounding of truth in the divine intellect is thus a metaphysical conclusion, not a theological premise.

However, this defense is insufficient. While the Five Ways might prove the existence of a first cause, they do not prove that this first cause is *truth* in the specific sense Aquinas requires. The argument for a first mover or a necessary being does not entail that this being has an intellect that serves as the *measure* of all created things. Aquinas’s argument for God’s intellect relies on the principle that the first cause must be the most perfect being, and that perfection includes intellect. But this is a leap. One could conceive of a first cause that is a blind, impersonal force—a necessary being that lacks consciousness. The Five Ways do not rule out such a possibility. To assert that the first cause is a knowing intellect, and specifically that it is *identical* with truth, is a theological claim that goes beyond the limits of natural reason. It is a specific interpretation of the nature of the divine, informed by Christian revelation (e.g., “I am the Way, the Truth, and the Life”).

Furthermore, the theory suffers from a vicious circularity. To know that human truth corresponds to the divine intellect, we must first have a concept of divine truth. But how do we acquire this concept? We derive it from our own human experience of truth, by way of negation (removing the limitations of human truth) and eminence (attributing perfections to God in a higher degree). We know that human truth is a relation of adequation; we then say that God’s truth is not a relation but an identity, and that it is the cause of human truth. But this process of analogy presupposes that we already understand what truth is. We use human truth to define divine truth, and then we use divine truth to ground human truth. This is a circular argument. The skeptic can ask: how do we know that our human truths are actually adequate to the divine intellect? We cannot step outside our own intellect to compare it with God’s. The only way to know this is through revelation or faith, which is not a philosophical justification.

This circularity reveals that the distinction between human and divine truth is not a purely philosophical solution. It is a solution that is only coherent *within* a theistic worldview. If one grants the existence of a God who is truth itself, then the theory works beautifully. But if one does not grant this premise, the theory offers no independent grounding for the stability of truth. The objection of theological reliance is therefore valid: Aquinas’s theory is not a self-contained philosophical system. It is a theological-philosophical synthesis that requires a prior commitment to a specific concept of God. This is not to say that the theory is false, but rather that it is not *universally* valid. It cannot resolve the contradictions of truth theory for the atheist, the agnostic, or the pluralist. It only resolves them for the Christian philosopher. This limitation is fatal if we demand that a theory of truth be independent of religious commitments.

**IV. The Objection of Semantic Imprecision: The Limits of Analogy**

The second major objection concerns the semantic imprecision that arises from applying the term “truth” to both human propositions and to God. The objection is that the distinction, while resolving the logical contradiction, does so at the cost of emptying the term “truth” of its precise meaning. If truth is said analogically of God and creatures, we must be able to specify the exact nature of this analogy. But Aquinas’s analogy of attribution is stretched to the breaking point when applied to truth.

Let us examine the definition of truth as *adaequatio rei et intellectus*. For this definition to hold, there must be a real distinction between the intellect and the thing, and a relation of conformity between them. In human truth, this is clear: the intellect is one thing, the object is another, and truth is the relation of agreement between them. But in God, there is no such distinction. God’s intellect is identical with His essence, and His essence is identical with His existence. There is no object external to God’s intellect that it conforms to, because God is the cause of all things. Therefore, the definition of truth as adequation simply does not apply to God. There is no *adequation* because there is no *relation* between two distinct terms. Aquinas recognizes this and says that in God, truth is not a relation but His very substance. But if we remove the relational aspect from the definition, we have changed the meaning of the term. We are no longer talking about truth in the sense of correspondence; we are talking about something else—perhaps about the divine essence or the divine simplicity.
