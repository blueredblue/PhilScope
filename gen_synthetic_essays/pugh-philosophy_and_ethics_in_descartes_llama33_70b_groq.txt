The Cartesian system, as presented in René Descartes's works, particularly in the *Discourse on the Method* and the *Meditations*, is often regarded as a paradigm of rationalist philosophy. At its core, it seeks to establish a firm foundation for knowledge, untainted by doubt and uncertainty. However, a closer examination of Descartes's philosophical project reveals a fascinating and complex relationship between ethics and philosophy, suggesting that ethics may, in fact, be prior to philosophy. This essay will evaluate the claim that ethics is prior to philosophy in the Cartesian system, focusing on the provisional moral code, the forestry traveler analogy, and the structure of the *cogito*, as well as the tension in the Fourth Meditation regarding the scope of the will versus the intellect.

To begin with, Descartes's provisional moral code, as outlined in the third part of the *Discourse on the Method*, serves as a foundation for his philosophical inquiry. This code consists of three maxims: to obey the laws and customs of his country, to be firm and resolute in his actions, and to change his desires rather than the order of the world. Additionally, he adopts a fourth maxim, which is to pursue the truth and to always judge things according to his own understanding. This provisional moral code is not merely a pragmatic decision, but rather a necessary condition for the pursuit of knowledge. By adopting this code, Descartes creates a moral shelter, which allows him to navigate the demolition of his previous knowledge and to establish a new foundation for understanding.

The forestry traveler analogy, presented in the same part of the *Discourse*, further illustrates the importance of ethics in Descartes's philosophical project. In this analogy, the traveler, who finds himself lost in a forest, must adopt a provisional moral code to guide his actions. He must choose a path and follow it, even if it is not the optimal one, in order to avoid being stuck in the same place. Similarly, Descartes, as a philosopher, must adopt a provisional moral code to guide his inquiry, even if it is not the absolute truth. This analogy highlights the importance of moral action in the pursuit of knowledge, as it allows the individual to make progress, even in the face of uncertainty.

The structure of the *cogito*, as presented in the Second Meditation, also reveals a complex relationship between ethics and philosophy. The *cogito* is often regarded as the foundation of Descartes's philosophical system, as it establishes the existence of the thinking subject. However, the *cogito* is not merely a metaphysical statement, but also a moral one. The act of doubting, which is at the core of the *cogito*, is a moral act, as it requires the individual to take responsibility for his own thoughts and actions. Furthermore, the *cogito* implies a certain degree of self-awareness and introspection, which are essential moral virtues.

The Fourth Meditation, which deals with the nature of the will and the intellect, presents a tension between the scope of the will and the intellect. Descartes argues that the will is more extensive than the intellect, as it is capable of affirming or denying things that are not clearly and distinctly perceived by the intellect. This tension highlights the importance of moral action in the Cartesian system, as the will is seen as a fundamental aspect of the human being. The act of willing, manifested in self-restraint and judgment, proves the ego to be fundamentally a morally-acting substance rather than merely a thinking thing.

Moreover, the Fourth Meditation reveals a complex relationship between the will and the intellect, which has implications for the nature of moral action. Descartes argues that the will is not simply a passive faculty, but rather an active one, which is capable of making decisions and taking actions. This active will is not only responsible for moral actions, but also for the pursuit of knowledge. The will is seen as a fundamental aspect of the human being, which is capable of shaping our thoughts and actions.

In light of this analysis, it can be argued that ethics is prior to philosophy in the Cartesian system. The provisional moral code, the forestry traveler analogy, and the structure of the *cogito* all suggest that moral action is a necessary condition for the pursuit of knowledge. The act of doubting, which is at the core of the *cogito*, is a moral act, as it requires the individual to take responsibility for his own thoughts and actions. Furthermore, the tension in the Fourth Meditation regarding the scope of the will versus the intellect highlights the importance of moral action in the Cartesian system, as the will is seen as a fundamental aspect of the human being.

The requirement of a provisional moral shelter during the demolition of knowledge, alongside the fourth moral maxim enjoining the pursuit of truth, establishes the normative and temporal priority of moral action over metaphysical inquiry. This means that moral action is not merely a byproduct of philosophical inquiry, but rather a necessary condition for it. The individual must adopt a provisional moral code, which guides his actions and decisions, in order to pursue knowledge.

In conclusion, the Cartesian system, as presented in Descartes's works, reveals a complex relationship between ethics and philosophy. The provisional moral code, the forestry traveler analogy, and the structure of the *cogito* all suggest that ethics is prior to philosophy. The act of doubting, which is at the core of the *cogito*, is a moral act, as it requires the individual to take responsibility for his own thoughts and actions. The tension in the Fourth Meditation regarding the scope of the will versus the intellect highlights the importance of moral action in the Cartesian system, as the will is seen as a fundamental aspect of the human being. Ultimately, the requirement of a provisional moral shelter during the demolition of knowledge, alongside the fourth moral maxim enjoining the pursuit of truth, establishes the normative and temporal priority of moral action over metaphysical inquiry.

This has significant implications for our understanding of the nature of knowledge and reality. If ethics is prior to philosophy, then moral action is not merely a byproduct of philosophical inquiry, but rather a necessary condition for it. This means that the pursuit of knowledge is not a neutral or objective endeavor, but rather a morally charged one. The individual must adopt a provisional moral code, which guides his actions and decisions, in order to pursue knowledge. This highlights the importance of moral virtues, such as self-awareness, introspection, and responsibility, in the pursuit of knowledge.

Furthermore, this has implications for our understanding of the nature of the human being. If the will is seen as a fundamental aspect of the human being, which is capable of shaping our thoughts and actions, then moral action is not merely a peripheral aspect of human nature, but rather a central one. This means that human beings are not merely thinking things, but rather morally-acting substances, which are capable of making decisions and taking actions.

In the end, the Cartesian system presents a complex and nuanced view of the relationship between ethics and philosophy. While it is often regarded as a paradigm of rationalist philosophy, it reveals a deep appreciation for the importance of moral action in the pursuit of knowledge. The provisional moral code, the forestry traveler analogy, and the structure of the *cogito* all suggest that ethics is prior to philosophy, and that moral action is a necessary condition for the pursuit of knowledge. As such, the Cartesian system offers a profound insight into the nature of knowledge and reality, and highlights the importance of moral virtues in the pursuit of understanding.
