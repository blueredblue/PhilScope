## The Fragile Intellect: Nussbaum, Aristotle, and the Unbridgeable Gap to Christian Doctrine

The *Nicomachean Ethics* (NE) begins with a deceptively simple question: what is the highest good achievable by action? Aristotle’s answer, *eudaimonia*—a life of flourishing—unfolds across ten books of rigorous, often frustrating, inquiry. Yet the text presents a notorious internal tension. Books I through IX meticulously analyze the practical moral virtues—courage, justice, temperance, and the intellectual virtue of *phronesis* (practical wisdom)—as the core of the ethical life, rooted in the messy, vulnerable realities of human emotions, social relationships, and fortune. Book X, however, abruptly ascends to a different register, declaring that the highest *eudaimonia* is *theoria* (contemplation), an activity so self-sufficient and godlike that it seems to transcend the fragile, embodied world of the earlier books. Martha Nussbaum, in her landmark work *The Fragility of Goodness*, offers a powerful, anthropocentric reading of this tension. She argues that Aristotle’s true ethical vision is a pluralistic, practical one, where the moral virtues are primary, ethical values are incommensurable, and universal principles are merely provisional summaries of wise particular judgments. On this view, Book X is either a late Platonic residue, a rhetorical flourish, or a formal gesture that must be subordinated to the richer practical account.

This essay will argue that Nussbaum’s interpretation, while illuminating the texture of the practical books, fundamentally misconstrues the architectonic structure of the *Ethics*. It does so by flattening the explicit, ontological hierarchy Aristotle establishes in Book X, where *theoria* is not merely one good among many but the *primary* and defining activity of human flourishing. However, this does not mean that Book X anticipates New Testament theological doctrines. While both posit a transcendent end beyond ordinary human practice, Aristotle’s transcendence is naturalistic, intellectualist, and achieved through human effort, whereas Christian transcendence is supernatural, personal, and received through grace. The apparent contradiction between human vulnerability and the pursuit of the divine life is resolved not by eliminating one pole, but by Aristotle’s sophisticated stratification of *eudaimonia* into a primary (contemplative) and secondary (practical) form. Consequently, Aristotelian ethics can provide a robust and necessary *natural* foundation for moral philosophy, and even a *propaedeutic* for Christian thought, but it cannot, by its own rational resources, successfully provide a complete foundation for Christian doctrine. The leap from the Aristotelian intellect to the Christian soul requires a metaphysical and soteriological intervention that reason alone cannot supply.

### Part I: Nussbaum’s Anthropocentric Virtue Ethics

Nussbaum’s project in *The Fragility of Goodness* is to rescue Aristotle from a tradition that reads him primarily as a rationalist or a precursor to Christian natural law. Instead, she presents him as a thinker deeply attuned to the tragedy of human life—a life where the goods we most need (love, friendship, political justice) are inherently vulnerable to luck, loss, and conflict. Her interpretation rests on three interlocking pillars: species-relative goods, the incommensurability of values, and the provisional nature of ethical principles.

First, Nussbaum argues that Aristotle’s *ergon* (function) argument in Book I does not yield a single, abstract, or otherworldly good, but rather a set of *species-relative* goods. The good for a human being is determined by what it is to be a human being—a rational animal with a specific set of capacities for nutrition, perception, and reason. The virtues are the excellent activities of these capacities. This is a deeply anthropocentric framework: the ethical life is defined *relative to* the human form of life, not in reference to a transcendent Form of the Good. Nussbaum famously uses this to develop her "capabilities approach," where the goal of ethics and politics is to enable human beings to function in ways that are characteristic of a flourishing human life. This reading grounds ethics in biology and psychology, making it empirically accessible and resistant to metaphysical flights.

Second, Nussbaum emphasizes the *incommensurability* of ethical values. In the practical books, Aristotle repeatedly insists that there is no single metric by which all goods can be measured. Courage cannot be weighed against friendship in the same units; justice is not reducible to pleasure. The *phronimos*—the person of practical wisdom—does not apply a mathematical calculus. Instead, they perceive the salient features of a particular situation and discern what action is fitting. Nussbaum argues that this pluralism is essential to Aristotle’s realism. The good life is not a harmonious whole where all values align; it is a tragic arena where pursuing one genuine good (e.g., a political career) may require sacrificing another (e.g., family life). The moral life is thus a matter of *perception*—a nuanced, quasi-aesthetic grasp of the concrete case—rather than the application of universal rules.

Third, and following from this, Nussbaum contends that Aristotle treats ethical principles (like the doctrine of the mean) as *provisional summaries* of good decisions, not as rigid axioms. The *meson* (mean) is "relative to us" and is determined by the *phronimos*. It is not a fixed midpoint but a flexible guide that captures the wisdom of past particular judgments. Principles are useful for teaching and for orienting the novice, but they can never substitute for the trained perception of the virtuous agent. When principles conflict with the demands of a unique situation, the situation wins. This is a profoundly anti-theoretical, or at least anti-rule-based, ethics. The ultimate standard is the *phronimos*—a living, breathing, fallible human being—not an abstract law.

This reading is immensely attractive. It makes Aristotle a champion of practical intelligence, emotional depth, and human vulnerability. It aligns him with modern virtue ethicists and critics of Kantian and utilitarian formalism. However, it achieves this coherence by performing a strategic act of interpretive violence upon Book X. Nussbaum acknowledges the tension but tends to resolve it by dismissing the contemplative ideal as either a vestige of Plato’s influence (which Aristotle supposedly never fully shed) or as a purely formal, non-substantive gesture that does not affect the "real" content of the ethical life. She suggests that the *theoria* of Book X is so abstract that it cannot provide any concrete guidance for living, and thus the practical books remain the true heart of the work. This move, while elegant, fails to take seriously the explicit metaphysical and psychological arguments Aristotle marshals in X.7-8.

### Part II: The Unmistakable Hierarchy of Book X

To read Book X as a mere addendum is to ignore the deliberate structure of the *Ethics*. Aristotle does not stumble into contemplation; he builds toward it. In Book I, he defines *eudaimonia* as "activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are several virtues, in accordance with the best and most complete" (NE 1098a16-18). This leaves open which virtue is "best and most complete." Books II-IX explore the moral virtues and *phronesis*, but they never settle the question of the ultimate hierarchy. It is only in Book X that Aristotle reveals the answer: the best virtue is *sophia* (theoretical wisdom), and the activity that corresponds to it is *theoria*.

In NE X.7, Aristotle presents a rigorous argument for the supremacy of contemplation. He identifies the criteria for the highest good: it must be the most self-sufficient, the most continuous, the most pleasant, and the most loved for its own sake. Contemplation, he argues, meets all these criteria better than practical virtue. Practical virtue requires other people (the objects of justice, courage, and friendship), external goods (wealth, power, good birth), and is subject to interruption by action and passion. Contemplation, by contrast, can be done alone, is continuous (we can think more continuously than we can act), and is intrinsically pleasant. He concludes: "This activity alone would seem to be loved for its own sake; for nothing arises from it beyond the contemplating, while from the practical activities we gain more or less something beyond the action" (NE 1177b1-4).

Crucially, Aristotle grounds this hierarchy in a metaphysical distinction between the human and the divine. He writes: "The activity of god, which surpasses all others in blessedness, must be contemplative; and of human activities, therefore, that which is most akin to this must be most of the nature of happiness" (NE 1178b21-23). The divine mind is pure actuality, thinking itself. Human beings, being composite (body + soul, passions + reason), cannot achieve this pure state, but they can approximate it through the activity of the intellect. This is why Aristotle famously exhorts us to "strain every nerve to live in accordance with the best thing in us; for even if it be small in bulk, much more does it in power and worth surpass everything" (NE 1177b33-1178a2).

This is a transcendent hierarchy. It does not merely rank contemplation *higher* than moral virtue; it establishes a different *kind* of life. The practical life is the life of the composite being, bound to the body and its passions. The contemplative life is the life of the *nous* (intellect), which is "either itself divine or the most divine part of us" (NE 1177a16). The moral virtues are thus demoted to a "secondary" form of happiness—a necessary but inferior mode of flourishing for beings who are not pure intellects. Nussbaum’s anthropocentric reading, which insists that the moral virtues are the *primary* locus of human good, directly contradicts this explicit textual hierarchy. She cannot explain why Aristotle would spend so much time on practical virtue if it were merely a stepping stone to something else, but the text is unequivocal: the moral virtues are valuable because they enable and protect the contemplative life, providing the leisure, justice, and internal harmony necessary for intellectual activity.

### Part III: Does Book X Anticipate New Testament Theology?

Given this transcendent hierarchy, one might be tempted to see Aristotle as a proto-Christian, anticipating the New Testament’s call to seek a higher, spiritual life beyond the mundane. The parallels seem striking: both posit a highest good that is beyond ordinary human practice; both emphasize self-sufficiency and freedom from worldly passions; both see the ultimate goal as a form of union with the divine. However, a careful examination reveals that the similarities are superficial, and the differences are metaphysical chasms.

First, consider the nature of the divine. Aristotle’s god, as described in the *Metaphysics*, is an unmoved mover—pure actuality, thinking itself. It is not a creator, not a providential governor, and certainly not a personal being who loves humanity. It is an object of thought, not a subject who acts. The New Testament God, by contrast, is a personal, loving Father who enters history, becomes incarnate in Jesus Christ, suffers, dies, and is resurrected for the salvation of humanity. The Christian God is fundamentally *agape*—self-giving love. There is no love in Aristotle’s god; there is only self-thinking thought. The "divine life" in Aristotle is an intellectual achievement of the human mind, an assimilation to a cold, impersonal principle. The "divine life" in Christianity is a gift of grace, a participation in the Trinitarian love of a personal God.

Second, consider the *means* of achieving the highest good. For Aristotle, *theoria* is achieved through the cultivation of intellectual virtue. It requires natural aptitude, education, leisure, and the moral virtues that provide the stability for intellectual work. It is an *achievement* of the human agent, attainable through one's own efforts (with some luck regarding external goods). For Christianity, salvation and the beatific vision are *received* through faith, repentance, and the grace of God. Human effort is necessary but utterly insufficient. Paul writes, "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God—not the result of works, so that no one may boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9). Aristotle’s wise man boasts in his own intellectual achievement; the Christian saint boasts only in the cross of Christ.

Third, consider the *content* of the highest good. Aristotle’s *theoria* is the contemplation of necessary truths—mathematics, metaphysics, the nature of the cosmos. It is a solitary, self-contained activity. The Christian beatific vision is a loving union with a personal God, which involves not just the intellect but the whole person—will, emotion, and body (in the resurrection). It is a communal activity, shared with the communion of saints. It is not merely *knowing* about God, but *knowing* God in an intimate, relational sense. Aristotle’s god is an object of knowledge; the Christian God is a subject of relationship.

Therefore, while Book X does introduce a "transcendent hierarchy" that points beyond the merely practical, it does not anticipate the *content* of New Testament theology. It anticipates a form of naturalistic intellectualism, a purely rational mysticism that sees the divine as the ultimate object of thought. The leap from Aristotle’s unmoved mover to the incarnate Christ is not a smooth ascent; it is a radical rupture that requires a new ontology of personhood, a new soteriology of grace, and a new eschatology of bodily resurrection.

### Part IV: Resolving the Tension: Vulnerability and the Divine Life

If Nussbaum is wrong to flatten the hierarchy, and if Book X is not Christian, how does Aristotle resolve the apparent contradiction between the vulnerable, practical life of Books I-IX and the self-sufficient, contemplative ideal of Book X? The resolution lies in Aristotle’s sophisticated concept of *eudaimonia* as a *stratified* or *pros hen* (focal meaning) concept. He does not claim that there is only one kind of happiness. Instead, he distinguishes between a *primary* happiness (theoria) and a *secondary* happiness (the life of moral virtue), and he carefully explains how they relate.

In NE X.8, Aristotle explicitly addresses this. He writes: "But that happiness is the activity of contemplation will also be clear from the following… But being human, one will also need external prosperity; for our nature is not self-sufficient for the purpose of contemplation, but our body must also be healthy and have food and other attention" (NE 1178b33-35). He acknowledges that even the contemplative life is vulnerable—it requires the external goods of health, food, and leisure, which can be lost to fortune. However, the *activity* of contemplation itself is less vulnerable than practical action. A philosopher can think in a prison cell, but a statesman cannot exercise justice in isolation.

The key to the resolution is the recognition that human beings are *not* pure intellects. We are composite beings—rational animals. We have a dual nature: we are capable of *nous*, but we are also subject to passions, needs, and mortality. Therefore, the highest good for us cannot be purely divine, but it must be the highest *human* good. Aristotle solves this by arguing that the contemplative life is the highest *because* it is the most divine, but it is also the most human because it actualizes the best part of our nature. The practical life is the *secondary* happiness because it is the best life for us *insofar as we are composite beings*.

This stratification resolves the tension. The vulnerability that so concerns Nussbaum is real, but it is a feature of the *secondary* happiness, not the primary one. The moral virtues are necessary precisely because we are vulnerable. We need courage to face dangers, justice to regulate our relations with others, and temperance to control our passions. These virtues make us good *human beings* in the social and political sphere. But the ultimate goal of this moral development is not to be a good citizen for its own sake; it is to create the conditions—internal harmony and external leisure—for the higher activity of contemplation. The *phronimos* is not the ultimate ideal; the *sophos* (the wise person) is. The moral life is a *preparation* for the intellectual life.

Thus, Aristotle does not reject vulnerability; he subordinates it. He acknowledges that the wise person is still a human being and can be crushed by terrible misfortune (e.g., the fate of Priam). But he insists that the *core* of eudaimonia—the activity of the intellect—is relatively invulnerable. A person who has achieved *theoria* has a kind of self-sufficiency that the merely virtuous person lacks. The tension is not a contradiction but a hierarchy of goods appropriate to a hierarchical nature. Nussbaum’s insistence on the primacy of vulnerability and practical wisdom, while emotionally compelling, misreads the *telos* of human nature. Aristotle is not a tragic humanist; he is a metaphysical intellectualist who sees the highest human achievement as a partial escape from the human condition.

### Part V: Can Aristotelian Ethics Found Christian Doctrine?

Having established that Aristotle’s Book X is a naturalistic, intellectualist transcendence, we can now address the final question: does Aristotelian ethics provide a successful rational foundation for Christian doctrine? The answer is a qualified but firm *no*—not as a complete foundation, though it provides a crucial and necessary *natural* substrate.

The problem is not that Aristotle’s ethics are incompatible with Christianity. On the contrary, the moral virtues of justice, temperance, courage, and friendship are perfectly compatible with Christian morality. Indeed, Thomas Aquinas successfully integrated Aristotelian ethics into his own *Summa Theologiae*, using Aristotle’s framework of the virtues and natural law to build a comprehensive moral theology. The cardinal virtues (prudence, justice, temperance, fortitude) are taken directly from Aristotle and baptized into Christian thought. The idea of a natural law—a rational order accessible to human reason—is deeply Aristotelian. In this sense, Aristotle provides an indispensable *rational foundation* for the *natural* part of Christian ethics.

However, Christian doctrine extends far beyond natural ethics. It includes doctrines of creation, incarnation, atonement, grace, and eschatology. These doctrines are not derivable from Aristotelian premises. Aristotle’s god does not create the world; it is eternal and unmoved. Aristotle has no concept of sin as a radical corruption of the will that separates humanity from God; he has only the concept of *akrasia* (weakness of will) and vice as a bad habit. Aristotle has no concept of grace—a supernatural gift that enables human beings to do what they cannot do by nature. His ethics are eudaimonistic: the goal is one’s own flourishing. Christian ethics are agapeistic: the goal is to love God and neighbor, even to the point of self-sacrifice. Jesus’ command to "love your enemies" is not a virtue Aristotle would recognize; for Aristotle, it would be a failure of proper pride and justice.

The deepest incompatibility lies in the nature of the ultimate end. For Aristotle, the highest good is *theoria*—the contemplation of necessary truths. This is an activity of the *intellect*, and it is achieved by one’s own intellectual effort. The Christian beatific vision is a *supernatural* union with a personal God, which is *infused* by grace. Thomas Aquinas recognized this gap. He distinguished between the *natural* end of human beings (which is Aristotelian—the flourishing life of virtue and contemplation) and the *supernatural* end (which is the beatific vision, attainable only through grace and faith). He argued that philosophy, including Aristotle’s, can demonstrate the existence of a first cause and the natural law, but it cannot demonstrate the Trinity, the Incarnation, or the necessity of grace. These are matters of revelation, not reason.

Therefore, Aristotle’s ethics can provide a *rational foundation* for the *natural moral law* that is presupposed by Christian theology. It can show that human beings have a natural inclination to virtue and that reason can discern the basic principles of justice and goodness. But it cannot provide a foundation for the *supernatural* doctrines of Christianity. The leap from the Aristotelian unmoved mover to the Christian personal God who loves and saves is a leap of faith that cannot be rationally justified on Aristotelian grounds. To attempt to do so would be to distort both Aristotle and Christianity. Nussbaum’s anthropocentric reading, ironically, is more compatible with a purely secular humanism than with Christian theology, because it rejects the very metaphysical hierarchy that Christian theology, in its Thomistic form, seeks to elevate and transform.

### Conclusion

Martha Nussbaum’s interpretation of the *Nicomachean Ethics* is a profound and influential contribution to Aristotelian scholarship. Her focus on species-relative goods, the incommensurability of values, and the provisional nature of principles brilliantly illuminates the practical wisdom of Books I-IX. She captures the fragility, the tragedy, and the rich particularity of the moral life. However, her anthropocentric framework ultimately misconstrues the architectonic of the text. By relegating Book X to an anomaly or a formal gesture, she ignores Aristotle’s explicit argument that *theoria* is the *primary* eudaimonia, with the moral virtues serving a subordinate, albeit essential, function. The hierarchy of Book X is not a Platonic leftover; it is the logical conclusion of the *ergon* argument and the metaphysical distinction between the composite human and the divine intellect.

This transcendent hierarchy, however, does not anticipate New Testament theology. Aristotle’s divine is an impersonal, self-thinking thought, achieved through human intellectual effort. The Christian God is a personal, loving Father, who gives himself in grace. The tension between human vulnerability and the pursuit of the divine life is resolved by Aristotle through a stratification of happiness: the practical life is the secondary, human happiness; the contemplative life is the primary, divine happiness. The wise person strives to live the latter while accepting the necessities of the former.

Ultimately, Aristotelian ethics cannot successfully provide a complete rational foundation for Christian doctrine. It provides a magnificent natural ethics, a rational account of the virtues and the human good, which Christian thinkers like Aquinas can and did integrate into a larger theological framework. But the core doctrines of Christianity—creation, incarnation, atonement, grace, and the beatific vision—lie beyond the reach of Aristotelian reason. They require a revelation that transforms, rather than merely extends, the intellectualist ideal. Nussbaum’s Aristotle, stripped of his metaphysical heights, is a humanist’s Aristotle. The real Aristotle, with his straining toward the divine, is a philosopher who points beyond himself, but he points to a god of pure thought, not the God of love. The gap between the two is the gap between reason and faith, and no rational foundation can bridge it on its own.
