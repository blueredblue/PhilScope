# The Nomological Gap: Assessing Boorse’s Physiological Model of Mental Health Against Davidsonian Anomalism

## Introduction

The conceptual foundations of mental health and pathology inhabit a contested borderland between the philosophy of science and the philosophy of mind. At the center of this terrain lies Christopher Boorse’s biostatistical model (BSM)—a resolutely naturalistic, value-free framework that defines health as normal functional ability, where normality is cashed out in terms of species-typical statistical contribution to survival and reproduction. Boorse extends this framework directly to the mind, positing that mental diseases are simply physiological dysfunctions of the central nervous system operating via causal-mechanistic pathways. 

However, this naturalistic reduction encounters a formidable philosophical obstacle: Donald Davidson’s principle of the *anomalism of the mental*. Davidson argues that while mental events are identical with physical events (anomalous monism), there are no strict psychophysical laws that can bridge the mental and the physical. This impossibility is rooted in the constitutive holism of the intentional domain, which is governed by the normative constraint of maximizing rationality. Because intentional states are holistic and normative, they cannot be captured within the closed, atomistic, and non-normative nomological net of natural science.

This essay evaluates whether Boorse’s physiological model of mental health can succeed in the face of Davidson’s anomalism. I argue that Boorse’s model cannot coherently sustain its physiological reduction of mental illness without collapsing either into a revisionist elimination of the mental or a contradiction of Davidson’s valid strictures against psychophysical laws. 

To demonstrate this, the paper proceeds in five stages:
1. It explicates Boorse’s physiological model of health and its direct application to mental pathology.
2. It examines Davidson’s anomalous monism, focusing on the holism of the mental, the normative constraint of rationality, and the resulting nomological closure of the physical.
3. It analyzes the direct friction between Boorse’s requirement for causal-nomological functional laws and Davidson’s exclusion of strict psychological laws.
4. It engages with Robert Klee’s *ceteris paribus* objection against anomalous monism, assessing whether intentional explanations and physical science explanations share identical background conditions.
5. It investigates the prospects for an autonomous science of mental health, resolving whether psychiatric nosology can survive without type-to-type physical reductions.

## 1. Boorse’s Biostatistical Model and the Physiological Reduction of Mental Illness

Christopher Boorse’s biostatistical model (BSM) of health and disease is constructed upon a commitment to value-free, biological naturalism. Boorse seeks to anchor medical definitions of pathology in objective, empirical facts rather than shifting social values, moral judgments, or normative ideals of human flourishing. 

The core architecture of the BSM rests on two foundational concepts: the **reference class** and **normal function**. 
* A reference class is defined as a natural population of organisms of a specific species, age, and sex. 
* Within any given reference class, a **normal function** is a statistically typical contribution of a physiological part or process to the individual’s survival and reproduction. 

Thus, Boorse defines health as *normal functional ability*: the state wherein all internal parts and processes of an organism perform their species-typical functions at or above efficiency levels adequate for biological success within their reference class. Conversely, a **disease** is a type of internal state that impairs health—namely, a physiological dysfunction that diminishes one or more functional capacities below the species-typical statistical average.

When Boorse turns his attention to *mental* health, he does not abandon this framework; rather, he doubles down on physicalist reductionism. Mental health, on the BSM, is simply the optimal physiological functioning of the brain and central nervous system. Mental diseases—ranging from major depressive disorder and schizophrenia to obsessive-compulsive disorder—are categorized as neurological diseases. They are identified as states wherein specific neurobiological mechanisms fail to execute their species-typical causal contributions to the organism's overall biological economy. 

Under this view, a psychiatric diagnosis is not a hermeneutic appraisal of a patient's narrative, a moral failure, or a social deviation. It is a factual report of a biological malfunction. A neurotransmitter imbalance, a structural lesion, or a dysregulated synaptic circuit constitutes a mental disease precisely because it is a physical dysfunction of a physical organ (the brain). 

Boorse’s model demands a robustly nomological conception of biology and medicine. For a part of an organism to have a "function," and for the failure of that function to constitute a "disease," there must be objective, causal laws governing the system's operations. We must be able to state, in law-like terms, how a healthy system processes inputs to maintain homeostasis or secure reproductive fitness, and how a pathological system deviates from these nomic pathways. 

Boorse’s model assumes that the mind-brain relationship is transparent to empirical science, and that mental health terms can be successfully mapped onto, or replaced by, physical-functional descriptions of neurobiological systems. However, this assumption brings the BSM into immediate, head-on collision with Davidson's philosophy of mind.

## 2. Davidson’s Anomalism of the Mental and the Normative Holism of Intentional States

Donald Davidson’s philosophy of mind, famously articulated in "Mental Events" (1970) and subsequent essays, presents an ontology and epistemology of the mental that radically challenges any reductive physicalist project like Boorse’s. Davidson’s position is structured by three foundational principles that initially appear to form an inconsistent triad:

1. **The Principle of Causal Interaction:** At least some mental events interact causally with physical events (mental events cause physical events, and vice versa).
2. **The Principle of the Nomological Character of Causality:** Where there is causality, there must be a law—events connected as cause and effect must be covered by strict, deterministic, or probabilistic causal laws.
3. **The Principle of the Anomalism of the Mental:** There are no strict deterministic or probabilistic laws on the basis of which mental events can be predicted and explained under mental descriptions.

Davidson resolves this apparent paradox through **Anomalism Monism**: *every* mental event is *some* physical event (token physicalism), but mental events cannot be given a reductive, nomological characterization in physical terms (type physicalism is false). While mental events possess physical descriptions under which they are subsumed by physical laws, they do not possess *mental* descriptions under which they are subsumed by strict laws.

The justification for the anomalism of the mental lies in the unique nature of the **intentional domain**. Mental states—such as beliefs, desires, intentions, and fears—are holistically interrelated and constitutively bound to the **Principle of Charity**. When we attribute mental states to an agent, we are not merely mapping isolated internal cogs; we are interpreting the agent as a rational creature. 

**Holism** dictates that no single mental state can be identified or understood in isolation. A belief about $X$ only has the content it does because of its logical, inferential, and causal connections to a vast, potentially infinite web of other beliefs, desires, and background assumptions. 

The **normative constraint of rationality** dictates that intentional interpretation aims to maximize consistency, coherence, and truth in the agent’s belief system. We make sense of others by assuming that, by and large, they believe what they ought to believe given their evidence, and they desire what makes sense for them to desire given their goals.

Because the mental is governed by constitutive norms of rationality, coherence, and justification, it cannot be reduced to the non-normative vocabulary of physics. Physical science is descriptive, atomistic, and value-free; it seeks to discover causal regularities among entities categorized by mass, charge, and spatial-temporal coordinates. 

The intentional domain, by contrast, is normative, holistic, and irreducibly complex. There can be no "strict laws" of the mental—let alone strict psychophysical laws bridging the mental and the physical—because establishing such laws would require bridging a fundamental conceptual chasm: the chasm between the *space of reasons* (normative justification) and the *space of causes* (nomological description). 

As Davidson writes, "A constitutive ideal of rationality charges the domain of the mental; but there are no constitutive ideals of this kind associated with the physical sciences." Consequently, any attempt to formulate strict laws linking intentional mental states to physical states is doomed to failure.

## 3. The Nomological Friction: Causal Laws vs. Intentional Holism

The intersection of Boorse’s BSM and Davidson’s anomalous monism reveals a deep conceptual tension. Boorse’s physiological model of mental health requires the existence of stable, law-like causal mechanisms linking biological substrates to functional capacities and their breakdowns. If mental diseases are neurophysiological dysfunctions, then identifying a mental disease presupposes that we can specify the nomic regularities governing the brain states underlying mental functions. 

However, Davidson’s thesis asserts that intentional mental states cannot enter into strict causal laws. When we designate a mental state as "pathological"—such as an irrational delusion, an incapacitating depression, or an anxiety disorder—we are operating squarely within the intentional, normative space of reasons. We are evaluating an agent's beliefs for coherence, their emotional responses for proportionality, and their desires for adaptive viability. 

If Davidson is correct, the attempt by Boorse and other neuro-reductionists to ground mental health in strict physiological laws faces a severe dilemma:

### The Scylla of Eliminativism
If one insists on maintaining strict physiological laws for mental illness, one must eliminate the mental vocabulary altogether. By translating intentional states (beliefs, desires, subjective suffering) entirely into neurophysiological descriptions (firing rates, neurotransmitter concentrations, receptor densities), the distinctively *mental* dimension of mental health evaporates. 

Pathology is no longer a failure of the person to navigate the world rationally, suffer emotionally, or maintain intentional agency; it is simply a local malfunction of physical tissue. But this move robs the "mental" in "mental health" of its identity. A physiological model that eliminates the intentional leaves behind neurology, rendering *psychiatry* a redundant sub-discipline of neurology devoid of its subject matter.

### The Charybdis of Nomological Collapse
Conversely, if one preserves the reality of intentional mental states and their specific phenomenology, one must accept their anomalism. But doing so shatters the nomological foundation of Boorse’s BSM. 

If there are no strict psychophysical laws, and if intentional states are constitutively holistic and normative, then we cannot map a physiological dysfunction to a specific "mental disease" via strict causal-nomological bridges. We cannot isolate a brain state as a "pathology" based on its failure to fulfill a biological function when that function is mediated by an infinitely complex, normative web of beliefs and desires that resist nomological capture.

To illustrate this tension, consider major depressive disorder (MDD). Boorse’s model must treat MDD as a physiological dysfunction of neural mechanisms regulating mood and energy, governed by causal laws. Yet, a patient’s depression is intimately bound up with their intentional life: their interpretation of loss, their existential beliefs, their self-conception, and their evaluative framing of the future. 

The attribution of MDD requires interpreting the patient within the space of reasons—assessing whether their despair is an intelligible (even if painful) response to circumstances or an irrational breakdown of cognitive functioning. 

Because intentional states are holistic, two patients exhibiting identical neurochemical profiles may have vastly different intentional lives, meaning their "pathological" states cannot be captured by a uniform, strict physiological law. Conversely, two patients with radically different neurophysiological substrates may exhibit the exact same intentional profile of depressive pathology. 

Boorse’s requirement for strict causal-functional laws of mental disease founders precisely on the normative and holistic recalcitrance of the mental demonstrated by Davidson.

## 4. Klee’s *Ceteris Paribus* Objection Against Anomalous Monism

To properly assess whether Boorse’s model can survive this tension, we must examine whether Davidson’s anomalism itself is vulnerable to critique. A prominent challenge to anomalous monism comes from philosophers of science like Robert Klee, who target Davidson’s claim that mental laws fail to be strict because they require *ceteris paribus* (all other things being equal) clauses that cannot be cashed out in physical terms.

In "Physicalism, Anomalous Monism, and the Ceteris Paribus Laws Objection," Klee argues that Davidson’s asymmetry between physical and mental laws is unearned. Davidson claims that physical laws are strict—they operate without *ceteris paribus* exceptions because the physical vocabulary is closed and comprehensive. 

Mental laws, by contrast, are irreducibly *ceteris paribus* because the intentional realm is open: any belief can potentially interfere with, modify, or cancel out the causal efficacy of any other belief, owing to the holistic nature of the mental.

Klee counters that *ceteris paribus* clauses are not unique to the mental; they are ubiquitous across all special sciences (such as biology, meteorology, and economics) and even within fundamental physics. When a physicist formulates a law, it often assumes idealized conditions—such as a vacuum, absence of friction, or isolated systems. These assumptions function as implicit *ceteris paribus* clauses. 

More importantly, Klee argues that **scientific explanations and intentional explanations share identical background conditions** in terms of their susceptibility to interfering factors. If a biological mechanism in Boorse’s model fails to perform its species-typical function, it is because of myriad environmental, epigenetic, and systemic variables that must be held constant for the biological law to hold. 

Why, then, should the *ceteris paribus* nature of psychological generalizations be singled out as an ontological barrier to lawhood, while biological and physical *ceteris paribus* laws are accepted as legitimate components of science?

If Klee is correct, Davidson overstates the gulf between the mental and the physical. If *ceteris paribus* laws are permissible in biology (and thus in Boorse’s physiological model of health), then psychology and psychiatry might formulate *ceteris paribus* psychophysical laws without needing to meet the impossible standard of exceptionless, deterministic physical laws. 

### Assessing Klee’s Objection Against Boorse and Davidson
How does Klee’s objection impact the debate between Boorse and Davidson? 

If Klee successfully rehabilitates *ceteris paribus* laws for the mental, Boorse’s model gains a lifeline. Boorse no longer needs to demand strict, exceptionless, type-to-type physical reductions. He can retreat to a **functional-nomic pluralism**, where mental diseases are understood via *ceteris paribus* physiological and psychophysical regularities. Just as a cardiac failure law holds *ceteris paribus* (barring sudden massive trauma or miraculous interventions), a psychiatric disease law might hold *ceteris paribus* (barring radical shifts in the patient's intentional environment or belief web).

However, Davidson’s defender can parry Klee’s objection by pointing to a fundamental difference between physical/biological *ceteris paribus* clauses and intentional ones. 

In physics and biology, *ceteris paribus* clauses can, in principle, be filled in by expanding the physical or biological vocabulary. If friction interferes with a mechanical law, one can add equations for friction; if an enzyme is inhibited, one can specify the inhibiting molecule. The vocabulary remains homogeneous and non-normative.

In contrast, *ceteris paribus* clauses in the intentional domain cannot be spelled out in physical terms. To cash out the *ceteris paribus* condition for an intentional generalization—to explain *why* a particular belief caused a particular action or breakdown in mental health—one must appeal to the agent’s *entire web of beliefs and desires*, evaluated under the constitutive norms of rationality. 

Because this web is infinite and normative, the *ceteris paribus* conditions for mental laws are not merely practically complex; they are conceptually heterogeneous to physical science. They require translating the space of reasons into the space of causes, which Davidson has shown to be a category mistake. 

Therefore, while Klee correctly highlights that science is rife with *ceteris paribus* laws, he underestimates the *constitutive* difference of the intentional. The holism of the mental is not just a matter of causal complexity (which physics and biology also face); it is a normative holism. 

Consequently, Klee’s objection fails to rescue Boorse’s model from Davidsonian anomalism: psychophysical *ceteris paribus* laws cannot be naturalized into strict physiological mechanisms without evacuating the intentional norms that define mental pathology in the first place.

## 5. The Autonomy of Psychiatric Science Without Type-Type Reductions

Where does this leave the science of mental health? If Boorse’s strict physiological model collapses under the weight of Davidson’s anomalism, and if Klee’s attempt to normalize mental laws via *ceteris paribus* clauses stumbles on the normative holism of the intentional, must we abandon the scientific study of mental health altogether? Is psychiatry condemned to be either an unscientific art or a eliminative neurology?

The resolution lies in recognizing the viability of an **autonomous science of mental health that operates without type-to-type physical reductions**. The failure of type physicalism and the truth of anomalous monism do not entail epiphenomenalism or the impossibility of scientific psychology and psychiatry. Instead, they point toward a non-reductive, functionalist, and multilevel approach to mental pathology.

### 1. Token Physicalism as Ontological Security
Davidson’s anomalous monism provides ontological security for mental health research. Because every mental event *is* a physical event, mental diseases have real physical substrates. When a patient suffers from schizophrenia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, or major depression, their suffering is physically realized in their brain. 

This ensures that neuroscientific investigations—functional neuroimaging, psychopharmacology, and genetic sequencing—are not chasing phantoms. These tools track the physical realizations of mental events. 

However, because of the anomalism of the mental, these physical realizations do not form neat, invariant *types* that map one-to-one onto psychiatric disease categories. A psychiatric diagnosis (e.g., Major Depressive Disorder) is a **heterogeneous natural kind** (or a clinically useful syndrome) realized by a vast, shifting mosaic of distinct neurobiological states across different individuals (multiple realizability).

### 2. Psychiatry as a Special Science
Without type-to-type reductions, psychiatry must be understood as an autonomous **special science**. Like economics, sociology, or cognitive psychology, psychiatry investigates patterns, regularities, and dysfunctions at a level of abstraction that resists translation into microphysics or basic neurobiology. 

An autonomous science of mental health relies on functional, intentional, and systemic taxonomies. It maps patterns of suffering, cognitive distortion, emotional dysregulation, and behavioral impairment not by reducing them to baseline neural firings, but by understanding their internal relations within the patient's intentional life and social environment.

### 3. Re-engineering Boorse’s BSM
To salvage Boorse’s biostatistical model from its untenable neuro-reductionism, we must reformulate it to accommodate intentional anomalism and multiple realizability. Boorse’s core insight—that health involves normal functional ability—can be retained if we expand "function" beyond narrow biological adaptation to encompass **normative functional integration**. 

In the mental sphere, a function is not merely a physiological contribution to survival and reproduction; it is the capacity of an agent to navigate the space of reasons coherently, to maintain an intelligible narrative identity, to adapt emotionally to life’s challenges, and to participate in intersubjective life.

A mental disease, under this revised BSM, is a systemic breakdown in the organism’s capacity to maintain this normative-functional integration. This breakdown may have physiological correlates, and medical interventions (such as psychotherapy or psychopharmacology) may target these physical substrates or intentional webs. 

However, the diagnosis and explanation of mental pathology remain irreducibly psychotherapeutic and intentional, requiring interpretive engagement with the patient's reasons, emotions, and values.

---

## Conclusion

Christopher Boorse’s physiological model of mental health, for all its naturalistic rigor, ultimately fails to sustain its reductive ambitions when confronted with Donald Davidson’s principle of the anomalism of the mental. Boorse’s demand for strict causal-functional laws governing mental disease founders on the normative holism of the intentional domain. 

Because mental states are bound by the constitutive ideal of maximizing rationality and are holistically interconnected within an infinite web of reasons, they cannot be captured by the closed, non-normative nomological net of natural science. Furthermore, Robert Klee’s attempt to equate psychological *ceteris paribus* laws with those of physical and biological sciences underestimates the unique, normative hurdle that distinguishes the space of reasons from the space of causes.

Yet, this failure does not spell the doom of psychiatric science. By embracing Davidson’s anomalous monism—specifically token physicalism combined with the rejection of type physicalism—we can secure an autonomous science of mental health. Psychiatry does not need type-to-type physical reductions to be objective, rigorous, or therapeutic. 

By re-engineering Boorse’s biostatistical model to account for multiple realizability and normative-functional integration, we arrive at a pluralistic framework: one that respects the physical reality of the brain while honoring the irreducibly intentional, normative, and holistic dimensions of the human mind.
