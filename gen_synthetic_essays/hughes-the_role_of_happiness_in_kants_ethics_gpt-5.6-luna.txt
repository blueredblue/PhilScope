# Happiness, Autonomy, and the Highest Good in Kant’s Ethics

## Introduction

Few concepts occupy a more ambiguous position in Kant’s moral philosophy than happiness. On the one hand, happiness is indispensable to human life. Kant does not deny that rational beings naturally seek it, nor does he regard the satisfaction of human needs and inclinations as morally irrelevant. On the other hand, he decisively rejects the eudaimonist claim that happiness provides the basis, standard, or ultimate end of morality. The moral law, Kant argues, cannot be derived from what human beings happen to desire, from a conception of their welfare, or from any account of the good life grounded in experience. Morality must instead rest on a principle that is universal, necessary, and autonomous: the categorical imperative.

This rejection of eudaimonism creates an apparent tension. Happiness seems to be both central to human practical life and unfit to ground moral obligation. It is something all human beings naturally pursue, yet it is notoriously indeterminate and empirically conditioned. By contrast, the categorical imperative commands with a necessity that does not depend on circumstances, preferences, or anticipated consequences. How, then, can happiness have any legitimate place in Kantian ethics? If morality is independent of happiness, does Kant reduce ethics to a cold formalism detached from human flourishing? And if happiness is eventually included in Kant’s conception of the “highest good,” does this reintroduce the very eudaimonism he sought to reject?

Kant’s answer is that happiness belongs to morality, but not as its foundation. Moral action is determined by respect for the moral law, not by the desire to become happy. Yet morality also requires that we regard happiness as a legitimate human good, both in ourselves and in others. The proper relation between virtue and happiness is therefore not that happiness constitutes the content of morality, but that virtue makes one worthy of happiness. In the idea of the highest good, Kant unites virtue and happiness in a morally ordered whole, while preserving the autonomy of the will by refusing to make happiness the motive of moral action. The resulting position distinguishes three questions that eudaimonism tends to conflate: What do human beings naturally seek? What are they morally obligated to do? And what ultimate condition may practical reason rationally hope for?

Kant’s rejection of happiness as the basis of morality is therefore not a rejection of happiness as such. It is an attempt to prevent morality from becoming dependent on the variable and subjective ends of desire. His mature ethical theory preserves happiness as a legitimate object of concern, but subordinates it to the unconditional authority of duty and reconnects it to virtue within the conception of the highest good.

## I. The Eudaimonist Conception of Morality

The eudaimonist tradition, especially in its Aristotelian form, conceives morality in relation to human flourishing. Although ancient theories of eudaimonia differ significantly, they generally hold that ethical reflection concerns the good life as a whole. Virtues are dispositions that enable a person to live well, and practical reason is directed toward the realization of a comprehensive form of human fulfillment. Happiness in this tradition does not merely mean a passing psychological feeling. It refers to a successful or flourishing life, one in which the characteristic capacities of a human being are properly developed and exercised.

Kant’s criticism of eudaimonism is not simply that happiness is a subjective emotion. He recognizes that philosophical accounts of happiness can be sophisticated and objective. His objection is more fundamental: any theory that makes happiness the basis of morality must derive moral principles from a conception of the good that is ultimately dependent on empirical facts about human nature, desire, and circumstance. Even if philosophers disagree about what happiness consists in, they will be attempting to specify the conditions under which human inclinations can be satisfied or human capacities fulfilled. For Kant, this cannot yield a universally binding moral law.

In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, Kant distinguishes between imperatives that are hypothetical and those that are categorical. A hypothetical imperative commands conditionally: if one wills a particular end, one ought to adopt the means necessary to achieve it. “If you want health, exercise,” or “if you want wealth, work diligently,” are hypothetical imperatives. Their force depends on the agent’s having a particular end. A categorical imperative, by contrast, commands unconditionally. It does not say that one ought to act in a certain way if one desires some further result. It says that one must act in that way simply because the action is required by reason.

Happiness appears to function as a universal end, since Kant claims that all human beings necessarily seek it. Yet this does not make happiness a suitable basis for a categorical imperative. The fact that everyone desires happiness does not establish what happiness consists in, which actions will produce it, or whether a given action is morally required. The desire for happiness supplies, at most, a hypothetical practical principle: if one wants to be happy, one ought to pursue what one believes will promote one’s happiness. But this remains conditional upon an empirical desire and an uncertain judgment about the means.

Kant’s argument is directed against the inference from “everyone desires happiness” to “everyone is morally obligated to pursue happiness.” The first claim is anthropological; the second is normative. A universal fact about human motivation cannot by itself generate an unconditional obligation. Indeed, if morality were founded on happiness, moral laws would vary with differences in temperament, social position, culture, and circumstance. What promotes happiness for one person may frustrate it for another. Even within a single life, what appears desirable at one stage may later prove destructive. A principle grounded in happiness would therefore lack the strict universality and necessity that Kant takes morality to require.

## II. The Indeterminacy of Happiness

The central difficulty with happiness is its empirical and indeterminate character. Kant describes happiness as the condition in which everything proceeds according to one’s wishes and inclinations. Yet no finite rational being possesses a complete and reliable knowledge of what would satisfy all of its desires over the course of life. Human beings do not have transparent access to their own future needs, nor can they foresee the consequences of their choices with certainty. Happiness is therefore an idea that each person possesses, but whose content cannot be fully determined in advance.

This indeterminacy arises from several sources. First, human desires are heterogeneous. Individuals seek pleasure, security, recognition, friendship, knowledge, health, power, tranquility, and countless other goods. These ends may conflict with one another. More security may require sacrifices that reduce spontaneity; more recognition may produce anxiety; more pleasure may undermine health. There is no single empirical formula that orders all inclinations into a stable and universally valid system.

Second, desires are contingent and changeable. What a person regards as essential to happiness at one time may later lose its value. The child, the ambitious professional, the parent, and the elderly person may have sharply different conceptions of the good life. Even a philosophical account of flourishing must make judgments about which desires are worthy of cultivation and which should be restrained. At that point, the theory risks relying on standards that are not derived from happiness itself.

Third, happiness depends on circumstances beyond the agent’s control. Health, social recognition, political stability, economic security, and the actions of other people all influence whether a person’s life goes well. If happiness were the foundation of morality, moral obligation would become dependent on conditions that agents cannot fully command. Yet Kant insists that morality must be grounded in the good will, whose value does not rise or fall with external fortune.

Finally, happiness is not merely a collection of particular pleasures. It is a total condition of life. To determine what will make one happy, one would need a comprehensive knowledge of one’s needs, inclinations, future experiences, and likely consequences. This is impossible for finite beings. The pursuit of happiness therefore involves prudential calculation under conditions of uncertainty. It can guide practical decision-making, but it cannot furnish the a priori law of morality.

Kant’s point is not that happiness is meaningless or entirely subjective. Rather, it is that happiness is insufficiently determinate to ground universal obligation. The categorical imperative must possess a form that can be recognized independently of the variable contents of desire. Its validity cannot depend on an empirical prediction about what will make a person’s life satisfying. It must instead arise from the rational structure of willing itself.

## III. The A Priori Necessity of the Categorical Imperative

The categorical imperative is necessary in a way that prudential principles are not. A prudential rule instructs an agent how to achieve a desired end. Its validity depends on both the desirability of the end and the effectiveness of the proposed means. A moral law, however, must bind every rational being, regardless of what that being happens to want. It must apply not merely because a certain outcome is desired, but because the maxim of action can be willed as a universal law.

Kant’s various formulations of the categorical imperative express this demand for universality and autonomy. The Formula of Universal Law requires that one act only on maxims that one can at the same time will as universal laws. The Formula of Humanity commands that one treat humanity, in oneself and in others, always as an end and never merely as a means. The Formula of the Kingdom of Ends presents rational agents as both authors and subjects of a common moral law. These formulations do not identify happiness as the content of morality. They identify the conditions under which a will can be rationally self-legislating and can respect the equal worth of persons.

This a priori structure is incompatible with grounding morality in happiness. If the moral law said, in effect, “Promote whatever makes human beings happy,” it would still leave unresolved what happiness means, whose happiness counts, how conflicting interests should be adjudicated, and whether happiness may be pursued through coercion or deception. The categorical imperative answers these questions by imposing formal and substantive constraints on willing. One may pursue happiness, but not by adopting maxims that cannot be universalized or that treat persons merely as instruments.

The distinction between autonomy and heteronomy is crucial here. A heteronomous will is determined by something external to the rational law it gives itself: desire, inclination, authority, social convention, divine command understood as an external order, or the anticipated consequences of action. A will determined by the pursuit of happiness is heteronomous because the principle of action comes from an object of desire. The agent acts in order to obtain a condition regarded as desirable, rather than because the maxim is rationally and universally valid.

Autonomy does not mean that the moral agent acts arbitrarily or invents morality according to personal preference. It means that the will is governed by a law that reason gives to itself. The agent is free not when liberated from all law, but when acting according to a law that can be justified to every rational being. This is why Kant considers the good will good “without qualification.” Talents, dispositions, and external goods can all be used for bad purposes. Even happiness can become dangerous when detached from moral restraint. The good will, by contrast, is good in itself because it acts from respect for the moral law.

This framework explains why Kant refuses to make moral worth depend on the consequences of action, including the agent’s own happiness. A person who tells the truth because honesty is necessary to secure social trust may perform an action that conforms to duty. But moral worth, in the strict Kantian sense, appears when the person acts from duty, including in circumstances where deception would be advantageous. The moral agent must be prepared to subordinate happiness to principle when the two conflict. This subordination is not a glorification of suffering for its own sake. It is a recognition that a person’s dignity cannot be measured by the amount of pleasure or success achieved.

## IV. Happiness and the Idea of Being Worthy of Happiness

Kant’s rejection of happiness as the basis of morality does not imply that happiness is morally insignificant. Rather, it leads him to transform the question. Morality does not tell us how to achieve happiness as its primary goal; it tells us how to become worthy of happiness.

This formula is easily misunderstood. To be worthy of happiness does not mean that moral conduct earns happiness as a reward in a straightforward causal or transactional sense. Experience plainly shows that virtuous people can suffer and vicious people can prosper. Nor does Kant mean that happiness is morally deserved in the sense that the virtuous have a claim against the world that must always be fulfilled. The idea of worthiness concerns the relation between a person’s character and the enjoyment of good fortune. A person is worthy of happiness when he or she possesses a good will and does not regard personal satisfaction as a license to violate the rights and dignity of others.

The concept of worthiness introduces a moral condition into the pursuit of happiness. Happiness is not itself the supreme good, because happiness without moral worth can become corrupting. A person who has wealth, power, or pleasure but uses these goods selfishly and unjustly does not represent the ideal of a good human life. Conversely, moral worth remains valuable even when external happiness is absent. The good will does not lose its dignity because it fails to achieve prosperity.

At the same time, Kant does not command people to become indifferent to happiness. The duty to promote one’s own happiness can be understood as part of the duty of self-care and rational self-development. Human beings have natural needs, and a person who systematically neglects health, stability, or the conditions of agency may undermine the capacity to fulfill moral duties. More importantly, Kant identifies a duty of beneficence: rational agents ought to adopt the happiness of others as an end. Since other people are ends in themselves, their legitimate purposes must be included in one’s practical deliberation.

The duty of beneficence shows that Kantian morality is not indifferent to human welfare. One must not use other people merely as means to one’s own happiness, but neither may one treat their happiness as morally irrelevant. The Formula of Humanity requires respect for their rational agency, which includes supporting their ability to pursue permissible ends. Kant’s ethics thus rejects both egoistic eudaimonism and an austere moralism that has no concern for human well-being.

The distinction between the motive of action and the object of duty is essential. Happiness may be an object that morality directs us to promote, particularly in the case of others. But it cannot be the motive that determines the moral law. If I help someone only because doing so gives me pleasure, my action may be beneficial but its moral worth is not grounded in the pleasure. If I help another because I recognize a duty of beneficence, the action expresses respect for humanity. Happiness is therefore morally relevant as an end, but not sovereign as a principle.

## V. The Highest Good: Virtue and Happiness Reunited

The most significant place Kant assigns to happiness appears in the *Critique of Practical Reason*, where he develops the idea of the highest good. The highest good is not simply virtue, nor is it happiness. It is the combination of perfect virtue with happiness proportionate to virtue. Kant therefore seems to reunite what his earlier argument had separated. If happiness cannot ground morality, why must it appear in the final object of practical reason?

The answer lies in distinguishing the supreme condition of the highest good from its complete content. Virtue has primacy. It is the condition that makes happiness morally appropriate. Happiness without virtue is not the highest good because it lacks moral worth; virtue without any relation to happiness is also not the complete highest good because practical reason naturally demands that a good will not be ultimately at odds with the well-being it deserves. The highest good is thus a moral order in which happiness is distributed in proportion to virtue.

This proportionality does not mean that virtuous action is a means to happiness. If virtue were adopted in order to obtain happiness, it would cease to be virtue in Kant’s strict sense, because the will would be determined by an external end. Virtue must be pursued for its own sake, out of respect for the law. The relation between virtue and happiness belongs to the ideal object of practical reason, not to the motive that gives moral action its worth.

This distinction prevents the highest good from collapsing into eudaimonism. Eudaimonism treats happiness as the end that determines which traits and actions are valuable. Kant instead treats virtue as the condition that determines when happiness is morally fitting. The order of dependence is reversed. Happiness does not validate virtue; virtue renders happiness worthy and intelligible within the moral whole.

The highest good also expresses the rational hope that the world can be regarded as a unified moral order. Human beings are not satisfied with the thought that virtue and happiness are permanently and necessarily disconnected. Practical reason demands the idea that the good will is not ultimately absurd in a world where injustice and suffering prevail. This demand does not establish that the world actually conforms to moral proportion through empirical evidence. Rather, it generates a practical postulate concerning the conditions under which the highest good could be realized.

## VI. The Antinomy of Practical Reason

Kant’s discussion of the highest good leads to an apparent antinomy. Practical reason commands us to promote the highest good, understood as the union of virtue and happiness. Yet virtue and happiness seem to belong to fundamentally different causal orders. Virtue depends on the moral disposition of the will; happiness depends largely on natural and social conditions. A person may become virtuous through rational self-discipline, but cannot guarantee that the world will reward virtue with prosperity. If the highest good is a necessary object of practical reason, how can it be possible?

The antinomy can be stated as a conflict between two propositions. On the one hand, virtue is the supreme condition of the highest good and must be pursued for its own sake. On the other hand, the highest good includes happiness proportionate to virtue, yet human beings cannot bring about this proportion through moral effort alone. If the highest good is obligatory, practical reason must regard it as possible. But its possibility cannot be secured by ordinary human agency.

Kant resolves this antinomy not by abandoning the highest good, nor by claiming that virtue naturally produces happiness. He instead appeals to the postulates of practical reason: the immortality of the soul and the existence of God. These are not theoretical conclusions or objects of demonstrative knowledge. They are practical assumptions required by the moral standpoint.

The postulate of immortality responds to the demand for complete virtue. Kant does not think that finite human beings can achieve perfect conformity of will to the moral law within a limited lifetime. Moral progress is an unending task. If the highest good requires complete virtue, practical reason must conceive the moral vocation as extending beyond the finite span of earthly existence. Immortality therefore represents the possibility of an endless approximation to moral perfection.

The postulate of God responds to the relation between virtue and happiness. Since human beings cannot ensure that happiness will correspond to moral worth, practical reason postulates a moral author of nature capable of bringing the two into harmony. God is not introduced as an external legislator whose commands replace autonomy. The moral law is known through reason itself, and its authority does not depend on divine threats or rewards. Rather, God functions within practical reason as the ground of hope that nature and morality can ultimately be reconciled.

This point is especially important for preserving autonomy. If a person obeys God because divine punishment is feared or divine reward is desired, the motive is heteronomous. The moral law would then be reduced to a system of prudential calculation. Kant’s postulate does not authorize such a motivational structure. The agent must do the right thing because it is right. The hope for happiness proportionate to virtue can accompany morality, but it cannot determine the will’s fundamental commitment.

The postulates therefore do not make happiness the foundation of obligation. They make possible the rational hope that moral action belongs within a world in which the highest good can be realized. Kant’s ordering remains intact: duty grounds conduct; virtue has primacy; happiness is included as a legitimate and necessary element of the complete good; and faith arises as a practical response to the limits of human power.

## VII. The Autonomy of the Will and the Problem of Incentive

One might object that if happiness is excluded as a motive, Kantian morality becomes psychologically implausible. Human beings are naturally motivated by pleasure, fear, affection, ambition, and hope. Can a moral theory that demands action from duty rather than inclination genuinely guide human conduct?

Kant does not deny the force of inclination. He denies that inclination can provide the principle of moral obligation. The distinction is not between having desires and having none, but between acting from a maxim grounded in desire and acting from respect for the law. A person may experience compassion while helping another, or satisfaction after fulfilling a duty. These feelings do not invalidate the action. What matters is whether the agent would still act rightly if the feeling were absent and whether the maxim is determined by duty rather than by the anticipated emotional reward.

Respect for the moral law is itself a distinctive moral feeling, though it is not an empirical incentive like pleasure. It arises when the agent recognizes the authority of reason over inclination. This respect is not externally imposed. It is the affective consciousness of autonomy: the rational subject experiences itself as bound by a law it can acknowledge as its own. Kant’s ethics is therefore not entirely devoid of motivation; it replaces the pursuit of happiness with the motive of respect for humanity and law.

Moreover, Kant’s exclusion of happiness as the moral motive protects individuals from manipulation. If morality were based on the promise of happiness, institutions could define and distribute happiness in ways that compromise freedom. Governments, religious authorities, or social majorities might claim to know what will make people happy and compel them to pursue it. Kant’s insistence that each person has an ultimate responsibility for his or her own permissible ends protects the plurality of human conceptions of happiness. The moral law sets limits on how we pursue happiness, but it does not prescribe one comprehensive lifestyle as universally binding.

The relation between happiness and autonomy is thus complex. A person who is governed entirely by desire is not fully autonomous, because he or she is subject to impulses that have not been rationally assessed. Yet a person whose happiness is treated as irrelevant may lack the material and psychological conditions necessary for meaningful self-government. Kant’s ethics attempts to avoid both forms of domination: domination by inclination and domination by paternalistic conceptions of welfare. Autonomy requires the capacity to pursue happiness within the boundaries established by universal moral law.

## VIII. Criticisms and Philosophical Tensions

Kant’s position has attracted several important criticisms. One concern is that his account of happiness is too thin compared with classical eudaimonism. By treating happiness primarily as satisfaction of inclinations, Kant may appear to reduce a rich conception of flourishing to a subjective state. Aristotle, for example, would object that happiness is not merely what one happens to want but an objective activity of a virtuous life. If happiness is understood in this richer way, perhaps it can serve as the basis of morality without the instability Kant identifies.

Kant’s response would be that any substantive account of flourishing must still be tested against the requirements of universal moral law. A conception of human excellence that permits slavery, deception, or domination cannot become morally valid simply because it is presented as part of a flourishing life. Kant’s formalism protects the status of each person as an end. Yet the criticism remains significant: Kant may be right that happiness cannot ground morality, while still providing too little guidance about the positive shape of a good human life.

A second criticism concerns the separation between morality and happiness. Kant’s strict distinction between acting from duty and acting from inclination can seem to undervalue love, friendship, sympathy, and the cultivation of emotionally integrated character. If a person helps a friend both from affection and from duty, it may seem artificial to regard the affection as morally secondary. Contemporary ethical theories often argue that moral motivation is pluralistic and that a life governed by duty alone would be deficient.

Kant need not deny that emotions can be cultivated and morally valuable. He can maintain that sympathy is a desirable support for beneficence while insisting that it cannot be the ultimate standard of obligation. A person should develop sympathetic dispositions, but should also help when sympathy fails. The limitation of Kant’s approach lies not in rejecting emotion altogether, but in his tendency to define moral worth through a contrast between inclination and duty that can appear sharper than the complexity of actual moral character.

A third criticism targets the highest good and its postulates. The appeal to God and immortality may seem to reintroduce a reward structure that Kant’s autonomy had excluded. If happiness is eventually guaranteed by divine order, does morality not become indirectly motivated by the hope of reward? Kant’s answer depends on maintaining the distinction between acting for the sake of duty and hoping that the moral world is ultimately coherent. Yet psychologically, the distinction may be difficult to sustain. The prospect of future happiness could influence conduct even if Kant insists that it should not.

There is also a deeper theoretical issue. If the highest good requires the union of virtue and happiness, perhaps Kant has not fully escaped the eudaimonist intuition that morality must somehow make life go well. His position could be interpreted as an unstable compromise: he denies that happiness grounds morality, but cannot accept that morality is complete without it. This tension is real, but it may be philosophically productive. It reflects the dual nature of human beings as both moral agents and sensible creatures. We cannot reduce morality to happiness, but neither can we regard the suffering and flourishing of embodied persons as irrelevant to practical reason.

Kant’s strongest achievement may therefore lie not in eliminating the tension but in ordering it. Virtue cannot be exchanged for happiness, and happiness cannot excuse injustice. Nevertheless, practical reason hopes for a world in which moral worth and well-being are not permanently opposed. The highest good expresses this hope without converting it into the source of moral obligation.

## IX. Happiness, Justice, and the Moral World

The Kantian conception of the highest good has implications not only for individual morality but also for social and political thought. If happiness is to be proportionate to virtue, then a world of systematic injustice is incompatible with the ideal of the highest good. Although individuals cannot guarantee the success of virtue, they can work to create institutions in which persons are free to pursue their own conceptions of happiness under universal laws.

This does not mean that the state should impose happiness. Kant sharply rejects paternalistic politics that treats citizens as children whose welfare may be managed by authorities. A state may secure external freedom, rights, and the conditions of lawful coexistence, but it should not legislate a single account of human flourishing. The political task is not to make citizens happy according to an official ideal, but to protect their autonomy so that they can pursue happiness in their own ways.

At the same time, the duty of beneficence and the idea of humanity as an end impose moral demands beyond mere noninterference. A society that formally respects rights while permitting extreme deprivation may fail to honor the conditions under which persons can exercise rational agency. Kant’s ethics does not straightforwardly yield a modern welfare state, but it supports the principle that human beings must not be abandoned to conditions that make autonomous life impossible.

The political significance of Kant’s rejection of eudaimonism is thus double-sided. It limits the authority of those who claim to know what will make everyone happy, while also forbidding the reduction of persons to instruments of collective prosperity. Happiness matters, but it must be pursued through institutions that respect freedom and equal dignity. The highest good becomes not a program for maximizing satisfaction but an ideal of a moral community in which persons are virtuous, free, and not arbitrarily deprived of the good fortune appropriate to their humanity.

## Conclusion

Kant rejects the eudaimonist doctrine that happiness serves as the basis of morality because happiness is an empirical, variable, and indeterminate object. It depends on inclinations that differ among individuals and change over time, and it requires prudential judgments about circumstances whose consequences cannot be known with certainty. Such a concept cannot generate the a priori necessity and universality of the categorical imperative. A morality founded on happiness would be hypothetical and heteronomous: it would tell us what to do if we wanted a certain end, rather than commanding us unconditionally as rational agents.

The categorical imperative instead grounds morality in autonomy. The moral law is not derived from the pursuit of any desired object but from the form of rational willing itself. To act morally is to act on maxims that can be universally legislated and that respect humanity as an end. This does not make happiness irrelevant. Human beings have legitimate needs and purposes, and morality includes duties of self-care and beneficence. We must promote happiness where we can, especially the happiness of others, but we must do so under principles that respect their autonomy.

Kant’s redefinition of the moral task as becoming worthy of happiness preserves the rightful place of happiness while denying it foundational authority. Virtue is not a means to happiness, and moral conduct cannot be justified by the promise of reward. Rather, virtue is the condition under which happiness becomes morally fitting. In the highest good, practical reason unites virtue and happiness without reversing their order. Virtue remains supreme, while happiness becomes the corresponding completion of a morally ordered life.

The antinomy of practical reason arises because human beings are required to promote a highest good that they cannot fully realize through their own power. Kant resolves this not by making happiness the motive of duty, but by postulating the immortality necessary for moral progress and the divine ground of a possible harmony between virtue and happiness. These postulates express rational hope rather than theoretical knowledge, and they do not compromise autonomy because the moral law retains its authority independently of reward.

Kant’s ethics therefore neither worships happiness nor despises it. It refuses to make happiness the measure of moral worth, because doing so would subject reason to desire and expose persons to paternalistic control. Yet it also refuses to accept the permanent moral absurdity of a world in which virtue and happiness have no intelligible connection. The good life, on Kant’s account, is not simply the happy life, nor is it the life of duty stripped of all human fulfillment. It is a life in which the will is autonomous, the person is worthy of happiness, and practical reason may hope that happiness and virtue can ultimately belong together.
