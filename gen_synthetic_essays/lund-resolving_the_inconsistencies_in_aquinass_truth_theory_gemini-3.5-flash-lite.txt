### Introduction

The trajectory of medieval epistemology is defined by a persistent, high-stakes tension: how to reconcile the absolute, immutable eternity of divine truth with the contingent, mutable, and frequently erroneous nature of human cognition. In the philosophy of Thomas Aquinas, this tension is not merely a peripheral theological curiosity, but the structural crux of his entire theory of knowledge and being. Aquinas operates at the intersection of Aristotelian empiricism and Augustinian illumination. He insists that our knowledge begins in the senses, yet he maintains that truth, in its primary and most rigorous sense, is divine and eternal. 

To navigate this chasm, Aquinas introduces a foundational distinction between *human truth*—rooted in the adequation of the human intellect to mutable created things—and *divine truth*—which is identical with the divine essence itself. A central question persists in contemporary scholarship: Does this dual-level distinction successfully resolve the internal contradictions of his truth theory, or does it merely paper over an unbridgeable metaphysical fault line? 

This essay evaluates whether Aquinas’s distinction between human and divine truth successfully reconciles his ontology and epistemology. It argues that while Aquinas constructs a brilliant architectonic synthesis that preserves both human cognitive agency and divine transcendence, his project ultimately buckles under the weight of its own metaphysical architecture. By anchoring mutable human propositions in an immutable, divine intellect, Aquinas imports an untenable theological dependency that strains the limits of philosophical argumentation. Furthermore, this move introduces profound semantic imprecision, stretching the term "truth" across ontological domains so vast that the univocity of the concept fractures. 

To demonstrate this, we will examine three pivotal dimensions of the Thomistic corpus: 
1. The deep structural reliance on the Aristotelian correspondence theory of truth (*adaequatio intellectus et rei*) and how Aquinas adapts it to bridge mind and reality.
2. Aquinas’s tactical engagement with and rejection of Pre-Socratic relativism, particularly the doctrines of Heraclitus and Protagoras, which serves as his defense against radical skepticism.
3. A close reading of *Summa Theologiae*, Question 16, where Aquinas systematically formalizes the ontological and logical dimensions of truth. 

Finally, we will address the critical objection that grounding truth in the divine intellect results in an overreliance on theological postulates and semantic equivocation, evaluating whether Aquinas’s system ultimately achieves coherence or collapses into a theological resort.

### Part I: The Metaphysical Foundation — The Correspondence Theory and the Convertibility of Being and Truth

To understand how Aquinas manages the dual spheres of human and divine truth, one must first grasp the machinery of his correspondence theory. For Aquinas, truth is not merely a property of propositions, language, or formal logic; it is fundamentally an ontological condition rooted in the real relationship between intellect and reality. Following his famous definition—derived from Isaac Israeli via Avicenna and filtered through Aristotle—truth is the *adaequatio intellectus et rei*, the adaquation or correspondence of the intellect and the thing. 

However, this simple formula conceals a profound metaphysical asymmetry. Correspondence requires two relata: the intellect and the thing (*res*). But whose intellect, and what kind of thing? Aquinas immediately splits this inquiry into two distinct domains: the human intellect and the divine intellect. 

In the case of the human intellect, truth is *sequential* and *posteriori*. The human mind is *tabula rasa* at birth; it possesses no innate ideas. It must abstract intelligible species from the phantasms provided by sense perception. Consequently, human truth is measured by the extramental thing. As Aquinas states in *De Veritate*, Q. 1, a. 2, the human intellect is measured (*mensurata*) by the thing. A human proposition—such as "The cat is on the mat"—is true if and only if the intellect’s internal synthesis of subject and predicate corresponds to the actual extramental state of affairs. Here, the thing is the measure, and the human intellect is the thing measured. 

Conversely, the divine intellect reverses this relation entirely. God does not look out upon an independent, pre-existing universe to measure His thoughts against it. Rather, God’s intellect is the creative cause of all things. As Aquinas asserts, the divine intellect is the measure of all things (*mensurans, non mensuratum*). Things exist because God conceives them; their very intelligibility is an emanation of the divine ideas. Therefore, the divine intellect is the primary exemplar of truth, and created things are true precisely insofar as they adequately express the divine ideas from which they proceed.

This ontological inversion leads directly to Aquinas’s doctrine of the convertibility of being and truth (*ens et verum convertuntur*). In *De Veritate*, Q. 1, a. 1, Aquinas argues that truth is an attribute co-extensive with being. Every being, simply by virtue of being actualized, possesses an intrinsic intelligibility. Just as every entity is a potential object of understanding, so too is every entity intrinsically "true" to the degree that it fulfills the conceptual archetype held within the divine mind. 

```
[Divine Intellect] --(Measures / Causes)--> [Created Things] --(Measures)--> [Human Intellect]
       |                                                                            |
       +----------------------(Divine Truth is Primary)-----------------------------+
```

This ontological framework creates an immediate structural tension. If being and truth are convertible, and if all finite beings are mutable, finite, and prone to decay, what happens to truth when the thing changes or ceases to be? If human truth is strictly dependent upon the adaquation of the human intellect to a mutable thing, then human truth itself appears radically unstable. If the cat leaves the mat, the proposition "The cat is on the mat" instantly transitions from true to false. 

To rescue human truth from Heraclitean flux—the chaotic view that everything is constantly changing, making stable knowledge impossible—Aquinas must find a way to anchor mutable human propositions within an immutable metaphysical framework. This brings us directly to his engagement with Pre-Socratic epistemology.

### Part II: The Pre-Socratic Horizon — Defeating Relativism and the Heraclitean Flux

Aquinas does not formulate his theory of truth in a historical vacuum; he is acutely aware of the ancient debates concerning change, perception, and stability. In constructing his defense of immutable truth, Aquinas must continuously parry the ghosts of Pre-Socratic relativism, particularly the metaphysical flux of Heraclitus and the epistemological subjectivism of Protagoras. 

Protagoras famously claimed that "man is the measure of all things: of the things which are, that they are, and of the things which are not, that they are not." In the *Theaetetus*, Plato had already exposed the fatal flaw of this position: if perception is the sole criterion of truth, and if perception varies infinitely from person to person (and from moment to moment within the same person), then all contradictory assertions are equally true, and truth collapses into subjective opinion. 

Aquinas inherits this critique through Aristotle’s *Metaphysics* (particularly Book Gamma), where Aristotle argues against those who deny the Principle of Non-Contradiction. Aquinas recognizes that if human truth is entirely at the mercy of mutable, physical things, and if physical things are trapped in an unceasing Heraclitean river of becoming, then human knowledge becomes impossible. If the object of knowledge changes while the knower is knowing it, the concept formed by the intellect is already obsolete by the time it is grasped.

Aquinas addresses this challenge by bifurcating the stability of the object of knowledge. He agrees with the Heracliteans that sensible, material particulars are mutable and subject to constant generation and corruption. A material horse grows old, changes color, and eventually dies. A human proposition based merely on the fleeting sensory experience of this specific horse is inherently unstable. 

However, Aquinas counters this relativism by introducing the Aristotelian mechanism of abstraction (*abstractio*). While the sensible particular is mutable, the human intellect possesses the capacity to abstract the *universal nature* (the quiddity or "whatness") from the particular. The individual horse (*hoc aliquid*) changes, but the universal nature of "horseness" (*equinitas*) remains stable, immutable, and eternal in its intelligibility. 

In his *Commentary on Aristotle’s Metaphysics*, Aquinas insists that while physical substances undergo substantial and accidental change, the intelligible forms by which we know them participate in a higher order of stability. The human intellect, by abstracting these universal forms, contacts a reality that transcends the immediate flux of sensory experience. 

Yet, this Aristotelian epistemological fix—abstraction—reveals a profound vulnerability in Aquinas’s system. Does abstraction truly secure immutable truth, or does it merely push the problem back one step? If universal concepts exist only *in re* (in the individual things) and *post rem* (constructed by the human mind through abstraction), then universals are still intrinsically tied to a material universe that is contingent and changing. If every physical horse were annihilated, the universal concept of "horseness" would, in a strict empiricist ontology, cease to have an extramental anchor. 

To prevent human truth from collapsing into a sophisticated form of conceptual relativism, Aquinas is forced to take a decisive theological turn. He argues that these universal intelligible forms do not merely float in a vacuum of human cognitive construction; they are eternally grounded in the immutable exemplar ideas of the divine mind. Thus, Aquinas’s defense against Pre-Socratic relativism ultimately requires him to leap from an Aristotelian epistemology of abstraction to an Augustinian ontology of divine illumination.

### Part III: A Close Reading of *Summa Theologiae*, Question 16

The definitive textual locus for Aquinas’s systematic treatment of truth is found in *Summa Theologiae*, First Part, Question 16: "Of Truth." Across its eight articles, Question 16 provides a masterclass in scholastic articulation, moving methodically from the ontological location of truth to its eternity, immutability, and multiplicity. 

In **Article 1**, Aquinas asks whether truth resides only in the intellect. He concludes that while truth is formally located in the intellect (as its proper subject), it is also found fundamentally in things, albeit by way of relation to an intellect. Here, Aquinas establishes his famous hierarchy: truth is primarily in the divine intellect, secondarily in human intellects, and derivatively in things (as they conform to intellects). 

In **Article 2**, he addresses whether truth is exclusively in the intellect combining and dividing (i.e., in propositional judgment), or also in simple concepts. Aquinas argues that in its primary, strict sense, truth resides in the intellect's act of composition and division—that is, when the mind asserts a predicate of a subject. A simple concept (e.g., "man" or "triangle") is neither true nor false in itself until the intellect judges that the concept corresponds to an actual or possible reality. 

However, **Article 5** and **Article 7** are the most critical for evaluating our core thesis regarding the reconciliation of human and divine truth. 

In **Article 5**, Aquinas raises the question: "Whether created truth is immutable?" The objection runs as follows: Everything created is mutable; therefore, if truth is created (as human truth certainly is), it must be mutable. But if truth is mutable, how can we have infallible, immutable scientific knowledge? 

Aquinas’s response is a masterpiece of metaphysical stratification. He distinguishes between truth *formally considered* in the human intellect and truth *materially considered* in the objects known, or viewed in its primary exemplar in the divine intellect. He writes:

> "As the truth of the divine intellect is eternal and immutable, so the truth of the human intellect, considered in itself, is mutable... yet with respect to the first truth, it has a certain immutability, insofar as it is regulated by the first truth." (*ST*, I, q. 16, a. 5, ad 2)

Aquinas admits that human truth is mutable *in ratione sui* (in its own formal character) because the human intellect is contingent, discursive, and capable of error. A human being can believe something is true today, discover new evidence tomorrow, and judge it false. Human opinions change; human propositions are bound to the ticking clock of historical existence. 

Yet, Aquinas insists that human truth participates in immutability when it attains necessary and universal principles. When a human mind grasps a mathematical truth (e.g., "The angles of a triangle equal two right angles") or a metaphysical axiom (e.g., "The principle of non-contradiction"), that truth does not change. Why? Because these necessary truths reflect the immutable divine ideas. Thus, human truth achieves stability not through its own intrinsic power, but through its structural participation in the eternal truth of the divine intellect.

In **Article 7**, Aquinas pushes this argument further, asking whether human truth is eternal. He answers with a resounding negative regarding human truth *in se*: human truth had a beginning in time because human minds are created things that begin to exist. However, he maintains that truth in the divine intellect is strictly eternal. Because God’s intellect is identical with His essence, and God’s essence is eternal and uncaused, divine truth has no beginning and no end. 

Furthermore, in **Article 8**, Aquinas addresses the unity of truth: "Whether all things are true by one truth?" He concludes that just as there is one divine essence, there is one primary truth—the divine truth itself—by which all things are called true. Yet, there are *many* created truths, corresponding to the multiple, diverse intellects of human beings and the countless contingent things that exist in the created order. 

Through this dense labyrinth of distinctions in Question 16, Aquinas attempts a daring philosophical balancing act. He preserves the reality, contingency, and mutability of human propositions (satisfying our empirical observation of a changing world), while anchoring them securely in the eternal, immutable, and singular truth of the divine mind (satisfying our metaphysical requirement for absolute, unshakeable epistemic foundations).

### Part IV: Critical Evaluation — The Objections of Theological Dependency and Semantic Imprecision

We must now confront the central evaluative question of this essay: *Does this sophisticated architecture successfully resolve the contradictions in Aquinas’s truth theory, or does it collapse under its own weight?* 

Despite its undeniable scholastic brilliance, Aquinas’s distinction between human and divine truth faces two devastating critical objections that threaten to undermine its coherence.

#### Objection 1: The Tenability of Theological Dependency
The first major objection targets Aquinas’s reliance on the divine intellect to secure the stability of human truth. When pressed to explain how finite, changeable human propositions can attain immutable truth, Aquinas ultimately appeals to participation (*participatio*) in the divine ideas. 

From a strictly philosophical standpoint, this move represents a methodological retreat from rational epistemology into dogmatic theology. By anchoring the validity of human logic and scientific demonstration in the hidden counsels of the divine mind, Aquinas arguably commits a category mistake. He uses a metaphysical postulate (the existence of an omniscient, creative God) to solve an epistemological problem (how human minds can attain universal, immutable knowledge amidst physical change). 

For a critic operating within a secular or strictly naturalistic framework—or even for an Aristotelian who wishes to keep natural philosophy distinct from sacred theology—this solution is philosophically unsatisfying. It essentially asserts that human truth is true because God thinks it. But this leaves the human knower in an epistemic vacuum. How does the finite human intellect verify that its propositions actually participate in the divine ideas, rather than merely reflecting the idiosyncratic projections of human neurobiology and culture? 

Aquinas assumes a harmonious pre-established alignment between the human intellect and the divine intellect, mediated by the abstraction of forms. But in the absence of an independent, purely rational demonstration of this divine participation, the entire framework becomes circular:
1. We know things are true because our intellects correspond to reality.
2. Reality is true because it corresponds to the divine intellect.
3. Our human intellects are able to grasp this truth because they are created by God and participate in His light.

This is not a resolution of the epistemological tension; it is a restatement of the theological premise. By making human truth entirely dependent upon divine immutability, Aquinas strips human cognition of its autonomous validity. If human truth is only truly "true" insofar as it reflects divine truth, then purely secular, empirical, or provisional human propositions are demoted to mere shadows of truth, bordering on illusion. This creates a severe internal contradiction in Aquinas’s system: he goes to great lengths in works like the *Summa Contra Gentiles* and his Aristotelian commentaries to validate the autonomy and dignity of natural reason, yet his ultimate theory of truth reduces natural reason to a passive receiver of divine intellectual illumination.

#### Objection 2: Semantic Imprecision and Analogical Stretching
The second major objection focuses on language and semantics: Does the word "truth" mean the same thing when applied to God, to a human proposition, and to a physical thing? 

Aquinas is acutely aware of the problem of language when speaking of God. He famously develops the doctrine of *analogy*—arguing that terms applied to God and creatures are neither strictly univocal (meaning the exact same thing) nor purely equivocal (meaning entirely different things), but *analogical*, exhibiting a proportional relationship. 

However, when applied to Question 16, Aquinas’s use of analogy strains semantic coherence to its absolute breaking point. Consider the vast ontological and logical chasm between the following three statements:
1. "God is truth itself." (Divine Truth: eternal, uncreated, identical with the divine essence).
2. "The proposition 'The snow is white' is true." (Human Truth: temporal, mental, discursive, depending on extramental correspondence).
3. "This is a true gold coin." (Ontological Truth of a thing: adequate to its divine exemplar).

Can a single philosophical concept of "truth" genuinely encompass all three of these applications without losing all determinate meaning? Critics argue that it cannot. When Aquinas applies "truth" to God, he is speaking of an infinite, self-contained act of divine being. When he applies it to a human proposition, he is speaking of a formal logical relation in a finite brain. When he applies it to a gold coin, he is speaking of physical authenticity or perfection. 

By stretching the predicate "truth" across these radically disparate categories, Aquinas runs the risk of committing the fallacy of equivocation under the protective cover of analogical language. If "truth" means one thing when applied to God and an entirely different thing when applied to a human scientific hypothesis, then invoking "divine truth" does nothing to resolve the contradictions of "human truth." It merely changes the subject. 

Instead of explaining how a mutable human proposition bridges the gap to immutable reality, Aquinas simply points to a different kind of truth altogether—divine truth—and asserts that the former participates in the latter. But "participation" in this context functions more as a rhetorical bridge than a rigorous logical explanation. It names the mystery rather than solving the contradiction.

---

### Conclusion

In evaluating Thomas Aquinas’s theory of truth, we witness one of the most ambitious intellectual syntheses in the history of Western philosophy. Faced with the profound challenge of reconciling the Heraclitean flux of the material world with the human yearning for stable, immutable knowledge, Aquinas constructs an architectonic masterpiece. Through the careful deployment of the Aristotelian correspondence theory, the rigorous defense of universal abstraction against Pre-Socratic relativism, and the nuanced metaphysical stratifications of *Summa Theologiae*, Question 16, he attempts to secure human truth by anchoring it in the eternal bedrock of the divine intellect.

Does this distinction between human and divine truth successfully resolve the internal contradictions of his truth theory? The answer must be nuanced. 

Within the closed circle of scholastic Christian theology and metaphysics, Aquinas’s system is remarkably coherent. It successfully preserves both the contingency of creaturely existence and the absolute sovereignty of divine being, preventing human knowledge from collapsing into radical skepticism or relativism while honoring the empirical reality of sensory experience. 

However, from a purely philosophical and analytical standpoint, the resolution is ultimately vulnerable. By anchoring human truth in the divine intellect, Aquinas relies upon a theological postulate that overreaches the boundaries of natural reason, trading epistemological rigor for metaphysical assurance. Furthermore, his expansive use of analogical predication stretches the semantics of "truth" so thin that the concept threatens to fracture into irreconcilable meanings. 

Ultimately, Aquinas’s theory of truth reveals the inherent limits of attempting to marry Aristotelian empiricism with Augustinian-Neoplatonic theology. The distinction between human and divine truth does not neatly resolve the contradictions of his system; rather, it brilliantly illuminates the deep, inescapable tension at the heart of medieval philosophy: the desperate, fragile attempt of a finite, changing human mind to articulate the infinite and unchanging reality of God.
