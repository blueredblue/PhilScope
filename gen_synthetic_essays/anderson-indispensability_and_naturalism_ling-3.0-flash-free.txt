**The Compromise of Truth: Naturalism, Indispensability, and the Architecture of Mathematical Realism**

The Quine-Putnam indispensability argument stands as one of the most formidable pillars in the philosophy of mathematics. Articulated by W.V.O. Quine and refined by Hilary Putnam, the argument contends that we must believe in the existence of all entities indispensable to our best scientific theories. Because mathematics is indispensable to empirical science, we must believe in the existence of mathematical entities. However, this argument has faced sustained criticism, most notably from Penelope Maddy, whose naturalistic philosophy of mathematics draws heavily on the nuances of mathematical and scientific practice. Maddy argues that the indispensability argument, when subjected to the constraints of naturalism, overreaches by demanding ontological commitments that are unsupported by the actual methodologies of science and mathematics. 

In response to Maddy’s criticisms, a position known as compromise naturalism has emerged as a sophisticated defense of the indispensability argument. Compromise naturalism seeks to preserve the core intuition of the Quine-Putnam thesis—that our best scientific theories rational belief in the mathematical structures they employ—while modifying the ontological and semantic commitments traditionally associated with it. By decoupling realism from strict ontological commitment, compromise naturalism navigates the treacherous waters between the demands of scientific holism and the constraints of naturalism. This essay evaluates the viability of compromise naturalism as a defense of the indispensability argument against Maddy’s criticisms. It analyzes how separating realism from ontological commitment resolves the alleged incompatibility between confirmation holism and naturalism, addresses whether alternative forms of realism can sustain determinate truth-values for indispensable mathematical entities lacking ontological rights, and defends the normative intervention of indispensability theory against charges of improperly overriding established scientific and mathematical methodology.

**I. Maddy’s Critique and the Naturalist Dilemma**

Penelope Maddy’s critique of the indispensability argument is rooted in her commitment to naturalism, a philosophical stance that insists philosophy must respect the methods and findings of empirical science and, crucially, of mathematics itself. Maddy’s naturalism, developed in works such as *Realism in Mathematics* and *Second Philosophy*, operates through what she calls "Second Philosophy"—an approach that evaluates philosophical theses by looking at how mathematicians and scientists actually practice their disciplines.

Maddy’s criticisms take a dual form, targeting both the mathematical and scientific dimensions of the indispensability argument. On the mathematical side, Maddy argues that set-theoretic realism is not supported by mathematical practice. She observes that working mathematicians routinely employ finite mathematics and do not engage in the ontological commitments demanded by set theory. For Maddy, the indispensability of set theory for physics is irrelevant because mathematics itself does not require it; finite mathematics suffices for the empirical sciences. Therefore, the Quine-Putnam argument improperly inflates the ontological requirements of science by dragging in the foundational apparatus of set theory, which is not indispensable to mathematical practice itself.

On the scientific side, Maddy invokes confirmation holism to undermine the argument’s empirical grounding. Confirmation holism, famously articulated by Pierre Duhem and W.V.O. Quine himself, posits that scientific theories are confirmed as wholes rather than as individual hypotheses. When we observe a prediction of a theory, we confirm the entire theoretical framework, not just the component dealing with the specific entity in question. Maddy argues that this holism makes the indispensability argument circular or vacuous. If scientific theories are confirmed holistically, we cannot isolate the mathematical component of a theory and claim it is independently confirmed. Moreover, because we can always revise our scientific theories holistically, we might revise the mathematics as well. If the indispensability of mathematics is undercut by our ability to revise our scientific theories holistically, the argument cannot force the belief in the existence of mathematical entities.

Under standard Quinean naturalism, this creates a dilemma: either we accept the ontological commitment to mathematical entities as dictated by the indispensability argument, thereby overriding the naturalist’s respect for the actual practice of mathematics and the holistic nature of scientific confirmation, or we reject the indispensability argument, thereby severing the necessary link between science and mathematics. Compromise naturalism proposes a third path.

**II. Separating Realism from Ontological Commitment: Resolving the Holism/Naturalism Incompatibility**

The central maneuver of compromise naturalism is the separation of realism from ontological commitment. In traditional Quinean philosophy, realism and ontological commitment are tightly bound together through the criterion of ontological commitment: "To be is to be the value of a bound variable." If a theory quantifies over mathematical entities, it is committed to their existence, and realism about those entities follows from that commitment. Compromise naturalism severs this link, arguing that we can be realist about the truth of mathematical statements without being ontologically committed to the existence of the mathematical objects those statements quantify over.

This separation resolves the alleged incompatibility between confirmation holism and naturalism. Under standard indispensability, holism threatens naturalism because holistic confirmation makes it impossible to determine which entities are truly indispensable. If we confirm a theory holistically, we confirm the whole package, including the mathematical entities. But because we can revise the whole package, we cannot be certain that the mathematics is indispensable in a way that forces ontological commitment. Furthermore, if holism forces us to accept the ontological consequences of our best scientific theories, we are forced to accept the existence of mathematical entities even when our mathematical practice suggests otherwise, thereby overriding naturalism’s methodological constraints.

Compromise naturalism dissolves this tension by restricting ontological commitment to what is empirically unavoidable while preserving realism about the truth of the mathematical theories we employ. When confirmation holism operates, it threatens the *truth* of our theoretical claims, not necessarily the *existence* of the entities quantified over. If our best scientific theory confirms a mathematical proposition holistically, compromise naturalism accepts that we must treat that mathematical proposition as true—it is a realist acceptance of the mathematical component of our best theory. However, it refuses to translate this truth into an ontological commitment in the Quinean sense. We are not forced to believe that the mathematical entities "exist" as mind-independent objects populating the furniture of the universe; we are only forced to believe that the mathematical statements are true and indispensable to the systematic success of our empirical science.

This resolution preserves naturalism by ensuring that ontological commitment—the heavy metaphysical baggage of "existence"—is only incurred for entities that are empirically manifest and indispensable in a way that cannot be revised without empirical catastrophe. Mathematical entities, being abstract and non-causal, do not carry the same empirical weight. Thus, holistic revision might lead us to revise our mathematical beliefs, but it does not threaten the ontological status of physical entities in the same way it might threaten our belief in unobservable physical entities. By separating realism (the commitment to truth) from ontological commitment (the commitment to existence), compromise naturalism allows us to embrace the indispensability of mathematics for science without falling prey to the holism-driven skepticism that plagues the traditional argument. We are naturalists about existence but realists about truth, and this bifurcation provides the necessary stability to withstand Maddy’s holistic and methodological critiques.

**III. Sustaining Determinate Truth-Values for Entities Lacking Ontological Rights**

A significant objection to compromise naturalism arises from the question of truth-values. If indispensable mathematical entities lack "ontological rights"—meaning they do not exist in the robust, Quinean sense—how can the mathematical propositions concerning them possess determinate truth-values? Maddy and other critics argue that truth is typically grounded in reference, and reference requires existence. If compromise naturalism strips mathematical entities of existence, it seems to leave mathematical statements without a referent, thereby rendering their truth-values indeterminate or merely subjective.

To sustain determinate truth-values for indispensable mathematical entities lacking ontological rights, compromise naturalism must appeal to alternative forms of realism that decouple truth from existence. The most prominent of these is structural realism, particularly as articulated by Stewart Shapiro and Michael Resnik. Structural realism posits that the objects of mathematics are not independent, self-subsistent entities, but rather positions within abstract structures. In this view, the number 2 does not "exist" as an ontologically privileged object; rather, it occupies the position of the second successor of zero within the structure of the natural numbers. The truth of the statement "2 + 2 = 4" is not a statement about the relation between two independent objects, but a structural truth about the relations that hold within the abstract system.

Under structural realism, indispensable mathematical entities lack ontological rights in the traditional sense—they are not Quinean objects—but they possess determinate truth-values because they are defined by their structural relations. The truth-value of a mathematical proposition is determined by its structural role within the system, not by the independent existence of its referents. This allows compromise naturalism to maintain that mathematical propositions are objectively true (realism) without committing to the existence of the entities as individuals (naturalism). The indispensability of the mathematics to science guarantees that the structures in question are the correct ones for organizing empirical phenomena, thereby grounding the determinate truth-values of the mathematical statements in their structural indispensability.

A second alternative form of realism that supports this view is deflationary or minimalist realism, often associated with the philosophical work of Hartry Field and his nominalistic reconstruction of science, though Field himself is an anti-realist about mathematics. However, a compromised version of this approach can be adapted. Here, the truth of mathematics is not grounded in a Platonic realm of objects, but in the system's capacity to function as a reliable instrument for scientific theorizing. The indispensability of the mathematics ensures that the mathematical claims have determinate truth-values within the framework of our best scientific theories. The truth is "deflated" in the sense that it does not require a heavyweight ontology, but it is "real" in the sense that it is objectively constrained by the empirical success and internal coherence of the scientific framework.

By adopting structural or deflationary realism, compromise naturalism successfully answers the charge that stripping entities of ontological rights renders their truth-values indeterminate. The truth-values are sustained by the structural indispensability of the mathematics and its integration into the holistic framework of our best scientific theories. The entities do not need ontological rights to have determinate truth-values; they only need to play determinate structural roles within a system that is indispensably true.

**IV. Defending Normative Intervention Against Methodological Override**

A final, potent criticism leveled against the indispensability argument, and one that resonates deeply with Maddy’s naturalism, is that it improperly overrides established scientific and mathematical methodology. The charge is that the indispensability argument dictates what scientists and mathematicians *ought* to believe, effectively overriding the methodological norms that govern their actual practices. If set theory is not indispensable to the working mathematician, or if confirmation holism prevents us from isolating the indispensability of mathematics, then the indispensability argument improperly forces a metaphysical conclusion upon practitioners who are simply going about their methodological business.

Compromise naturalism must defend the normative intervention of indispensability theory against this charge. The defense hinges on a careful distinction between methodological norms and epistemic norms, and on the specific way compromise naturalism scopes the indispensability argument.

First, the indispensability argument does not override the methodology of scientific practice; it is derived from it. The methodology of science, as understood by naturalists, involves the acceptance of our best empirical theories as approximately true. The indispensability argument simply points out a consequence of this methodology: if our best empirical theories are true, and they are inseparable from their mathematical formulations, then the mathematical formulations must also be accepted as true. The argument is not an external imposition on scientific methodology; it is an internal articulation of the rational constraints that methodology places on belief. When a scientist uses a mathematical model to predict an experimental outcome, the methodology of science demands that the scientist take the model seriously. Compromise naturalism argues that taking the model seriously entails taking the mathematical truths it employs as true. This is not an override of methodology; it is the logical extension of the scientific norm of theoretical acceptance.

Second, compromise naturalism explicitly limits the normative scope of the argument by separating realism from ontological commitment. The charge of overriding methodology often assumes that the indispensability argument forces a specific *metaphysical* picture onto the practitioner—that the mathematician must now regard sets as physically existing objects. Compromise naturalism rejects this. By severing realism from ontological commitment, the argument only demands that the practitioner accept the *truth* of the mathematical statements, not their *existence* in a Quinean sense. The working mathematician can continue to practice as they always have, treating mathematical objects as useful fictions or structural positions, while the philosopher notes that, epistemically, the truth of those objects is rationally mandated by their indispensability to science. The normative intervention is thus confined to the domain of rational belief, not practical methodology. The mathematician is not told how to do mathematics; they are told what they are rationally entitled to believe about the mathematics they do.

Furthermore, compromise naturalism addresses Maddy’s specific worry about set theory. Maddy argues that because mathematical practice does not require set-theoretic realism, the indispensability argument improperly overrides the methodology of mathematics by demanding we believe in sets. Compromise naturalism counters that the indispensability argument is not about the methodology of mathematics, but about the epistemology of our best scientific theories. Even if set theory is not indispensable to the working mathematician's daily practice, if our best scientific theories—holistically confirmed—require set-theoretic mathematics, then the rational constraints of our best science demand that we accept the truth of set-theoretic propositions. The override charge fails because it conflates the methodology of a specific discipline with the epistemological norms of inquiry as a whole. The indispensability argument intervenes at the level of overall rational belief, not at the level of disciplinary methodology.

Finally, the holism that Maddy invokes actually strengthens, rather than weakens, the normative force of compromise naturalism. If confirmation holism means we cannot isolate the indispensability of mathematics from the physics, then the rational response is to accept the entire package of our best theory—including its mathematical components—as true. To reject the mathematics is to reject our best scientific theory, which violates the naturalist's methodological commitment to empirical adequacy. Thus, the normative intervention of indispensability theory is not an improper override; it is the necessary consequence of maintaining rational consistency in the face of holistic confirmation. We are not overriding the methodology of science; we are obeying it.
