# Aesthetic Universality and Environmental Obligation: Kant, Faulconer, and the Problem of Arbitrary Policy

Matthew Faulconer’s argument that Kant’s philosophy of aesthetic judgment can provide a foundation for environmental obligations addresses one of the most persistent difficulties in Kantian ethics. Kant’s moral philosophy appears to restrict genuine moral duties to relations among rational beings. The categorical imperative commands us to treat humanity, whether in ourselves or in others, always as an end and never merely as a means. Since animals, ecosystems, species, and natural objects are not rational agents, they do not seem to possess the kind of intrinsic moral standing that would make them direct objects of duty. Kant’s official position therefore appears anthropocentric: human beings have direct duties to one another, while our duties concerning nature are at most indirect duties grounded in duties to humanity. We should not be cruel to animals, for example, because cruelty can damage our humanity and make us less capable of treating persons morally.

Faulconer’s appeal to Kantian aesthetics seeks to unsettle this conclusion without simply abandoning the categorical imperative. Judgments of beauty, on Kant’s account, are neither merely private preferences nor ordinary cognitive judgments. When one judges an object beautiful, one claims that others ought to agree, even though one cannot prove the judgment by means of a determinate concept. Aesthetic judgment therefore possesses a distinctive form of universality. It also presents nature as purposive without assigning it a determinate external purpose. This structure may seem to offer a way of valuing nature that is neither reducible to human utility nor dependent upon attributing rational agency to natural entities. Nature can matter to us as beautiful, and the universality implicit in aesthetic judgment may appear to generate a norm against its destruction.

The promise of this argument is substantial. It offers an alternative to utilitarian environmental ethics, with its controversial calculations of aggregate consequences, and to a strong rights-based environmental ethics, which faces the metaphysical difficulty of explaining how nonrational entities can bear rights. Yet the argument also contains a serious dialectical tension. If nature lacks inherent moral value within Kant’s moral philosophy, then aesthetic value may remain a value for rational subjects rather than a value belonging to nature itself. And if aesthetic judgments demand universal assent, it does not immediately follow that environmental policies are universally obligatory. The universality of a judgment of beauty is not the same as the universality of a moral law, nor is either equivalent to a determinate policy rule. The specific objection that consequence-based environmental assessments are inherently arbitrary therefore cannot be answered merely by invoking aesthetic universality.

My central claim is that Faulconer’s aesthetic strategy succeeds in establishing an important noninstrumental orientation toward nature and may ground duties of preservation in a limited, indirect, and regulative sense. It helps explain why environmental destruction can be wrong even when no immediate human interest is demonstrably harmed. It also supplies a morally significant standard of judgment that is not identical with preference or economic utility. Nevertheless, the transition from aesthetic universality to moral obligation does not, by itself, resolve the problem of nonarbitrary environmental decision-making. Aesthetic judgment can constrain and criticize environmental policy, but it cannot determine policy without the assistance of moral principles, empirical knowledge, and procedures for resolving conflicts among legitimate values. The strongest version of Faulconer’s position is therefore not that aesthetics replaces practical reason or consequence-based reasoning, but that it provides a necessary evaluative dimension that prevents environmental deliberation from collapsing into mere instrumental calculation.

## I. The Kantian Problem: Moral Duties and the Status of Nature

Kant’s moral theory is organized around autonomy. A moral agent is capable of giving the law to itself through reason. The categorical imperative expresses the form of this self-legislation: one must act only on maxims that one can will as universal law, and one must treat rational nature as an end in itself. The moral community, or “kingdom of ends,” consists of beings capable of legislating and recognizing moral laws. Rational beings have dignity because their value is not conditional upon their usefulness, desirability, or exchangeability. They are ends in themselves.

Nature, considered as a collection of nonrational entities, does not occupy this status in Kant’s ethical system. Plants, animals, landscapes, rivers, and ecosystems cannot legislate principles or bind themselves through reasons. They may be objects of human concern, but they are not moral persons. Kant’s well-known account of duties concerning animals illustrates the difficulty. Since animals cannot be ends in themselves in the strict moral sense, cruelty toward them is wrong indirectly: it cultivates dispositions that may lead us to mistreat human beings. The animal is not wronged as a bearer of a direct right; rather, the human agent is morally corrupted.

This view creates at least three environmental problems. First, it seems to make nature’s value dependent on human interests. A forest may deserve protection because people need clean air, because future generations will use its resources, or because exposure to natural beauty improves human character. But if the forest has no value apart from these human-related effects, its preservation appears ultimately instrumental. Second, the view seems unable to condemn environmental destruction when human welfare is not obviously at stake. If a species is exterminated without causing measurable harm to present or future humans, why would its destruction be morally wrong? Third, even where human interests are relevant, the Kantian framework may seem unable to specify how competing environmental policies should be assessed.

Faulconer’s use of aesthetics responds to the first two problems by focusing on a kind of value that is not equivalent to utility. Kant’s aesthetics is especially attractive for this purpose because it does not treat beauty as a property established by ordinary conceptual knowledge, nor as a merely private feeling. A judgment such as “this landscape is beautiful” arises from a feeling of pleasure, but it is not asserted merely as “I happen to like this.” The speaker presents the judgment as one that others ought to share. In this respect, aesthetic judgment contains a claim to universality.

At the same time, the pleasure in beauty is “disinterested.” To take pleasure in something aesthetically is not to desire to possess it, consume it, or use it for some external end. A beautiful river is not beautiful merely because it irrigates crops, generates electricity, raises property values, or offers recreational benefits. Aesthetic appreciation suspends these instrumental considerations. It attends to the form of the object and to the harmonious activity of the faculties in the perceiver. In the case of natural beauty, the object can appear purposive even though no determinate human or divine purpose is attributed to it. Nature seems ordered, expressive, or fitting without being reduced to a tool.

These features make aesthetic judgment appear to bridge the gap between subjective human experience and objective moral significance. It is subjective because it is grounded in feeling rather than in a concept that determines the object. But it is also universalizing because the subject demands assent from others. It is noninstrumental because the pleasure is disinterested. And it is directed toward nature without requiring that nature be rational. Perhaps, then, aesthetic appreciation reveals that nature can be valued in a way that is neither merely useful nor strictly moral in Kant’s original sense. If environmental obligations can be connected to this value, Kantian ethics might acquire a broader ecological reach.

## II. Aesthetic Judgment and the Claim to Universal Assent

The crucial concept in Kant’s aesthetics is not simply beauty but the peculiar normativity of a judgment of taste. A judgment of taste does not report a private psychological state in the manner of “I prefer chocolate to vanilla.” Nor does it subsume an object under a determinate concept, as in “this is a triangle” or “this plant belongs to a certain species.” It expresses pleasure in the free play of imagination and understanding, and it does so in a form that presumes a shared human capacity for such pleasure. When one calls an object beautiful, one speaks as if beauty ought to be recognized by others.

This “ought,” however, is unusual. It is not an obligation imposed by a moral law. One cannot compel another person to find a mountain beautiful by threatening punishment or by producing a deductive proof. Nor can one infer beauty from a set of sufficient and necessary conditions in the way one might infer the properties of a geometrical figure. The universal validity of taste is therefore not demonstrative or coercive. It is reflective rather than determinative. The subject seeks assent by inviting others to inhabit a common standpoint.

This distinction is decisive for evaluating Faulconer’s argument. Aesthetic universality can challenge the assumption that environmental value is simply a matter of individual preference. If a landscape is judged beautiful, the judgment is not adequately represented by saying that one person likes it. The judgment aspires to a shared human validity. Moreover, the object’s value is not reducible to its usefulness for the person who appreciates it. The beautiful object is encountered as worthy of attention in itself, even if “in itself” must be understood phenomenologically rather than metaphysically.

But aesthetic universality remains weaker and more indeterminate than moral universality. The categorical imperative commands categorically: it applies to every rational agent independently of contingent desires. A judgment of taste, by contrast, makes a claim to assent that depends upon the presumed common sense of human beings. It is a demand for agreement, not a law that determines conduct. One can coherently say, “You ought to see the beauty here,” but this does not yet mean, “You are morally forbidden to destroy this place.” The first statement belongs to aesthetic reflection; the second belongs to ethics.

Faulconer’s argument must therefore establish more than the existence of aesthetic universality. It must show why a failure to respect something judged beautiful is a moral failure rather than merely an aesthetic or cultural loss. One possible route is to claim that aesthetic judgment reveals a normative relation between human freedom and nature. In appreciating beauty, the subject experiences nature not as a mechanism to be manipulated but as something that solicits reflective attention. To destroy natural beauty for trivial gain may then manifest a disposition incompatible with rational self-legislation. It treats the world as nothing but material for arbitrary desire and thereby undermines the very capacities of reflection and judgment that make moral agency possible.

This argument has genuine Kantian resources. Kant himself connects aesthetic cultivation with morality. Beauty can symbolize morality because both involve a form of freedom from determinate inclination and a harmony that is not imposed by external coercion. Aesthetic culture may prepare the mind for moral feeling by developing receptivity, discrimination, and a capacity to take pleasure in forms not reducible to private advantage. An individual who destroys a beautiful environment for a momentary convenience may exhibit not merely bad taste but a defective relation to rational freedom.

Still, this line of thought appears to ground primarily duties concerning human character. The wrongness of environmental destruction would lie in what the action expresses about the agent: callousness, domination, greed, or incapacity for disinterested judgment. Nature remains the occasion for a moral judgment about human beings. This is not necessarily a defect. Indirect duties can be serious and demanding. But it does not establish that nature has value independently of its role in human moral development. The argument must either accept this indirect grounding or explain how aesthetic judgment discloses a kind of value that exceeds the subject’s own moral formation.

## III. The Dialectical Tension: Nature Without Inherent Value and Nature as Universally Valuable

The central tension can be stated as a dilemma. If nature has no inherent value because it is not rational, then aesthetic value appears to be conferred by the human subject. A beautiful forest is not morally considerable in itself; rather, rational beings experience it as beautiful, and the experience is connected to their capacities for judgment and moral agency. In that case, aesthetic environmental obligations remain anthropocentric, even if they are not narrowly utilitarian.

If, on the other hand, aesthetic judgment genuinely reveals that nature possesses value independently of human purposes, then Kant’s restriction of intrinsic value to rational nature has been revised. The argument would no longer be merely an application of Kant’s aesthetics within Kant’s moral theory. It would be a transformation of Kantian ethics into a view on which nonrational nature can be a bearer of value. That transformation may be philosophically defensible, but it cannot be presented as a straightforward deduction from the categorical imperative.

Faulconer’s position is most plausible if it occupies an intermediate position. Nature need not possess “inherent value” in the sense of being a moral person or a holder of rights in the same way as rational agents. Yet nature may possess a form of noninstrumental significance disclosed through aesthetic experience. The distinction between moral status and value is important here. Something can matter without being a subject of rights. A work of art, a historical monument, or a sacred place may deserve preservation even though it cannot make claims or participate in a moral community. Environmental obligations could similarly concern the protection of forms of natural beauty, integrity, and purposiveness without treating rivers or species as rational persons.

The question is whether such value is objective enough to support obligation. Aesthetic value is not merely arbitrary, but neither is it objective in the same way as a physical fact or a moral law. Kant’s account deliberately places beauty between subjectivity and objectivity. Beauty is not a property entirely independent of human faculties; it is also not reducible to private preference. It is universally communicable because human beings share the faculties involved in aesthetic judgment. This provides a basis for public reasoning, but not an algorithm for determining what must be preserved.

That indeterminacy may be viewed positively. Environmental ethics often confronts places and organisms whose value cannot be captured by a single metric. A wetland is a habitat, a source of flood control, a carbon sink, a landscape of beauty, and part of a cultural history. Aesthetic judgment allows us to acknowledge the irreducibility of such value. It resists the idea that every environmental decision should be translated into monetary or preference-based units. It also helps disclose the violence of certain forms of development: a quarry, highway, or dam may not merely alter the distribution of benefits but destroy a form of natural appearance that cannot be recreated once lost.

Yet indeterminacy can also generate the very arbitrariness that Faulconer seeks to avoid. People disagree about beauty. A landscape that one community experiences as sublime may strike another as barren or threatening. Cultural histories shape aesthetic perception. Industrial infrastructures can be beautiful to some observers, while apparently pristine environments may be experienced as inaccessible or meaningless by others. If aesthetic judgment cannot be proven by concepts, what prevents environmental policy from becoming a contest among elite tastes? The demand for universal assent may be more aspirational than actual.

Kant’s response would not be to deny disagreement but to distinguish between subjective caprice and reflective judgment. A person may revise a taste judgment through conversation, attention, and exposure to others’ perspectives. The fact that assent is not guaranteed does not make the claim meaningless. Nevertheless, public environmental policy must decide under conditions in which disagreement persists. The aesthetic claim “this wetland is beautiful and ought to be preserved” may be morally serious without being sufficient to settle whether preservation should prevail over housing, food production, energy infrastructure, or protection from disease.

## IV. The Objection from Arbitrary Consequences

The objection concerning consequence-based assessments is particularly important. Environmental policy is often defended or criticized by predicting consequences: economic growth, public health, climate stability, biodiversity, employment, energy security, or effects on future generations. The objection is that such assessments are inherently arbitrary. Predictions are uncertain, values are incommensurable, and the selection of relevant consequences depends on prior normative assumptions. If one counts only market benefits, a forest may be worth more as timber than as a living ecosystem. If one counts long-term ecological stability, the calculation changes. If one gives equal weight to future and present persons, still another result follows. There seems to be no neutral standpoint from which all consequences can be measured.

Aesthetic universality appears to offer a way out. Instead of asking which policy maximizes aggregate satisfaction, one asks which policy respects the universally communicable value of nature. This seems less arbitrary because it does not depend on assigning numerical weights to every consequence. The destruction of a unique landscape would be objectionable not because it produces a calculable deficit in welfare but because it violates a form of value that reflective judgment can demand others recognize.

There is force in this criticism of consequence-based reasoning. Environmental harms are often irreversible, diffuse, and distributed across generations and species. Monetary valuation can conceal rather than resolve ethical disagreement. A policy that preserves a wetland for its flood-control services may be justified when the wetland’s beauty is ignored, but the same policy may be abandoned if an engineered substitute appears cheaper. Consequentialism can therefore encourage substitution and commensuration where moral judgment should register loss. It may also permit the sacrifice of a minority community or a fragile ecosystem whenever enough aggregate benefits are projected.

However, the claim that consequence-based assessment is inherently arbitrary needs qualification. Not every non-aesthetic judgment is arbitrary, and not every aesthetic judgment is nonarbitrary. Consequences can be assessed through evidence, causal reasoning, public standards, and attention to uncertainty. A policy’s effects on climate, health, biodiversity, and human capabilities are not simply matters of taste. The fact that consequences are difficult to compare does not mean that all comparisons are equally reasonable. Nor can an aesthetic standard eliminate empirical questions. One must still know what a proposed policy will do to a landscape, how many people it will affect, whether damage is reversible, and what alternatives exist.

More fundamentally, the aesthetic alternative itself requires interpretation. Which features of a landscape are relevant to its beauty? How should aesthetic value be weighed against the needs of persons? Is the preservation of a beautiful forest obligatory when its preservation would expose a population to serious danger? Would a less beautiful but ecologically more resilient landscape be preferable? Aesthetic judgment cannot answer these questions without entering into practical reasoning about consequences, rights, justice, and human need.

The most defensible response is therefore not to contrast arbitrary consequences with nonarbitrary aesthetics, but to distinguish different kinds of arbitrariness. Consequence-based reasoning is arbitrary when it treats all values as commensurable preferences, ignores uncertainty and irreversibility, or allows powerful interests to define the relevant benefits. Aesthetic reasoning is arbitrary when it elevates private taste, excludes affected communities, or treats beauty as a license to preserve landscapes for privileged observers while disregarding human deprivation. Both approaches require norms that regulate their use.

Kantian moral principles can supply some of these norms. Policies must not treat persons merely as means. Communities cannot be displaced or exposed to serious harm simply because an aesthetically admired landscape is desired by outsiders. Decision procedures must be capable of justification to those affected. The interests of future persons cannot be dismissed merely because they lack present political power. These requirements do not yield a complete environmental calculus, but they rule out certain forms of arbitrary decision-making. Aesthetic judgment can then operate within a framework that protects both natural value and human dignity.

## V. From Aesthetic Universality to Moral Obligation

The transition from aesthetic judgment to moral obligation can take several forms, and they should not be conflated.

The first is a motivational transition. Aesthetic appreciation cultivates a disposition to care for nature. Someone who experiences a forest as beautiful is less likely to regard it as disposable material. Such appreciation can generate attachment, humility, gratitude, and restraint. It can counteract the technological imagination in which every natural form is merely a resource awaiting transformation. On this interpretation, aesthetics does not prove an obligation but helps form the moral subject capable of recognizing and acting upon obligations.

The second is a character-based transition. A systematic refusal to recognize beauty may reveal a defect in moral character, especially when destruction is undertaken for trivial or selfish reasons. Environmental degradation can express arrogance, greed, or indifference to the conditions of human and nonhuman life. Since Kantian ethics evaluates maxims and dispositions, a maxim such as “I may destroy any natural place whenever doing so serves my immediate convenience” may be rejected as incompatible with rational self-legislation. The aesthetic significance of nature helps reveal why such a maxim is morally objectionable.

The third is a maxim-based transition. One might formulate environmental maxims that incorporate the preservation of natural beauty or integrity: “I will not destroy irreplaceable natural forms merely for contingent advantage”; “I will preserve conditions under which natural beauty can be encountered by present and future persons”; or “I will use nature only in ways consistent with the continued possibility of its free appreciation.” These maxims can be tested for universalizability and for compatibility with the equal freedom of others.

This approach strengthens the argument because it does not claim that an aesthetic judgment itself is a moral command. Rather, it treats aesthetic experience as informing the content of maxims that practical reason then evaluates. The categorical imperative supplies the form of obligation, while aesthetics contributes material that Kant’s original account may have underdeveloped. Nature becomes morally relevant through principles of restraint and preservation that rational agents can will universally.

The fourth transition is a claim about public justification. Because aesthetic judgment demands universal assent, environmental decisions should not be based solely on private preference or economic power. A policy that destroys an irreplaceable landscape must be defensible to a community of rational judges, including those who do not directly profit from the destruction and, in a suitably extended sense, future generations whose capacity for aesthetic experience will be affected. The requirement of universal communicability imposes a burden of justification. It encourages policies that can be articulated in publicly shareable terms rather than concealed behind narrow interests.

Yet none of these transitions is a deduction in the strict sense. The move from “this landscape ought to be appreciated” to “this landscape ought to be preserved” depends on an additional premise. That premise might be that rational agents have a duty to preserve the conditions of valuable human judgment; that destruction of irreplaceable beauty expresses a maxim inconsistent with respect for humanity; or that universalizable agency requires a world not wholly subordinated to arbitrary human purposes. Each premise is defensible, but each extends or interprets Kant’s moral theory. Aesthetic universality alone does not entail moral prohibition.

This point is especially clear in cases where preservation conflicts with urgent human needs. Suppose a beautiful valley contains a mineral necessary for preventing a widespread disease, and no reasonable substitute exists. Aesthetic universality would still make the valley’s destruction a loss, but it would not establish that destruction is impermissible. Respect for rational beings may require accepting the loss. Conversely, suppose a development project destroys a landscape solely to increase luxury consumption while preserving a less damaging alternative. Here aesthetic considerations can reinforce the moral judgment that the policy is objectionable. The difference between these cases is not aesthetic value alone; it involves necessity, proportionality, justice, and the distinction between basic and trivial interests.

Thus, aesthetic universality may generate a presumption of preservation rather than an absolute duty. The more unique, irreplaceable, and publicly communicable the natural value, the stronger the presumption. Overriding it would require reasons capable of justification to all affected persons. This structure avoids both utilitarian reductionism and aesthetic absolutism. It also gives environmental policy a principled direction without pretending that every conflict can be resolved by a single rule.

## VI. Can Aesthetics Make Environmental Decisions Nonarbitrary?

The central evaluative question is whether the appeal to aesthetic universality successfully resolves the problem of nonarbitrary environmental decision-making. The answer is qualified: it makes decisions less arbitrary in one important respect, but it does not make them determinate or fully nonarbitrary.

It makes decisions less arbitrary by exposing the insufficiency of preference and price. A natural object can be valuable even when it is not profitable, and its destruction can be objectionable even when the short-term consequences appear beneficial. Aesthetic judgment also introduces the ideas of irreplaceability and integrity. The loss of a particular ecosystem may not be compensated by preserving another ecosystem elsewhere, just as the destruction of a particular work of art cannot be fully compensated by producing another attractive object. These concepts help environmental policy recognize qualitative losses that ordinary cost-benefit analysis tends to obscure.

Furthermore, the demand for universal assent encourages impartiality. An aesthetic judgment is not supposed to mean “this place should be preserved because it benefits me.” It invites a standpoint from which others can recognize the object’s value. Translated into political terms, this requires decision-makers to account for perspectives beyond those of owners, investors, or present consumers. The standpoint is not perfectly neutral, but it is broader than self-interest.

Aesthetic judgment can also serve as a critical standard. It may reveal when policy is being driven by arbitrary desire rather than necessity. If a forest is destroyed for a trivial convenience, the action appears disproportionate. If a community is denied access to a landscape that embodies its history, the injustice is not exhausted by lost recreation or income. If future generations inherit a world stripped of natural variety, their loss cannot be represented adequately by the market price of timber or land. Aesthetic experience makes these losses intelligible.

Nevertheless, the standard remains underdetermining. First, aesthetic values are plural. The sublime, the beautiful, the wild, the cultivated, the ordinary, and the culturally significant do not always coincide. A managed landscape may be aesthetically valuable even though it is not “untouched.” A technologically altered environment may be ugly from one perspective and expressive of human creativity from another. Second, aesthetic judgments can be socially unequal. Historically, privileged groups have sometimes protected scenic landscapes while displacing local populations or restricting their traditional practices. Aesthetic preservation can become a form of exclusion if the people who live in a landscape are treated as obstacles to its visual purity.

Third, environmental decisions concern more than landscapes. Climate change, soil degradation, ocean acidification, groundwater contamination, and the extinction of obscure species may have profound moral significance without producing an immediately compelling aesthetic experience. Aesthetic judgment is strongest where form and appearance are salient; it is less adequate for invisible processes and systemic risks. An environmental ethic cannot rely exclusively on what is beautiful or perceptually striking, since this would bias protection toward scenic places and charismatic species.

Fourth, policy requires institutional choices. Even if universal assent could be approximated, one still must decide who participates, how scientific evidence is incorporated, how uncertainty is handled, and what remedies are available when values conflict. Aesthetic universality is a norm of judgment, not a political constitution. It cannot by itself establish a procedure for democratic deliberation or a rule for allocating scarce resources.

For these reasons, the transition from aesthetics to obligation does not eliminate the need for consequence-based reasoning. It changes its status. Consequences should not be treated as the sole measure of value, but they remain relevant to determining what can be done consistently with respect for persons and nature. Kantian environmental deliberation might therefore adopt a plural structure: categorical prohibitions against treating persons merely as means; presumptive duties to preserve irreplaceable natural and aesthetic values; consequential assessment of risks, needs, and alternatives; and public procedures designed to make decisions justifiable to those affected.

This plural structure does not compromise the anti-utilitarian insight. The point is not to aggregate all values into one numerical total. Rather, consequences can function within constraints. Some actions are excluded because they violate rights or express impermissible maxims. Among the permissible options, foreseeable consequences help determine which policy best protects human freedom, ecological conditions, and opportunities for aesthetic and moral experience. In cases of uncertainty, the irreversibility of environmental loss may justify precaution. The result is not a calculation-free ethics but a nonreductive practical reason.

## VII. The Scope and Limits of Faulconer’s Contribution

Faulconer’s argument is most compelling as a reconstruction of what Kantian environmental ethics can contribute, not as a complete environmental theory. It shows that the apparent opposition between Kantian moral universalism and environmental concern is less absolute than often supposed. Kant’s moral theory restricts direct moral personality to rational beings, but his aesthetics reveals a mode of relation to nature that is noninstrumental, communicable, and normatively significant. This relation can support duties of humility, restraint, preservation, and public justification.

The argument also reveals why the question of environmental value cannot be settled by metaphysics alone. One need not decide whether a river is a moral person before recognizing that its destruction may be wrong. Moral significance can arise through the relation between natural objects, human judgment, and the conditions of a shared world. The fact that nature is not rational does not make it morally irrelevant. It means only that the form of obligation cannot be identical to the obligations owed directly to persons.

At the same time, the argument should not overstate what aesthetic universality accomplishes. The universal validity of taste is not the categorical imperative. It does not automatically generate rights for nature, establish a lexicon of environmental prohibitions, or settle conflicts between preservation and human necessity. If Faulconer’s proposal is interpreted as claiming a direct deduction from beauty to duty, it fails. The gap between aesthetic appraisal and moral obligation remains.

The proposal becomes stronger if it is understood dialectically. Kant’s ethics appears to deny nature intrinsic value; Kant’s aesthetics shows that nature can be encountered as purposive, meaningful, and worthy of disinterested appreciation. This does not simply overturn the moral theory, but it places pressure on its anthropocentric boundary. The experience of beauty reveals that human autonomy need not mean domination. Rational freedom can consist in responding appropriately to a world that is not made solely for human use. The subject’s autonomy is deepened, rather than diminished, when it recognizes limits to arbitrary choice.

Such recognition can ground an environmental ideal of non-domination. Human beings need not treat nature as a person in order to refrain from treating it as mere disposable material. A world entirely organized around consumption would impoverish not only ecosystems but the forms of attention, reflection, and shared judgment through which human freedom is realized. Environmental obligation therefore concerns both the protection of natural systems and the preservation of a world in which rational beings can encounter something other than their own projected purposes.

The danger is that this formulation may remain too human-centered. If nature is protected only because it sustains human moral and aesthetic capacities, then the theory may fail to condemn the destruction of entities that no human will ever encounter or appreciate. A strict Kantian may accept this consequence. A broader environmental ethic may not. Faulconer’s argument can be extended toward ecocentrism, but that extension requires a further philosophical step: the claim that natural processes or living systems have value not merely as objects of human judgment but as forms of organized life deserving respect. Such a claim is compatible with Kantian inspiration, but it is not contained wholly within Kant’s account of rational nature.

## Conclusion

Faulconer’s appeal to Kantian judgments of beauty offers a significant but limited foundation for environmental obligations. It successfully challenges the idea that environmental value must be either intrinsic in the strong sense of belonging to rational persons or instrumental in the narrow sense of serving human preferences. Aesthetic judgment discloses a third possibility: nature can be valued noninstrumentally within a shared human standpoint, even though it is not itself a rational moral agent. The disinterested pleasure of beauty resists possession and consumption, while the demand for universal assent resists the reduction of environmental concern to private taste.

This aesthetic universality also responds to the objection that consequence-based environmental policy is inherently arbitrary. It exposes the limits of cost-benefit reasoning, especially where environmental losses are irreversible, incommensurable, or concealed by market prices. It introduces standards of irreplaceability, integrity, proportion, and public communicability. In doing so, it makes environmental deliberation more critical and less vulnerable to the arbitrary preferences of powerful actors.

But it does not completely resolve the problem. Aesthetic judgments are universalizing without being determinate, and their claim to assent is not equivalent to a moral command. They cannot alone determine which environments must be preserved, how conflicts with human needs should be resolved, or how policy should address invisible ecological processes. Aesthetic values can themselves be culturally contested and politically abused. The transition from beauty to obligation therefore requires mediating principles concerning human dignity, universalizable maxims, justice, precaution, necessity, and the rights of present and future persons.

The most defensible conclusion is that aesthetic universality provides a necessary corrective rather than a complete substitute for consequential reasoning. It establishes that environmental decisions must recognize forms of value that cannot be translated into utility, and it cultivates the dispositions through which nature can appear as worthy of respect. Practical reasoning must still consider consequences, but it should do so within moral and aesthetic constraints rather than treating all values as items in a single calculus. Environmental decisions become nonarbitrary not when calculation is eliminated, but when calculation is subordinated to principles that respect persons, preserve irreplaceable natural value, and remain open to public justification.

Faulconer’s argument thus succeeds in expanding the moral imagination of Kantian ethics. It does not fully solve the problem of environmental obligation, but it shows why a morally serious relation to nature must involve more than concern for consequences. The preservation of nature is not merely a strategy for maximizing human welfare. It is also a demand to resist arbitrary domination and to maintain a shared world in which both human freedom and the reflective experience of natural beauty remain possible.
