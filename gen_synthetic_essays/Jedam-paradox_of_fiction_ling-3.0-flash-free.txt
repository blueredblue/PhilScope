**The Grammar of Fictional Emotion: Wittgensteinian Quietism and the Dissolution of Radford’s Paradox**

The experience of art frequently presents us with a profound philosophical puzzle. We weep at the death of Anna Karenina, we tremble at the sight of the shark in *Jaws*, and we feel a visceral dread when the shape of Michael Myers emerges from the shadows of a suburban home. Yet, we possess the simultaneous knowledge that these entities do not exist; they are merely ink on paper, pixels on a screen, or figments of a collective imagination. This tension between the genuineness of our emotional responses and the ontological status of their objects constitutes Colin Radford’s famous paradox of fiction. Radford’s paradox poses a severe challenge to our understanding of emotion, requiring that we account for how we can experience genuine, irrational emotions toward things we know to be non-existent. 

The traditional approach to resolving this paradox has often relied upon a metaphysical distinction between mind-dependent fictional states of affairs and mind-independent reality. By positing that fictional objects are merely mind-dependent constructs while emotional responses are calibrated to mind-independent reality, philosophers have attempted to isolate the source of the paradox. However, this essay argues that Radford’s paradox rests upon an untenable metaphysical distinction between these two domains, a distinction that fundamentally misconstrues the nature of both fictional objects and emotional grammar. By adopting Ludwig Wittgenstein’s use-theory of meaning and the framework of language-games, we can successfully dissolve the requirement that genuine emotional responses imply belief in the existence of their objects. Furthermore, Wittgensteinian quietism reframes emotional expressions toward characters such as Michael Myers not as irrational psychological states, but as rule-governed linguistic practices within specific contexts, thereby eliminating the very pseudo-problems that generate the paradox.

**I. Radford’s Paradox and the Untenable Metaphysical Distinction**

Radford’s paradox is typically formulated around the notion of emotional authenticity. When we engage with a tragedy or a horror narrative, our emotional responses feel immediate, visceral, and deeply personal. We do not merely intellectualize the plight of a fictional character; we feel sorrow, fear, or pity as if the stakes were real. Yet, the philosopher’s demand for consistency compels us to acknowledge that these characters do not exist in the mind-independent world. The paradox arises from the conjunction of two propositions: first, that emotional responses require belief in the existence of their objects (the belief condition); and second, that we do not believe in the existence of fictional entities. 

To resolve this tension, many philosophers have invoked the metaphysical distinction between the mind-dependent and the mind-independent. On this view, fictional states of affairs are entirely mind-dependent; they exist only insofar as they are conceived by the minds of authors, readers, and audiences. Mind-independent reality, conversely, consists of the actual, physical world governed by natural laws and devoid of intentionality. The paradox, on this account, arises because our emotional apparatus—the system evolved to navigate the mind-independent world—is being improperly deployed toward a mind-dependent domain. We are experiencing a genuine emotion (a real physiological and psychological state) directed at an object that lacks ontological solidity. 

This reliance on the mind-dependent/mind-independent distinction, however, is fundamentally untenable. The untenability of this distinction stems from its Cartesian origins and its subsequent failure to account for the public, rule-governed nature of cultural artifacts. Fictional entities are not merely private mental images or phantoms floating in individual consciousness; they are public, shared, and culturally embedded constructs. Michael Myers exists not merely as a subjective impression in my mind, but as a character defined by a specific cinematic tradition, a narrative structure, and a shared cultural understanding. To treat fictional states of affairs as purely mind-dependent is to commit a category mistake: it reduces a public, linguistic phenomenon to a private, psychological one. 

Moreover, the metaphysical distinction obscures the functional role of fiction. By treating fictional objects as ontologically inferior or merely mental, the traditional view forces us into a strained and ultimately unsatisfying explanation of emotional responses. If the emotion is genuine, then either the fictional object must be granted some form of existence (which violates our epistemic standards), or the emotion must be deemed counterfeit (which contradicts our phenomenological experience). The untenability of the distinction lies in its assumption that the grammar of emotion is strictly tethered to the grammar of ontological existence. It assumes that to feel something toward an object, one must believe that object exists in the mind-independent realm. Yet, this assumption is precisely what requires examination. The paradox does not expose a flaw in the nature of reality or the mind; it exposes a flaw in our philosophical understanding of the relationship between language, use, and emotional expression.

**II. The Use-Theory of Meaning and the Grammar of Emotional Expression**

Ludwig Wittgenstein’s later philosophy offers a radical departure from the metaphysical frameworks that underpin Radford’s paradox. In the *Philosophical Investigations*, Wittgenstein abandons the search for an essence of meaning in favor of an investigation into how language actually functions. His use-theory of meaning posits that the meaning of a word or a sentence is not determined by a correspondence to mind-independent objects, but by its use within a specific form of life. Language is not a mirror of reality; it is a tool, a set of activities embedded in human practices. 

Crucially, Wittgenstein introduces the concept of language-games to illustrate this point. A language-game is a specific context in which language operates according to particular rules, much like a game of chess operates according to the rules of chess. The language-game of giving orders is distinct from the language-game of reporting events, which is distinct from the language-game of telling jokes or expressing aesthetic appreciation. The meaning and function of an utterance are entirely dependent on the language-game in which it is deployed. 

When applied to the paradox of fiction, Wittgenstein’s framework successfully dissolves the requirement that genuine emotional responses imply belief in the existence of their objects. The demand that we must believe in the existence of a fictional character to feel a genuine emotion toward them is a confusion of grammar. It is the mistaken imposition of the grammar of the empirical language-game onto the grammar of emotional expression. 

In the language-game of empirical assertion, the verb "exists" functions as a predicate that distinguishes real entities from imaginary ones. When I say "Napoleon exists," I am making a claim about the mind-independent world that can be verified or falsified. Consequently, the grammar of belief and knowledge governs this language-game: to assert that something exists is to take a stance toward the world, to treat it as a fact. However, the language-game of emotional expression toward fiction operates under entirely different rules. When I exclaim, "I am terrified of Michael Myers," I am not making an empirical claim about the mind-independent existence of a physical entity. I am not asserting a fact about the world. Instead, I am participating in a practice of aesthetic engagement, signaling my reaction to a constructed narrative, and sharing in a communal experience of suspense.

The use-theory reveals that the word "fear" does not function identically in the sentence "I fear the bear" and "I fear Michael Myers." In the first instance, "fear" tracks a relation to a mind-independent threat; it presupposes the existence of the bear and is governed by the rules of self-preservation. In the second instance, "fear" tracks a reaction to an aesthetic stimulus within a shared context; it is governed by the rules of imaginative engagement and the conventions of horror cinema. To demand that the second usage imply a belief in the existence of Michael Myers is to misunderstand the use of the word "fear" in that specific language-game. The genuine nature of the emotional response is not contingent upon the ontological status of its object; it is contingent upon the rules of the language-game in which it is expressed. The use-theory dissolves the belief requirement by showing that it is an alien imposition, a confusion that arises when we mistake the grammar of description for the grammar of expression.

**III. Wittgensteinian Quietism and the Reframing of Emotional Expression**

Wittgensteinian quietism represents a specific therapeutic approach to philosophical problems. Rather than attempting to solve philosophical puzzles through metaphysical speculation or scientific reduction, quietism seeks to dissolve them by demonstrating that they arise from a misunderstanding of language. The philosopher, in this view, is not a theorist who constructs systems of reality, but a therapist who diagnoses and cures conceptual confusion by showing the proper functioning of words in their everyday contexts. Quietism instructs us to "look and see," to cease asking metaphysical questions that have no answer and instead to observe the grammar of our language.

Applied to the paradox of fiction, quietism reframes the entire discourse surrounding emotional responses toward characters like Michael Myers. The paradox insists that fearing a fictional character is irrational because it violates the principle that genuine emotions require real objects. Quietism dismisses this as a pseudo-problem generated by a grammatical confusion. When we say we fear Michael Myers, we are not expressing an irrational psychological state that contradicts our knowledge of reality; we are participating in a rule-governed linguistic practice within a specific context. 

Consider the context of a horror film. The rules of this language-game dictate how we respond to the unfolding narrative. When the screen darkens, the string music swells, and the shape of Michael Myers appears, the practiced response—screaming, gripping the armrest, holding one's breath—is not a delusion. It is a rule-governed move within the game of cinematic horror. The expression of fear in this context follows the grammar of aesthetic participation, not the grammar of survival. Just as a person cheering at a sporting event is not expressing a genuine belief that the athletes are engaged in a mortal combat, nor is the filmgoer expressing a delusional belief that Michael Myers is lurking in their basement. Both are participating in shared, rule-governed practices that allow for the expression of heightened states of engagement within a bounded context.

The quietist reframing strips away the irrationality that the paradox attributes to the emotional response. The paradox views the fear of Michael Myers as irrational because it violates the belief-desire model of emotion: one desires safety, believes a threat exists (falsely), and therefore experiences fear. But Wittgensteinian quietism reveals that this model is inapplicable to the language-game of fiction. In the context of the horror film, the rules do not require belief in the existence of the threat. The rules only require a certain kind of engagement with the narrative. The "fear" is not a misfiring of a cognitive mechanism; it is a meaningful expression that fits perfectly within the rules of the practice. 

Furthermore, quietism dissolves the notion that the emotional response is merely "make-believe" or "as-if." The language-game of fiction is not a game of pretending that Michael Myers exists; it is a game of reacting to him according to the conventions of the genre. The emotional expression is authentic because it is a genuine move in a genuine language-game. The authenticity of the emotion is not measured by the ontological status of its target, but by its appropriateness within the context of the practice. When we cry at a fictional tragedy, we are not crying over a non-existent person; we are crying as participants in the language-game of tragedy, following the rules that allow for the expression of sorrow in response to aesthetic forms.

**IV. The Collapse of the Metaphysical Distinction**

The success of the Wittgensteinian framework in dissolving Radford’s paradox necessitates a re-evaluation of the initial claim that the paradox rests upon an untenable metaphysical distinction between mind-dependent fictional states and mind-independent reality. The claim is correct in its diagnosis of the problem, but Wittgensteinian philosophy reveals that the untenability of the distinction is not merely a matter of metaphysical category errors; it is a fundamental grammatical confusion. 

The mind-dependent/mind-independent distinction assumes that the primary axis of reality is ontological—that the fundamental divide is between what exists "out there" and what exists "in here." By applying this distinction to fiction, we are forced to locate fictional objects on the "in here" side, treating them as mere mental constructs. But this placement is arbitrary and misleading. Fictional objects are neither mind-independent physical entities nor private mental images; they are public, intersubjective entities constituted by their role in shared language-games. 

The use-theory of meaning demonstrates that the distinction between mind-dependent and mind-independent is irrelevant to the functioning of language and emotion. What matters is not where the object "really" resides—in the mind or in the world—but what role the word or concept plays in a given form of life. When we say "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street," the truth-value of this statement is not a matter of mind-independent correspondence; it is a function of its use within the language-game of detective fiction. Similarly, when we say "I am afraid of Michael Myers," the truth or falsity of the emotional expression is not a matter of whether a mind-independent entity exists, but whether the expression is appropriate within the language-game of horror cinema. 

By abandoning the metaphysical distinction, we no longer view emotional responses toward fictional characters as misfirings of a cognitive system designed for mind-independent reality. Instead, we view them as expressions that follow the grammar of a specific linguistic practice. The mind-dependent fictional state of affairs and the mind-independent reality are not two separate realms between which the emotional response must awkwardly bridge; they are simply different contexts within which the same words and expressions operate according to different rules. The paradox vanishes not because we solve the metaphysical problem of how the mind can interact with non-existent objects, but because we recognize that the problem was never metaphysical to begin with. It was, and remains, a grammatical illusion.

**V. Conclusion**

Radford’s paradox of fiction exposes the profound difficulty of reconciling our intense emotional engagement with art with our rational knowledge of the fictional nature of its subjects. Traditional philosophy has attempted to resolve this tension by drawing a sharp metaphysical line between mind-dependent fictional states and mind-independent reality, only to find that this line generates more confusion than clarity. The distinction is untenable because it reduces public, cultural artifacts to private mental constructs and mistakenly assumes that the grammar of emotional expression requires ontological belief in its objects.

Wittgenstein’s use-theory of meaning and the framework of language-games offer a powerful alternative. By shifting the focus from the metaphysical status of fictional objects to the functional use of language within specific forms of life, Wittgensteinian philosophy dissolves the requirement that genuine emotional responses imply belief in the existence of their objects. Emotional expressions are not descriptions of mind-independent facts; they are moves within rule-governed linguistic practices. 

Wittgensteinian quietism further reframes our understanding of emotional responses toward characters like Michael Myers. It dispels the notion that fearing a fictional entity is an irrational psychological state, revealing instead that such fear is a rule-governed practice within the language-game of horror cinema. The quietist therapeutic approach dissolves the pseudo-problem of the paradox by showing that the confusion arises from applying the grammar of empirical belief to the grammar of aesthetic expression. 

Ultimately, the Wittgensteinian resolution demonstrates that the paradox of fiction is not a window into the strange metaphysics of fiction, but a symptom of a philosophical misunderstanding of how language and emotion function in our shared form of life. Once we recognize that the meaning of our emotional expressions is determined by their use within specific contexts, the tension between the genuineness of our feelings and the non-existence of their objects evaporates. We are free to weep for Anna Karenina, tremble at the shark, and fear the shape in the hallway, not because we have solved the metaphysical riddle of their existence, but because we have finally understood the grammar of our own emotional lives.
