**The Paradox of the Gaze: Evaluating Kant’s Subjective Universality in Aesthetic Judgment**

Immanuel Kant’s *Critique of Judgment* occupies a singular and paradoxical position in the history of philosophy. It is tasked with mediating between the deterministic laws of nature, explained by the understanding, and the teleological purposes of morality, governed by reason. Yet, its most enduring contribution lies in its account of aesthetic experience, specifically the concept of subjective universality. This concept presents an apparent oxymoron: how can a judgment of taste, which is rooted in the deeply personal and person-relative experience of pleasure, lay claim to universal validity? If I declare a sunset beautiful, I am expressing a private sentiment, yet I simultaneously expect others to agree. Kant’s philosophical achievement is to resolve this paradox not by collapsing the subjective into the objective, nor by relativizing the universal to mere individual caprice, but by constructing a rigorous normative framework grounded in the specific psychological and cognitive conditions of the judging subject. Through the requirement of disinterestedness and the non-conceptual free play between the faculties of understanding and imagination, Kant transforms private pleasure into a claim to universal validity. However, the tenacity of conflicting aesthetic judgments—such as the polarizing reception of a Picasso painting—threatens this account. Defending Kant’s subjective universality requires a careful distinction between actual psychological disagreement and the normative requirements of disinterested contemplation, ultimately revealing the radical implication that aesthetic judgments can theoretically apply to any object whatsoever.

The tension between person-relative pleasure and universal validity is the fundamental problem Kant seeks to overcome. Aesthetic judgments begin with the declaration, “I like this,” which is inherently subjective. Unlike a cognitive judgment of the “beautiful,” which asserts a property of the object independent of the subject, the aesthetic judgment is an expression of the subject’s feeling. If I find a wine delicious, my pleasure is contingent upon my physiological constitution and personal history; it is a dependent pleasure, tied to a specific end or need. If I am thirsty, the wine pleases me; if I am intoxicated, it does not. Such pleasures are person-relative and cannot serve as a basis for universal claims. Conversely, a purely objective judgment, such as “this triangle has three sides,” is universally valid but devoid of the pleasure or displeasure that characterizes aesthetic experience. The oxymoron of subjective universality is the demand that an aesthetic judgment, which is rooted in the private feeling of pleasure, must nonetheless be valid for everyone. Kant recognizes that if we ground aesthetic validity in the object itself (as objective beauties) or in the subject’s private desires (as mere gratification), we either lose the universality of aesthetic claims or reduce them to mere subjective preferences. The challenge, therefore, is to find a ground for universality that does not destroy the subjectivity of the pleasure, nor does it surrender the claim to validity.

Kant’s resolution begins with the concept of disinterestedness, which he identifies as the defining characteristic of aesthetic pleasure. To be interested, in Kant’s specific philosophical vocabulary, means to be determined by a desire or a need. When I am interested in an object, I relate to it as a means to an end; I desire its existence because it satisfies a private inclination or serves a practical purpose. The pleasure I derive from the object is contingent upon my particular state of need. In contrast, disinterested pleasure is the pleasure taken in the mere presentation of the object, independent of any desire for its existence. When I contemplate a beautiful landscape, I do not wish to possess it, consume it, or use it for any practical end; I simply want it to be present as a form of appearance. By stripping away all private interests and needs, the subject is liberated from the particularities of their empirical self. Kant argues that in this state of disinterested contemplation, the subject ceases to be an individual with specific desires and becomes a “typical human being.” The pleasure is no longer the pleasure of *this* person, but the pleasure of the rational subject in general. Because the disinterested subject represents the universal cognitive apparatus of humanity, the pleasure they experience is not merely personal; it is a sensation of the harmonious operation of faculties that all humans share. Thus, disinterestedness is the bridge that connects person-relative pleasure to universal validity: by making the judgment independent of private interests, the subject elevates their personal feeling into a claim that is valid for anyone capable of disinterested contemplation.

However, disinterestedness alone does not fully explain how the subjective feeling of pleasure translates into a universal claim. Kant requires a second mechanism: the non-conceptual free play between the faculties of understanding and imagination. To understand why this is necessary, we must first appreciate the nature of aesthetic cognition. Cognitive judgments are determinant; they subsume a particular under a universal concept. When I see a dog, my understanding provides the concept “dog,” and my cognition is determined by the rule that defines what a dog is. In such judgments, the imagination provides the intuitions, but the understanding dominates, forcing the particulars into conformity with the general rule. Aesthetic judgment, however, is not determinant; it is reflective. When I judge a painting beautiful, I am not subsuming the painting under the concept of beauty. Instead, I am seeking a concept for the particular intuition, but failing to find one that determines it. The beauty of the object lies precisely in this indeterminacy; it pleases us because it allows our cognitive faculties to operate freely. In the aesthetic experience, the imagination and the understanding enter into a harmonious free play. The imagination offers its manifold of sensible intuitions, and the understanding attempts to grasp them, but because the object is not conceptually determinate, neither faculty dominates the other. They harmonize, flowing into one another without friction. This harmonious free play is the objective ground of the subjective feeling of pleasure. Because this cognitive harmony is the product of the universal human faculties—imagination and understanding—it is inherently communicable. The pleasure I feel in this free play is the subjective sign that my cognitive faculties are operating in accordance with their universal nature. Therefore, I am warranted in claiming that anyone whose faculties are functioning properly will experience the same harmony and, consequently, the same pleasure.

This Kantian framework, however, faces a formidable objection: the reality of conflicting aesthetic judgments. If subjective universality relies on disinterestedness and the free play of faculties, how do we account for the fact that two people can look at the exact same object under the exact same external conditions and arrive at diametrically opposed aesthetic conclusions? The example of a Picasso painting is particularly instructive. One observer may find *Guernica* profoundly moving and beautiful, perceiving a harmonious, albeit chaotic, free play of forms that communicates a universal truth about the horrors of war. Another observer may find it an incomprehensible mess, a discordant jumble of forms that yields no cognitive harmony and only displeasure. If Kant’s account is correct, why does the second observer not experience the subjective universality of the first? Does the conflicting judgment not undermine the claim that aesthetic judgments can lay claim to universal validity?

To defend Kant’s account against this objection, we must rigorously distinguish between actual psychological disagreement and the normative requirements of disinterested contemplation. The actual psychological disagreement between the two observers of *Guernica* is a matter of empirical fact. It is shaped by a multitude of contingent factors: cultural conditioning, art historical knowledge, personal trauma, and psychological temperament. One observer may have been trained in modernist abstraction, allowing their imagination to harmonize with the fragmented forms of Picasso; another may be deeply invested in representational art, meaning their understanding demands determinate concepts that the painting cannot provide. In the latter case, the observer is not making a purely aesthetic judgment; they are making a dependent judgment, tainted by practical or conceptual interests. They are interested in the painting serving as a recognizable representation of reality, and because it fails to satisfy this need, they experience displeasure. Their judgment is not disinterested.

Kant’s account of subjective universality is not an empirical description of what people actually do when they look at art; it is a normative account of what they *ought* to do, and what they *can* do under ideal conditions. The universal validity of an aesthetic judgment is a claim to validity under the “ideal” of disinterested contemplation. When the observer of *Guernica* finds it ugly, they are expressing their actual psychological state, which is contaminated by interests. Kant would argue that if this observer were to achieve a state of pure disinterestedness—if they were to suspend all practical interests, all demands for conceptual determinacy, and all personal preferences—they would find that the painting does, in fact, afford a free play between their imagination and understanding. The normative requirement is that if the judgment is to be genuinely aesthetic, it must be made from the standpoint of disinterestedness. Therefore, the conflicting judgment does not undermine subjective universality; rather, it reveals that the second observer has not yet met the normative criteria of the aesthetic attitude. The objection conflates the contingent psychological reality of disagreement with the necessary normative structure of aesthetic validity. The universal validity of the judgment is a claim about the ideal cognitive harmony that *should* be accessible to any rational being, not a guarantee that every empirical subject will effortlessly achieve it.

Furthermore, defending Kant’s account requires us to appreciate the profound implication that aesthetic judgments can theoretically apply to any object whatsoever. Because the ground of aesthetic validity is not an objective property of the object—such as a mathematical proportion or a Platonic form—but rather the subjective condition of the human cognitive faculties, the scope of the aesthetic is radically expanded. Any object can serve as the occasion for the free play of imagination and understanding, provided the subject adopts the requisite disinterested stance. A piece of driftwood on a beach, a shattered fragment of pottery, or a deliberately chaotic modern installation can all be objects of aesthetic judgment. The object does not need to possess an inherent "beautiful" essence; it only needs to be capable of being contemplated in a way that elicits a harmonious free play from the judging subject. This is the radical implication of Kant’s subjectivism: beauty is not a property of the world, but a way of relating to the world. The universality of the judgment rests entirely on the universality of the human cognitive apparatus. Since all normal human beings possess the same faculties of imagination and understanding, any object can theoretically be the focus of a universally valid aesthetic judgment, provided the subject suspends their private interests.

Evaluating Kant’s concept of subjective universality requires weighing its profound philosophical elegance against its practical limitations. On the one hand, Kant’s resolution of the oxymoron is masterful. By grounding universal validity not in the object, but in the universal structure of the subject’s cognitive faculties, Kant avoids both the naive objectivism of finding beauty "out there" in the world and the crude subjectivism of reducing beauty to mere personal taste. The concepts of disinterestedness and free play provide a sophisticated framework for understanding how aesthetic experience is simultaneously an individual feeling and a shared human capacity. The disinterested subject speaks not as a mere individual, but as a representative of humanity, and the free play of imagination and understanding is the very mechanism by which the subjective feeling of pleasure becomes a claim to intersubjective validity.

On the other hand, the Kantian account is vulnerable to the charge of being an unattainable ideal. The normative demand for disinterestedness is so stringent that it is questionable whether pure disinterested contemplation is ever actually achieved in empirical life. Our aesthetic experiences are almost always entangled with our interests, our cultural backgrounds, and our practical concerns. The defense of Kant’s account—that conflicting judgments merely prove the failure of the observer to achieve disinterestedness—risks making the theory unfalsifiable. If any disagreement is simply dismissed as a failure to be disinterested, the concept of subjective universality becomes a tautology: we agree when we are disinterested, and when we disagree, we are not disinterested. Moreover, the historical and sociological dimensions of aesthetic experience suggest that what counts as a "harmonious free play" is heavily influenced by cultural training. The observer who finds *Guernica* incomprehensible may not simply be failing to be disinterested; they may be operating within a different aesthetic paradigm that has trained their faculties to expect a different kind of harmony. Kant’s account, while powerful in its abstraction, may struggle to account for the pluralism of lived aesthetic experience.

Despite these criticisms, Kant’s concept of subjective universality remains a cornerstone of aesthetic philosophy precisely because it captures the essential duality of aesthetic experience. We do not experience beauty as mere private pleasure, nor do we experience it as a detached scientific observation. We experience it as a feeling that demands to be shared, that reaches out across the divide of individual subjectivity and claims a place for itself in the common world of human cognition. By positing that the ground of this shared validity lies in the universal human capacity for disinterested contemplation and cognitive free play, Kant provides a framework that respects the irreducibility of subjective pleasure while insisting on the rational communicability of aesthetic claims. The tension between person-relative pleasure and universal validity is not a flaw in Kant’s system, but its very engine. It is the friction between the individual and the universal, the personal and the normative, that drives the aesthetic act. In the end, Kant’s subjective universality is not a description of how we always agree, but an articulation of the ideal condition under which our private pleasures can aspire to become the shared property of all rational beings.
