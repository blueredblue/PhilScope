In Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, the concept of moral virtue plays a pivotal role in the attainment of human happiness, or *eudaimonia*. However, the interpretation of moral virtue's necessity in Aristotle's account of human happiness has been the subject of ongoing debate among scholars. The dominant intellectualist interpretation, exemplified by John Cooper, suggests that moral virtue serves as a necessary developmental prerequisite and practical foundation for theoretical wisdom, but is not an intrinsic constituent of the good life. In contrast, the inclusive view, defended by J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche, posits that moral virtue is an essential component of human happiness, operating in tandem with intellectual virtue to achieve the chief good. This essay will evaluate the necessity of moral virtue in Aristotle's account of human happiness, examining the tension between the inclusive view and the dominant intellectualist interpretation, and assessing the implications of each perspective for our understanding of human flourishing.

At the heart of the debate lies the concept of *teleion*, or completeness, which Aristotle employs to describe the chief good. According to Ackrill and Roche, the concept of *teleion* implies that the chief good is a comprehensive and inclusive notion, encompassing both moral and intellectual virtues. They argue that Aristotle's use of *teleion* in Book I of the *Nicomachean Ethics* suggests that the chief good is not solely intellectual, but rather a holistic concept that integrates both practical and theoretical aspects of human existence. This interpretation is supported by Aristotle's function argument, which posits that human beings have a unique function, or *ergon*, that consists in rational activity. Ackrill and Roche contend that this function is not limited to theoretical wisdom, but rather encompasses both practical and theoretical reasoning, thereby implying that moral virtue is an essential component of human happiness.

In contrast, John Cooper's dominant intellectualist interpretation suggests that moral virtue serves as a necessary developmental prerequisite and practical foundation for theoretical wisdom, but is not an intrinsic constituent of the good life. Cooper argues that Aristotle's account of human happiness in Book X of the *Nicomachean Ethics* prioritizes theoretical wisdom, or *theoria*, as the highest human activity. According to Cooper, *theoria* is a pure intellectual activity that transcends the practical concerns of moral virtue, and is the ultimate goal of human existence. Cooper's interpretation implies that moral virtue is merely a means to an end, facilitating the development of theoretical wisdom, but not an end in itself.

However, this interpretation raises significant concerns regarding the nature of human existence and the relationship between practical and theoretical reasoning. If moral virtue is merely a necessary developmental prerequisite, then it is unclear how mortal existence can reconcile practical reasoning with the pure intellectual activity described in Book X. Aristotle's emphasis on the importance of practical wisdom, or *phronesis*, in the earlier books of the *Nicomachean Ethics* suggests that practical reasoning is an essential aspect of human existence, and cannot be simply relegated to a secondary role. Furthermore, the concept of *eudaimonia* implies a sense of flourishing and fulfillment that is not limited to theoretical wisdom, but rather encompasses a broader range of human experiences and activities.

Ackrill and Roche's inclusive view provides a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between moral virtue and human happiness. By emphasizing the comprehensive and inclusive nature of the chief good, they highlight the importance of integrating practical and theoretical reasoning in the pursuit of human flourishing. This interpretation is supported by Aristotle's discussion of the mean in Book II of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, which suggests that moral virtues are not simply a matter of habituation, but rather require a deep understanding of the human condition and the ability to navigate complex practical situations. The mean is not simply a mathematical concept, but rather a nuanced and context-dependent principle that requires practical wisdom and moral virtue to apply effectively.

Moreover, the inclusive view is consistent with Aristotle's emphasis on the importance of friendship and social relationships in human existence. In Book VIII and IX of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle argues that friendships based on virtue are essential for human happiness, and that the cultivation of moral virtues is necessary for the development of these relationships. This suggests that moral virtue is not merely a means to an end, but rather an intrinsic constituent of human happiness, operating in tandem with intellectual virtue to achieve the chief good.

In conclusion, the necessity of moral virtue in Aristotle's account of human happiness is a complex and multifaceted issue. While the dominant intellectualist interpretation prioritizes theoretical wisdom as the ultimate goal of human existence, the inclusive view defended by Ackrill and Roche provides a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between moral virtue and human happiness. By emphasizing the comprehensive and inclusive nature of the chief good, the inclusive view highlights the importance of integrating practical and theoretical reasoning in the pursuit of human flourishing. Ultimately, moral virtue operates as an intrinsic constituent of the good life, rather than merely serving as a necessary developmental prerequisite and practical foundation for theoretical wisdom. As Aristotle himself notes, "the whole of life, then, should be lived in accordance with a single guiding principle, since this is what produces a unified and coherent life" (NE 1178a5-7). By reconciling practical reasoning with the pure intellectual activity described in Book X, Aristotle's account of human happiness provides a rich and nuanced understanding of the human condition, one that emphasizes the importance of moral virtue and intellectual virtue in the pursuit of *eudaimonia*.
