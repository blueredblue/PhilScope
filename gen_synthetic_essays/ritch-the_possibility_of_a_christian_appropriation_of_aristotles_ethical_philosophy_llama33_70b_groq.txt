In her extensive work on Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Martha Nussbaum presents a compelling interpretation of the philosopher's conception of human flourishing, or eudaimonia. Nussbaum's anthropocentric framework emphasizes the importance of practical moral virtues, species-relative goods, and the incommensurability of a plurality of ethical values. However, some critics argue that her interpretation misconstrues the relationship between the practical moral virtues of Books I–IX and the contemplative ideal of Book X, introducing a transcendent hierarchy that anticipates New Testament theological doctrines. This essay will evaluate Nussbaum's interpretation, assess the tension between human vulnerability and the pursuit of the divine life, and determine whether Aristotelian ethics can provide a rational foundation for Christian doctrine.

Nussbaum's interpretation of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics is rooted in her anthropocentric framework, which emphasizes the importance of human experience and the particularities of human existence. She argues that Aristotle's concept of eudaimonia is closely tied to the idea of species-relative goods, which are goods that are specific to human beings and are essential for human flourishing. According to Nussbaum, Aristotle's ethics is concerned with the cultivation of practical moral virtues, such as courage, justice, and temperance, which enable individuals to achieve eudaimonia. These virtues are developed through habituation and practice, and are essential for human beings to live a life of flourishing and fulfillment.

However, some critics argue that Nussbaum's interpretation neglects the importance of the contemplative ideal in Book X of the Nicomachean Ethics. In Book X, Aristotle appears to introduce a transcendent hierarchy, in which the life of contemplation is presented as the highest human good. This has led some to argue that Aristotle's ethics is not solely focused on human flourishing, but rather on the pursuit of a divine or transcendent life. This interpretation is often seen as anticipating New Testament theological doctrines, which emphasize the importance of spiritual contemplation and the pursuit of a divine life.

Nussbaum responds to these criticisms by arguing that the contemplative ideal in Book X is not a rejection of the practical moral virtues, but rather a completion of them. According to Nussbaum, the life of contemplation is not a separate or higher good, but rather the culmination of the practical moral virtues. She argues that the virtues of Books I–IX are essential for human flourishing, and that the life of contemplation is the highest expression of these virtues. In this sense, Nussbaum's interpretation emphasizes the continuity between the practical moral virtues and the contemplative ideal, rather than introducing a transcendent hierarchy.

One of the key concepts in Nussbaum's interpretation is the idea of incommensurability, which refers to the idea that there are multiple ethical values that cannot be reduced to a single, overarching principle. According to Nussbaum, Aristotle's ethics recognizes the complexity and diversity of human experience, and acknowledges that there are multiple goods and values that cannot be compared or ranked. This incommensurability is reflected in the idea of principles as provisional summaries of good decisions, which emphasizes the importance of practical wisdom and discernment in ethical decision-making.
