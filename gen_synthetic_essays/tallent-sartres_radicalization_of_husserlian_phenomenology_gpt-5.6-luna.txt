# Consciousness Without Substance? Sartre’s Radicalization of Husserl

Jean-Paul Sartre’s phenomenological ontology is often presented as a decisive liberation from what he regarded as the residual idealism of Edmund Husserl. Husserl had rejected the naïve metaphysical picture of a world existing independently of experience and had insisted that philosophy begin with the way things are given to consciousness. Yet, Sartre argued, Husserl did not carry this phenomenological revolution far enough. By retaining a transcendental ego and by assigning a constitutive role to hyletic data—sensory materials or contents—Husserl allegedly preserved a substantialist conception of consciousness. Consciousness, on Sartre’s view, must not be understood as a container, a subject behind experience, or a mental entity composed of contents. It is instead an empty, self-transcending activity: a “nothingness” whose being consists in disclosing what it is not.

This essay defends a qualified version of Sartre’s position. Sartre is right that consciousness should not be treated as a mental substance, and he successfully clarifies the non-substantial, relational, and self-transcending character of intentionality. Consciousness does not add a second realm of mental objects to the world; it is the pre-reflective openness through which an existent appears as meaningful. Nevertheless, Sartre’s criticism of Husserl is not wholly fair. He tends to interpret Husserl’s transcendental ego as though it were a hidden Cartesian soul, and he often treats hyle as though it were a self-standing psychic stuff. In Husserl’s *Cartesian Meditations* and *Ideas*, however, the transcendental ego is not simply a substantial entity located behind experiences, while hyle is not ordinarily an independent mental substance. Husserl’s analyses can therefore be read as an unfinished or differently oriented attempt to articulate precisely the non-substantial structure Sartre radicalizes. Sartre’s achievement is less the exposure of a straightforward Husserlian error than the transformation of transcendental phenomenology into phenomenological ontology.

The central question is consequently not whether Sartre eliminates a substantial ego that Husserl clearly and explicitly affirmed. He does not. The more important question is whether Sartre’s rejection of the hyle and the transcendental ego establishes that consciousness is an empty nothing rather than a substantial entity. The answer is yes, if “nothing” means that consciousness has no independent content, essence, or interior stuff apart from its intentional relations and projects. The answer is no, if “nothing” is taken to mean an absolutely featureless void. Consciousness is not a substance, but neither is it sheer absence. It is a structured activity of appearing, pre-reflective self-awareness, and transcendence toward the world.

## Husserl’s Phenomenological Reduction and the Problem of Subjectivity

Husserl’s phenomenology begins with a methodological demand: return “to the things themselves.” The phrase does not mean that philosophy should simply reproduce ordinary perception. Rather, it means that philosophical analysis should attend to phenomena as they are given, while suspending unexamined assumptions about their ultimate metaphysical status. In the natural attitude, one takes the world to exist independently and takes oneself to be an empirical psychological subject within it. The phenomenological epoché brackets this general positing. It does not deny the world; it suspends the question of its existence in order to investigate how world and objectivity are constituted in experience.

This procedure makes intentionality central. Every consciousness is consciousness of something: a perceived table, remembered friend, imagined landscape, desired outcome, judged proposition, or feared possibility. Consciousness is thus not first a thing that subsequently enters into a relation with objects. Its being is expressed in its directedness. The object is not simply copied into the mind; it is given through intentional structures, profiles, horizons, temporal syntheses, and modes of access. Phenomenology seeks to describe these structures without reducing them either to physical causation or to speculative metaphysics.

Yet Husserl’s language can appear to place a subject at the center of this field. In *Ideas I*, he distinguishes the empirical ego from the transcendental ego revealed through reduction. The empirical ego is a worldly human being with psychological traits and a location in nature. The transcendental ego is the pole of intentional acts, the subject for whom objects are constituted as meaningful and valid. Husserl sometimes says that the transcendental ego is not itself a phenomenon in the same manner as the objects appearing to it. It is the subject of experiences rather than one more object within them.

In the *Cartesian Meditations*, this problem becomes more explicit. Husserl presents transcendental phenomenology as a radicalized form of Cartesian inquiry. The reduction leads not to the empirical self but to the transcendental field in which objectivity is constituted. The ego is described as an identical pole of acts, a center of habitualities, and a substrate of capacities. Through genetic phenomenology, the ego is not merely the abstract “I think” of a single moment. It has a history: it acquires dispositions, establishes interests, sedimentations, and styles of response. It is also involved in the constitution of a shared intersubjective world.

From Sartre’s perspective, such formulations threaten to reintroduce an entity behind consciousness. If the transcendental ego is the subject of intentional acts, is consciousness not ultimately grounded in a subject that exists independently of those acts? If the ego is the pole of experiences, does phenomenology not end up positing an invisible spectator who owns mental states? Sartre’s answer is that it must not. Consciousness cannot contain an ego as one of its objects, nor can it require an ego as an inner cause. The “I” arises through reflection. At the pre-reflective level, consciousness is simply consciousness of its object and is implicitly aware of itself as consciousness of that object. There is no need to posit a transcendental ego standing behind the act.

Sartre’s criticism is developed most directly in his early essay “The Transcendence of the Ego,” but it governs the ontology of *Being and Nothingness*. The ego, he argues, is not inside consciousness but outside it, in the world. It is the unity of the subject’s actions, states, and qualities as they can be apprehended reflectively or intersubjectively. The ego is therefore a transcendent object, not the inner subject of consciousness. Consciousness is impersonal at its most basic level. It is not owned by an ego; rather, the ego is constituted as something that consciousness can come to apprehend.

This claim is not a denial that persons exist. Sartre is not saying that human beings are merely disconnected episodes of awareness. He is denying that personal identity must be grounded in a mental substance. The unity of consciousness is temporal, practical, and intentional rather than substantial. A person is unified through projects, commitments, memories, bodily existence, and relations with others. The ego can be real without being a metaphysical core hidden behind appearances.

## The Transcendental Ego: Misinterpretation or Radicalization?

Sartre’s rejection of Husserl’s transcendental ego has considerable force, but it also risks an unfair simplification. Husserl’s transcendental ego is not straightforwardly equivalent to Descartes’s thinking substance. Descartes discovers the *cogito* as a thinking thing, whereas Husserl’s transcendental ego is not necessarily a thing with independent properties that could exist outside its acts. It is the subject-pole of intentional life, disclosed through the reduction. In many passages, Husserl emphasizes that the ego is inseparable from its stream of experiences. The ego is not an occult causal mechanism producing consciousness from behind the scenes.

Moreover, Husserl’s ego is not merely an inert container. It is constituted through the temporal flow of experience and through the acquisition of habitualities. In the *Cartesian Meditations*, the ego is described as a pole of identity, but also as a subject that becomes what it is through its acts. Its identity is not fixed in advance. The ego has a “history” in the sense that its earlier decisions and experiences sediment into later possibilities. Such a view is already far removed from the picture of an immaterial substance possessing representations.

Sartre nevertheless has a legitimate reason for resisting Husserl’s language. Even if Husserl does not posit a Cartesian substance, the transcendental ego can appear to occupy a position that is functionally substantial. It is the ultimate subject of constitution, the pole to which experiences are attributed, and the center of synthesis. If consciousness is already self-aware and intentional in each pre-reflective act, then an additional ego-pole seems theoretically unnecessary. The ego risks becoming a redundant explanatory principle. Worse, it may encourage the mistaken view that consciousness is first a private inner domain and only subsequently directed toward the world.

Sartre’s alternative begins from the idea that consciousness is self-conscious without needing to become an object to itself. When I see a tree, I am not ordinarily attending to an inner state called “my seeing.” My attention is directed toward the tree. Yet my seeing is not unconscious in the sense of being entirely hidden from me. It is implicitly present as the very mode in which the tree is disclosed. Sartre calls this pre-reflective self-consciousness. Consciousness is consciousness of the tree, and in being consciousness of the tree it is non-positionally conscious of itself as such.

This structure explains why a transcendental ego is unnecessary. If the ego were required to make an experience mine, then the ego itself would need to be present either as an object or as an unexperienced owner. If it were an object, it would require another act of consciousness to apprehend it, generating a regress. If it were an unexperienced owner, its connection with the experience would be unintelligible. Pre-reflective self-consciousness avoids this dilemma. An experience is “mine” not because an ego possesses it but because it is intrinsically self-aware in its intentional directedness.

Sartre therefore radicalizes one of Husserl’s most important insights: consciousness is not a receptacle of mental contents but intentional activity. Yet he turns that insight against Husserl’s transcendental vocabulary. For Husserl, the ego remains the pole of constitution; for Sartre, the structure of consciousness itself is sufficient. The ego is an object constituted within the world of meaning, not the foundation of meaning.

Whether this is an unfair misinterpretation depends on what kind of disagreement is at issue. It would be unfair to claim that Husserl simply reintroduced a soul-substance after having eliminated naïve metaphysics. His transcendental ego is methodological and structural, not a hidden material or spiritual object. But Sartre is justified in arguing that the ego performs a role phenomenology can dispense with. The question is not whether Husserl’s ego is a substance in the traditional sense. It is whether the transcendental ego adds anything indispensable to the description of intentional life. Sartre’s answer is that it does not. His critique is therefore best understood as a radicalization rather than a mere refutation.

## Hyle and the Temptation of Mental Stuff

A parallel issue concerns Husserl’s account of hyletic data. Husserl distinguishes between the intentional or noetic aspect of experience and its hyletic content. Hyle refers to sensory material: colors, tones, tactile sensations, bodily feelings, and other data that are organized and apprehended through intentional acts. A perceived red apple, for example, involves sensory moments that are not themselves the apple as an intentional object. The apple is constituted through a perceptual act that interprets and organizes sensory appearances.

Sartre objects to treating hyle as a genuine ingredient of consciousness. If sensory data are understood as inner contents upon which consciousness imposes form or meaning, then consciousness becomes a container or a synthesizing faculty. The world would no longer be directly encountered. Instead, consciousness would first receive private mental materials and then construct objects from them. This picture threatens to produce the very representationalism phenomenology was meant to overcome.

Sartre’s objection is especially forceful against any interpretation of hyle as an independent psychic stuff. Suppose the visual experience of red contains a mental red datum that exists in consciousness prior to its being experienced as the surface of an apple. What is the relation between that datum and the apple? If the datum is the immediate object of awareness, then we do not perceive the apple itself but infer it from an internal representation. If the datum is not an object, then it is difficult to say what sort of entity it is. It cannot be known independently of the act that apprehends it, but neither can it be treated as nothing. The result is an ambiguous intermediate realm.

Sartre’s ontology eliminates this intermediary. Consciousness is not a passive receiver of sensory atoms. It is an intentional disclosure of the world. The red is red-as-the-surface-of-the-apple, red-as-signaling-danger, red-as-pleasing-or-objectionable within a practical situation. This does not mean that perception is arbitrary or that objects are invented. The object resists, exceeds, and constrains consciousness. But its appearing cannot be separated into a bare inner content plus a later act of interpretation. The sensory is already situated within a field of significance.

In *Being and Nothingness*, Sartre describes consciousness as a lack or nothingness in relation to being-in-itself. Being-in-itself is what it is, massive, self-identical, and without internal distance. Consciousness, by contrast, introduces a fissure or negation. It is not identical with the object it apprehends, and it is not identical with itself as a fixed essence. To be conscious of the table is to be the table’s not-being. This “not” is not a separate mental object. It is the distance by which the table can appear as an object for a perspective, an action, or a project.

The rejection of hyle is thus connected to Sartre’s broader ontology. Consciousness cannot be composed of positive elements because its being is relational and negative. It does not have a nature that precedes its engagements. Its contents are not pieces of mental matter; they are the ways in which being is disclosed from a situated standpoint. Even sensations are not private substances. They are modes of embodied consciousness, shaped by attention, practical relevance, bodily orientation, and the world in which the perceiver is involved.

Again, however, Husserl’s position is more subtle than Sartre sometimes allows. Husserl does not necessarily regard hyle as a mental object perceived internally. Hyletic data are often described as non-intentional components of experience. They are not themselves objects but materials that intentional acts animate or interpret. Husserl’s distinction is designed precisely to account for the difference between the sensory occurrence and the intentional object. A color sensation is not identical with the perceived color of the object. The latter is transcendent and appears through changing profiles; the former is an immanent moment of the act.

This distinction has genuine phenomenological value. It allows Husserl to analyze the difference between the sensory event and the object as perceived. It also helps explain why identical objects can be presented through different sensory modes and why perception involves synthesis. A cube, for instance, is never given all at once in perception. Its visible sides, tactile qualities, and possible appearances are synthesized into the unity of the cube. Hyletic data help describe the material basis of this synthesis.

The difficulty arises when the material basis is interpreted as a quasi-substantial inner content. Husserl’s analyses can invite such an interpretation, especially when he contrasts passive sensation with active intentional form. Sartre’s criticism is therefore not that hyle is an absurd concept but that it should not be granted ontological independence. Hyle may be a useful descriptive abstraction within the analysis of experience, but it is not a mental thing that exists before or beneath intentional disclosure.

On this point, Sartre offers a genuine correction. The sensory is not best understood as a neutral stuff waiting for consciousness to give it meaning. Meaning is not an added layer placed over an otherwise complete inner datum. Perception is embodied, situated, and world-directed from the beginning. Yet Sartre’s elimination of hyle should not be taken to deny the passivity, opacity, or bodily materiality of experience. Consciousness does not freely manufacture its appearances. It encounters a world that affects it and that it cannot simply replace with another. Sartre’s anti-hyletic position succeeds only if it preserves this receptivity without turning it into mental substance.

## Consciousness as Nothingness

Sartre’s most provocative thesis is that consciousness is “nothing.” This formula can be misleading if read as the claim that consciousness does not exist or that experience is an illusion. Sartre’s point is ontological: consciousness has a mode of being different from the being of objects. A rock is what it is. It does not stand at a distance from itself, question its identity, imagine alternatives, or reveal itself as situated within possibilities. Consciousness, by contrast, is characterized by internal negation. It is never simply identical with what it is at a given moment.

This nothingness has several dimensions. First, consciousness is a lack of substantial content. It is not a self-contained interior entity filled with representations. It exists only in relation to what it is conscious of. Second, consciousness is a power of negation. It can recognize that the absent is absent, that a person is not present, that the current state of affairs need not continue, and that it is not reducible to its past. Third, consciousness is transcendence. It projects itself beyond what is immediately given toward possibilities, goals, and meanings.

The famous analysis of bad faith illustrates this structure. A person may attempt to define herself entirely by a social role, a past decision, or a fixed character. Yet consciousness can never be identical with any such fact. The waiter is not simply a waiter; he is a consciousness playing the role of waiter. Conversely, one cannot deny all facticity and pretend to be pure freedom. Consciousness exists as a tension between facticity and transcendence. It is neither a substantial identity nor an arbitrary void.

Sartre’s account of nothingness is therefore not a theory of an empty mental chamber. Nothingness is productive and structured. It makes possible questioning, choice, imagination, refusal, and responsibility. Because consciousness is not a thing, it can take a position toward things. Because it is not identical with its past, it can reinterpret its past. Because it is not identical with its present situation, it can project possibilities. The emptiness of consciousness is the condition of its openness.

This conception supports the rejection of mental substance. A mental substance would be something that exists in itself, possessing determinate properties independently of its relations. Consciousness, however, has no independent mode of being apart from its intentional acts. It is not a substance that happens to perceive, imagine, and desire. It is perceiving, imagining, and desiring as modes of an existence that is always beyond itself. Its unity is dynamic rather than static.

Nevertheless, Sartre’s formula risks overstating the negativity of consciousness. Consciousness is not devoid of structure. It has intentionality, temporality, embodiment, affectivity, and pre-reflective self-awareness. It is not a blank non-being opposed to a fully positive world. Nor is being-in-itself entirely without organization or differentiation. Sartre’s duality between in-itself and for-itself is a powerful ontological contrast, but it can become too absolute if it suppresses the richness of embodied experience.

For this reason, “empty nothing” should be understood carefully. Consciousness is empty in the sense that it contains no mental substance and has no fixed essence that would determine its acts. It is not empty in the sense of lacking all internal articulation. Its structure is precisely the structure of openness to appearances. It is a nothing that functions, a lack that discloses, and a distance that makes relation possible.

## Pre-Reflective Self-Consciousness and the Appearance of the World

The strongest positive alternative to substantial consciousness is Sartre’s account of pre-reflective self-consciousness. Consciousness does not first exist as an unobserved inner state and then become aware of itself through reflection. Reflection is a secondary act in which consciousness takes an earlier act as its object. If all consciousness required a further act to become self-aware, an infinite regress would follow. There must therefore be a basic form of self-awareness internal to every conscious act.

Consider reading a philosophical text. At the pre-reflective level, consciousness is directed toward the meanings of the sentences. It is not thematically attending to “my reading experience.” Yet there is a difference between reading consciously and processing marks without awareness. The activity is implicitly present to itself. If someone asks what I am doing, I can answer that I am reading, not because I first observed an inner reading-state but because the activity was already self-manifesting.

This self-awareness does not require an inner ego. The reading is self-aware precisely as a directed activity. The “self” in pre-reflective self-consciousness is not a little person inside the mind. It is the non-objective self-presence of the act. Consciousness does not split into subject and object at this level. It is consciousness-of-an-object in such a way that it is also consciousness-of-itself, without making itself a thematic object.

This account clarifies Sartre’s claim that consciousness “adds” nothing to appearances. Consciousness is not an additional entity placed alongside the table, tree, or body. It is the appearing of the table, tree, or body from a situated perspective. This does not collapse the object into consciousness. On the contrary, intentionality preserves the distinction between appearance and that which appears. The object is never exhausted by a single appearance; it is given through a horizon of possible appearances. Consciousness is the openness in which this excess can be encountered.

The phrase “pre-reflective self-consciousness of an existent’s appearances” is therefore apt, provided that “appearances” are not understood as private images. Consciousness does not merely register an existent’s appearances as if they were internal photographs. It discloses the existent as meaningful, stable, resistant, usable, threatening, attractive, or otherwise significant. The object appears within a world structured by practical and affective possibilities.

Here Sartre’s phenomenological ontology diverges from Husserl’s transcendental idealism. Husserl asks how the unity and validity of the object are constituted through intentional synthesis. Sartre asks what kind of being must consciousness have if it is to disclose an object while remaining distinct from it. Husserl’s analysis is transcendental and constitutive; Sartre’s is ontological and existential. But the two projects share an opposition to the idea that consciousness is a substance containing representations.

## The Dialectical Tension Between *Ideas* and *Being and Nothingness*

The relation between Husserl and Sartre is best understood dialectically. Sartre depends on Husserl’s discoveries even as he rejects Husserl’s theoretical framework. Without intentionality, the epoché, the distinction between the natural and phenomenological attitudes, and the analysis of appearance, Sartre’s account of consciousness would lack its foundation. But Sartre believes that Husserl’s transcendental language prevents phenomenology from fully recognizing the ontological consequences of intentionality.

In *Ideas I*, Husserl’s reduction distinguishes the transcendental field from the natural world. The world is not abolished but investigated as constituted in correlation with transcendental subjectivity. The ego, however, remains the organizing pole of this field. Husserl’s formulations can thus suggest that the subject has priority over the world, not as a worldly cause but as the condition for the world’s meaningful disclosure.

Sartre resists this priority. He wants consciousness to be radically outside itself. If the world is constituted by a transcendental ego, then consciousness risks becoming a kind of absolute interiority, a source from which objectivity derives. Sartre instead emphasizes that consciousness is nothing apart from its relation to being. It is not the creator of the world but the disclosure of a being that precedes and exceeds it. In this respect, Sartre shifts from transcendental constitution to ontological relation.

The *Cartesian Meditations* complicate this opposition. Husserl’s transcendental ego is not a world-creating God. Constitution is not production. To say that the world is constituted in transcendental subjectivity is to say that its sense, unity, objectivity, and validity are articulated through intentional life. The world is not manufactured by the ego. Husserl explicitly analyzes the dependence of consciousness on passive synthesis, embodiment, temporality, and intersubjectivity. The transcendental ego is not omnipotent.

Still, Sartre’s concern remains significant. The transcendental reduction can make consciousness appear too detached from the very world it discloses. Sartre’s insistence on being-in-the-world, facticity, situation, and the body corrects this tendency. Consciousness does not float above existence and then constitute it. It is always already engaged in a world, affected by it, limited by it, and oriented toward changing it. In *Being and Nothingness*, intentionality is inseparable from practical projects. To perceive a chair is often to see it as sit-on-able, moveable, obstructive, or available. Consciousness is not primarily a spectator but an existent’s way of exceeding its situation.

The same dialectic applies to the ego. Husserl’s ego is meant to secure the unity of experience; Sartre argues that intentionality and temporality can perform this function without a transcendental owner. Husserl’s ego is not a substance; Sartre shows that it need not be posited even as a transcendental pole. Husserl preserves a structural center; Sartre disperses the center into the movement of consciousness itself. What Husserl treats as a pole, Sartre treats as an achievement or object of reflection.

Neither position is simply victorious. Husserl is stronger in analyzing the continuity, sedimentation, and historical unity of subjective life. Sartre is stronger in explaining why such unity should not be hypostatized into an inner entity. Husserl’s account guards against the fragmentation of experience; Sartre guards against turning unity into substance. The most defensible position preserves Husserl’s analyses while accepting Sartre’s ontological warning.

## Is Sartre’s Reading of Husserl Unfair?

Sartre’s reading is unfair in at least two respects. First, he sometimes treats the transcendental ego as though Husserl had posited a determinate entity behind consciousness. But Husserl’s ego is fundamentally a formal and functional principle of unity. Even where Husserl uses substantial-sounding language, his phenomenological method prevents the ego from being understood as an independently observable object. It is not a second mind inside the mind.

Second, Sartre’s treatment of hyle can suggest that Husserl believes consciousness is literally built out of inner mental objects. Yet Husserl’s distinction between hyletic data and intentional objects is intended as a descriptive distinction, not necessarily a metaphysical one. Hyle is a moment within the act, not an object that consciousness contemplates. Its status is admittedly difficult, but difficulty is not equivalent to substantialism.

However, Sartre’s criticism remains philosophically productive because Husserl’s distinctions carry a real danger. The transcendental ego can become a placeholder for whatever makes experience unified, without the analysis showing how this unifying function operates. If the ego is simply declared to be the pole of acts, the explanatory burden has not disappeared. Likewise, if hyle is treated as a non-intentional material that intentionality later animates, one may end up with an unexplained division between inert sensation and meaningful experience.

Sartre forces phenomenology to ask whether these concepts are necessary or whether they merely preserve inherited assumptions. His refusal of the ego is not based on the claim that Husserl is secretly a Cartesian; it is based on a structural argument. Consciousness is already self-aware in its intentional activity. An additional ego is therefore superfluous. His refusal of hyle is similarly motivated by the demand to describe experience without interposing mental contents between consciousness and the world.

The proper verdict is thus neither “Sartre misunderstood Husserl” nor “Sartre simply exposed Husserl’s metaphysical error.” Sartre interprets Husserl polemically, selecting the tendencies in Husserl’s system that support a substantialist reading and pushing them to their limit. This is an unfair interpretation as historical exegesis but a legitimate radicalization as philosophical development. Sartre’s Husserl is not always the whole Husserl; he is the Husserl whom Sartre must overcome in order to articulate his own ontology.

## Does Consciousness Add a Mental Substance?

The defended position can now be stated directly: consciousness does not add a mental substance to the world. It functions as the pre-reflective self-consciousness through which an existent appears, without itself being an additional existent of the same kind. This position does not entail that consciousness is unreal, epiphenomenal, or merely linguistic. Consciousness is real as a mode of being and as a condition of disclosure. But its reality is not substantial in the manner of a physical object or Cartesian soul.

The argument has four parts.

First, intentionality shows that consciousness is essentially relational. A consciousness wholly detached from all objects, possibilities, affects, or projects would not be a consciousness at all. Its being is not first established and then directed outward. It exists as outwardness. This makes it difficult to understand consciousness as a self-contained substance.

Second, pre-reflective self-consciousness eliminates the need for an inner owner. Experience does not become mine through its relation to an ego behind it. It is self-aware in its very enactment. Reflection can identify an ego, but the ego is then an object or interpretation within the field of consciousness, not the hidden source of the field.

Third, the rejection of hyle blocks the picture of consciousness as an inner container. Sensory life is not a stock of mental materials waiting to be organized into a world. The world is encountered through embodied, intentional, and situated activity. The appearance is not a private substitute for the existent but the existent as disclosed from a perspective.

Fourth, Sartre’s nothingness explains freedom and transcendence. A substance with a fixed essence would be what it is independently of its projects. Consciousness, however, can take a position toward its facticity, reinterpret its past, and project possibilities. Its lack of fixed substantial identity is not a defect but the basis of agency.

Husserl’s contributions remain indispensable to this conclusion. His analyses of intentionality, the constitution of objectivity, the temporal stream, and intersubjectivity establish that appearances are structured and that consciousness cannot be reduced to naturalistic objects. Sartre’s contribution is to prevent these structures from being reified into a transcendental substance. The result is a conception of consciousness as an activity rather than an entity.

The phrase “merely functions” should nonetheless be avoided if it suggests that consciousness is only a useful instrument. Consciousness is not a tool that an independently existing self employs. Nor is it merely a function in the technical sense of an operation performed by some underlying substance. Consciousness is the existent’s mode of relating to being. It is a mode of being-for-itself, distinct from being-in-itself but not separable from embodied existence. It is best described as an activity or relation whose being is to disclose and transcend.

## Conclusion

Sartre’s critique of Husserl succeeds in establishing a powerful negative thesis: consciousness is not a substantial inner entity. Neither a transcendental ego nor hyletic data should be understood as hidden mental stuff that exists behind the appearances of the world. Consciousness is not a container, a spectator, or a spiritual object. It is pre-reflective self-consciousness directed toward beings, possibilities, and projects. Its “nothingness” names the absence of fixed essence and the internal distance by which it can disclose, question, and transcend what is given.

Yet Sartre’s argument should not be confused with a fully accurate historical account of Husserl. Husserl’s transcendental ego is not simply a Cartesian soul, and hyle is not unambiguously a mental substance. Husserl’s own phenomenology had already eliminated naïve metaphysical realms and had identified intentional correlation as the basic field of philosophical analysis. Sartre radicalizes rather than merely corrects this achievement. He takes Husserl’s anti-representational insight and gives it an ontological, existential, and atheistic interpretation.

The dialectical result is that Husserl provides the descriptive resources, while Sartre exposes the danger of turning those resources into entities. Husserl explains how experience has unity, structure, and objectivity; Sartre insists that these cannot be grounded in a hidden subject-substance. Husserl distinguishes the materials and forms of experience; Sartre warns that such distinctions must not become a two-world metaphysics of inner contents and outer objects.

Consciousness therefore adds no mental substance to an existent’s appearances. It is the existent’s pre-reflective openness to those appearances, an intentional movement that allows beings to show themselves as more than any one presentation. It is “empty” because it contains no independent stuff and possesses no predetermined essence. But it is not a blank void. Its structure is the very activity of appearing, negating, projecting, and relating. Sartre is right to reject consciousness as a thing; Husserl is right to show that this non-thing has an intricate and analyzable order. The most adequate phenomenological ontology emerges by retaining Husserl’s descriptions while accepting Sartre’s demand that consciousness never be converted into a substantial entity behind experience.
