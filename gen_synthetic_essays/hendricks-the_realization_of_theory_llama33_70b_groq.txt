In Plato's *Republic*, the dramatic interaction between Socrates and his interlocutors, particularly Glaucon and Adeimantus, has led some to argue that the dialogue prioritizes the inculcation of virtue over metaphysical doctrine. This essay will evaluate this claim by analyzing how Socrates constructs theoretical models, such as the just city and the three-part soul, as pedagogical tools designed to shape the characters of his interlocutors. We will examine the tension between explicit statements regarding the ontological priority of theory and later passages where the text undermines this hierarchy, focusing on the role of word and deed in the realization of theory into imitative practice.

The *Republic* begins with a discussion of justice, and Socrates' interlocutors, Glaucon and Adeimantus, present their own definitions of justice, which are rooted in conventional wisdom. However, Socrates challenges these definitions and argues that true justice can only be understood through a deeper understanding of the human soul and the ideal city. To achieve this understanding, Socrates constructs the theoretical model of the just city, where justice is defined as the harmonious relationship between the three parts of the soul: reason, spirit, and appetite. This model is not presented as a discovery of transcendental reality but rather as a pedagogical tool designed to shape the characters of Glaucon and Adeimantus.

Socrates' use of the just city as a pedagogical tool is evident in the way he constructs the model. He begins by asking Glaucon and Adeimantus to imagine a city where justice reigns supreme, and then, through a series of questions and answers, he guides them in constructing the ideal city. This process is not meant to reveal an objective truth about the nature of justice but rather to lead Glaucon and Adeimantus to a deeper understanding of the concept. The just city is a theoretical model that allows Socrates to explore the relationship between justice and the human soul, and it is through this exploration that Glaucon and Adeimantus come to understand the importance of virtue and the need to cultivate it in their own lives.

The three-part soul is another theoretical model that Socrates uses as a pedagogical tool. He argues that the human soul is composed of three parts: reason, spirit, and appetite, and that justice is achieved when these parts are in harmony with one another. This model is not presented as a metaphysical truth but rather as a way of understanding the human psyche and the importance of cultivating virtue. Socrates uses the three-part soul to explain why people act unjustly, arguing that it is because the appetitive part of the soul is in control, rather than the rational part. This explanation is not meant to be a definitive statement about human nature but rather a way of encouraging Glaucon and Adeimantus to reflect on their own motivations and actions.

The tension between explicit statements regarding the ontological priority of theory and later passages where the text undermines this hierarchy is a central theme in the *Republic*. On the one hand, Socrates argues that the world of forms is the true reality, and that the physical world is only an imitation of this reality. This suggests that theory is prior to practice and that the goal of philosophy is to understand the eternal and unchanging world of forms. However, as the dialogue progresses, it becomes clear that this hierarchy is not as straightforward as it initially seems. Socrates argues that the just city is not a real city but rather a theoretical model, and that the goal of philosophy is not to understand the world of forms but rather to cultivate virtue and wisdom in the individual.

The role of word and deed in the realization of theory into imitative practice is also a central theme in the *Republic*. Socrates argues that words are not enough to bring about change; rather, it is through deeds that we can truly realize our ideals. This is evident in the way that Socrates encourages Glaucon and Adeimantus to reflect on their own actions and motivations, rather than simply accepting his arguments at face value. The *Republic* is not just a philosophical treatise but also a call to action, encouraging readers to cultivate virtue and wisdom in their own lives.

The question of whether the dialogue succeeds in abandoning metaphysical truth in favor of cultivating behavioral harmony through rational discourse is a complex one. On the one hand, the *Republic* does present a clear vision of the ideal city and the just soul, and it argues that this vision is rooted in a deeper understanding of the world of forms. However, as the dialogue progresses, it becomes clear that this vision is not meant to be taken as a metaphysical truth but rather as a pedagogical tool designed to shape the characters of Glaucon and Adeimantus.

Ultimately, the *Republic* suggests that the goal of philosophy is not to understand the world of forms but rather to cultivate virtue and wisdom in the individual. The dialogue presents a vision of the ideal city and the just soul, but this vision is not meant to be taken as a definitive statement about reality. Rather, it is a theoretical model designed to encourage readers to reflect on their own actions and motivations, and to cultivate virtue and wisdom in their own lives. In this sense, the *Republic* can be seen as a successful attempt to abandon metaphysical truth in favor of cultivating behavioral harmony through rational discourse.
