# Toleration, Public Reason, and the Limits of Multicultural Rights

Multicultural societies confront a difficult moral and political problem. They must preserve a common framework of equal citizenship while allowing individuals and communities to sustain distinctive languages, religions, customs, and forms of association. A liberal state that insists upon complete cultural uniformity risks suppressing legitimate forms of life. Yet a state that treats every cultural practice as immune from criticism may authorize domination within minority communities, especially the domination of women, children, sexual minorities, religious dissenters, and those who wish to leave inherited traditions. The question is therefore not simply whether cultural difference should be tolerated, but what kinds of cultural claims can justify legal accommodation and where toleration must end.

John Rawls’s idea of public reason provides a powerful framework for addressing this question. Public reason requires that coercive political power be justified by reasons that citizens who regard one another as free and equal can reasonably accept, despite their disagreement about religion, morality, and the meaning of human flourishing. This principle neither demands cultural assimilation nor permits groups to exempt themselves from basic liberal rights. It suggests that multicultural rights are legitimate when they secure the fair conditions of equal citizenship, compensate for structural disadvantage, or protect the social contexts within which individuals can exercise meaningful autonomy. They become illegitimate when they entrench unequal status, disable exit, or subject vulnerable members to coercive authority that cannot be justified to them as free and equal persons.

The instrumental value of particular cultures in fostering individual autonomy can therefore justify some special group rights, but only conditionally. Culture is often a precondition of autonomy because it supplies meanings, relationships, practices, and options through which individuals form and revise their life plans. However, this fact does not establish a right of cultural groups to preserve every internal practice. The relevant beneficiary of liberal protection remains the individual, not an unaccountable collective. In addition, Susan Moller Okin’s objection concerning the epistemological difficulty of determining genuine consent within patriarchal traditions exposes a serious weakness in simple appeals to voluntary cultural accommodation. Consent is not necessarily genuine merely because an adult formally agrees to a practice. Socialization, economic dependency, fear of ostracism, lack of alternatives, and internalized hierarchy may shape the person’s preferences before any apparent choice occurs.

The most defensible position is thus a qualified form of multicultural liberalism. The state should recognize cultures as important conditions of personal autonomy and should sometimes grant group-differentiated rights. But those rights must be assessed through public reason, subjected to scrutiny concerning internal power relations, and limited by a substantive floor of equal basic liberties, effective exit, non-domination, and the protection of vulnerable members. Cultural pluralism deserves respect, but not because communities possess an unlimited collective authority. It deserves respect because persons need cultural contexts in which to exercise freedom—and those contexts must themselves remain compatible with the equal freedom of the persons who inhabit them.

## Rawlsian Public Reason and the Structure of Toleration

Rawls’s political liberalism begins from the fact of reasonable pluralism. In a free society, citizens will inevitably hold divergent and often incompatible religious, philosophical, and moral doctrines. These disagreements are not merely temporary failures of education or rationality. They arise from what Rawls calls the “burdens of judgment”: differences in evidence, experience, moral priorities, and the relative weight people assign to competing considerations. Because no single comprehensive doctrine can reasonably be imposed on all citizens, political principles must be justified independently of any one controversial worldview.

Public reason applies especially to constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice. Citizens and officials should offer political reasons when deciding how coercive institutions are to be arranged. This does not mean that religious or cultural reasons are forbidden in public life. Individuals may of course argue from their faith or tradition. The point is rather that laws should ultimately be defensible in terms that citizens who do not share those commitments can understand as political reasons. Public reason is therefore a norm of reciprocity: citizens should explain the exercise of state power in terms consistent with the equal status of those over whom that power is exercised.

This idea has two implications for multicultural toleration. First, the state cannot reject a cultural practice simply because it is unfamiliar, non-Western, or inconsistent with the majority’s preferred way of life. Public reason demands reasons connected to political values such as basic liberty, equality, security, fair opportunity, and the protection of citizens from domination. A ban on a religious dress practice, for example, cannot be justified merely by an aesthetic preference for secular appearance. It would require a public justification grounded in some sufficiently weighty political concern.

Second, cultural claims cannot automatically defeat objections based on equality and liberty. A minority community cannot justify forced marriage, exclusion from education, or legal subordination of women simply by invoking its religious or cultural identity. The fact that a practice is central to a community’s self-understanding may explain why its members value the practice, but explanation is not the same as justification. When the state uses coercion to enforce or protect that practice, it must justify its action to all affected citizens, including those who bear the burdens of the practice.

Rawls’s distinction between the political and the comprehensive is important here. The state should not decide whether a cultural tradition is spiritually true, morally complete, or intrinsically superior. It should ask instead whether a proposed legal arrangement is consistent with a fair system of cooperation among free and equal citizens. A practice may be viewed as sacred by one community and misguided by another, yet still be legally protected if it does not violate the political rights of others. Conversely, a practice may be religiously meaningful and culturally central but still be impermissible if its institutional enforcement denies equal basic liberty.

Public reason thus establishes a presumption in favor of toleration without establishing an unlimited principle of deference to communities. The state should be cautious about coercive intervention because cultural and religious practices are often bound up with identity and social meaning. Yet the state also has a duty to preserve the basic structure of equal citizenship. Toleration is not a surrender of judgment; it is a disciplined refusal to use political power against difference except for reasons grounded in public justice.

## Culture as a Condition of Individual Autonomy

The strongest liberal argument for special multicultural rights is not that groups have mysterious or absolute rights independent of their members. It is that individuals require cultural membership to exercise autonomy meaningfully. Will Kymlicka’s influential account emphasizes that culture provides a “context of choice.” People do not choose from an abstract menu of options. They understand, evaluate, and pursue options through a language and social world that give them meaning. A person may formally possess freedom of religion or expression, but if the institutions and practices through which those freedoms become intelligible are destroyed, the person’s practical ability to choose may be seriously diminished.

This argument is compatible with Rawls’s emphasis on the social bases of self-respect. Self-respect is not simply a private feeling; it depends partly on being recognized as a competent and equal participant in social life. Members of stigmatized minorities may be denied self-respect if the majority treats their language, religion, or historical identity as inferior. Group-differentiated rights can therefore be justified when they protect the conditions under which minority members can participate as equals. Language rights, for example, may be necessary where a dominant language would otherwise exclude a minority from education or public institutions. Indigenous self-government may be justified not because indigenous communities possess unlimited sovereignty, but because historical dispossession and continuing institutional domination have deprived their members of control over the social conditions of their lives. Religious accommodations may protect citizens from having to choose between civic participation and fidelity to conscience.

Culture also gives individuals more than a set of choices. It offers forms of belonging, memory, solidarity, and moral orientation. A liberal theory focused exclusively on individual choice might underestimate the harm caused by the loss of a community. If a culture is weakened or stigmatized, the harm is not necessarily limited to the inconvenience of finding alternative customs. Individuals may lose relationships, narratives, practices, and identities that are central to their ability to understand themselves. In this sense, cultural membership resembles a primary good: it is a condition that enables people to develop and pursue diverse conceptions of the good.

Nevertheless, the instrumental argument must be formulated carefully. If culture is protected because it supports autonomy, then cultural practices cannot be protected at the expense of the very autonomy they are supposed to serve. A group right that preserves a language while expanding educational opportunity may be autonomy-enhancing. A group right that preserves a religious school while allowing students access to scientific education and avenues of exit may also be defensible. But a group right that authorizes elders to punish dissenters, prevents women from controlling property, or denies children the education necessary to become independent agents undermines the liberal justification for the right.

The distinction between external protections and internal restrictions is crucial. External protections shield a minority from the majority: for example, measures against discrimination, territorial protections, language rights, or political representation. Internal restrictions regulate the conduct of group members within the community, often in the name of preserving solidarity or authenticity. The former are generally easier to justify because they protect people against a dominant society. The latter are morally suspect because they may enable collective authorities to constrain their own members. A liberal state may give a minority community resources to maintain its language while refusing to give that community legal power to prevent members from leaving or criticizing its leaders.

The instrumental defense of culture therefore supports differentiated rights, but not collective control as such. The question is always whether a right expands the effective autonomy of persons, especially those vulnerable to marginalization, or whether it transfers power from the state to a cultural elite. Culture matters because it is lived by individuals. It cannot be treated as an independent organism whose survival overrides the rights of its members.

## Can Illiberal Practices Among Consenting Adults Be Accommodated?

The hardest cases concern practices that appear illiberal but are undertaken by competent adults who claim to consent. Consider a religiously grounded family arrangement, a gender-differentiated association, a community-based arbitration process, or a form of dress that symbolizes submission to a traditional authority. Liberalism should not assume that every departure from egalitarian norms is oppressive. Adults may choose hierarchy, discipline, dependence, or traditional roles. Autonomy includes the ability to organize one’s life around values that others regard as restrictive. To deny this would impose a single model of emancipation and would risk treating minority women, in particular, as incapable of judgment.

A person may rationally value religious obedience over individual self-expression, communal belonging over mobility, or traditional gender roles over occupational independence. Such choices can be shaped by culture without being invalid. Indeed, a liberal commitment to autonomy must protect not only the right to choose options approved by liberal elites, but also the right to choose ways of life that express nonliberal or partially illiberal values, so long as the person retains meaningful agency and equal legal status.

This consideration weighs against an aggressively interventionist approach. If every unequal relationship were prohibited, the state would have to police friendships, marriages, religious communities, and voluntary associations. It would risk confusing inequality of outcome with coercion, or disagreement with oppression. Moreover, intervention could make minority members more vulnerable by driving communities underground, stigmatizing their identity, or increasing their dependence on patriarchal leaders who present themselves as defenders against state hostility.

There are also cases in which a practice may be objectionable from a liberal perspective but permissible because adults can safely enter and leave it. A person might voluntarily join a monastic order, accept strict religious discipline, or participate in an association with traditional gender roles. The state need not guarantee that every voluntary institution reflects ideals of interpersonal equality. It must instead ensure that participation is not compelled, that legal rights remain intact, and that members have realistic alternatives.

Yet “consenting adults” cannot be treated as a magic formula. The legal category of consent is necessary but not sufficient for political legitimacy. A person’s apparent agreement may be produced by threats, deception, economic dependence, or the absence of alternatives. Even where there is no overt coercion, social institutions may shape preferences in ways that make refusal extremely costly. A woman who “chooses” an arranged marriage because refusal would result in homelessness, expulsion, or violence has not necessarily exercised the same kind of autonomy as someone choosing among secure alternatives. Nor is a person fully protected merely because formal law permits exit if exit means losing children, livelihood, housing, citizenship, or all meaningful social ties.

The relevant standard should therefore be meaningful consent under conditions of equal basic rights and non-domination. This does not require that all choices be made independently of social influence. No human choice satisfies that condition. It requires that individuals possess sufficient information, legal capacity, material resources, and social freedom to refuse or leave without facing impermissible forms of coercion. Consent is compromised not only by direct force but also by structured vulnerability.

A further issue concerns the difference between tolerating a practice and legally enforcing it. The state may permit adults to engage in an unequal religious or familial arrangement without giving that arrangement special legal force. A person may privately promise obedience to a religious authority, but the authority should not thereby acquire power to deprive the person of property, bodily security, education, or access to courts. This distinction enables a degree of cultural and personal freedom while preserving a common floor of rights. It is one thing for a person to live according to a demanding tradition; it is another for public institutions to make the person legally subordinate to the tradition.

Accordingly, some illiberal practices among adults can be accommodated, but only under demanding conditions. The practice must not violate basic rights, must not involve force or fraud, must leave participants with an effective right of exit, and must not impose significant external harms on nonparticipants. The state should also distinguish symbolic or associational inequality from material and legal subordination. A voluntary dress practice or religious role may be protected even if it expresses hierarchy. By contrast, exclusion from education, denial of legal personhood, forced marriage, and physical punishment cannot be justified by consent or culture.

## Okin and the Epistemology of Consent

Susan Moller Okin’s critique is especially significant because it challenges not merely the moral conclusion that patriarchal practices are acceptable, but the confidence with which liberals often identify them as voluntary. Okin argues that multicultural accommodations frequently take the community as the unit of analysis while overlooking inequalities within the community. Cultural defenses can allow male leaders to present themselves as authentic representatives of a group, while women’s interests are treated as secondary or as evidence of disloyalty. The language of cultural survival may conceal the preservation of male authority.

Her epistemological challenge concerns the conditions under which consent is formed. A person raised in a patriarchal tradition may learn from childhood that obedience is virtuous, independence is shameful, and resistance threatens family honor. If alternatives are systematically absent or condemned, can the resulting preference be regarded as genuine consent? The problem is not that socialization automatically invalidates choice. All preferences are socially shaped, including liberal preferences for autonomy and equality. The problem is that patriarchal socialization may deprive individuals of the conceptual, material, and relational resources needed to imagine or pursue alternatives.

This objection undermines a simplistic liberal procedure: ask whether the person consents, receive an affirmative answer, and then treat the matter as settled. The state often cannot know whether consent is free merely by observing a formal declaration. A person may deny coercion because she fears retaliation, because she has internalized the belief that her suffering is deserved, because she lacks knowledge of her legal rights, or because she cannot imagine a viable life outside the group. In some situations, the person may publicly defend a practice because group solidarity requires her to do so. The apparent consent of a representative may therefore be especially unreliable.

Okin’s argument also raises the problem of unequal voice. If decisions about cultural accommodations are made through negotiations between the state and male community leaders, women may not be represented in the process that determines their rights. A policy designed to respect the autonomy of a minority can thus strengthen the authority of those who already control the minority’s institutions. For example, exemptions from general family law may be requested by religious authorities but experienced by women as the loss of protections they had never been in a position to waive. The state’s deference to the group may produce precisely the internal domination that liberalism is supposed to prevent.

Still, Okin’s critique should not lead to the opposite error of assuming that minority women lack agency whenever they embrace traditional practices. Treating them as dupes of patriarchy can itself be paternalistic and culturally imperial. Women may attach real value to family roles, religious obligations, modesty, communal identity, or forms of solidarity that do not conform to dominant feminist ideals. A politics that recognizes only one path to freedom can silence the very people it claims to liberate. The epistemic difficulty is therefore symmetrical: the state may be wrong both when it accepts consent too readily and when it dismisses consent because the choice is culturally unfamiliar.

The proper response is not to eliminate consent from the analysis but to thicken it. Consent should be evaluated in relation to background conditions, not merely individual statements. Several considerations are relevant.

First, individuals must have knowledge of their rights and of available alternatives. A person cannot meaningfully choose if she has been prevented from learning that departure, education, employment, or legal protection are possible. Second, exit must be practically available. Formal legal freedom is insufficient if leaving results in destitution, loss of children, violent retaliation, or permanent social exclusion. Third, the person must have access to independent advice and institutions outside the group. Courts, shelters, schools, social services, and community organizations can give individuals the resources necessary to assess their situation. Fourth, the state must attend to patterns rather than isolated statements. If a practice consistently produces gendered dependency or makes dissent dangerous, the burden of justification should increase. Finally, those affected must participate in decisions about accommodation; the state should not rely exclusively on communal elites.

These requirements do not create a perfect test of genuine consent. No political institution can enter another person’s consciousness or determine with certainty whether a preference is authentic. But liberal law routinely operates under conditions of uncertainty. The impossibility of perfect knowledge does not justify either unrestricted deference or blanket prohibition. It justifies institutions designed to reduce epistemic error, distribute power, and preserve future opportunities for revision.

## Public Reason, Vulnerability, and the Protection of Internal Members

Rawls’s public reason can mediate between cultural pluralism and protection from oppression by shifting the central question. Instead of asking whether a practice is “really” cultural or whether the group as a whole approves of it, we should ask whether the legal accommodation can be justified to each citizen whose basic interests are affected. This perspective prevents the majority from imposing its comprehensive values, but it also prevents the community from treating its members as subjects rather than citizens.

The public-reason standard is particularly demanding when the state is asked to enforce an internal restriction. Suppose a community seeks authority to regulate marriage, inheritance, education, or dress through special legal rules. Such a request affects the distribution of power among group members. The state must then ask who benefits, who bears the risks, and whether those subject to the rule can reasonably regard themselves as equal participants in its justification. A defense based solely on the community’s collective identity is inadequate because the burdens fall on particular individuals, not on an abstract culture.

This does not mean that every public policy must reflect a complete theory of gender equality or personal independence. Public reason is not identical to the imposition of a comprehensive liberal perfectionism. Citizens may reasonably disagree about the virtues of family life, religious authority, sexual morality, and communal obligation. The state should preserve a zone in which people can pursue these ideals. But it must secure the political conditions that make disagreement noncoercive: equal basic liberties, legal personality, freedom of conscience, bodily integrity, political participation, and fair access to education and economic opportunity.

These conditions can be described as an anti-oppression floor. Below the floor, accommodation is impermissible even if a practice is culturally central and even if some participants express consent. The floor includes protection against violence, forced marriage, slavery-like dependency, arbitrary confinement, denial of education, and legal discrimination that marks one group of citizens as naturally subordinate. It also includes the right to criticize the group, to form relationships outside it, and to leave. These are not privileges of an individualistic culture; they are conditions of citizenship in a liberal constitutional order.

Above the floor, however, there remains substantial room for pluralism. Communities may maintain distinctive educational curricula, religious ceremonies, internal leadership structures, forms of dress, dietary practices, and family expectations, provided that members retain access to public education, legal remedies, and meaningful alternatives. The state may permit voluntary associations to be internally hierarchical without endorsing their hierarchy as a model for public law. It may accommodate practices that impose burdens on willing participants, provided that those burdens are reversible and do not make future autonomy impossible.

The principle of reversibility is especially valuable. A practice is less threatening to autonomy when an individual can reconsider and leave later. Children and adolescents deserve special protection because their capacity to evaluate inherited norms and secure independence is still developing. Parents may transmit a religion or culture, but they should not be able to close off the child’s future by denying basic education, isolating the child from public institutions, or arranging an irrevocable marriage. The state should preserve the child’s future capacity for autonomous judgment without demanding premature detachment from family and culture.

Public reason also supports a presumption against majoritarian suspicion. The majority cannot invoke women’s equality selectively to attack minority cultures while ignoring analogous forms of domination within the majority. If the state condemns forced marriage, it must protect all victims of forced marriage. If it protects access to education, it must do so without treating minority families as uniquely backward. Neutrality does not mean blindness to social context, but it does require consistency in the application of rights. Otherwise, multicultural policy becomes a vehicle for racial or religious stigmatization rather than a framework of equal citizenship.

## The Institutional Form of Multicultural Rights

Whether a group right is legitimate depends partly on its institutional design. Rights that give individuals resources and recognition are generally less dangerous than rights that give communal authorities coercive jurisdiction. A language right that funds bilingual schooling can expand individual options. A political representation measure can correct exclusion from democratic institutions. An anti-discrimination rule can enable minority members to participate without concealing their identity. These are external protections with an autonomy-enhancing rationale.

By contrast, legal exemptions that permit a community to deny members access to ordinary courts, education, property rights, or family protections require much stronger justification. Even arbitration or mediation systems that appear voluntary can become coercive if social pressure makes refusal impossible. Any alternative dispute-resolution system should therefore be genuinely optional, transparent, reviewable by public courts, and unable to waive non-negotiable rights. The parties should have access to independent legal advice, and the process should not presume that communal harmony is more important than the rights of the weaker participant.

The same reasoning applies to education. Cultural communities have a legitimate interest in teaching their language, history, religion, and traditions. But education is also the primary means by which children acquire the capacity to assess inherited practices and participate in society. A state may permit culturally distinctive schools while requiring instruction in literacy, civic rights, science, and the legal equality of all citizens. This is not an attempt to erase culture. It ensures that cultural transmission occurs without transforming children into permanent subjects of an authority they were never able to assess.

Special rights should also be sensitive to internal diversity. A cultural group is rarely homogeneous. It may contain secular and devout members, young and old members, women with different views, sexual minorities, converts, dissenters, and people of mixed identity. A policy that treats the group as a single corporate person may empower its most organized and conservative representatives. Rights should therefore be framed, where possible, as rights of individuals to maintain and participate in cultural life, rather than as unrestricted rights of leaders to govern others. Collective institutions may receive recognition, but their authority should be conditional upon internal accountability and compatibility with individual rights.

This institutional perspective also reveals why the instrumental defense of culture cannot support every demand for exemption. A group may claim that a practice is necessary for cultural survival, but survival is not a sufficient public reason if the practice preserves itself by preventing members from dissenting or departing. A culture that can survive only through coercive closure has a problematic relationship to autonomy. The state should support cultural vitality through resources, recognition, and equal participation rather than through the suppression of internal freedom.

## A Reconciliation of Pluralism and Equality

The apparent conflict between cultural pluralism and protection from oppression may be less absolute than it first appears. Cultures are not fixed entities whose integrity requires preserving every inherited rule. They are historical practices capable of reinterpretation. Internal critics, including women and younger members, often seek not the destruction of their culture but its transformation. A legal regime that protects their equality may enable a culture to become more inclusive rather than disappear. Conversely, a regime that empowers patriarchal authorities in the name of cultural survival may freeze the tradition in its most hierarchical form.

This observation supports what might be called an internalist approach to multiculturalism. The state should not presume that liberation requires abandoning one’s culture. It should ask how members themselves understand the relationship between their identity and their rights. It should support dissenters who seek reform within the tradition and avoid allowing communal leaders to monopolize the definition of authenticity. Consultation should include women, young people, sexual minorities, and those who have left or been excluded from the community. Their perspectives are not external threats to cultural understanding; they are evidence about the culture’s internal plurality.

Okin is right that the state must be wary of accepting patriarchal spokesmen as the voice of the group. But respect for internal critics does not require the state to decide in advance that cultural traditions are oppressive. The liberal task is to establish conditions under which members can debate, revise, and reject practices without fear of coercive retaliation. This is a more demanding and less paternalistic project than either uncritical accommodation or assimilationist prohibition.

Rawls’s idea of an overlapping consensus offers a useful political ideal here. Members of different traditions may endorse equal basic rights for different reasons. A religious community may support women’s legal equality because it interprets its faith as affirming equal moral worth. A secular feminist may support it because of autonomy and anti-domination. An indigenous community may support self-government together with internal rights because it understands authority as accountable to the people. The state need not settle the ultimate truth of these doctrines. It needs only a shared political agreement on the principles governing coercion.

Such an agreement can recognize that freedom sometimes requires protection from the majority and sometimes protection from one’s own community. These are not competing principles but two applications of equal citizenship. A minority’s right not to be assimilated protects its members from majority domination. An individual’s right to leave, dissent, and receive equal legal protection safeguards against domination by the minority’s internal authorities. Multicultural justice must hold both principles together.

## Conclusion

The limits of toleration for multicultural rights should be determined neither by a simple commitment to cultural neutrality nor by an unqualified commitment to individual autonomy understood as independence from tradition. Culture is often indispensable to autonomy. It gives individuals a language of self-understanding, a social world of meaning, and a range of choices that would otherwise be unavailable. For that reason, special group rights can be justified within a Rawlsian liberal order. They may protect minorities from majority domination, correct unequal participation, preserve the social bases of self-respect, and secure the cultural contexts in which individuals form and pursue their conceptions of the good.

But the instrumental value of culture places limits on the rights it can justify. If culture is protected for the sake of persons, it cannot authorize the systematic subordination of persons. External protections are generally more defensible than internal restrictions because they shield minorities from external power without transferring coercive authority to communal elites. Illiberal practices among consenting adults may sometimes be accommodated, particularly when they concern voluntary association, symbolic identity, or demanding religious commitments. Yet formal consent is not enough. Consent must be informed, noncoerced, supported by realistic alternatives, and protected by the possibility of exit.

Okin’s critique shows why this standard must be institutionally and socially informed. The state cannot simply ask whether a woman says she agrees to a practice while ignoring the patriarchal conditions under which that agreement was formed. At the same time, it must avoid the opposite paternalism of assuming that minority women cannot choose traditional ways of life. The appropriate response is a presumption of agency accompanied by safeguards against domination: independent advice, access to education and courts, economic security, meaningful exit, protection from retaliation, and participation of marginalized members in decisions concerning group rights.

Rawlsian public reason provides the normative discipline for this balance. Coercive accommodations must be justifiable to all citizens as free and equal, not merely to the organized representatives of a cultural community or to the majority’s comprehensive moral doctrine. The state should preserve a floor of equal basic liberties and non-domination while leaving considerable room for diverse forms of life above that floor. Its aim should not be to make cultures liberal in every respect, but to ensure that cultural membership remains compatible with citizenship, dissent, and personal revision.

The deepest principle is therefore reciprocal respect. The majority must respect minorities as authors of their own forms of life, while communities must respect their members as authors of their own lives within those forms. Cultural pluralism is legitimate when it enlarges the range of meaningful human possibilities. It reaches its limit when it becomes a justification for preventing persons from questioning, resisting, or leaving the social world into which they were born. A liberal society should tolerate difference not because every difference is beyond criticism, but because equal persons must be free to live together amid disagreement. That freedom requires both cultural belonging and the rights that make belonging genuinely voluntary.
