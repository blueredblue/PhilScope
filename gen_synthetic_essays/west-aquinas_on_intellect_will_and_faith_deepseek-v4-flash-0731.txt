## The Will’s Command and the Intellect’s Assent: Evaluating Aquinas’s Indirect Descriptive Volitionalism

The acquisition of Christian faith presents a profound philosophical puzzle. On the one hand, faith is an act of belief—an intellectual assent to a set of propositions, such as the Trinity, the Incarnation, and the Resurrection. On the other hand, these propositions are not self-evident, nor are they demonstrable through the natural light of reason. The evidence for them is, by the believer’s own admission, insufficient to compel the intellect. How, then, does a rational agent come to assent to such propositions? St. Thomas Aquinas offers a sophisticated answer: the will moves the intellect to assent. This account has been characterized as a form of *indirect descriptive volitionalism*—descriptive because it purports to explain the psychological mechanics of belief formation, indirect because the will does not directly cause the belief but rather commands the intellect to perform the act of assent, and volitionalist because it assigns a causal role to the will in the genesis of belief. This essay evaluates this claim, examining the philosophical machinery Aquinas employs, the epistemic tensions it generates, and the resolution he offers through the metaphysical identity of being and goodness. I will argue that while Aquinas indeed advances such a volitionalism, the charge of epistemic unjustifiedness and the absence of certainty are not refuted by a simple appeal to the will’s inclination toward the universal good. Rather, the dilemma is resolved only by recognizing that Aquinas’s account presupposes a teleological and participatory epistemology, in which the transformation of the human agent’s heart and desires—effected by grace—constitutes the ultimate justification for the intellect’s assent. This resolution, however, comes at the cost of abandoning any purely evidentialist standard of justification, replacing it with a moral and theological norm that is internally coherent but philosophically audacious.

### I. The Mechanics of Faith: The Will’s Indirect Command

To understand Aquinas’s account, one must first grasp his faculty psychology. The intellect is the power by which the soul knows truth; its natural operation is to assent to what is true. The will is the rational appetite, ordered toward the good. In the natural order, the intellect moves the will by presenting it with a good to be pursued; the will, in turn, moves the intellect to exercise its act by commanding attention or deliberation. This reciprocal causality is crucial for understanding faith.

In the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, q. 2, a. 1), Aquinas defines faith as an act of the intellect assenting to Divine truth, but he immediately qualifies this by noting that the intellect is not moved to assent by the object itself—that is, by the evidence of the propositions—but by an act of the will. He writes: “To believe is an act of the intellect assenting to the Divine truth by command of the will moved by God through grace.” This is the core of the volitionalist thesis. The intellect, when confronted with the articles of faith, does not see their truth with the clarity of first principles or the certainty of demonstration. The evidence is insufficient. The intellect, left to its own devices, would suspend judgment or merely opine. Yet the will, inclined toward the ultimate good (beatitude, the vision of God), commands the intellect to assent. This command is *indirect* in the sense that the will does not supply new evidence or alter the intellectual content; it simply directs the intellect to stop weighing evidence and to give its firm assent.

This is a *descriptive* account because Aquinas is not primarily prescribing how one *ought* to believe, but rather describing how faith *actually* occurs in the believer. He observes that the act of faith is a complex operation involving both intellect and will, and he seeks to map its psychological structure. The will’s role is not to fabricate the content of belief—that content is given by revelation—but to motivate the intellect to embrace it. Aquinas explicitly distinguishes faith from *scientia* (demonstrative knowledge) and from *opinio* (mere opinion). In *scientia*, the intellect is compelled by the evidence; in *opinio*, the intellect assents but with fear of the opposite; in *faith*, the intellect assents firmly, but not because of the evidence. The firmness comes from the will. Thus, faith is a mean between knowledge and opinion: it has the certainty of adherence (the will’s firmness) but lacks the certainty of vision (the intellect’s comprehension).

The motive for the will’s command is the promise of eternal life—the universal good that is God Himself. The will, being ordered to the good, naturally desires the highest good. When grace infuses the will with the theological virtue of charity, this desire is elevated, and the will moves the intellect to assent to the truths that promise this good. Aquinas states in II-II, q. 2, a. 1, ad 3: “The will moves the intellect to assent to what is proposed to it, because it sees that it is good to believe.” This is the essence of indirect descriptive volitionalism: the will perceives the *goodness* of believing (the attainment of beatitude), and on this basis commands the intellect to assent. The intellect does not perceive the *truth* of the propositions directly; it perceives the *goodness* of the act of believing them.

### II. The Epistemic Problem: Insufficient Evidence and the Charge of Unjustifiedness

The immediate philosophical objection to this account is that it seems to sanction epistemic irrationality. If the evidence for the articles of faith is insufficient, then assenting to them—regardless of the will’s motivation—appears to violate the norm that belief should be proportioned to evidence. This is the evidentialist challenge, articulated by John Locke and later by W.K. Clifford, which holds that “it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence.” Aquinas’s account seems to embrace precisely this wrongness: the will, driven by a desire for happiness, overrides the intellect’s natural caution.

Aquinas’s defense against this charge relies on the metaphysical identity of being and goodness. In his metaphysics, the transcendentals—being, goodness, and truth—are convertible. That is, every being is good insofar as it is actual, and every being is true insofar as it is intelligible. Goodness is not something extrinsic to being; it is being considered under the aspect of desirability. Truth is being considered under the aspect of intelligibility. Since they are identical in reality, the will’s inclination toward the good is not fundamentally opposed to the intellect’s inclination toward the true. In fact, they are two aspects of the same fundamental orientation of the soul toward reality.

Thus, when the will moves the intellect to assent to the articles of faith, it is not moving it toward a fiction or an illusion. The will is drawn to the *summum bonum*—the ultimate good, which is God. And since God is also the *prima veritas*—the first truth—the will’s desire for the good is implicitly a desire for the true. The will’s command to the intellect is not an arbitrary whim; it is a movement toward the very source of all truth. In this light, the assent of faith is not epistemically unjustified in the sense of being blind. It is justified by the will’s apprehension of the goodness of the object, which, due to the convertibility of the transcendentals, is a genuine, albeit indirect, apprehension of its truth.

However, this defense is not without its problems. The convertibility of being and goodness establishes a general harmony between the will’s pursuit of the good and the intellect’s pursuit of truth, but it does not establish that any *particular* proposition the will commands the intellect to accept is true. The will’s inclination toward the universal good is a general orientation, not a specific warrant for the truth of the doctrine of the Trinity or the Incarnation. One could desire the highest good and yet be mistaken about the specific propositions that purport to describe the path to that good. The will’s desire for happiness does not logically entail the truth of Christian revelation. It might just as easily lead one to accept a different religious system that also promises beatitude.

Aquinas is aware of this, and his response introduces the concept of grace. The will is not merely moved by its natural inclination toward the good; it is moved by God through grace. The infused virtue of faith is a supernatural habit that disposes the intellect to assent to the articles of faith, and the infused virtue of charity disposes the will to love God above all things. This supernatural intervention ensures that the will’s movement is not arbitrary but is directed by God Himself. The will is moved to assent because God, who is Truth itself, reveals these propositions. The motive for belief is not merely the general desire for happiness but the specific authority of God revealing. Aquinas writes in II-II, q. 1, a. 1: “The object of faith is something unseen, but the motive for belief is the authority of God, who reveals it.”

This introduces a further tension: the authority of God is itself an article of faith. One cannot appeal to God’s authority to justify belief in God’s authority without circularity. The believer is in the position of accepting the proposition “God reveals this” on the basis of the very revelation that is in question. Aquinas’s response is that the will, moved by grace, perceives the *fittingness* of the articles of faith—their congruity with the divine nature and the human condition—and this fittingness, combined with the desire for beatitude, suffices to move the intellect. But this is still a far cry from epistemic certainty. Aquinas readily admits that faith lacks the certainty of *scientia*. He distinguishes between the *certainty of evidence* (which faith lacks) and the *certainty of adhesion* (which faith possesses). The intellect adheres firmly to the articles of faith, but it does not see them. This absence of certainty is not a defect but a condition of the virtue of faith, which is, by definition, “the evidence of things not seen” (Hebrews 11:1).

### III. The Philosophical Tension: Direct Demonstration vs. Volitional Inclination

The philosophical tension at the heart of Aquinas’s account is stark. The intellect is the faculty of truth, and its natural mode of operation is to assent to what is evident. To override this natural mode with the will’s command seems to violate the intellect’s integrity. This is the dilemma of volitionalism: if the will moves the intellect to assent without sufficient evidence, then the intellect is being coerced into accepting something it cannot see; if the will does not move the intellect, then faith is not a rational act but a blind leap. Aquinas seems to embrace the former horn, but he does so with a crucial qualification.

The qualification is that the intellect’s natural capacity for demonstration is *inadequate* for the object of faith. The articles of faith concern God as He is in Himself—a reality that exceeds the natural power of the human intellect. The intellect is like an eye that can see physical objects but cannot see the sun directly; it can only see by the sun’s light. In this life, the human intellect cannot grasp the divine essence. It can know God only through creatures (natural theology) or through faith (supernatural theology). The natural light of reason is simply insufficient to comprehend the mysteries of grace. Therefore, the intellect’s failure to see the truth of the articles of faith is not a sign of epistemic failure but a sign of the *excess* of the object. The object is *supra-rational*, not *contra-rational*.

Given this, the will’s movement is not a violation of reason but a *transcendence* of it. The will, moved by grace, reaches out toward the good that the intellect cannot fully grasp. This is what Aquinas calls *connatural knowledge*—a knowledge by inclination or sympathy. Just as a virtuous person knows the goodness of chastity through a well-ordered appetite, so the believer knows the truth of the faith through a well-ordered will. The will’s love for God is a form of cognition, albeit a non-discursive one. It is a knowledge that arises from the transformation of the agent’s desires, not from the accumulation of evidence.

This resolution, however, generates a new problem. If faith is justified by the will’s inclination toward the good, then the justification is *agent-relative*. It depends on the state of the believer’s soul, not on the objective evidence. A skeptic who lacks the virtue of charity will not see the fittingness of the articles of faith, and thus will not assent. Does this mean that the skeptic is epistemically blameless? Aquinas would say no—the skeptic’s lack of faith is a moral failure, a failure of the will to respond to grace. But this is a theological judgment, not a philosophical one. The skeptic can retort that the believer has simply been psychologically manipulated by desire, and that the “connatural knowledge” is nothing more than wishful thinking. The convertibility of being and goodness does not rescue Aquinas from this charge, because it establishes a *metaphysical* harmony between truth and goodness, not a *psychological* guarantee that any particular desire is veridical. Desires can be misdirected; the will can pursue apparent goods that are not genuine goods. How, then, can the will’s movement serve as a reliable guide to truth?

Aquinas’s answer lies in the doctrine of grace. The will is not moved by its natural appetite alone but by a supernatural habit infused by God. This grace is not a natural desire but a divine gift that reorients the will toward its true end. The believer’s desire for beatitude is not a blind impulse; it is a response to God’s self-communication. The will is moved by the *authority of God*—the First Truth—to assent to what He reveals. This authority is not an article of faith that is believed on the basis of something else; it is the *formal object* of faith. The intellect assents to the articles of faith because it sees, through the light of faith, that they are revealed by God. This “seeing” is not the vision of the intellect but the *judgment* of the will, which perceives the credibility of the revealer. The will’s movement is thus a rational movement, but it is a rationality of the *practical order*, not the speculative order. It is the rationality of a person who trusts a reliable witness.

### IV. Resolving the Dilemma: The Transformation of Heart and Desires

The ultimate resolution of the tension between direct intellectual demonstration and volitional inclination lies in the moral and theological transformation of the human agent. Aquinas’s account of faith is not merely a description of a cognitive act; it is a description of a *conversion*. To believe is to be transformed. The will’s movement toward the universal good is not a one-time event but an ongoing reorientation of the entire person. This transformation has profound implications for both epistemology and morality.

First, the transformation of the heart resolves the epistemic problem by redefining the very nature of justification. Modern evidentialism assumes that justification is a property of propositions—a proposition is justified if there is sufficient evidence for it. Aquinas rejects this assumption. For him, justification is a property of persons—a person is justified if their cognitive faculties are properly ordered toward the truth. Faith is justified because the believer’s will is properly ordered toward the Good, which is the source of all truth. The absence of certainty is not a defect but a condition of the virtue. If faith were demonstrative, it would compel assent, leaving no room for free choice, love, or trust. The darkness of faith is what allows the will to exercise its love. As Aquinas writes in II-II, q. 4, a. 1: “It is a greater perfection to know the truth without seeing it, than to see it, because it shows a greater love for the truth.” The believer is like a child who trusts a parent’s word without fully understanding the reasons; the trust is not irrational but a higher form of rationality that acknowledges the limits of one’s own understanding.

Second, the transformation of desires has profound moral implications. Faith is not merely an intellectual assent; it is a moral act that reorders the believer’s loves. The will’s inclination toward the universal good is a movement of *conversion*—a turning away from created goods and toward the uncreated Good. This conversion requires humility (acknowledging one’s cognitive limits), obedience (submitting to God’s authority), and charity (loving God above all things). The believer’s desires are purified: they no longer seek happiness in wealth, pleasure, or power but in God alone. This moral transformation is not a consequence of faith but a constitutive element of it. The will does not merely command the intellect; it transforms the person. The believer becomes a new creation, one whose heart is set on things above.

Third, the theological implications are profound. Faith is a gift of grace, not a human achievement. The will’s movement is itself caused by God, who infuses the virtues of faith, hope, and charity. This means that the acquisition of faith is not something the human agent can produce by natural effort; it is a divine act that draws the soul to God. The indirect descriptive volitionalism is thus not a purely naturalistic account; it is a theological account of how God works in the soul. The will is moved by grace, and the intellect is elevated to assent to truths that exceed its natural capacity. The transformation of the heart is the work of the Holy Spirit, who pours the love of God into the believer’s heart (Romans 5:5).

The metaphysical identity of being and goodness, when combined with this transformative account, resolves the dilemma. The will’s desire for the good is not a blind impulse but a participation in the divine goodness. Since God is both the *summum bonum* and the *prima veritas*, the will’s movement toward God is simultaneously a movement toward truth. The intellect, moved by the will, is not being coerced but *elevated*. It is being drawn into a higher mode of knowing—a knowing that is not based on evidence but on love.
