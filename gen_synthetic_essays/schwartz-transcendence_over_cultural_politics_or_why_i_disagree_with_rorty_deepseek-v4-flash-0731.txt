## The Unspoken Ground: Rorty, Heidegger, and the Impossibility of a Purely Social Ontology

Richard Rorty’s philosophical project is one of the most audacious and seductive of the late twentieth century. It promises liberation from the twin tyrannies of representationalism and metaphysical foundationalism by collapsing the distinction between discovering truth and inventing vocabularies. In Rorty’s hands, ontology—the inquiry into what there is—is dissolved into cultural politics, the contest over which vocabulary we should adopt to make life better. Yet beneath this liberating surface lies a profound and unresolved tension: the very standard Rorty uses to adjudicate between vocabularies—human happiness, drawn from William James—smuggles in a teleological structure that his own anti-foundationalism cannot accommodate. Furthermore, when we examine the phenomenological foundations of social practice through Martin Heidegger’s categories, as interpreted by Robert Brandom and Thomas Sheehan, we find that the givenness of entities—the very condition for the possibility of any social practice—requires a *tertium quid* that transcends the ontological primacy of the social. This essay argues that Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics fails to avoid the necessity of metaphysical transcendence. While he successfully dismantles the classical notion of a transcendent object—a “God’s-eye view” or a mind-independent reality—he inadvertently relies on a structural transcendence: a normative *telos* (happiness) and a constitutive ground (*Ereignis*) that cannot be reduced to the immanent flux of social conventions. A rigorous analysis of Brandom’s pragmatist reading of *Being and Time*, contrasted with Sheehan’s phenomenological reading, reveals that the social is not self-grounding but is itself a derivative mode of a more primordial event of givenness.

### I. The Pragmatic Reduction: From Ontology to Cultural Politics

Rorty’s critique of traditional philosophy is rooted in a rejection of the “mirror of nature” metaphor. In his seminal work, he argues that the mind does not accurately represent an external world; rather, our beliefs are tools for coping with the environment. This anti-representationalism leads him to dismiss the very notion of “truth” as a property of propositions corresponding to reality. Instead, truth is “what it is good to believe” or, more precisely, what is justified within a given set of social practices. Consequently, the task of philosophy is not to discover the hidden essence of Being, but to engage in “edifying conversation”—to propose new vocabularies that enhance human solidarity and reduce cruelty.

In this framework, ontology is replaced by cultural politics. Questions like “What is the nature of justice?” or “What is the essence of being?” are recast as “Which vocabulary should we adopt to achieve our goals?” Rorty explicitly rejects any notion of a “background of all possible practices”—a transcendental condition that would constrain or ground the contingency of our social conventions. He insists that we are always already within a language, a tradition, a set of practices, and there is no “outside” from which to survey them. This is a radical historicism and nominalism: there are no natural kinds, no essential natures, only the contingent webs of belief and desire that we weave.

The strength of this position is its humility. It acknowledges the finitude and fallibility of human inquiry, freeing us from the illusion of a privileged access to reality. However, this strength is also its Achilles’ heel. If all epistemic and ontological authority reduces to social practice, then what grounds the authority of the social itself? Rorty’s answer is that nothing does—it is simply the way we happen to be. Yet this answer is profoundly unsatisfying, as it leaves the very notion of “practice” unexamined. What makes a practice a *practice*? What is the condition for the possibility of normativity? To answer this, we must turn to Heidegger, whose analysis of *Dasein* provides the most rigorous account of the structure of practice, and to the interpretations of that account offered by Brandom and Sheehan.

### II. Brandom’s Pragmatist Heidegger: The Social Constitution of Being

Robert Brandom’s reading of Heidegger in *Tales of the Mighty Dead* and *Reason in Philosophy* is a masterful attempt to domesticate the German philosopher for Anglo-American pragmatism. Brandom argues that Heidegger’s central insight in *Being and Time* is that our primary mode of engagement with the world is not theoretical contemplation but practical coping. This is captured in Heidegger’s distinction between *Zuhandensein* (readiness-to-hand) and *Vorhandensein* (presence-at-hand).

For Brandom, *Zuhandensein* refers to entities as they are used in our everyday practical dealings. A hammer, for example, is not first an object with properties (weight, color, shape) that we subsequently put to use. Rather, it is a piece of equipment that has a specific role in a web of social practices—hammering, building, repairing. Its “being” is constituted by its functional role within a normative space of reasons. The hammer is “ready-to-hand” when we are seamlessly using it, not consciously attending to it. *Vorhandensein* is a derivative mode, arising when our practical coping breaks down—when the hammer breaks, or when we step back to inspect it as a mere object. In this moment, the hammer becomes “present-at-hand,” stripped of its functional significance and reduced to a set of observable properties.

Crucially, Brandom argues that *Dasein*—the being for whom Being is an issue—is not a transcendental ego or a Cartesian subject, but the social community itself. *Dasein* is the “we” that engages in these practices. Its “being” is constituted by the norms and inferential roles that govern its social interactions. For Brandom, all authority—epistemic (what counts as true) and ontological (what counts as real)—is rooted in the social practices of giving and asking for reasons. The hammer’s “readiness-to-hand” is simply its being caught up in a network of social norms that determine what it is *for* and what it *means* to use it correctly. There is no “given” that is not already socially mediated; there is no raw datum that exists prior to our normative commitments.

Brandom’s interpretation is compelling because it provides a rigorous, inferentialist account of Heidegger’s categories. It successfully shows that the distinction between *Zuhandensein* and *Vorhandensein* is not a distinction between two types of entities but between two modes of engagement with the same entities, and that this engagement is fundamentally social. However, this reading is ultimately reductive. By equating *Dasein* with the social community and reducing the “being” of entities to their functional roles in social practices, Brandom collapses the ontological distinction between the *ontic* (the particular entities and practices) and the *ontological* (the meaning of Being itself). He fails to ask the question that Heidegger insists is fundamental: *What is the condition for the possibility of social practices as such?* What must be the case for there to be a “there” in which entities can show up as meaningful at all?

Brandom’s account is a form of social constructivism. It claims that the world is entirely a product of our normative practices. But this raises a classic paradox: if the world is entirely constructed by social practices, then what makes those practices possible? What is the background against which norms can be established? Rorty would answer that there is no background—practices are just there, historically contingent. But this answer is evasive. It fails to account for the *givenness* of the practices themselves. Why is there something rather than nothing? Why is there a social world at all? This is not a question about the existence of entities but about the *event* of their disclosure.

### III. The Tension Within Rorty: James’s Standard of Human Happiness

Before turning to Sheehan’s alternative reading, we must address the internal inconsistency in Rorty’s own position. Rorty’s rejection of a “background of all possible practices” is meant to foreclose any appeal to a transcendental ground. Yet, his political and ethical vision relies heavily on a specific normative standard: human happiness. This standard is explicitly derived from William James, who defines the true as “the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief.” For James, beliefs are true if they lead to satisfactory outcomes—if they fulfill our needs, satisfy our desires, and promote our flourishing.

Rorty adopts this Jamesian criterion but attempts to strip it of any teleological or metaphysical residue. He argues that “happiness” is not a fixed, trans-historical essence of human nature, but a contingent, ethnocentric preference. We, the liberal ironists of the West, happen to value the avoidance of cruelty and the expansion of freedom. We do not claim that these values are objectively true or universally valid; we simply assert them as our own, and we try to persuade others to adopt them. This is what Rorty calls “sentimental education”—the cultivation of empathy and solidarity through narrative and rhetoric, rather than through rational argument or metaphysical grounding.

However, this position is deeply unstable. If “human happiness” is merely a contingent preference, then it cannot function as a standard of critique. Rorty wants to say that the Nazis were wrong, not just that they were different. He wants to say that cruelty is bad, not just that we don’t like it. But if all values are reducible to social practices, then the Nazi vocabulary is just as valid as the liberal vocabulary—it is simply a different way of coping. Rorty’s appeal to “happiness” as a goal for social action implies that there is something *structurally* good about happiness, that it is a *telos* that transcends any particular set of practices. Otherwise, why privilege it? Why not privilege power, or domination, or aesthetic purity?

This is the classic problem of pragmatism: the criterion of “what works” requires a prior specification of what “working” means. If “working” means “leading to happiness,” then we need a theory of happiness. But Rorty refuses to provide such a theory, insisting that happiness is whatever we say it is. This leads to a performative contradiction. He uses the concept of happiness as a normative standard to adjudicate between vocabularies, but he denies that it has any normative authority beyond the mere fact that we happen to prefer it. In doing so, he inadvertently reintroduces a form of metaphysical transcendence: the idea that there is a human good—a structural end—that is not merely a product of social convention. He cannot have it both ways. He cannot reject a “background of all possible practices” while simultaneously relying on a standard (happiness) that presupposes a background of human needs and flourishing.

This tension is not merely a logical quibble. It has profound consequences for Rorty’s political project. If happiness is a purely contingent preference, then there is no reason to prefer a liberal society over a totalitarian one, except for the fact that we happen to be liberals. Rorty’s ethnocentrism—his admission that he speaks only for “us”—is honest but ultimately nihilistic. It leaves no room for genuine critique or for the possibility that our own practices might be deeply flawed. To escape this nihilism, Rorty must either abandon the standard of happiness or acknowledge that it functions as a *tertium quid*—a normative ground that transcends the immanence of social practice.

### IV. Sheehan’s Heidegger: *Ereignis* as the *Tertium Quid*

Thomas Sheehan’s interpretation of Heidegger provides the most powerful challenge to Rorty’s and Brandom’s social constructivism. Sheehan argues that the central question of Heidegger’s entire corpus—from *Being and Time* to the later works on *Ereignis*—is not “What is Being?” but “What is the meaning of Being?” This “meaning” is not a concept or an essence, but the very *givenness* of entities—the fact that entities show up as meaningful at all. Sheehan translates *Ereignis* as the “event of appropriation” or the “disclosedness” in which things are given to us.

In *Being and Time*, Heidegger analyzes *Dasein* as the being who is thrown into a world and who projects possibilities. *Dasein* is characterized by *Sorge* (care), which is the structure of its being-in-the-world. However, Sheehan emphasizes that *Dasein* is not the source of meaning. Rather, *Dasein* is the “there” (*Da*) in which Being (*Sein*) can show itself. The “there” is not a product of *Dasein*’s activity; it is the *clearing* (*Lichtung*) that is opened up *for* *Dasein*. This clearing is not something *Dasein* creates through its social practices. It is the condition for the possibility of those practices.

The *Kehre* (turning) in Heidegger’s thought is not a rejection of *Being and Time* but a deeper exploration of this clearing. In the later works, Heidegger moves from analyzing *Dasein*’s projection to analyzing the *Es gibt*—the “It gives” of Being. This “It” is not a subject, not a collective, not a social practice. It is the anonymous, impersonal event of giving that makes it possible for entities to be disclosed. Sheehan calls this the “thrownness of understanding” or the facticity of meaning. We find ourselves always already in a world that is meaningful, but we do not produce this meaningfulness ex nihilo. It is given to us.

This is the crucial point: *Ereignis* is a *tertium quid*. It is not an entity (ontic), nor is it a social practice (ontic). It is the *ontological* condition for the possibility of both. Social practices, norms, and vocabularies are all *within* the clearing—they are ways in which the clearing is articulated. But the clearing itself is not a social practice. It is the very event of givenness that precedes and enables any particular practice. Without *Ereignis*, there would be no “there” in which social norms could operate. There would be no entities to refer to, no reasons to give, no community to constitute. Brandom’s account of *Zuhandensein* and *Vorhandensein* is correct as far as it goes—it describes the structure of our ontic engagement with entities. But it fails to account for the *ontological* horizon that makes this engagement possible.

Sheehan’s reading reveals that Rorty’s reduction of ontology to cultural politics is a category mistake. It confuses the ontic (the specific practices and vocabularies we adopt) with the ontological (the event of givenness that makes any practice possible). Rorty’s rejection of a “background of all possible practices” is a rejection of the *clearing* itself. He thinks that by denying a transcendental ground, he is being more radical, more historicist. But in fact, he is merely ignoring the most fundamental level of analysis. He is like a fish who denies the existence of water because he has never seen it.

The necessity of this *tertium quid* is not a return to classical metaphysics. It is not a claim that there is a transcendent God or a Platonic Form that grounds reality. Rather, it is a phenomenological claim about the structure of experience. The givenness of entities is not something we construct; it is something we undergo. We are thrown into a world that is already meaningful. Our social practices are responses to this givenness, not its creators. This is what Heidegger means by *Geworfenheit* (thrownness). We do not choose our situation; we find ourselves in it. And this “finding ourselves” is the *Ereignis*—the event of appropriation that grants us the “there.”

### V. The Inevitability of Transcendence

Does Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics successfully avoid the necessity of metaphysical transcendence? The answer is a qualified no. Rorty successfully avoids the *ontic* transcendence of a mind-independent reality—the notion that there is a world of things-in-themselves that our beliefs must correspond to. He also avoids the *epistemic* transcendence of a transcendental subject—the Kantian ego that constitutes the world. However, he fails to avoid the *ontological* transcendence of the *Ereignis*—the event of givenness that is the condition for the possibility of any social practice. This transcendence is not a thing, not an entity, but the very structure of finitude. It is the “not” that precedes and enables the “is.”

Furthermore, Rorty’s reliance on James’s standard of human happiness reveals a *normative* transcendence that he cannot escape. By positing happiness as the goal of social action, Rorty introduces a *telos* that functions as a structural end. This end is not merely a contingent preference, because it is used to critique and evaluate other practices. If happiness were merely a preference, it could not serve this critical function. Therefore, happiness must be understood as a normative standard that transcends the immanence of any particular practice. This is a form of metaphysical transcendence, even if it is immanent to human nature.

The tension between Rorty’s rejection of a background and his reliance on happiness is not a minor inconsistency; it is the central aporia of his philosophy. He wants to be a thoroughgoing nominalist and historicist, but he cannot avoid positing a universal standard to make his political project coherent. He wants to say that truth is just what we agree on, but he also wants to say that some agreements are better than others. The only way to resolve this tension is to acknowledge that there is a ground—a *tertium quid*—that is not reducible to social practice. This ground may not be a metaphysical essence, but it is a transcendental condition. It is the *Ereignis* that gives us a world, and the *telos* of happiness that guides our action within that world.

Brandom’s reading of Heidegger is a brilliant attempt to ground the social in the pragmatic. It shows how our norms and practices constitute the meaning of entities. But it fails to see that these norms and practices are themselves grounded in a more primordial givenness. The hammer is not just a social role; it is also a piece of wood and metal that has been disclosed to us through the *Ereignis*. The social is not the ultimate reality; it is a derivative mode of the ontological. Sheehan’s interpretation corrects this by emphasizing that the meaning of Being is not a product of *Dasein*’s activity but a gift of the event of appropriation.

In conclusion, Rorty’s project is a noble one. It seeks to free us from the illusions of foundationalism and to promote a more tolerant, pluralistic society. However, it is ultimately self-defeating. By reducing ontology to cultural politics, Rorty eliminates the very ground that makes cultural politics possible. He cannot avoid the necessity of metaphysical transcendence because that transcendence is not an option but a condition. It is the *Ereignis* that gives us a world, and the *telos* of happiness that gives us a direction. A truly coherent pragmatism must acknowledge these conditions, not deny them. It must embrace the finitude of the clearing and the contingency of our practices, while also recognizing that this finitude and contingency are themselves the gifts of a transcendence that we can never fully master. Only then can pragmatism fulfill its promise of a philosophy that is both humble and hopeful, both critical and compassionate.
