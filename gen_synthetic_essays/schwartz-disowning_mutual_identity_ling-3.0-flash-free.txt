**The Ontology of the Other: Skepticism, Deification, and the Spirit-Murdering of the Marginalized**

The Enlightenment project promised a world emancipated from the shackles of superstition, dogma, and arbitrary hierarchy, grounded instead in the universal light of reason. Yet, the history of modernity reveals a paradox: the very rationality that was supposed to liberate humanity became the engine of unprecedented violence against marginalized groups. This violence is not merely a failure of political institutions or a regression into primal savagery; it is a metaphysical catastrophe. It stems from two profound errors in the construction of personal identity and the application of Enlightenment rationality: the skeptical mistake and the deific mistake. When these errors converge, they produce the phenomenon of "spirit-murdering" the Other—a violent ontological assassination that reduces the marginalized to mere objects, stripping them of their subjectivity and moral standing. This essay evaluates this claim by analyzing the metaphysical roots of such violence, examining concrete cases such as the Matthew Shepard murder and the Howard Beach incident, and assessing the legal codification of this violence through victim responsibility defenses. Ultimately, it resolves the underlying tension by assessing whether alternative ontological frameworks, specifically physicalist accounts of shared human identity and revised standards of reasonability, can overcome the solipsistic alienation that breeds true social parity.

**The Skeptical Mistake and the Fracturing of the Subject**

The skeptical mistake, in the context of personal identity and Enlightenment rationality, refers to the radical epistemological and ontological doubt cast upon the interiority and unified selfhood of the marginalized Other. Enlightenment rationality, heavily influenced by Cartesian dualism and Humean empiricism, privileged the detached, objective observer as the arbiter of truth and the locus of identity. In its quest for universal laws, it inadvertently constructed a rigid boundary between the knowing subject and the known object. The skeptical mistake occurs when this boundary hardens into an absolute chasm, leading the dominant group to fundamentally doubt the capacity of the marginalized to possess a coherent, unified, and rational self.

In the Enlightenment paradigm, personal identity was implicitly equated with the capacity for abstract, detached rationality—the ability to transcend the body and the passions. When this standard is applied universally but enforced selectively, it becomes a tool of exclusion. The skeptical mistake manifests as the radical doubt that marginalized individuals possess the inner lives, coherent narratives, and rational capacities necessary to be recognized as full persons. Instead of encountering the Other as a subject with an interior world, the dominant group encounters them as a phenomenon to be cataloged, feared, or dismissed. The Other becomes a void, a projection screen for the dominant group's anxieties and desires, rather than a subject with their own irreducible interiority. This skepticism is not merely an intellectual error; it is an ontological violence that denies the marginalized the very status of being a "who" rather than an "it."

**The Deific Mistake and the God-Complex of the Subject**

If the skeptical mistake denies the interiority of the Other, the deific mistake inflates the self—particularly the dominant, rationalized Enlightenment subject—to a status of absolute ontological primacy. The deific mistake is the projection of a divine, unchanging essence onto the dominant group, treating the self not merely as a subject, but as a god-like creator and arbiter of meaning. In the Cartesian *cogito*, the self becomes the measure of all things; the deific mistake takes this solipsism to its logical extreme, transforming the individual ego into an absolute reality to which all else is subordinate.

Under the deific mistake, the marginalized Other does not merely lack subjectivity; they are actively constituted as the negation of the dominant self. They are the demon, the beast, the abomination—existing only in relation to the divine order of the dominant subject. The deific mistake transforms the dominant group into the arbiters of the social and moral cosmos, granting them a god-like prerogative to define what is human, what is rational, and what is worthy of life. This creates a rigid hierarchy of souls: the dominant self, possessing the divine spark of pure reason, and the marginalized, relegated to the realm of nature, passion, and sub-humanity. Because the dominant self is deified, its actions are rendered beyond reproach; its violence is not cruelty, but the maintenance of cosmic order. The deific mistake thus provides the moral alibi for atrocities, allowing the perpetrator to view their violence not as a violation of the Other's personhood, but as the natural enforcement of a higher, self-ordained law.

**Spirit-Murdering the Other**

When the skeptical mistake and the deific mistake converge, they produce the phenomenon of spirit-murdering the Other. Spirit-murdering is an ontological assassination; it is the violent destruction of the Other’s subjectivity, agency, and capacity to be recognized as a full human being. It is the reduction of a living, breathing subject into an object—a thing to be used, discarded, or annihilated without moral consequence. Spirit-murdering does not merely end a life; it erases the conditions under which that life could be understood as a life at all.

The Matthew Shepard murder of 1998 stands as a harrowing exemplar of this convergence. Shepard, a gay university student, was kidnapped, tortured, and left to die tied to a fence. The perpetrators, Aaron McKinney and Russell Henderson, did not merely kill a man; they spirit-murdered him. Through the lens of the deific mistake, they viewed themselves as the arbiters of a moral order that deemed homosexuality an abomination—a violation of their own constructed divinity. Through the skeptical mistake, they were incapable of recognizing Shepard as a subject with his own desires, loves, and interiority; he was merely a "faggot," a category defined entirely by their negation. Shepard’s humanity was stripped away, and he became an object upon which their violent, deified impulses could be enacted. His spirit was murdered before his body was discarded, his personhood annihilated so that his murder could be justified in the minds of those who committed it.

Similarly, the Howard Beach incident of 1986 illustrates the same metaphysical violence. In this racially motivated attack in Queens, New York, a mob of white men chased and killed Willie Turks, a Black man, after a minor traffic altercation. The deific mistake was at work in the racialized construction of whiteness as the normative, fully human standard, granting the mob a god-like authority over the bodies and lives of those deemed less than human. The skeptical mistake manifested in the mob's radical inability to perceive the Black men as subjects capable of rational agency or innocent existence; they were perceived as threats, as vermin to be exterminated. The spirit-murdering of the Other in Howard Beach was not an aberration but a logical conclusion of a social ontology where the marginalized are denied the status of personhood and the dominant are granted the divine right to eliminate them.

**The Legal Codification of Alienation: Victim Responsibility Defenses**

The metaphysical errors of the skeptical and deific mistakes do not merely inform extrajudicial violence; they are deeply embedded in the legal frameworks that adjudicate justice. The legal concept of victim responsibility defenses—such as the "gay panic" or "trans panic" defenses—serves as a juridical manifestation of spirit-murdering. These defenses operate by attributing the perpetrator's violent actions to the victim's identity or behavior, effectively arguing that the victim's existence provoked the murder.

In a victim responsibility defense, the skeptical mistake is legally enshrined: the law doubts the victim's right to exist peacefully and questions the validity of their subjectivity. The victim's identity becomes a mitigating factor for the perpetrator's actions, suggesting that the victim's mere presence was an affront so severe that it justified violence. Conversely, the deific mistake is legitimized: the perpetrator is granted a god-like prerogative to react violently to the perceived transgression of the Other's existence. The legal system, in adopting these defenses, does not merely fail to protect the marginalized; it actively participates in the spirit-murdering process. It validates the ontological framework in which the marginalized are objects whose existence can be met with lethal force, and the dominant are subjects whose violent reactions are understandable, even excusable. By shifting the locus of moral responsibility from the perpetrator to the victim, the law codifies the solipsistic alienation at the heart of the skeptical and deific mistakes, ensuring that the spirit of the marginalized is murdered not only by the mob, but by the very institutions designed to uphold justice.

**Resolving the Tension: Physicalist Accounts of Shared Identity**

The tension between solipsistic alienation and true social parity presents a profound philosophical challenge. Can the underlying metaphysical errors be overcome, or are the skeptical and deific mistakes inherent to the human condition? To resolve this, we must assess alternative ontological frameworks that can bridge the gap between self and Other. The first such framework is the physicalist account of shared human identity.

Physicalism posits that all human beings are constituted by the same biological matter, subject to the same neurobiological processes, and equally vulnerable to the same existential conditions of pain, suffering, and mortality. By grounding personal identity in our shared physicality rather than in abstract, detached rationality, physicalism provides a robust ontological foundation for recognizing the Other. Unlike the Enlightenment subject, which was a disembodied mind capable of transcending the messy particulars of the body, the physicalist subject is inherently vulnerable and finite. 

This shared vulnerability is the antidote to the skeptical mistake. When we recognize that the Other shares our biological substrate—that they feel pain, fear, and love through the same physiological mechanisms that we do—the epistemological chasm between self and Other narrows. The radical doubt that the Other possesses a coherent interiority becomes untenable when we acknowledge that their interiority is constituted by the same flesh and blood as our own. Furthermore, physicalism undermines the deific mistake by denying the dominant group any ontological superiority. The deified, rational self is a myth; we are all merely complex arrangements of matter, equally subject to the blind forces of biology and the universe. In this framework, the marginalized are not ontologically inferior or demonic; they are simply fellow travelers in a shared biological existence. 

However, a purely physicalist account faces limitations. The mere recognition of shared biological matter does not automatically translate into moral recognition. History is replete with instances where dominant groups recognized the physical humanity of marginalized groups while still treating them as subhuman. Physicalism can be co-opted by biological determinism, which might argue that differences in physical constitution justify different treatments. Therefore, while physicalism provides a necessary ontological foundation, it is insufficient on its own to overcome solipsistic alienation. It must be paired with a revised understanding of how we relate to this shared physicality.

**Revised Standards of Reasonability**

To fully overcome the alienation produced by the skeptical and deific mistakes, we must supplement physicalist ontology with revised standards of reasonability. The Enlightenment standard of reasonability was rooted in the cold, detached, and abstract rationality of the *cogito*—a rationality that prized the transcendence of the body and the emotions. This standard was never truly universal; it was always already gendered, racialized, and classed, defining as "rational" only those who already possessed power. To establish true social parity, we must revise our standards of reasonability to include intersubjectivity, empathy, and the recognition of vulnerability.

A revised standard of reasonability moves away from the rationality of domination—the cold, calculating logic that treats the Other as a mere object to be managed—and toward a rationality of communion. Drawing on the communicative rationality of Jürgen Habermas or the capabilities approach of Martha Nussbaum, this revised standard insists that reason is not an individualistic, god-like faculty, but an inherently social and embodied capacity. Reasonability is achieved not in isolation, but through dialogue, empathy, and the willingness to recognize the legitimacy of the Other's experiences. 

Under this revised standard, the skeptical mistake is overcome because reasonability requires us to take the Other's testimony seriously—to accept their claims to interiority and suffering as valid data for our moral and epistemic frameworks. The deific mistake is overcome because reasonability is no longer a monopoly of the dominant subject; it is a shared, intersubjective practice. When we revise our standards of reasonability, we dismantle the god-complex of the Enlightenment subject and replace it with a humble, dialogic engagement with the world. We recognize that our own identities are constituted in relation to the Other, and that true rationality demands the recognition of the Other's full subjectivity.

**Conclusion**

The societal violence against marginalized groups is not a mere political or economic phenomenon; it is a metaphysical crisis born of the skeptical and deific mistakes. By doubting the interiority of the Other and inflating the dominant self to an absolute, ontological status, Enlightenment rationality produced the conditions for spirit-murdering. The Matthew Shepard murder and the Howard Beach incident are not isolated tragedies but logical enactments of this alienated ontology, where the marginalized are reduced to objects for the dominant's violent projections. The legal system, through victim responsibility defenses, further entrenches this violence by codifying the skepticism and deification that undergird the spirit-murder. 

Yet, the path forward is not blocked. By adopting a physicalist account of shared human identity, we ground our recognition of the Other in our shared biological vulnerability, breaking the solipsistic barrier that separates self from Other. However, physicalism alone is insufficient; it must be coupled with revised standards of reasonability that embrace intersubjectivity, empathy, and the embodied, dialogic nature of human existence. Together, these frameworks offer a way out of the ontological dead end of the Enlightenment. They replace the alienating, deified subject with a vulnerable, relational self, and they replace radical skepticism with a reasoned commitment to recognizing the full personhood of all. In doing so, they hold the promise of overcoming the spirit-murdering of the Other, establishing not merely a tolerance of difference, but a true social parity rooted in the shared reality of our flesh and the shared practice of our reason.
