## The Aesthetic Imperative: Evaluating Faulconer’s Bridge from Beauty to Environmental Duty

The modern environmental crisis presents a profound philosophical challenge, one that exposes the stark limitations of traditional ethical frameworks. When we confront the despoliation of rainforests, the acidification of oceans, or the silent extinction of species, we instinctively feel that a moral wrong has occurred. Yet, when we turn to the dominant moral theories of the Western canon, we often find ourselves bereft of a vocabulary to articulate this wrong. Deontological ethics, particularly in its Kantian formulation, appears to slam the door on any direct moral consideration of non-rational nature. The categorical imperative, with its supreme principle of treating rational beings as ends in themselves, seems to relegate the natural world to the status of a mere means, a repository of resources for human projects. It is precisely at this impasse that the provocative argument of philosopher Matthew Faulconer emerges. Faulconer contends that Kant’s philosophy of aesthetic judgment—specifically, the judgment of the beautiful and the sublime—provides a rigorous, non-consequentialist foundation for environmental obligations, a foundation that bypasses the strictures of the categorical imperative without abandoning the Kantian project. However, while Faulconer’s attempt to bridge the chasm between aesthetic universality and moral duty is intellectually ingenious and offers a potent rejoinder to the arbitrariness of utilitarian environmental calculus, it ultimately fails to achieve a seamless transition. The leap from the subjective yet universal demand of taste to the objective, binding force of moral law remains a precarious one, leaving the argument vulnerable to the very arbitrariness it seeks to overcome, albeit in a subtler, more insidious form.

To appreciate the magnitude of Faulconer’s task, one must first fully grasp the forbidding terrain of Kant’s moral philosophy. In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* and the *Critique of Practical Reason*, Kant grounds morality in the autonomy of the rational will. The moral law, the categorical imperative, is not derived from any empirical content—not from happiness, utility, or inclination—but purely from the formal condition of rationality itself. The most famous formulation, the Formula of Universal Law, commands us to “act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.” The second formulation, the Formula of Humanity, is even more restrictive: “So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, and never merely as a means.” This principle identifies the sole bearers of intrinsic value as *rational beings*—persons—who possess dignity by virtue of their capacity for self-legislation. Consequently, animals, plants, and ecosystems, lacking this rational capacity, possess no intrinsic worth. They are, in Kant’s stark terminology, mere things, possessing only instrumental value relative to the ends of rational agents.

This anthropocentric stricture creates a seemingly insurmountable obstacle for environmental ethics. If we have no direct duties to nature, then any obligation to preserve it must be indirect, derived from our duties to other humans. We might preserve a forest because it provides clean air for future generations, or we might protect a species because it holds potential medicinal value. But this is a purely utilitarian calculus, subject to the whims of human preference and the shifting sands of technological advancement. If a swamp serves as a breeding ground for malaria-carrying mosquitoes, and we can drain it to build a housing development that provides greater aggregate human utility, then the Kantian framework, in its strictest reading, offers no principled objection. Indeed, it might even *mandate* the drainage if it serves the rational ends of the community. This is the bleak picture that Faulconer seeks to rectify. He recognizes that a purely instrumental view of nature is both morally and practically untenable, yet he wishes to remain faithful to the core insights of Kant’s transcendental idealism.

Faulconer’s solution lies in a creative and audacious reinterpretation of Kant’s *Critique of Judgment*, specifically the analysis of the judgment of taste. For Kant, the judgment of beauty is a peculiar and paradoxical phenomenon. It is *subjective* in the sense that it is based on a feeling of pleasure, not on a determinate concept of the object. When I declare a sunset beautiful, I am not asserting a property of the sunset (like its wavelength) but rather expressing a feeling that arises from the harmonious free play of my cognitive faculties—imagination and understanding. Yet, crucially, this judgment is not merely private or idiosyncratic. When I judge something beautiful, I *demand* universal assent. I speak with a “universal voice,” claiming that everyone *ought* to feel the same pleasure I do. This claim to universality is not based on a concept (I cannot prove the sunset is beautiful), but rather on a *sensus communis*, a presumed shared ground of feeling that underpins all possible cognition.

This is the fulcrum of Faulconer’s argument. He argues that the *demand for universal assent* inherent in the judgment of taste carries a normative weight that transcends mere preference. The pleasure we take in natural beauty is not an arbitrary, hedonistic thrill; it is a *disinterested* pleasure, one that is free from any concern for the object’s utility or existence. We delight in the *form* of a flower, its purposive arrangement of parts, without any desire to consume it or use it for a practical end. This disinterestedness elevates the aesthetic experience above the realm of mere appetite. Furthermore, the free play of the faculties that constitutes the experience of beauty is a condition of cognition itself. It is a harmonious state in which our mental capacities are exercised in a way that feels purposive, even though we cannot identify a specific purpose. Faulconer contends that this state of cognitive harmony is intrinsically valuable for rational beings—it is a flourishing of our rational nature, a moment of unity between our sensible and intellectual capacities.

From this, Faulconer constructs his bridge to moral obligation. His argument can be reconstructed as follows: (1) Rational beings have a moral duty to cultivate the conditions of their own rationality and to promote the conditions under which rational agency can flourish. (2) The experience of natural beauty is a necessary (or at least uniquely potent) condition for the flourishing of our cognitive faculties—it stimulates the free play of imagination and understanding in a way that artificial or utilitarian objects cannot. (3) Therefore, rational beings have an indirect moral duty to preserve and protect the natural environments that give rise to these aesthetic experiences. This is not a duty *to* nature, but a duty *regarding* nature, grounded in our self-legislating rational nature. We are not obligated to the Grand Canyon, but we are obligated to preserve the Grand Canyon because its existence is a condition for a specific kind of rational flourishing—the aesthetic judgment—that is part of our moral vocation.

This argument brilliantly navigates the dialectical tension between nature’s lack of inherent value and the claim of aesthetic universality. Kant famously stated that nature has no intrinsic value, that it is merely a means. However, Faulconer points out that while the *object* of aesthetic judgment has no value in itself, the *experience* of that object has profound value for the rational subject. The judgment of beauty is not about the object’s existence but about its *form*, which stimulates a universalizable mental state. By shifting the locus of value from the object to the *judgment* and its cognitive conditions, Faulconer preserves Kant’s anthropocentrism while simultaneously generating a powerful environmental imperative. The tension is resolved not by granting nature moral standing, but by recognizing that nature is a *necessary condition* for a morally significant human experience. The universal assent demanded by the aesthetic judgment is not a claim about the object’s objective properties, but a claim about the universal validity of the cognitive response it elicits in all rational beings. If this response is a component of rational flourishing, then the preservation of its stimulus becomes a rational requirement.

The most compelling aspect of Faulconer’s argument is its robust response to the specific objection that consequence-based assessments of environmental policy are inherently arbitrary. Utilitarian or cost-benefit approaches to environmental ethics are plagued by intractable problems of measurement and commensurability. How does one quantify the aesthetic value of a wilderness area in terms of dollars? How does one weigh the pleasure of a future generation against the economic utility of a current one? These calculations are, at bottom, arbitrary. They rely on subjective preferences, contingent market prices, and discount rates that are themselves chosen based on contested ethical assumptions. If we value a forest because it sequesters carbon, we are making a contingent claim about climate science and human welfare. If we value it because it provides recreational opportunities, we are privileging a particular set of human interests. In all cases, the value is extrinsic and revocable. A better technological solution (e.g., carbon capture machines) could render the forest redundant.

Faulconer’s aesthetic grounding offers a way out of this morass. The judgment of beauty, as Kant analyzed it, is *a priori* in its form. It is not based on empirical considerations of utility or pleasure. The claim to universal assent is a *necessary* claim, rooted in the transcendental structure of cognition. When I judge a natural landscape beautiful, I am not saying “I like it” or “it is useful to me,” but rather “this *ought* to be appreciated by all rational beings.” This “ought” is not a hypothetical imperative (if you want pleasure, then appreciate this) but a categorical demand of taste. For Faulconer, this categorical nature of aesthetic judgment provides a non-arbitrary foundation for environmental preservation. We do not preserve nature because of a contingent calculation of consequences, but because the *experience* of nature is a necessary condition for a universalizable cognitive harmony. The duty to preserve nature is thus grounded in the same formal, a priori structure that grounds the moral law itself—the structure of rationality. This elegantly sidesteps the arbitrariness of utilitarianism by locating the source of obligation not in the empirical world of consequences, but in the transcendental conditions of judgment.

Despite its considerable elegance, Faulconer’s argument faces a decisive challenge at the crucial point of transition from aesthetic universality to moral obligation. The problem lies in the *nature of the normativity* involved in each domain. In Kant’s moral philosophy, the categorical imperative is a *determinant* judgment. It applies a universal law (the moral law) to a particular maxim of action. It commands with absolute necessity. The moral law is *binding* because it is derived from the pure concept of rational will; to violate it is to contradict one’s own rational nature.

In contrast, the judgment of taste is a *reflective* judgment. It does not apply a pre-existing concept to an object; rather, it seeks a concept *for* a particular intuition that is given. The “universal assent” demanded by taste is not a demand that anyone *act* in a certain way; it is a demand that they *feel* a certain way. It is a demand for agreement in *feeling*, not in *action*. The normativity of taste is thus a normativity of expectation, not of command. When I judge a flower beautiful, I am not issuing a moral command to others to admire it; I am expressing a hope or an expectation that they will share my feeling. This is a crucial distinction. A moral imperative tells me what I *must do*; an aesthetic judgment tells me what I *should feel* (or rather, what I can reasonably expect others to feel).

Faulconer attempts to bridge this gap by arguing that the *capacity* for aesthetic judgment is a condition of rational agency, and that we have a duty to cultivate this capacity. But this move introduces a subtle yet fatal equivocation. The duty to cultivate one’s rational capacities is a real Kantian duty—it is a duty of virtue (an imperfect duty) to perfect oneself. However, this duty is *indirect* and *imperfect*. It does not specify *which* particular objects I must preserve to fulfill it. The duty to cultivate aesthetic sensibility could be fulfilled by appreciating art, music, or the beauty of mathematical proofs. It does not, by itself, generate a specific obligation to preserve a particular wilderness area or a specific species. The transition from “I have a duty to cultivate my rational faculties through aesthetic experience” to “I have a duty to preserve this specific swamp” requires a further premise—namely, that this swamp is a unique and irreplaceable stimulus for that experience. But this premise is an empirical, consequentialist claim. It reintroduces the very contingency that Faulconer sought to eliminate.

If a perfect virtual reality simulation of a redwood forest were created, capable of eliciting the exact same cognitive free play as the real forest, would Faulconer’s argument obligate us to preserve the real forest? Kant’s own analysis of beauty suggests that the judgment concerns the *form* of the object, not its material existence. A perfect simulacrum would possess the same form. Therefore, on a strict Kantian reading, the simulation would be just as beautiful as the real forest, and the duty to preserve the *experience* would be satisfied by the simulation. Faulconer might respond by appealing to the Kantian concept of the *sublime*, which involves a feeling of the supersensible and the infinite, and which might be more difficult to simulate. But even this is an empirical claim. The point is that Faulconer’s argument has not successfully generated a categorical duty *to nature*; it has only generated a conditional duty *to the conditions of aesthetic experience*, and which conditions count is left dangerously open.

This brings us to the crux of the matter: does Faulconer’s transition successfully resolve the problem of making nonarbitrary environmental decisions without utilitarian calculations? The answer is a resounding *no*, at least not in the robust sense he intends. While he successfully avoids the *empirical* arbitrariness of cost-benefit analysis (which relies on contingent preferences), he introduces a *transcendental* arbitrariness of aesthetic taste. The judgment of beauty is universalizable in its *form*, but the *content* of what we find beautiful is not determined a priori. I might find a desert landscape sublime, while another person finds it barren and ugly. I might find a teeming, chaotic wetland beautiful, while another finds it repulsive. Kant’s analysis tells us that *if* we judge something beautiful, we demand universal assent, but it does not tell us *what* we must judge beautiful. The sensus communis is a presupposed ground of agreement, but it is an ideal, not a reality.

Consequently, Faulconer’s framework cannot provide a determinate answer to the question of *which* environmental goods to prioritize. Should we preserve the Grand Canyon or a local prairie? Should we save the panda or the snail darter? Faulconer’s principle would say: preserve whatever elicits the disinterested pleasure of beauty. But this is a purely subjective criterion. What if the majority of people find strip mines strangely beautiful in their industrial sublime, or find monoculture farms aesthetically pleasing in their orderliness? The argument would then mandate preserving those, which is clearly contrary to environmentalist goals. To avoid this, Faulconer would have to argue that there is an objective hierarchy of natural beauties, that some natural forms are intrinsically more conducive to cognitive free play than others. But such a claim is deeply problematic. It would require a teleology of nature that Kant explicitly rejected in the third Critique. He argued that we cannot know the purposes of nature; we can only judge its forms subjectively.

The inherent arbitrariness of taste thus re-enters the equation through the back door. Faulconer has merely replaced the arbitrariness of *consequences* (which is empirical and calculable) with the arbitrariness of *aesthetic preference* (which is subjective and incommensurable). While the former is often crudely quantitative, the latter is qualitatively opaque. A utilitarian can at least attempt to measure utility, however imperfectly. A Kantian aestheticist cannot even begin to measure beauty; it is a pure feeling. Therefore, when faced with a concrete environmental policy decision—say, whether to dam a river for hydroelectric power or preserve its free-flowing state for its scenic beauty—Faulconer’s framework offers no nonarbitrary method of adjudication. It merely pits one subjective judgment against another, with no higher court of appeal.

In conclusion, Matthew Faulconer’s argument is a masterful and provocative attempt to salvage environmental ethics from the clutches of Kant’s anthropocentric moral law. By grounding environmental obligation in the universalizable structure of aesthetic judgment, he offers a powerful critique of the arbitrary nature of utilitarian cost-benefit analysis. He correctly identifies that the *demand for universal assent* in judgments of beauty carries a normative weight that transcends mere preference, and that the preservation of natural beauty is a condition for a specific kind of rational flourishing. However, the transition from aesthetic universality to moral obligation is not a deduction but an equivocation. The categorical nature of the moral law is fundamentally different from the hypothetical and reflective normativity of taste. While Faulconer successfully demonstrates that we have a duty to cultivate our aesthetic capacities, he fails to demonstrate that this duty translates into a determinate, nonarbitrary obligation to preserve specific natural entities. The problem of arbitrariness is not solved; it is merely relocated from the empirical realm of consequences to the transcendental realm of taste. Ultimately, Faulconer’s aesthetic imperative provides a necessary corrective to a purely instrumental view of nature, reminding us of the profound value of natural beauty for the human spirit. Yet, as a foundation for a complete and actionable environmental ethic, it remains insufficient. It offers a compelling *reason* to value nature, but it does not offer a reliable *method* for making the difficult, concrete decisions that environmental policy demands. Without a supplementary principle—one that perhaps grants intrinsic value to life itself or acknowledges the inherent dignity of non-rational beings—the leap from beauty to duty remains a bridge too far, leaving us stranded on the island of subjective feeling, unable to reach the solid ground of objective moral command.
