# Dialectical Failure and the Rules of Religious Belief

The debate between theism and atheism is often presented as a dispute about an object: whether God exists. On this model, the parties occupy opposed positions before a common tribunal of reason. The theist affirms a proposition; the atheist withholds assent or denies it; and the central question is whether the available evidence warrants one attitude rather than the other. Yet this apparently straightforward picture conceals a prior dispute about the tribunal itself. What counts as evidence? What does it mean for a proposition to be rationally acceptable? Who bears the burden of proof? Is the absence of verification evidence against God, or merely a reason for suspension of judgment? Is the presumption of atheism a neutral starting point, or does it silently privilege a particular conception of inquiry?

The claim that the debate rests on a dialectical failure driven by epistemological rules rather than objective truth should therefore be taken seriously. “Dialectical failure” here does not mean that there is no truth about God. It means that the participants may fail to confront one another at the level at which their disagreement actually occurs. They appear to argue about the existence of God, while in fact disputing the legitimacy of the rules under which existence-claims may be evaluated. Each side can then present its own methodological commitments as if they were simply the requirements of reason itself. Atheism may portray itself as the absence of an additional commitment, while theism may portray its standards of explanation, rationality, or existential adequacy as self-evident. The resulting argument risks becoming less an inquiry into objective truth than a contest over who may define the norms of inquiry.

Scott A. Shalkowski’s critiques of the presumption of atheism are especially useful for exposing this structure. His discussions of parsimony, verifiability, and burdens of proof challenge the idea that atheism can occupy an epistemically privileged position merely by presenting itself as the default. Michael Scriven’s defense of atheism, by contrast, illustrates how an apparently modest appeal to ordinary reasoning and sense-certainty can conceal substantial epistemological assumptions. G. W. F. Hegel’s account of the confrontation between Faith and Enlightenment in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* provides a broader framework for interpreting this conflict. Hegel shows how Enlightenment criticism can reduce faith to superstition while treating its own historically situated commitments as pure reason; but he also shows how faith can fail communicatively when it refuses to translate its content into publicly intelligible forms.

The most defensible resolution is neither that objective truth is irrelevant nor that epistemic rules are arbitrary. Rules of inquiry are historically and ethically formed, but they can be criticized, revised, and partially universalized. They cannot entirely transcend their contextual origins, yet they need not remain imprisoned within them. Epistemology is not reducible to ethics, but it is inseparable from ethical questions concerning intellectual responsibility, fairness, trust, and the recognition of other persons as participants in a shared world. The debate between theism and atheism becomes rationally productive only when it acknowledges this interdependence instead of disguising methodological decisions as neutral facts.

## I. The Presumption of Atheism

The phrase “presumption of atheism” can express several different theses. It may mean that no one should believe in God without sufficient evidence. It may mean that atheism is the default position prior to argument. It may mean that the burden of proof lies entirely with the theist. Or it may mean that, in the absence of demonstrative evidence, disbelief is more rational than belief. These claims are not equivalent. A person who lacks belief in God because the evidence seems insufficient is not necessarily committed to the stronger proposition that God does not exist. Much of the controversy arises when this distinction is blurred.

Shalkowski’s critique begins from the insight that atheism is not simply a neutral absence of commitment when it is formulated as the claim that there is no God. Negative existential claims can be difficult to establish, but they are still claims. The statement “there are no gods” differs from the attitude “I do not believe that there are gods.” The former requires some account of the domain being denied; the latter reports a condition of belief. If atheism is defined in the weaker psychological sense, then it may plausibly be described as a default. But if it is defined as a substantive metaphysical conclusion, the presumption of neutrality becomes less secure.

This does not automatically vindicate theism. The point is methodological: one cannot settle the dispute by assigning atheism the status of nonbelief and theism the status of belief unless one first justifies that asymmetrical classification. The atheist may respond that all unsupported entities should be rejected, and that this is not a controversial metaphysical assumption but a basic rule of rational economy. Shalkowski’s analysis asks whether this response successfully establishes the alleged presumption or merely restates it in methodological language.

### Parsimony

The argument from parsimony is often expressed through a maxim such as: do not multiply entities beyond necessity. Since God is an additional entity, and since the world can supposedly be described without God, the rational inquirer should refrain from positing God. This argument has intuitive force. Scientific inquiry often advances by preferring simpler explanations, and everyday reasoning generally benefits from avoiding needless assumptions. If one can explain a phenomenon without invoking an extra cause, why invoke it?

Shalkowski’s criticism is that parsimony does not by itself identify what counts as “needless.” Simplicity is not a purely formal property independent of explanatory aims. A theory may be ontologically simpler but explanatorily poorer; another may posit more entities while organizing a wider range of facts under a more unified account. Newtonian physics introduced theoretical structures not available in ordinary observation, while evolutionary theory multiplied causal mechanisms relative to simple design explanations in some contexts. Whether a posit is excessive depends on what one is trying to explain and what standards govern explanatory adequacy.

Applied to theism, parsimony can function only after the scope of explanation has been determined. If the question concerns the predictive behavior of physical systems, then invoking God may indeed be unnecessary. But if the questions concern why there is a contingent world at all, why the universe is intelligible, whether moral obligations are objective, or whether finite consciousness can be understood in wholly naturalistic terms, then the relevant comparison is not between a theory with God and an otherwise identical theory without God. The issue is whether theism provides an explanatory framework for features that atheism either explains differently or treats as brute facts.

The atheist may reply that brute facts are preferable to an even greater mystery. But this is a legitimate methodological preference only if the costs of explanatory unification are weighed against the costs of unexplained contingency. Theism does not win simply because it explains more in a loose sense. Nor does atheism win simply because it adds fewer entities. Parsimony is a rule of comparison, not a conclusion about which account is objectively true.

There is also a deeper problem. If parsimony is applied indiscriminately, it can become self-defeating. The rule itself is not an observable entity. It is a norm of reasoning. If the atheist treats norms, logical principles, mathematical objects, or conscious subjects as acceptable despite their theoretical status, then the exclusion of God cannot rest on a crude ban against all nonempirical or nonphysical commitments. The real issue must be comparative: which commitments are warranted, and by what explanatory or practical standards? Once the question is framed this way, the presumption of atheism loses its appearance of simple neutrality.

### Verifiability

A second argument for atheism appeals to verifiability. God, it is said, cannot be observed, measured, or experimentally tested. Assertions about God therefore fail the conditions of meaningful or rational discourse. This line of thought is associated with empiricist and positivist tendencies, though contemporary atheism need not accept strict verificationism.

Shalkowski’s criticism is that verifiability is not a self-evident criterion of meaningfulness. Many propositions that are rationally accepted are not directly verifiable in the narrow empirical sense. Historical claims, claims about other minds, moral judgments, mathematical truths, and theoretical entities in science all require forms of justification that are indirect, inferential, or conceptual. Even the proposition that only verifiable claims deserve belief is not itself straightforwardly verifiable. It is a philosophical rule, not an empirical discovery.

This does not show that religious claims are true. It shows only that the demand for verification cannot be used as an unargued instrument for disqualifying them. Moreover, the God of classical theism is not ordinarily proposed as one object among others within the universe. To ask for empirical verification of God may therefore involve a category mistake. If God is conceived as the ground of being, the condition of the world’s existence, or a transcendent personal reality, then a laboratory test may not be the appropriate mode of access. The relevant question would concern metaphysical inference, phenomenological experience, historical testimony, moral rationality, or the structure of intelligibility.

Again, this defense must be carefully limited. The fact that a claim is not empirically verifiable does not exempt it from all standards of criticism. A religious claim may be incoherent, ad hoc, morally objectionable, or incompatible with well-established knowledge. The failure of strict verificationism does not entail that every unverifiable assertion is rationally acceptable. Shalkowski’s point is not that theology should be placed beyond criticism, but that the atheist cannot establish its exclusion simply by appealing to a rule whose own status is philosophical and contestable.

Verifiability also reveals the communicative dimension of the debate. When an atheist says, “There is no evidence for God,” this may mean that no evidence meets the standards the atheist accepts. When a theist replies, “There is abundant evidence,” the theist may be using “evidence” to include religious experience, moral awareness, or metaphysical argument. The disagreement is then not merely about the quantity of evidence but about the grammar of evidentiality. If this grammar is not made explicit, both sides can accuse the other of irrationality while failing to address the underlying issue.

### Burdens of Proof

The burden-of-proof argument appears more modest. It says that the person who makes an extraordinary claim must support it. Since the theist asserts the existence of an invisible and unlimited being, the theist bears the burden of proof. The atheist need only ask for reasons and decline to believe in their absence.

There is considerable truth in this principle. Belief should not be forced upon us merely because a possibility cannot be disproved. If someone claims that an invisible entity inhabits a distant planet, it would be unreasonable to demand that skeptics disprove the claim before withholding assent. Intellectual life would be impossible if every unsupported assertion had to be refuted individually.

Yet Shalkowski’s critique concerns the extension of this local rule into a general presumption of atheism. The burden of proof depends on how a thesis is formulated and what commitments it carries. If the atheist merely suspends belief, no positive burden may arise. But if the atheist asserts that there is no God, or that the world is ultimately explicable without God, then the atheist has assumed a burden of a different kind. The distribution of proof cannot be fixed solely by labeling one side “default.”

Furthermore, debates about fundamental worldviews are not always structured like ordinary disputes about isolated objects. Theism and atheism offer comprehensive interpretations of reality. Each may contain positive claims about causation, rationality, value, consciousness, and human destiny. The atheist can avoid a burden only by defining atheism minimally, but that definition may not represent the substantive naturalism or secular humanism often defended in practice. Conversely, the theist cannot escape responsibility by pointing to the limits of disproof. A burden of proof is reciprocal whenever both sides make affirmative claims.

The deepest issue is that the burden-of-proof rule has an ethical dimension. It protects people from manipulation, credulity, and coercion. It embodies a norm of intellectual fairness: do not demand that others accept what you have not adequately supported. But fairness also requires not loading the argumentative structure in advance through a disputed definition of the default position. To assign burdens responsibly, one must distinguish psychological nonbelief, methodological naturalism, metaphysical naturalism, and explicit denial of God.

## II. Scriven and the Authority of Ordinary Reason

Michael Scriven’s defense of atheism can be understood as an attempt to make disbelief the rational posture by appealing to familiar standards of everyday reasoning. Ordinary life teaches us to distinguish what is present from what is merely asserted, to demand evidence for unusual claims, and to rely on what can be publicly checked. If a person claims that an extraordinary object exists but provides no convincing evidence, we do not treat belief and disbelief as equally warranted. We withhold belief. Scriven’s defense gains strength by presenting atheism not as a daring theory but as an extension of common sense.

This rhetorical strategy has an important advantage. It resists the tendency to portray atheism as requiring a comprehensive proof that God does not exist. The atheist need not inspect every possible reality or solve every metaphysical problem. In ordinary circumstances, absence of adequate evidence is sufficient reason not to believe. Scriven can therefore characterize religious belief as a departure from normal standards rather than as the result of a neutral evaluation in which theism and atheism are symmetrical hypotheses.

But this appeal to ordinary reasoning rests on presuppositions. Common sense is not a transparent access to reality; it is a historically developed set of habits adapted to practical life. It presupposes that the world is relatively stable, that perception is generally trustworthy, that other people are similar to us, that causes operate consistently, and that publicly accessible objects are the primary candidates for knowledge. These assumptions are indispensable in daily activity, but they do not automatically settle metaphysical questions. The fact that ordinary reasoning is appropriate for deciding whether there is a chair in a room does not show that it is sufficient for deciding whether reality has a transcendent ground.

Scriven’s appeal to sense-certainty also risks confusing epistemic priority with ontological priority. Sensory experience may be the first source of many beliefs, but it does not follow that what cannot be sensed is unreal or irrationally believed. We do not see justice, causality, logical validity, or another person’s consciousness in the same manner that we see physical objects. We infer, interpret, and rely on forms of trust. The world disclosed in experience is already structured by concepts and expectations. “Sense-certainty,” in Hegel’s phrase, is not as immediate as it imagines itself to be.

This criticism does not imply that religious experience is automatically reliable. It instead places religious claims within a wider epistemological landscape. If ordinary reasoning includes testimony, memory, inference to the best explanation, moral perception, and existential self-understanding, then the assessment of theism must consider whether religious belief can be rational within those practices. Scriven’s position is strongest when it emphasizes that mere possibility is not evidence. It is weaker when it treats a particular model of public, sensory evidence as the uncontroversial measure of all rational belief.

The question, then, is not whether Scriven secretly relies on assumptions. Every argument does. The question is whether his assumptions are acknowledged and defensible. His position may be defended by saying that public evidence is more reliable than private revelation because it allows correction, comparison, and intersubjective testing. That is a serious epistemic consideration. Yet it is not simply a fact of sense-certainty; it is a normative preference for fallibility, publicity, and revisability. These are virtues of inquiry, but they are also ethical commitments concerning how we ought to treat claims and one another.

## III. Hegel’s Faith and Enlightenment

Hegel’s account of the confrontation between Faith and Enlightenment provides an illuminating diagnosis of this conflict. In the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, Enlightenment presents itself as the champion of pure insight against the darkness of faith. It seeks to expose religious representations as products of fear, priestly manipulation, or naïve projection. Faith, in turn, understands Enlightenment as destructive negation: a force that strips the world of meaning and leaves only an abstract, empty matter.

Hegel’s point is not to choose simply between superstition and rationalism. He shows that each side misrecognizes its own dependence on the other. Enlightenment claims to speak for universal reason, but its critique often proceeds by reducing the content of faith before examining it. It translates religious objects into crude natural or psychological substitutes and then triumphantly refutes those substitutes. Faith may speak of God as spirit, absolute ground, or reconciliation, while Enlightenment treats the religious object as though it were merely an oversized physical thing. The critique then succeeds by attacking a caricature.

At the same time, faith can commit a parallel failure. It may insist on the sacredness of its content while refusing to render that content intelligible in shared conceptual terms. When challenged, it may retreat to inward certainty, authority, or an appeal to mystery. In doing so, it turns a claim about truth into a declaration of allegiance. It demands recognition without entering the space in which recognition could be rationally negotiated.

This is a communicative failure because each side disguises its standpoint. Enlightenment disguises historical and ethical commitments as pure epistemic rules. It may declare that only what is empirically verifiable is rational, although this principle is itself neither an empirical observation nor a neutral fact. It may appeal to ordinary common sense while overlooking the social conditions that determine whose experiences count as ordinary. It may present parsimony as a universal law while privileging a specific ideal of explanation.

Faith, for its part, can disguise ethical and communal authority as revelation. It may present inherited norms, institutional interests, or affective needs as direct knowledge of the divine. It may accuse criticism of godlessness in order to avoid answering criticism. The problem is not merely that one side uses bad arguments. It is that both sides can conceal the normative conditions under which their arguments become persuasive.

Hegel’s larger lesson is that consciousness advances when it recognizes the limits of its own one-sidedness. The Enlightenment’s negative critique is necessary because faith can become dogmatic and politically oppressive. But critique must also become self-critical. If reason defines itself only as the destruction of religious representation, it remains dependent on what it negates and fails to articulate a positive account of freedom, truth, and shared life. Faith must likewise move beyond the immediacy of inward certainty toward a form in which its content can be mediated, interpreted, and examined.

The question whether the critic disguises ethical assumptions as epistemic principles can therefore be answered affirmatively in many cases, though not universally. Whenever an atheist says that no reasonable person believes what cannot be verified, the statement may contain an ethical maxim: responsible belief requires public accountability and protection against deception. Whenever a theist says that faith is rational because it expresses trust in the ultimate good, the statement may contain an ethical maxim: human existence ought to be oriented toward a transcendent source of value. Neither maxim is illegitimate. The failure occurs when either is presented as if it were merely a neutral rule dictated by the structure of reality.

## IV. Objective Truth and the Conditions of Its Pursuit

Does the diagnosis of dialectical failure undermine objective truth? It should not. The fact that arguments are shaped by epistemological rules does not show that truth itself is created by those rules. It shows that access to truth is mediated. We do not encounter “objective truth” without concepts, practices, languages, institutions, and standards of justification. But mediation is not the same as fabrication. A map is shaped by purposes and conventions, yet it can still represent a terrain more or less accurately.

The debate between theism and atheism therefore requires distinguishing three levels. The first is the truth-condition: whether God exists independently of anyone’s beliefs. The second is the epistemic condition: what reasons justify belief, disbelief, or suspension of judgment. The third is the practical or ethical condition: what forms of life, responsibility, and communal relations are associated with one’s worldview. Dialectical confusion arises when one level is substituted for another. The atheist may infer from the inability to verify God that God does not exist. The theist may infer from the existential fruitfulness of faith that its object must exist. Neither inference is automatically valid.

At the same time, the levels cannot be completely separated. Epistemic standards are not ethically innocent. A demand for public evidence may protect autonomy and prevent coercion, but it may also exclude kinds of testimony historically marginalized by dominant institutions. A willingness to trust religious experience may honor dimensions of human life neglected by reductive naturalism, but it may also enable authority and abuse. The rules of belief shape the distribution of credibility. They determine whose testimony is accepted and which experiences are dismissed as irrational. Epistemology is therefore partly a theory of intellectual justice.

This is why the debate cannot be resolved by appealing to an allegedly context-free rule. Rules acquire meaning within practices. “Believe only what is verifiable” means one thing in a laboratory, another in a courtroom, and another in intimate moral life. “Trust testimony” can be rational when the witness is competent and sincere, but irrational when authority is insulated from correction. “Prefer simpler explanations” is useful when the explanatory targets and comparison class are clear, but misleading when simplicity is defined to exclude precisely the dimensions under dispute.

The contextual origin of epistemic rules does not entail relativism. Contexts can be compared and criticized. A community that forbids questioning may be epistemically defective because it prevents error correction. A method that systematically ignores counterevidence may be criticized even if culturally entrenched. We can defend norms of consistency, responsiveness to evidence, openness to correction, and equal consideration of relevant testimony as superior because they enable shared inquiry and reduce domination. These norms are historically articulated, but their validity need not be limited to the contexts in which they first emerged.

## V. Can Epistemic Frameworks Transcend Their Origins?

The final issue is whether the rules governing the philosophy of religion can transcend their contextual origins, or whether all epistemic frameworks ultimately emerge from post-contextual ethical maxims. The phrase “post-contextual” is ambiguous. If it means principles formed after reflection upon particular contexts and then applied universally, it describes a plausible account of philosophical norms. If it means principles that somehow stand outside all history, language, embodiment, and social practice, it is likely impossible.

No human inquiry begins from nowhere. Even the demand for neutral evidence presupposes a community of investigators, practices of correction, concepts of objectivity, and ethical commitments to honesty and noncoercion. Yet the presence of origins does not prevent transcendence in a qualified sense. A norm can originate in a context and still become criticizable by standards that exceed the intentions of that context. The idea of universal human equality, for example, may arise within particular historical traditions while becoming a basis for criticizing those traditions’ exclusions. Its historical origin does not invalidate its universal significance.

Epistemic norms can transcend context through reflexive universalization. One asks whether a rule can be accepted by participants who do not already share the interests of the rule’s advocates; whether it permits criticism of its own application; whether it treats others as sources of reasons rather than objects to be managed; and whether it remains open to correction by experiences it initially excludes. Such criteria are not purely epistemic in a narrow sense. They are ethical and communicative. They concern the conditions under which inquiry can be shared without domination.

This is close to the lesson of Hegel’s dialectic. Consciousness does not achieve universality by simply rejecting particularity. It achieves a richer universality by passing through its own conflicts and recognizing what each standpoint contributes and distorts. The Enlightenment’s insistence on criticism must be preserved, but detached from the conceit that its own historical form exhausts reason. Faith’s concern with ultimate meaning and trust must be preserved, but translated beyond unexamined authority and private immediacy.

Should we therefore say that all epistemic frameworks ultimately emerge from ethical maxims? In one sense, yes. The choice of evidence, standards of testimony, and acceptable risk reflects values concerning what kinds of error matter, whom we trust, and how we distribute the costs of being wrong. A strict evidentialist fears gullibility and oppression; a religious epistemologist may fear spiritual blindness and the reduction of persons to objects. These fears encode ethical visions.

But it would be excessive to reduce all epistemology to ethics. Logical consistency, empirical adequacy, explanatory power, and responsiveness to counterexample are not merely disguised moral preferences. They constrain belief independently of what one wants morally. A theory that contradicts itself is defective even if it supports admirable conduct. A belief that fails every relevant test is not rescued simply by its ethical attractiveness. The relation is better described as co-constitution: epistemic norms have ethical presuppositions, while ethical commitments require epistemic discipline if they are to avoid becoming fantasy or ideology.

## Conclusion

The dispute between theism and atheism often becomes dialectically defective because each side presents its own conditions of rational acceptability as neutral and universal. Shalkowski’s critiques expose the weakness of treating parsimony, verifiability, and burden of proof as automatic grounds for the presumption of atheism. Parsimony requires a judgment about explanatory necessity; verifiability is not the only form of rational warrant; and burdens of proof shift according to the formulation and scope of the claims at issue. These points do not establish theism, but they prevent atheism from securing victory merely by occupying the rhetorical position of default.

Scriven’s defense of atheism is more persuasive when it emphasizes intellectual responsibility, resistance to unsupported assertion, and the practical virtues of public evidence. It becomes less persuasive when it treats everyday reasoning and sense-certainty as transparent measures of metaphysical reality. Ordinary reasoning is indispensable, but it is not epistemically innocent. It presupposes a world of stable objects, reliable perception, and shared practices, and it cannot by itself determine whether reality has a transcendent ground.

Hegel helps explain why the conflict persists. Faith and Enlightenment misunderstand one another when faith withdraws into unmediated certainty or when Enlightenment reduces faith to superstition and presents its own historical commitments as pure reason. Both can commit communicative failures by disguising ethical assumptions as epistemic principles. The task is not to eliminate normativity from inquiry, which is impossible, but to make normativity explicit and subject it to reciprocal criticism.

Epistemic frameworks cannot transcend context if transcendence means escaping all historical, linguistic, social, and ethical conditions. Yet they can transcend their origins through reflection, criticism, and universalization. They can become more adequate, more inclusive, and less coercive than the contexts from which they arose. Objective truth remains a regulative and ontological possibility even though our access to it is mediated by practices of justification. The truth about God, if there is one, is not manufactured by the rules of debate; but what we count as a responsible approach to that truth is shaped by rules whose origins are contextual and whose authority is partly ethical.

The mature philosophy of religion therefore cannot ask only whether theism or atheism has “the evidence.” It must also ask what conception of evidence is being used, what forms of life it protects or excludes, and whether the standards of inquiry can be defended to those who do not already share them. The debate becomes rational not when it discovers a view from nowhere, but when each side acknowledges the situated character of its rules while remaining answerable to a truth that is not reducible to either side’s preferences.
