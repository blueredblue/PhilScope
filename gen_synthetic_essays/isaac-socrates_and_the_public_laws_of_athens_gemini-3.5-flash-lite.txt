### Title: The Unity of Socrates: Law, Conscience, and the Limits of State Authority in Plato’s *Apology* and *Crito*

---

### Introduction

The tension between conscience and civil obedience stands as one of the foundational dilemmas of political philosophy. Nowhere is this tension more acutely dramatized than in the works of Plato, particularly in the juxtaposition between the *Apology* and the *Crito*. In the *Apology*, Socrates presents himself as a man under a divine mandate, commissioned by the Delphic Oracle to interrogate his fellow Athenians in the pursuit of wisdom, declaring defiantly that he will obey God rather than the citizens of Athens, and that he would continue philosophizing even if granted a conditional acquittal. Yet, in the *Crito*, penned against the backdrop of his impending execution, Socrates appears as an unyielding legalist. He argues that he is bound by an unbreakable social contract or agreement to obey the city’s laws, asserting that one must never do wrong, nor return wrong for wrong, even when suffering injustice, and that to evade his execution is to commit an act of parricide against the state.

Generations of scholars have diagnosed this juxtaposition as a profound contradiction, oscillating between the radical, civil-disobedient prophet of the *Apology* and the arch-conservative state apologist of the *Crito*. This essay will evaluate this alleged contradiction. I shall argue that a careful reconstruction of Socrates’ moral framework reveals a coherent, unified philosophy. 

First, I will examine the *Apology*, demonstrating that Socrates’ apparent civil disobedience is actually the fulfillment of a higher, divine law that does not necessarily invite lawlessness, but challenges the *administration* of laws when they demand the cessation of philosophical inquiry. 

Second, I will analyze the *Crito*, focusing on the argument from harm and the argument from just agreements, evaluating whether these arguments commit Socrates to an unconditional duty of obedience that invalidates his courtroom defiance. 

Third, I will resolve the apparent inconsistency by analyzing Socrates’ famous refusal to obey the Thirty Tyrants. By distinguishing between *de jure* constitutional authority and *de facto* tyrannical power, and by applying his unwavering meta-ethical maxim—that one must never commit an injustice—we can reconcile his resistance to the oligarchs with his submission to the democratic court. Ultimately, Socrates’ political obligation is not an unconditional duty to obey the state’s commands blindly, but a conditional duty grounded in justice, reciprocity, and a commitment to the rational improvement of the soul.

---

### Part I: The Divine Mandate and the Limits of Human Judgment in the *Apology*

To understand the character of Socrates’ political obligation, one must first confront the foundational premise of his defense in the *Apology*: his divine mission. Socrates does not present his philosophical life as a hobby, a professional choice, or a personal caprice; he frames it as a sacred service to Apollo. Recounting the story of Chaerephon’s query to the Delphic Oracle—who asked if any man was wiser than Socrates, and received the negative reply—Socrates explains that he understood this divine pronouncement as a riddle. Knowing himself to be wise in no significant measure, he set out to test the oracle by examining those reputed to be wise: politicians, poets, and craftsmen. This elenctic examination revealed that these individuals possessed a false conceit of knowledge, claiming wisdom they did not possess. 

Socrates concludes that his mission—to expose this ignorance and urge his fellow citizens to care for their souls rather than their bodies or property—is an obedience to the god: 

> "And I believe that no greater good has ever happened in the state than my service to the god. For I go about doing nothing other than persuading you, young and old alike, not to care for your bodies or your wealth in preference to or as strongly as for the best possible state of your soul" (*Apology* 30a–b).

Crucially, this divine mandate places Socrates in an immediate conflict with the political authority of Athens. When the jurors threaten to acquit him on the condition that he abandon his philosophical practice, Socrates delivers his famous counter-declaration: 

> "Men of Athens, I salute you and I love you, but I will obey God rather than you, and as long as I draw breath and am able, I shall not cease to practice philosophy..." (*Apology* 29d).

This passage is frequently read as an outright declaration of civil disobedience—a proclamation that an individual’s private religious or philosophical conscience trumps the legal verdicts of the state. If Socrates is willing to disobey a direct legal injunction to stop philosophizing, how can he later argue in the *Crito* that one must never disobey the state's legal verdicts?

To resolve this, we must examine the specific nature of Socrates’ resistance in the *Apology*. Socrates is not advocating for general lawlessness, nor is he claiming a right to pick and choose which laws to follow based on subjective whim. His defense rests on a hierarchical conception of authority: divine law (or the objective demand of virtue and wisdom) supersedes human enactment when the two irreconcilably collide. However, Socrates does not actively break the law and flee; rather, he stands trial, offers a rational defense, accepts the procedural legitimacy of the court, and refuses to compromise his moral integrity by agreeing to a corrupting restriction. 

Moreover, Socrates views his philosophical activity not as a harm to the state, but as its greatest benefit. He characterizes himself as the "gadfly" attached by the gods to the state, which is like "a great and noble horse who is rather sluggish because of his size and needs to be awakened by some sort of gadfly" (*Apology* 30e). From Socrates' perspective, the court's injunction to stop philosophizing is not a legitimate exercise of civic authority aiming at justice, but an ignorant, harmful aberration that commands him to commit an injustice—namely, to neglect the care of the soul and to disobey the god. 

Thus, the *Apology* establishes a crucial principle: human laws and legal verdicts possess no moral binding force when they command an individual to act unjustly or to violate their fundamental duty to pursue virtue. Yet, Socrates does not couple this principle with a strategy of evasion or violent resistance. He submits to the penalty imposed by the court, even an unjust death penalty, because he believes that committing an injustice is infinitely worse than suffering one. It is this exact commitment to non-maleficence that forms the bridge—and the potential battleground—between the *Apology* and the *Crito*.

### Part II: The *Crito*—Arguments from Harm and Just Agreements

In the *Crito*, Socrates is visited in prison by his wealthy friend Crito, who has arranged a plan for his escape. Crito’s appeals are pragmatic, emotional, and social: he worries about the loss of a irreplaceable friend, the damage to his own reputation among the many who will assume he was too cheap to bribe the guards, and the abandonment of Socrates’ parental duties to his sons. Socrates dismisses these considerations, insisting that they must be guided not by the opinion of the many (*hoi polloi*), but by the truth and by the judgments of those who possess understanding (*Apology* / *Crito* 47a–d). 

To evaluate Socrates’ refusal to escape, we must analyze the two central arguments presented by the personified Laws of Athens: **the Argument from Harm** (and destruction of the state) and **the Argument from Just Agreements** (tacit contract).

#### 1. The Argument from Harm
The Laws pose a devastating rhetorical question to Socrates: 

> "Do you think it possible for a city not to be destroyed if the verdicts of its courts have no force but are nullified and set at naught by private individuals?" (*Crito* 50b).

Socrates’ overarching moral axiom, maintained consistently across the dialogues, is that **one must never willingly do wrong (injustice), nor return wrong for wrong, no matter how one has been mistreated** (*Crito* 49a–d). Escaping from prison would constitute a direct violation of this principle in two ways. First, by illegally nullifying a legal verdict, Socrates would be striking a blow against the legal system itself, thereby threatening the city with dissolution. Because the laws and the state provide the very conditions for human flourishing, education, and social life, destroying them—or contributing to their subversion—is an act of extreme violence and harm against the community.

Second, Socrates argues that escaping would be an act of returning evil for evil. The Athenian court has treated him unjustly; it has misjudged the evidence and condemned an innocent man. Crito’s instinct is retaliatory or protective: *they* have done wrong to you, so you are justified in circumventing their injustice. Socrates rejects this utterly. To break out of prison is to break one’s word and to injure the Laws, thereby committing an injustice in return for an injustice. For Socrates, moral agency is strictly symmetrical regarding wrongdoing: one's own purity of soul must be maintained above all else, and committing an injustice is the ultimate corruption of the soul.

#### 2. The Argument from Just Agreements (The Social Contract)
The second and more elaborate argument rests on the nature of the relationship between the citizen and the state, articulated through the metaphor of a valid agreement or contract. The Laws argue that by living in Athens, enjoying its protection, marrying within its civic structures, educating his children there, and never having expressed a desire to emigrate, Socrates has entered into a tacit agreement with them:

> "We maintain that anyone who has stayed here, seeing how we administer justice and how we run the city in other ways, has in fact come to an agreement with us to do what we command" (*Crito* 51e).

This agreement is characterized by three alternatives: **obey or persuade, or emigrate**. A citizen who finds the laws unjust has ample opportunity to leave, taking their property with them. Alternatively, if they remain, they have a duty either to persuade the city to change its laws through lawful means or to obey whatever commands the city issues. To break this agreement by fleeing from prison after having lived a long, prosperous life under the protection of these very laws would be a betrayal. It would be akin to striking one's parents or rebelling against one's master; the state and the laws hold a higher, more foundational claim upon the citizen than biological parents.

#### Does the *Crito* Impose an Unconditional Duty?
This brings us to the critical evaluative question: Do these arguments commit Socrates to an *unconditional* duty of legal obedience that directly invalidates his defiance of the court in the *Apology*?

At first glance, the language of the *Crito* appears absolute. The Laws demand either persuasion or obedience. If persuasion fails (as it did during the trial), absolute obedience—even unto death—is mandated. If this is an unconditional duty, then Socrates’ declaration in the *Apology* ("I will obey God rather than you") is exposed as a rhetorical flourish or a direct contradiction. How can one simultaneously hold that a citizen must always obey or persuade, and yet declare that one will disobey a legal command to cease philosophizing?

To resolve this, we must look closely at the structure of the "obey or persuade" formula. The disjunction is not "obey blindly or submit passively"; it includes **persuasion**. Socrates *did* attempt to persuade the Athenian court throughout his trial. He presented his philosophical defense, argued for his innocence, and explained the divine nature of his mission. He engaged in the democratic process of persuasion, albeit unsuccessfully. 

However, what happens when persuasion fails and the state issues a command that requires the citizen to commit an *injustice*? This is where the profound nuance of Socrates’ framework emerges. The obligation to obey the laws is derivative of, and subordinate to, the higher obligation to **justice**. 

Let us trace this hierarchy:
1. The fundamental meta-ethical rule: **Never commit an injustice.**
2. The laws of Athens generally promote justice, order, and the conditions for a good life; therefore, one has a moral obligation to obey them (the social contract).
3. If the laws or the state command an individual to commit an injustice, that command lacks moral authority. 

In the *Apology*, the court commands Socrates to cease philosophizing—an act that would require him to disobey God and neglect the care of the soul, which is itself an injustice. Therefore, Socrates refuses to comply with that specific demand *during the trial process*, while still submitting himself to the legal jurisdiction and the ultimate penalty of the court. He does not evade the verdict; he accepts the sentence.

In the *Crito*, the question is not whether he should obey a command to stop philosophizing, but whether he should **break the law by escaping from prison**. Escaping would violate his tacit agreement, undermine the legal order, and commit a positive injustice against the state. Thus, while the duty to obey the laws is extremely strong, it is **conditional** upon those laws not compelling the citizen to commit an ultimate moral wrong—*and*, crucially, when the state imposes a punishment, the citizen must either persuade the state or suffer the penalty without resorting to illegal evasion, violence, or subversion.

Socrates’ stance is therefore not an unconditional legal positivism (where the law is valid simply because the sovereign decreed it), nor is it an individualistic anarchism (where private conscience overrides all laws at will). It is a **principled constitutionalism** grounded in virtue ethics: obey the laws because they are generally just and because you have agreed to them, but never commit an injustice, and accept the consequences when your duty to truth collides with the state's judicial errors.

### Part III: The Refusal to Obey the Thirty Tyrants

To test the coherence of this interpretation, we must examine a vital historical and biographical episode referenced in the *Apology*: Socrates’ refusal to obey the orders of the **Thirty Tyrants**. 

In 404 BCE, following Athens’ defeat in the Peloponnesian War, Sparta installed an oligarchic puppet regime known as the Thirty Tyrants. This regime instituted a reign of terror, executing political opponents and confiscating property. In the *Apology*, Socrates recounts an incident during this dark period:

> "The Thirty summoned five of us to the Tholos and ordered us to bring Leon of Salamis from Salamis to be executed. They gave many such orders to many others, wanting to implicate as many as possible in their guilt. Then I showed again, not in word but in deed, that death—if that were not too crude a word—means nothing to me, but that to commit no unjust or impious act is everything. That government, powerful as it was, did not frighten me into doing an unjust wrong; when we left the Tholos, the other four went to Salamis and brought Leon, but I went home" (*Apology* 32c–d).

This passage provides a crucial litmus test for Socrates’ political philosophy. Why is Socrates praised—and why does he praise himself—for *disobeying* the Thirty Tyrants, whereas in the *Crito* he condemns the idea of disobeying or evading the verdicts of the democratic court? Does this reveal an opportunistic double standard, or does it fit seamlessly into his moral framework?

#### Resolving the Apparent Inconsistency
Critics who accuse Socrates of inconsistency often argue that he obeys the democratic court because he likes democracy, and disobeys the oligarchic Thirty because he dislikes oligarchy. But this interpretation collapses under textual scrutiny. Socrates was no uncritical democrat; he was deeply critical of the democratic mob that condemned him, frequently expressing skepticism about the competence of the masses to rule. His refusal to obey the Thirty has nothing to do with their political label and everything to do with their **moral status and constitutional legitimacy**.

We can resolve the apparent inconsistency by analyzing three crucial distinctions between the democratic court and the Thirty Tyrants:

##### 1. De Jure Authority vs. De Facto Tyranny
The democratic court that judged Socrates in 399 BCE operated under the established, constitutional laws of Athens. Even if the jurors erred grievously, misjudged the facts, and rendered an unjust verdict, the institution itself was the lawful arbiter of the city. 

By contrast, the Thirty Tyrants were not a legitimate constitutional government established through lawful civic processes; they were an illegal, tyrannical faction hoisted upon Athens by a foreign military conqueror (Sparta) following a war. They possessed *de facto* physical power, but no *de jure* moral or legal authority. An individual has a social contract with a lawful state, but one cannot have a tacit contract of allegiance with a violent criminal gang that seizes power through terror.

##### 2. The Nature of the Command: Administration of Justice vs. Complicity in Murder
The democratic court commanded Socrates to stop philosophizing (an unjust demand, which he resisted by refusing to comply and accepting trial) and subsequently sentenced him to death (a verdict he accepted because he would not commit the injustice of escaping). 

The Thirty Tyrants commanded Socrates to participate directly in an act of state-sponsored murder: the illegal arrest of an innocent man, Leon of Salamis. Complying with this order would have required Socrates to commit a direct, undeniable act of injustice (*adikia*). 

As we established from the *Crito*, Socrates’ supreme moral maxim is that one must **never** commit an injustice. Therefore, when an illegitimate regime commands an act of manifest wickedness, disobedience is not merely permitted; it is morally mandatory. 

##### 3. Acceptance of Consequences vs. Perpetration of Evil
In both cases—the democratic trial and the encounter with the Thirty—Socrates’ behavior is governed by the same consistent principle. When the democratic court condemns him, he does not take up arms, start a civil war, or violently break out of prison; he accepts the legal process, speaks his truth, and is prepared to accept death rather than commit an injustice. 

When the Thirty Tyrants order him to commit murder, he simply goes home. He does not launch a revolution against them; he quietly refuses their criminal command, knowing full well that his defiance could result in his immediate execution (and he notes in the *Apology* that he likely would have been put to death had the regime not fallen shortly thereafter). 

Thus, Socrates’ response to the Thirty Tyrants and his response to the Athenian court are two sides of the exact same coin. He distinguishes sharply between:
* **Lawful authority** (which demands general obedience, even when it errs, up to the point where it commands direct injustice, and whose judicial penalties must be borne without illegal evasion).
* **Tyrannical usurpation** (which commands direct injustice and possesses no legitimate moral claim on the citizen’s obedience).

In neither case does Socrates ever commit an injustice. In both cases, he is willing to face death rather than stain his soul. Far from generating inconsistency, Socrates’ resistance to the Thirty Tyrants brilliantly illuminates the profound coherence of his moral universe: his loyalty is not to the men currently holding power, nor to the mere label of a political regime, but exclusively to **Justice** and the divine order of virtue.

---

### Part IV: Synthesis and Conclusion

The alleged contradiction between the *Apology* and the *Crito* dissolves when we abandon superficial readings of Socrates as either an absolute anarchist or an unconditional state-worshipper. Socrates is neither. He is a moral rationalist whose entire philosophical existence is anchored by a single, unshakeable axiom: **the care of the soul in the pursuit of virtue and truth is the highest human calling, and one must never, under any circumstance, commit an injustice.**

In the *Apology*, Socrates demonstrates that human political authorities—whether democratic juries or arrogant tyrants—overstep their moral boundaries when they command individuals to abandon their philosophical duty to seek truth and virtue. Socrates refuses to obey such injunctions, standing firm in his divine mandate. Yet, true to his principles, he does not resort to lawless violence or sneaky subversion; he submits to the legal procedures of the city and faces his accusers openly.

In the *Crito*, Socrates explicates the flip side of this coin. While the state may commit judicial errors and treat an individual unjustly, the citizen retains a profound, binding moral obligation—grounded in gratitude, reciprocity, and a tacit social contract—not to destroy the legal fabric of the community by illegally evading its verdicts. To break out of prison would be an act of returning evil for evil and striking a blow against the very laws that nurtured him. Therefore, he chooses to drink the hemlock, maintaining the purity of his soul rather than preserving his biological life through an unjust act.

Finally, his defiance of the Thirty Tyrants reinforces this entire framework. By refusing to participate in the murder of Leon of Salamis, Socrates proves that his obedience to Athens was never an uncritical surrender to whoever held power, but a principled allegiance to lawful, constitutional order directed toward justice. When confronted with a tyrannical gang demanding direct complicity in evil, he refused; when confronted with the flawed, tragic, but lawful verdict of his democratic city, he accepted his punishment rather than commit a counter-injustice.

Through this rigorous moral lens, Plato presents in Socrates not a contradictory bundle of rhetorical postures, but a seamlessly integrated philosophical life. Socrates teaches us that true political obligation is neither blind obedience nor self-serving lawlessness, but a lifelong, courageous dialogue with justice—a dialogue in which the citizen must respect the rule of law, honor the integrity of the state, and yet remain unfailingly obedient to that higher divine voice which commands us to examine our lives, care for our souls, and never, under any provocation, commit a wrong.
