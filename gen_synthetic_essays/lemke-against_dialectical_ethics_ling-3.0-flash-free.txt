**The Sacrifice of the Feminine and the Limits of Conceptualization: Kelly Oliver’s Critique of Hegel and Shari Neller Starrett’s Defense of Hegelian Critical Relations**

G.W.F. Hegel’s dialectical system represents one of the most ambitious attempts in Western philosophy to render the entirety of reality fully conceptualizable. From the *Phenomenology of Spirit* to the *Philosophy of Right*, Hegel constructs an all-encompassing architecture of Spirit (*Geist*) that moves through a series of necessary shapes—consciousness, self-consciousness, reason, spirit, and the absolute—until reality is entirely comprehended within the bounds of rational thought. Yet, this very ambition has drawn sustained feminist critique, most notably from Kelly Oliver, whose work exposes the gendered costs of Hegel’s dialectical machinery. Oliver’s critique focuses on Hegel’s treatment of the Family, particularly the structural assignment of women to the unconscious realm of divine law through the symbolic mechanism of burial rites. This essay evaluates Oliver’s claim that Hegel’s dialectical system requires the sacrifice of the feminine to achieve the transition from the immediate unity of the Family to the conscious universality of civil society. It further examines whether this structural necessity undermines Hegel’s overarching project of making all reality fully conceptualizable. Finally, it contrasts Oliver’s critique with Shari Neller Starrett’s defense of Hegelian Critical Relations, determining whether an ongoing dialogical tension between family and community can successfully preserve an empowering ethics without collapsing into traditional Hegelian sublation.

Oliver’s critique is rooted in a meticulous reading of Hegel’s *Phenomenology of Spirit* and *Philosophy of Right*, where the development of Spirit is intimately tied to the dissolution of immediate, natural bonds. For Hegel, the Family represents the first shape of ethical life (*Sittlichkeit*), an immediate unity founded on love (*Liebe*). Within the Family, individuals are not yet distinct, self-conscious subjects; they are moments of a larger whole, absorbed into the universal feeling of the family bond. However, the dialectic demands that this immediate unity be negated. The Family must dissolve so that its members can enter the realm of civil society, where individuals operate as free, self-conscious, and universal subjects under the rule of law and contract.

It is at this point of dissolution that Oliver identifies a profound structural violence directed at the feminine. As the Family transitions into civil society, its immediate unity must be "buried." Oliver draws attention to the fact that in Hegel’s system, women are structurally assigned to the unconscious realm of the divine law that governs the Family. In *Philosophy of Right*, Hegel explicitly states that women are excluded from full participation in the universal, civic, and political spheres, remaining tethered to the particular, immediate, and feeling-based life of the household. The "burial rites" of the dialectic, therefore, are not merely the end of the family as an economic and social unit; they are the symbolic interment of the feminine into the unconscious. Women are left with the "feeling" of the immediate divine unity—the maternal, the emotional, the particular—which is relegated to the background of conscious, rational life. 

The assignment of women to the unconscious realm of divine law is not an incidental historical bias for Oliver; it is a structural necessity of Hegel’s dialectic. The transition to civil society requires the emergence of the conscious, universal subject who can recognize and be recognized by other such subjects. This requires the abandonment of the immediate, feeling-based unity of the family. The feminine, as the embodiment of this immediate unity, must be sacrificed to the unconscious so that the universal can become conscious. The feminine is sacrificed because it represents the unmediated, the particular, and the emotional—all of which must be negated and left behind for Spirit to progress toward self-consciousness and rational autonomy. In Oliver’s terms, the feminine is the necessary Other against which the masculine, conscious subject defines itself. The dialectic requires a sacrifice, and the feminine provides the fuel for its upward trajectory.

This structural necessity raises a critical question: does it undermine the overarching Hegelian project of making all reality fully conceptualizable? Hegel’s system aims to be total. The Absolute Idea is the concept that comprehends everything; nothing is to be left outside the circle of reason. The dialectic is the mechanism by which the irrational, the immediate, and the unconscious are dragged into the light of conceptual thought. Yet, Oliver’s analysis reveals a devastating paradox at the heart of this project. If the feminine must be relegated to the unconscious realm of divine law for the dialectic to proceed, then the system depends upon an unconceptualized remainder. The feminine, as the unconscious maternal and the feeling of the immediate, becomes the excluded outside that makes the conceptualized inside possible. 

The "sacrifice of the feminine" thus exposes the incompleteness of Hegel’s system. By structurally assigning women to the unconscious, Hegel ensures that a significant portion of human reality—the lived experience, relationality, and affectivity associated with the feminine—remains conceptually inaccessible. The system cannot make all reality fully conceptualizable because it requires the feminine to remain unconceptualized as the unconscious ground of its own movement. The Absolute Idea is not truly absolute if it relies on an unconscious, unconceptualized residue to fuel its own self-realization. Oliver’s critique demonstrates that Hegel’s dialectic is not a neutral engine of rational progression, but a patriarchal apparatus that systematically sacrifices the feminine to the unconscious, thereby proving that his project of total conceptualization is fundamentally compromised by its own structural necessities.

In stark contrast to Oliver’s devastating critique, Shari Neller Starrett offers a defense of Hegelian Critical Relations that attempts to salvage an empowering ethics from the dialectical framework. Starrett argues that Hegel’s dialectic does not have to be understood as a zero-sum game of sublation (*Aufhebung*), where the immediate is absolutely negated and consumed by the universal. Instead, she defends an interpretation of Hegel that emphasizes the constitutive role of ongoing, dialogical relations. For Starrett, the relationship between the Family and the Community is not a linear progression where the former must be entirely destroyed for the latter to emerge. Rather, it is a critical, dialogical tension that can be maintained without collapsing into traditional sublation.

Starrett’s Hegelian Critical Relations draws on the insight that the family and the community are mutually constitutive. The community needs the family to provide the affective, particular bonds that ground the universal, while the family needs the community to sustain itself against the particularizing tendencies of immediate love. This mutual dependence creates a dialogical tension—a dynamic, ongoing interplay between the particular and the universal—that does not require the sacrifice of the feminine. In this framework, the feminine is not relegated to the unconscious realm of divine law; rather, the feminine is an active, resisting subject within the dialogical relation. The feeling and the immediate are not buried; they are preserved as necessary counterweights to the rational and the universal.

The crucial question is whether this ongoing dialogical tension can successfully preserve an empowering ethics without collapsing into traditional Hegelian sublation. Traditional Hegelian sublation is a mechanism of closure: the thesis (the Family) is negated by the antithesis (Civil Society), and the synthesis (the State) preserves the truth of both while elevating them to a higher, more rational unity. Sublation is inherently hierarchical; it resolves tension by absorbing the lower into the higher, thereby erasing the particularity of the negated moment. The empowering ethics of Starrett’s dialogical tension relies on the refusal of this closure. It insists that the tension between the family and the community must remain open, that the particular must not be absolutely negated by the universal.

Starrett’s defense is compelling precisely because it refuses the sacrificial logic that Oliver exposes. By maintaining the dialogical tension, the feminine is not sacrificed to the unconscious; instead, it is recognized as an essential, irreducible moment of ethical life. The empowering ethics emerges from this recognition: individuals are not forced to abandon their particular, affective bonds to become abstract, universal subjects. Rather, they are invited to navigate the tension between the particular and the universal, finding their freedom within the relational field rather than despite it. This is a radical departure from the traditional Hegelian model, which demands that the individual surrender their immediate, natural ties to the universal law of the State.

However, the viability of Starrett’s defense hinges on whether the dialogical tension can truly resist the structural pull of sublation. Hegel’s dialectic is inherently teleological; it moves toward the Absolute, a final synthesis where all contradictions are resolved. The structural necessity of Hegel’s system is to overcome negation, to resolve tension, to arrive at the finished concept. If the dialogical tension is merely a temporary pause on the way to a higher synthesis, then Starrett’s defense collapses back into traditional sublation. The tension would be preserved only as a moment to be overcome, and the empowering ethics would ultimately be subsumed by the universalizing logic of the State. 

To avoid this collapse, Starrett must fundamentally reinterpret the dialectic away from its Hegelian teleology. She must treat the dialogical tension not as a moment in a progressive march toward the Absolute, but as an open-ended, ongoing condition of ethical life. This requires a critical break from Hegel’s system, treating it not as a final metaphysical account but as a heuristic for understanding the perpetual negotiation between the particular and the universal. If the dialogical tension is understood as an open-ended practice rather than a closed logical necessity, then it can successfully preserve an empowering ethics. The tension between family and community becomes a site of continuous ethical production, where the feminine is not sacrificed but continually re-articulated in relation to the universal.

Yet, even if we grant Starrett this reinterpretation, we must ask whether an "ongoing dialogical tension" is sufficient to sustain an empowering ethics without some form of closure. Ethics, by its nature, requires a certain degree of resolution; without norms or boundaries, the tension between the particular and the universal can devolve into an unmanageable relativism where the universal is entirely consumed by the particular. Starrett’s defense must navigate this risk carefully. If the tension is never resolved, the ethical life of the community may remain fragmented, unable to enact the universal rights and laws necessary for true freedom. Conversely, if the tension is resolved through sublation, the empowering ethics is compromised by the sacrifice of the feminine. Starrett’s framework, therefore, walks a tightrope between the Scylla of sublative closure and the Charybdis of unresolved fragmentation.

Ultimately, the contrast between Oliver’s critique and Starrett’s defense reveals the deep structural fissures within Hegel’s system. Oliver demonstrates that the traditional Hegelian dialectic is built upon a foundational sacrifice: the relegation of the feminine to the unconscious realm of divine law through the symbolic burial of the family’s immediate unity. This structural necessity fatally undermines Hegel’s overarching project of making all reality fully conceptualizable, as it proves that the system depends upon an unconceptualized, excluded outside to fuel its own rational progression. The Absolute Idea is not truly absolute if it must perpetually sacrifice the feminine to remain conceptually pure.

Starrett’s defense of Hegelian Critical Relations offers a path forward, but it is a path that requires a fundamental revision of Hegel’s teleological framework. By insisting on an ongoing dialogical tension between family and community, Starrett preserves the empowering potential of an ethics rooted in relation rather than sublation. She refuses to bury the feminine in the unconscious, instead keeping the particular and the universal in a dynamic, productive interplay. However, this dialogical tension can only successfully preserve an empowering ethics if we abandon the Hegelian drive toward a final, sublative synthesis. It requires us to accept that the dialectic is not a closed system arriving at the Absolute, but an open, critical process of continuous negotiation.

In conclusion, Kelly Oliver’s critique exposes the profound gendered cost of Hegel’s dialectical system: the structural necessity of sacrificing the feminine to the unconscious realm of divine law in order to achieve the transition to conscious civil society. This sacrifice reveals that Hegel’s project of total conceptualization is inherently compromised by its reliance on the unconceptualized. Shari Neller Starrett’s defense of Hegelian Critical Relations provides a vital counter-narrative, arguing that an ongoing dialogical tension can preserve an empowering ethics without collapsing into traditional sublation. Yet, the success of Starrett’s defense depends entirely on our willingness to treat the dialectic not as a closed system of absolute knowing, but as an open, dialogical practice that refuses the sacrificial logic of the Hegelian synthesis. It is only by maintaining this tension—by keeping the family and the community in a perpetual, unresolved dialogue—that we can hope to construct an ethics that does not require the sacrifice of the feminine to achieve its universalizing aims.
