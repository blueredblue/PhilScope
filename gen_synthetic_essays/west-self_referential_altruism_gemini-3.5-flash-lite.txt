# The Mirror and the Other: Personal Friendship, Self-Referential Altruism, and the Structural Paradox of *Eudaimonia* in Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics*

### Introduction

At the heart of the Aristotelian ethical project lies a profound tension regarding the nature of human relationality. In books VIII and IX of the *Nicomachean Ethics* (*EN*), Aristotle elevates friendship (*philia*) from a mere utility or pleasant diversion to an indispensable component of the good life (*eudaimonia*). Famously declaring that "no one would choose to live without friends, even if he had all other goods" (*EN* 1155a5–6), Aristotle assigns to friendship an architectonic role in human flourishing. Yet, this elevation occurs within an ethical framework ostensibly anchored in agent-relative flourishing. The ultimate end of human action is the realization of the agent’s own potential, the perfection of the agent’s own soul, and the contemplation of the agent’s own intellect. 

This structural alignment has led modern commentators to interrogate the integrity of Aristotelian *philia*, pressing a central diagnostic question: Is personal friendship in the *Nicomachean Ethics* ultimately an instance of **self-referential altruism**? 

To probe this claim is to investigate whether Aristotle collapses other-regarding concern into self-regarding acquisition, treating the friend merely as a mirror through which the virtuous agent contemplates and stabilizes their own excellence. The tension is acute. On one hand, Aristotle insists that complete friendship involves loving the friend *for the friend's own sake*—a formulation that appears to preempt charges of crude egoism and establish a robust baseline of genuine altruism. On the other hand, the foundational mechanics of *philia* are inextricably bound to self-love (*philautia*). The friend is defined as "another self" (*heteros autos*), and the activities of friendship are justified, at least in part, by their necessity for the agent’s own self-knowledge, emotional sustenance, and continuous virtuous activity. 

This essay evaluates the claim that Aristotelian friendship is fundamentally self-referential altruism. It argues that while Aristotle successfully dissolves the crude modern dichotomy between pure egoism and pure altruism, his synthesis remains teleologically tethered to the agent's *eudaimonia*. Consequently, what appears to be other-regarding love is structurally integrated into a comprehensive project of self-actualization. However, far from rendering friendship hypocritical or instrumental, this integration reveals a sophisticated psychology in which self-concern and other-concern are not opposed, but co-constitutive. Nevertheless, significant limitations emerge: by restricting the horizon of deep altruistic concern to a closed circle of moral peers and family, Aristotle exposes the particularistic and exclusionary nature of his framework, leaving the *eudaimon* agent vulnerable to charges of an elevated, yet insular, self-absorption.

### I. The Architectural Necessity of Friendship in the Pursuit of *Eudaimonia*

To understand the place of friendship in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, one must first situate it within Aristotle’s broader teleological framework. *Eudaimonia* is the highest human good: that which is chosen always for itself and never for the sake of something else (*EN* 1097a34–b6). It is defined as an activity of the soul in accordance with complete virtue over a complete life (*EN* 1101a14–16). Because humans are by nature political and social animals (*politikon zoon*), this flourishing cannot be achieved in isolation. 

Aristotle initiates his treatise on friendship by noting its ubiquity and indispensability. It holds cities together, is deemed greater than justice itself, and accompanies every stage and station of life. Whether one is rich or poor, young or old, powerful or destitute, friendship is represented as the ultimate external good. Yet, the inclusion of friendship among external goods creates an immediate philosophical friction. If *eudaimonia* is an activity of the soul—something internal, self-sufficient (*autarkeia*), and dependent primarily on the agent’s own virtuous disposition—why should it require external entities like friends?

Aristotle addresses this by redefining self-sufficiency. He explicitly argues that self-sufficiency does not mean a solitary, ascetic existence:
> "By self-sufficient we do not mean that which suffices for a man by himself, for one who lives a solitary life, but also for parents, children, wife, and in general for his friends and fellow citizens, since man is naturally a social being." (*EN* 1097b8–11)

The *autarkeia* of the *eudaimon* agent is not atomistic; rather, it is structurally relational. To live well requires the exercise of the virtues, and virtues are inherently practical—they manifest in relation to others. One cannot exercise generosity without someone to receive the gift, nor justice without a framework of interpersonal transactions. Friendship provides the primary arena for the actualization of virtue. 

More pressingly, Aristotle introduces the paradox of the happy person and the need for friends in *EN* IX.9. Why would the supremely happy person, who possesses all internal and external goods, need friends? The conventional view might suggest that friends are needed in times of misfortune for assistance, or in times of prosperity for the pleasant deployment of leisure. Aristotle rejects these utilitarian reductions as inadequate for the best kind of friendship. Instead, he grounds the necessity of friends in the very nature of conscious human agency and activity:
> "If it is pleasant to realize that we are good, and we can contemplate our neighbours better than ourselves and their actions better than our own, then the actions of virtuous men who are their friends are pleasant to good men..." (*EN* 1169b31–35)

Here, the foundational motivation for having friends begins to reveal its self-referential character. The virtuous person desires friends not merely because the friend suffers or thrives, but because the friend functions as an epistemological and perceptual catalyst. Human beings, due to the structural limitations of self-consciousness, struggle to perceive their own lives, actions, and goodness directly. We see others more clearly than we see ourselves. The friend acts as a distinct yet intimately connected locus of observation, enabling the agent to apprehend virtuous activity in a concrete, externalized form. 

Thus, friendship is architecturally necessary for *eudaimonia* because *eudaimonia* is an *activity*, and the most complete human activity requires shared perception, shared conversation, and shared living (*synzen*). However, this relational necessity immediately raises the question of motivational purity: does the agent love the friend for the friend's own sake, or does the agent love the friend because the friend is instrumentally or constitutively required for the agent’s own flourishing?

### II. The Taxonomy of Friendship and the Problem of "Loving for the Friend’s Sake"

To evaluate the charge of self-referential altruism, one must examine Aristotle’s famous tripartite typology of friendship in *EN* VIII.3. Aristotle distinguishes friendships based on the object of affection: friendships of utility, friendships of pleasure, and friendships of character (or complete friendship).

In friendships of utility, the parties do not love one another for who they are intrinsically, but insofar as they derive some material or practical benefit from the association. Similarly, in friendships of pleasure, the affection is directed toward the wit, charm, or agreeable disposition of the other, rather than their moral identity. Both of these lower forms are inherently unstable and accidental, dissolving the moment the utility or pleasure ceases. They are transparently self-regarding: the friend is an instrument for the satisfaction of an external desire.

Complete friendship (*teleia philia*), by contrast, is characterized by the mutual recognition of virtue:
> "Those who wish well to their friends for their sake are most truly friends; for they do this by reason of own nature and not by accident. Therefore their friendship lasts as long as they are good—and virtue is an enduring thing." (*EN* 1156b9–12)

This formulation—wishing well to a friend *for the friend's own sake* (*di' hautous*)—is the cornerstone of Aristotle's claim to transcend egoism. It explicitly separates complete friendship from utilitarian calculations. When I possess a complete friend, my desires are not exhausted by what the friend can *do* for me. I recognize their intrinsic worth as a moral agent, rejoice in their excellence, and desire their good even at a cost to myself.

Yet, this is precisely where the philosophical tension deepens. How are we to reconcile the demand to love the friend "for the friend's own sake" with Aristotle’s assertion that friendship is necessary for the agent's *own* *eudaimonia*? 

Commentators have taken divergent paths in resolving this tension. Broadly speaking, interpretations fall into two camps: the **Instrumental/Egoistic reading** and the **Inclusive/Harmonious reading**.

#### The Instrumental or Egoistic Reading
Proponents of this view (often influenced by modern analytical critiques of ancient ethics) argue that despite Aristotle’s rhetoric of "for the friend's sake," Aristotelian friendship remains fundamentally egoistic. On this account, the ultimate reason the agent loves the friend is that having a friend—and engaging in the activities of complete friendship—is a necessary constituent of the agent's *own* flourishing. The friend's well-being is valued not as an independent, terminal end, but as a subordinate means or constituent part of the agent's master-project: achieving *eudaimonia*. 

Under this interpretation, "loving the friend for the friend's own sake" is not a denial of self-interest, but a sophisticated, long-range strategy of self-realization. Because human beings are social and rational, we *cannot* flourish without exercising the other-regarding virtues within a shared life. Therefore, valuing the friend for their own sake is psychologically required in order to reap the egoistic rewards of a fully realized human life. The altruism is "self-referential" because the ultimate beneficiary of the love is always, strictly speaking, the agent themselves.

#### The Inclusive or Harmonious Reading
Conversely, defenders of Aristotle argue that this critique relies on a modern, post-Kantian false dichotomy between egoism and altruism—a dichotomy foreign to Greek moral psychology. In ancient eudaimonism, the question is not "Should I pursue my own interests or someone else's?" but rather "What does a well-lived human life consist of?" 

On the inclusive reading, the good of the friend and the good of the agent are not locked in a zero-sum conflict. For the virtuous person, the friend's welfare has been integrated into their own conception of the good life. To say that I value my friend's good for their own sake is entirely compatible with saying that my life is better—and my *eudaimonia* more complete—precisely because I am capable of such unselfish devotion. 

Aristotle does not view the self as a bounded, atomistic psychological unit whose interests are strictly distinct from those of others. Rather, in complete friendship, the boundaries of the self are expanded. This brings us directly to Aristotle's most radical psychological concept: the friend as "another self."

### III. The Friend as "Another Self" (*Heteros Autos*) and the Psychology of *Philautia*

To grasp how Aristotle bridges the gap between self-concern and other-concern, one must analyze his treatment of the friend as *heteros autos* ("another self") and his defense of proper self-love (*philautia*) in *EN* IX.4 and IX.8.

In *EN* IX.4, Aristotle explores the psychological markers of friendship, noting that all the feelings that characterize friendship toward others—wishing goods, wishing life and preservation, taking pleasure in shared experiences, sharing griefs and joys—are derived from the attitudes a person holds toward themselves. He writes that "the friend is another self" (*EN* 1166a31–32). This phrase is frequently misunderstood as a poetic metaphor for psychological merging, but in its Aristotelian context, it carries precise metaphysical and psychological weight.

Just as an individual perceives their own existence as good and desirable, they perceive the existence of their complete friend in the same manner. The friend’s consciousness, agency, and moral identity are recognized as structurally analogous to one's own. Because the virtuous agent regards their own life as worth living and their own moral activity as choice-worthy, extending that exact valuation to the friend is a natural consequence of recognizing another rational agent who embodies virtue.

This brings us to the pivotal role of *philautia* (self-love) in *EN* IX.8. Aristotle addresses a common linguistic and moral prejudice: the condemnation of self-love as base and selfish (*philautoi* is typically used pejoratively to describe those who assign to themselves the lion's share of wealth, honors, and bodily pleasures). Aristotle introduces a vital distinction between two kinds of self-love:
1. **Vicious Self-Love:** Exhibited by the mass of mankind, who gratify their irrational appetites, passions, and base desires, yielding to the rule of the non-rational parts of the soul.
2. **Virtuous Self-Love:** Exhibited by the person who loves and gratifies the *master part* of themselves—the intellect (*nous*) and the rational soul—by consistently choosing what is noble (*kalon*), just, and virtuous, even if it requires sacrificing money, status, or life itself for the sake of their friends or country.

Aristotle's defense of virtuous self-love is breathtakingly paradoxical to modern ears: the supremely virtuous person *must* be a lover of self above all others. They must desire the noble above all things and seek to maximize the exercise of virtue in their own life. 

> "And so the good man should be a lover of self, for he will both profit himself by doing noble acts and benefit his fellows; but the wicked man should not be, since he will follow his evil passions..." (*EN* 1169a12–15)

How does this vindication of self-love illuminate the problem of self-referential altruism? It reveals that for Aristotle, **altruism is not the negation of self-love; it is its highest expression.** 

When the virtuous agent acts altruistically—when they surrender their money, endure hardships, or even die for the sake of a friend—they are not acting against their own best interest. Rather, they are actualizing their commitment to the *kalon* (the noble). To let a friend perish, or to fail to support them, would be a failure of virtue, a corruption of the agent's own soul, and a betrayal of their own identity as a noble person. Therefore, the agent benefits the friend *because* doing so fulfills the agent's own character requirement to be noble. The ultimate motivation remains tied to the perfection of the agent’s own soul: "He gives himself the greater good; for it is noble to appropriate the larger share of nobility" (*EN* 1169a34–35).

This is the crux of the self-referential altruism claim. The altruistic act—giving up one's life or goods for the friend—is performed because it is the *noble* thing to do, and the acquisition of nobility is the supreme self-regarding goal of the *eudaimon* agent. The friend is the necessary occasion and the direct recipient of this noble action, but the formal cause of the action is the agent's realization of their own moral excellence.

### IV. False Dichotomy or Genuine Paradox? Evaluating the Modern Critique

Having mapped the Aristotelian mechanics, we must now evaluate the core philosophical question: Does this synthesis successfully overcome the false dichotomy of pure egoism and pure altruism, or does the persistent presence of self-seeking elements undermine the unselfish nature of good friendship?

To answer this, we must examine the assumptions underpinning the critique. The objection that Aristotelian friendship is "merely" self-referential altruism presupposes a modern, post-Enlightenment taxonomy of motivation, largely inherited from Kantian deontology and British utilitarianism. In these modern frameworks, an action possesses moral worth *only if* it is divorced from self-interest. Kantian ethics demands that duty be executed purely for the sake of duty, untainted by inclinations, emotional resonance, or personal fulfillment. Utilitarianism, conversely, demands the maximization of aggregate pleasure or preference-satisfaction, often treating the agent as an interchangeable node in a calculus of universal welfare.

Within these paradigms, Aristotelian ethics looks hopelessly compromised. If Aristotle’s virtuous agent loves a friend because doing so constitutes their own *eudaimonia*, then the friend is ostensibly being used as a means to the agent's flourishing. The Kantian critic cries foul: the friend is treated as a means, not as an end in themselves.

Yet, applying Kantian or utilitarian categories to Aristotle is anachronistic and profoundly distorts the Greek ethical landscape. For Aristotle, the opposition between "my own flourishing" and "my friend's welfare" is not absolute, because the human good is not conceived as a private psychological state (such as feeling happy or having my preferences met) that I can hoard in isolation. *Eudaimonia* is an *activity*—specifically, the activity of the soul expressing virtue. 

Consider what happens when a virtuous agent acts on behalf of a friend. The agent does not calculate: *"If I help my friend, I will gain points toward my own eudaimonia."* That would be the behavior of a calculating utilitarian or a crude egoist, which Aristotle explicitly denounces. Instead, the agent perceives that the friend is in need, recognizes the friend's intrinsic worth, and acts directly out of love (*philia*) and a sense of nobility. The thought process is genuinely other-regarding: *"My friend is suffering/needs support; I must help them."* 

The self-referential aspect of the action is *structural* and *teleological*, not psychological or motivational. It is a feature of the metaphysical framework of the ethics, not a description of the agent’s day-to-day phenomenological state of mind. Just as a healthy organism acts to maintain its health—not by calculating how health will benefit it, but by exercising its biological functions—the virtuous agent acts virtuously because that is what it means to be a functioning, flourishing human being.

Therefore, Aristotle successfully overcomes the crude dichotomy between egoism and altruism by proposing an agent-relative framework where **other-regarding love is constitutive of self-actualization**. I cannot flourish unless I love others for their own sake; if I were incapable of loving a friend for their own sake, my soul would be deficient, and I would fail to achieve *eudaimonia*. Consequently, other-regarding altruism and self-realization are bound together in a double helix: you cannot climb to the heights of human excellence without reaching out to the "other self."

### V. The Limitations of Aristotelian *Philia*: Particularism, Exclusion, and the Bounded Self

While Aristotle’s synthesis successfully bridges the gap between self-concern and other-concern within his specific framework, evaluating his claim requires confronting the severe structural limitations of his model. The most significant vulnerability of Aristotelian friendship lies in its **particularism and exclusivity**.

Aristotle’s ethics of *philia* is intensely elitist. Complete friendship is not for everyone; it is exclusively reserved for moral peers—those who are already good and alike in virtue. Aristotle explicitly notes that such friendships are rare because bad people cannot be complete friends (they lack stability, cannot trust one another, and are not lovable in themselves) and good people take time to know and test:
> "Those who quickly show the marks of friendship to each other wish to be friends, but are not friends unless they are also worthy of being loved and know each other; for a wish for friendship may arise quickly, but friendship does not." (*EN* 1156b28–32)

Furthermore, the number of complete friends one can have is strictly limited. Because a complete friend is "another self," and sharing a life requires deep, continuous, face-to-face interaction (*synzen*), one can only maintain such an intense relationship with a very small circle of people:
> "One cannot be a great friend to many people... therefore neither can one have many friends to whom one can apply this standard." (*EN* 1158a11–14)

What are the implications of this bounded, particularistic horizon for the claim of self-referential altruism? 

It reveals that the altruism practiced in Aristotelian friendship is radically circumscribed. The agent feels deep, unselfish concern, but only for those who mirror their own excellence—those who belong to the privileged in-group of moral and social elites. Beyond this narrow circle of friends and family lies a vast expanse of humanity toward whom the Aristotelian agent owes little to no deep altruistic duty. Strangers, non-citizens, slaves, and enemies do not evoke the response of *philia*. While general justice requires treating them fairly according to civic laws, they are not objects of love or mutual self-actualization.

This exposes the deep self-referentiality of the entire project. If the friend is valued because they are "another self," and the self that is mirrored is the *virtuous, elite Greek male agent*, then the circle of altruism is little more than an expanded mirror-room. The agent loves the friend because the friend reflects back to the agent the agent's own image of moral nobility. The other-regarding concern is ultimately validated by its capacity to confirm and sustain the agent's self-image.

In contrast to universalizing modern ethical frameworks—such as Kantian deontology, which demands respect for the rational agency of *all* human beings, or utilitarianism, which counts every sentient being's welfare equally—Aristotelian *philia* is structurally partial. It is an aristocratic ethic of friendship. The altruism it fosters is genuine, deep, and capable of supreme self-sacrifice, but it is fundamentally tribal and self-referential in its ultimate teleological orientation.

---

### Conclusion

The claim that personal friendship in the *Nicomachean Ethics* constitutes **self-referential altruism** captures both the brilliance and the limitation of Aristotle’s moral psychology. 

By defining complete friendship as loving the friend "for the friend's own sake," Aristotle establishes a robust form of genuine other-regarding care that resists reduction to crude utilitarianism or psychological egoism. The virtuous agent genuinely desires the friend's good, takes joy in their existence, and is willing to lay down their life for the noble ends of friendship. 

Yet, this altruism is inextricably bound to a comprehensive project of self-actualization. The friend is the epistemological mirror through which the agent contemplates their own goodness; the friend is the vital arena for the actualization of virtue; and the friend is structurally conceived as "another self" (*heteros autos*) whose well-being is harmonized with the agent's own supreme pursuit of *eudaimonia*. Far from being a contradiction, this integration reflects Aristotle’s profound insight that human flourishing is inherently relational: we cannot become fully ourselves without stepping outside ourselves to embrace an "other self."

Ultimately, Aristotle’s synthesis successfully overcomes the false modern dichotomy between pure egoism and pure altruism by demonstrating that self-love and other-love are mutually reinforcing dimensions of a well-lived life. However, by restricting this sublime synthesis to a closed circle of moral peers and embedding it within a teleology of elite self-perfection, Aristotle reveals the boundaries of his vision. Aristotelian *philia* is a magnificent achievement of classical philosophy, but it remains an altruism of the mirror—deep, transformative, and noble, yet stubbornly turned inward toward the flourishing soul of the agent.
