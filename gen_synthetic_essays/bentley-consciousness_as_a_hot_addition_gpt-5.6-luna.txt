# Object Awareness, Fact Awareness, and the Higher-Order Basis of Consciousness

## Introduction

A central question in the philosophy of mind is what it means for a mental state to be conscious. The question is not merely whether an organism is conscious in the broad sense of being awake, responsive, or capable of experience. It concerns *state consciousness*: what makes a particular perceptual, bodily, emotional, or cognitive state a conscious state rather than an unconscious one? A higher-order theory of consciousness gives a distinctive answer. A mental state becomes conscious when the subject has a suitable higher-order representation of it—roughly, when the subject is aware of being in that state. On this view, consciousness is not exhausted by a first-order relation to an object. It requires a further relation in which the mind represents one of its own states.

The claim considered here is that state consciousness requires both object awareness and fact awareness. Object awareness consists in a subject’s direct acquaintance with an object or property: seeing a red apple, feeling pain, or hearing a tone. Fact awareness is awareness that some fact obtains—for example, awareness that there is a red apple before one, or that one is in pain. In a higher-order thought (HOT) framework, fact awareness is not necessarily linguistic or explicitly conceptual. It is a suitable higher-order representation of a first-order mental state. The central issue is whether such a HOT is strictly necessary for a first-order state to be conscious.

Derek Browne’s objection, drawing on Fred Dretske’s distinction between object awareness and fact awareness, challenges the higher-order thesis. Browne argues that object awareness may be sufficient for consciousness and that fact awareness is unnecessary. If a subject is directly acquainted with an object, then the corresponding perceptual state is conscious, even if the subject has no further awareness that it is perceiving the object. Blindsight and unconscious driving appear to support this possibility. A blindsighted subject can respond appropriately to objects in the blind field without consciously seeing them, while an ordinary driver can navigate a familiar route with little or no conscious awareness of the detailed perceptual and motor states guiding the activity. These cases might suggest that object-directed mental states can be conscious without being accompanied by a HOT.

This essay argues that Browne’s objection identifies a genuine pressure point for HOT theories but does not establish that object awareness alone is sufficient. The decisive distinction is between an organism’s being appropriately connected to an object and its having a conscious presentation of that object. Blindsight shows that object-directed processing can occur without conscious awareness; it therefore tends to support, rather than refute, the need for a higher-order condition. Unconscious driving shows that sophisticated behavior and even extensive first-order representation can proceed without conscious states, though it also raises questions about the scope of the HOT requirement. Nevertheless, the strongest version of the HOT thesis should be qualified. Consciousness is plausibly fundamentally a higher-order phenomenon, but the required higher-order representation need not amount to explicit fact awareness in the robust, reflective, or conceptual sense suggested by ordinary language. What is necessary is a suitable higher-order awareness of the first-order state, not necessarily a judgment or thought with the form “I am seeing an apple.”

## 1. State consciousness and the two kinds of awareness

The debate is complicated by the ambiguity of “consciousness.” At least three notions should be distinguished.

First, there is *creature consciousness*: whether an organism is awake, alert, or capable of having experiences at all. Second, there is *transitive consciousness*: consciousness of something, such as a tree, a melody, or a bodily sensation. Third, there is *intransitive consciousness*: the condition of a mental state’s being conscious, as opposed to unconscious. The present issue concerns the relation between the second and third notions. A subject may be conscious of an object, but what makes the relevant perceptual state itself conscious?

Dretske’s distinction between object awareness and fact awareness helps expose the difficulty. A creature may be aware of an object without being aware of a fact involving that object. For example, a person may see a red object without seeing *that it is red*. A dog may see a ball, pursue it, and retrieve it without representing the fact that the ball is present or that it has a particular color. Dretske’s point is not that animals lack all consciousness, but that object awareness and fact awareness involve different kinds of representational achievement.

Object awareness is plausibly relational and non-propositional. The visual system may be perceptually connected to a particular object. The subject’s perceptual state carries information about the object, and the object guides behavior. Fact awareness, by contrast, is often characterized as a form of propositional or epistemic awareness. To be aware that the apple is red is to represent a fact about the apple. The subject is not merely visually related to the apple; the subject represents the apple as having a certain property.

A HOT theory transfers this distinction into the theory of consciousness. A first-order perceptual state represents an object or feature in the environment. A higher-order state represents that the subject is in that first-order state. For example, a visual state may represent an apple, while a HOT represents the subject as seeing an apple. The HOT need not itself be the object of a further thought. It can be unconscious, dispositional, or nonconceptual. What matters is that it makes the first-order state available to the subject in the right way.

This yields the basic HOT proposal:

1. A subject has a first-order mental state, such as a visual or painful state.
2. The subject has a suitable higher-order representation of that state.
3. In virtue of the higher-order representation, the first-order state is conscious.

The theory thus denies that a first-order state becomes conscious merely by representing an object. The first-order state must also be represented as a state of the subject. This explains why consciousness seems to involve not merely the presence of information but its availability as experience. A conscious visual state is not merely a state that detects a tree; it is a state that presents the tree to the subject.

The crucial question is whether step two is genuinely necessary, or whether it merely accompanies consciousness in normal adult human cases.

## 2. Why a HOT might be necessary

The strongest motivation for a HOT requirement concerns the difference between possession of a mental state and consciousness of that state. A visual system can encode color, shape, location, and motion without producing a conscious visual experience. The brain can also generate motor responses, emotional reactions, and verbal dispositions without these processes entering consciousness. If first-order representation were sufficient, then every appropriately functioning perceptual state would be conscious. That result is implausibly liberal.

The HOT theorist explains the difference by distinguishing first-order representation from higher-order awareness. A first-order state can occur in the mind without the subject being aware of having it. When the state is targeted by a suitable HOT, the state is conscious. The higher-order representation need not add new sensory information about the external object. Rather, it changes the status of the first-order state for the subject. It makes the state an experience.

This idea also captures an important intuition about intransitive consciousness. If a mental state is conscious, then there is something it is like for the subject to be in that state. A state that merely processes information unconsciously may guide conduct, influence other mental states, and even be reportable under special conditions, but it does not thereby become an experience. The difference between conscious pain and unconscious nociceptive processing, for instance, is not simply that the former carries more information about bodily damage. It is that the subject is aware of the pain as occurring.

The HOT need not be an explicit judgment. Consider the thought “I am in pain.” That judgment is a higher-order representation, but it is not necessary that every conscious pain involve such a reflective or linguistically structured thought. Infants, nonhuman animals, and perhaps some nonconceptual human experiences may be conscious even though they cannot entertain the proposition that they are in pain. A defensible HOT theory therefore requires a suitably structured higher-order representation, not necessarily a conceptually articulated fact awareness.

The distinction between a HOT and an ordinary introspective judgment is important. A HOT is a representation whose content concerns a first-order mental state. It may be fleeting, implicit, and noninferential. The subject need not infer the existence of the first-order state from evidence. Nor need the HOT be itself conscious. If it were conscious, one might ask what makes the HOT conscious, leading to an infinite regress. HOT theories avoid this by treating the higher-order representation as unconscious while allowing it to render its target state conscious.

This model also helps explain why consciousness is often associated with a sense of presence or givenness. The apple is not merely represented by the visual system; it is presented to the subject through a first-order state that is itself represented as occurring. The subject need not form a judgment about the apple. But there must be a relation between the subject and the first-order state that makes the latter available as an experience.

## 3. Browne’s objection: object awareness without fact awareness

Browne’s objection challenges the assumption that the transition from first-order representation to consciousness requires a distinct higher-order awareness. If object awareness is already a genuine form of awareness, then adding fact awareness may be theoretically redundant. A creature that sees an object is conscious of it, even if it does not represent the fact that it sees the object. On this proposal, the visual state is conscious in virtue of the subject’s direct acquaintance with the object, not because the subject also represents its own visual state.

The objection gains support from the thought that direct acquaintance seems more fundamental than higher-order representation. When one sees a tree, one is directly confronted with the tree. The perceptual relation does not seem to depend on first representing one’s own seeing. Indeed, if one had to be aware of seeing the tree in order to see it consciously, then ordinary perception would seem to involve an implausibly complicated form of self-monitoring. We do not ordinarily experience the world by first experiencing an inner perceptual state and then inferring the external object.

Browne can also appeal to Dretske’s distinction. Dretske’s object awareness does not require fact awareness. If dogs and infants can be aware of objects without representing facts about them, then perhaps they can have conscious perceptual states without HOTs. The first-order perceptual relation may itself constitute consciousness. Fact awareness could be a later, more sophisticated capacity associated with reflection, judgment, or introspection, but not with basic experience.

This objection has a powerful phenomenological basis. In ordinary visual experience, one is primarily aware of the world, not of one’s own visual states. The apple appears as located in front of one, not as an internal representation that one is inspecting. A higher-order theory might therefore seem to misdescribe experience by making self-awareness fundamental. If consciousness requires awareness of a mental state, why does consciousness not ordinarily feel like awareness of an internal state?

The HOT theorist can answer that the content of a HOT need not be phenomenologically foregrounded. A subject can represent a first-order state as occurring without consciously attending to that representation. The first-order state remains world-directed in its own content. The HOT does not replace or duplicate the perceptual presentation of the apple; it confers conscious status on that presentation. Thus, the experience can remain transparently directed toward the apple even though it depends on a higher-order representation.

Still, Browne’s objection exposes a potential equivocation in the phrase “fact awareness.” If fact awareness means an explicit proposition such as “I am seeing a red apple,” then it is not plausible that such awareness is necessary for every conscious experience. If fact awareness means only a nonconceptual, implicit representation of being in a perceptual state, then the HOT theory becomes more plausible, but it is also less clear that it has retained Dretske’s original notion of awareness of a fact. The debate therefore depends heavily on how demanding the notion of fact awareness is.

## 4. Blindsight: object-directed processing without conscious seeing

Blindsight is the most important case for evaluating the dispute. Patients with damage to primary visual cortex report blindness in a region of their visual field, yet they can sometimes guess the location, orientation, movement, or emotional expression of stimuli presented in that region. Their performance is above chance even though they deny seeing anything. Blindsight thus appears to involve object-directed visual processing without conscious visual awareness.

At first glance, blindsight may support Browne. The patient’s visual system is aware of objects in some sense: it detects them, discriminates their properties, and guides behavior. If object awareness is sufficient for consciousness, then the blindsighted patient’s experience should be conscious. But the patient’s own testimony suggests otherwise. The patient does not consciously see the stimulus. The case therefore appears to demonstrate that object-directed information processing is not enough for conscious experience.

Browne might respond by distinguishing object awareness from mere detection. Perhaps blindsight does not involve genuine object awareness at all. The patient’s visual system may process information about stimuli without the subject being directly acquainted with the objects. If so, blindsight does not show that object awareness can occur without consciousness; it shows only that subpersonal information processing can occur without object awareness. This response would preserve the claim that genuine object awareness is sufficient.

The difficulty is that “object awareness” then risks becoming defined as conscious perception. If the blindsighted patient can discriminate an object’s location and shape, identify its category, and act upon it, it is natural to say that the subject has some form of awareness of the object, even if that awareness is degraded or inaccessible to report. Denying object awareness altogether may simply stipulate that awareness means consciousness. The important empirical phenomenon is precisely that visual information can be used as if the subject sees while the subject lacks visual phenomenology.

Blindsight therefore supports a distinction between *sensitivity* and *conscious awareness*. The visual system is sensitive to the stimulus, but the subject does not consciously experience it. In HOT terms, the first-order visual representations are present and behaviorally efficacious, but they are not accompanied by suitable higher-order representations. The absence of a HOT explains the subject’s denial of seeing.

There are complications. Blindsight patients sometimes report vague visual feelings, such as a sense that something is present. This may indicate that consciousness is not all-or-nothing. A weak or partial higher-order representation may produce a degraded conscious experience, while more precise first-order processing remains available for forced-choice discrimination. Alternatively, the case may show that the patient has a conscious but nonvisual awareness, such as a feeling of guessing or a nonspecific sense of presence. Either interpretation is compatible with the central HOT insight: the first-order processing that supports discrimination is not by itself sufficient for full conscious seeing.

Blindsight also weakens a simplistic identification of consciousness with reportability. Patients may report blindness while nevertheless making accurate guesses. Conversely, a person may report seeing because of a higher-order belief or confabulation even when first-order perception is absent or abnormal. A HOT theory must therefore require a suitable relation between the HOT and an actual first-order state, rather than accepting any metacognitive report. But this refinement does not undermine the general idea that conscious perception involves more than first-order object processing.

The case thus poses a dilemma for Browne’s position. If blindsight involves object awareness, then object awareness is not sufficient for conscious experience. If it does not involve object awareness, then the term must be sharply distinguished from perceptual sensitivity, and the claim that ordinary object awareness is sufficient remains untested. In either event, blindsight provides no clear counterexample to the need for a higher-order condition.

## 5. Unconscious driving and automatized action

Unconscious driving offers a different challenge. A driver may travel a familiar route, negotiate traffic, obey road signs, and arrive at the destination while having little recollection of the journey. The driver may say that the driving occurred “automatically” or that attention was occupied by conversation or thought. If the driver’s perceptual and motor states represent the road, vehicles, and traffic signals, then it may seem that the driver was aware of these objects without being aware of being aware. This appears to support object awareness without a HOT.

The first point to note is that the example is ambiguous. In many cases described as “unconscious driving,” the driver is not literally unconscious. The driver remains awake, responsive, and capable of adjusting behavior to novel events. What is absent may be focused attention, explicit recall, or reflective awareness of the details of the trip. The driver may consciously see an unexpected pedestrian, feel anxiety when another car swerves, or notice a red light, even if the routine portions of the journey are later forgotten. Thus, the case may concern memory and attention rather than consciousness.

If the driver really has no conscious visual experiences during the routine driving, the case can be explained by automatization. First-order perceptual and motor systems continue to represent relevant environmental features and guide behavior. However, the states controlling routine action are not accompanied by HOTs. They remain unconscious despite their complexity. This is exactly the kind of dissociation a HOT theory predicts.

Browne could argue that the driver is nevertheless object-aware. The driver sees the road and other cars in the relevant sense because the objects are directly guiding action. Consciousness would then consist in this object-directed relation, while the absence of later memory or reflection would show only that fact awareness and memory are unnecessary. But again, the notion of “sees” is doing important work. If it means that information from the objects is causally available to the motor system, then it is too weak to entail consciousness. If it means that the driver has a perceptual experience of the objects, then the conclusion that object awareness is present has already assumed the point at issue.

Unconscious driving also illustrates that behavioral competence cannot be used as a simple test for consciousness. A system can be highly intelligent and flexible in a narrow domain while operating without conscious experience. Automatic driving involves learned routines, predictive control, and selective processing. These capacities show that consciousness is not required for every form of intelligent behavior. They do not show that consciousness is present whenever such capacities are exercised.

At the same time, the example raises a serious issue for the HOT theory. If a driver can consciously see the road while not attending to or remembering it, then HOTs cannot be identified with attention or subsequent report. A conscious state need not be attended to, verbally reportable at the time, or stored in memory. The theory must allow for unconscious or minimally accessible HOTs. The fact that the driver later cannot recall the journey does not show that no HOT occurred during it.

This suggests a useful hierarchy:

- First-order processing: information about the road is encoded and used.
- Conscious perception: some of these states are accompanied by suitable higher-order representations.
- Attention: some conscious or unconscious states are selected for enhanced processing.
- Report and memory: some attended states are retained and made available for later use.

These dimensions can come apart. Unconscious driving may involve first-order processing without consciousness in some phases, and conscious but unattended perception in others. The example therefore does not refute the HOT requirement; it shows that the requirement must not be confused with attention, report, or memory.

## 6. Is fact awareness too demanding?

The most serious criticism of the claim that consciousness requires fact awareness concerns animals, infants, and nonconceptual experience. If fact awareness requires grasping a proposition, then many creatures may be excluded from consciousness. A dog can feel pain and see a ball without possessing concepts of pain, seeing, or facthood. A human infant may experience colors and bodily sensations before acquiring the conceptual resources needed to think “I am seeing red.” Yet it would be implausible to deny that such subjects are conscious.

There are two possible responses. The first is to reject the HOT theory and maintain that object awareness is sufficient. The second is to weaken the notion of HOT so that it does not require conceptual or propositional thought. The second response is preferable. A higher-order representation can be nonconceptual. It may represent the subject as being in a certain state without representing the state under a linguistic or theoretical description.

Whether such a nonconceptual representation counts as “fact awareness” depends on terminology. It may not amount to knowing that a fact obtains in the reflective sense. But it can still represent a fact-like structure: that the subject is undergoing a particular perceptual or affective condition. The representation need not be available for deliberate reasoning. It need only place the first-order state in a format that makes it conscious for the subject.

This qualification preserves the explanatory purpose of HOT theory while avoiding an implausible intellectualism. Consciousness does not require self-consciousness in the sense of explicitly thinking about oneself. It requires only a primitive form of awareness of one’s own mental condition. Such awareness can be implicit and nonconceptual.

However, the weakened theory faces a challenge: if the HOT is too minimal, it may become indistinguishable from the first-order state itself. A mere change in the causal role or availability of a first-order representation should not automatically count as a higher-order representation. The theory must specify what makes a representation genuinely higher-order. It must represent the occurrence, possession, or instantiation of the first-order state, rather than merely representing the same external object in greater detail.

One possibility is to characterize a HOT dispositionally. A first-order state is conscious when the subject is disposed to represent itself as being in that state under suitable conditions. Another possibility is to posit an actual but unconscious higher-order representation. The first approach avoids requiring a constantly present metacognitive state, but it risks making consciousness depend on counterfactual capacities. The second is more faithful to the traditional HOT theory but raises empirical and architectural questions about the mechanism producing the higher-order representation.

The important point is that the theory need not demand a conscious thought about the state. The HOT itself may be unconscious, while its target is conscious. This may seem paradoxical, but it is no more paradoxical than the claim that a disposition to report can exist without an actual report. The higher-order representation functions as a mechanism of consciousness without itself necessarily becoming another conscious object.

## 7. Does consciousness require fact awareness in Dretske’s sense?

The debate can now be framed more precisely. Dretske’s distinction concerns two forms of awareness:

- awareness of an object;
- awareness that a fact involving the object obtains.

The HOT theorist identifies conscious statehood with the second kind of awareness, at least structurally. A subject is not merely aware of the external object; the subject is aware, in a nonconceptual or implicit way, that it is in a mental state directed toward that object.

Browne’s objection is strongest if Dretske’s object awareness is already sufficient for transitive consciousness. If seeing an object is itself a conscious relation, then the corresponding state appears to be conscious without any further representation. The higher-order theory would add an unnecessary layer.

But this inference may conflate consciousness *of an object* with consciousness *of a mental state*. A subject can stand in a causal or informational relation to an object without having an experience of it. The issue is not whether the subject is connected to the object, but whether the subject’s perceptual state is presented as an experience. Dretske’s object/fact distinction is therefore not decisive by itself. Object awareness may be sufficient for a certain kind of organism-environment coordination while being insufficient for intransitive consciousness.

Consider a camera or an automated system that detects an object and adjusts its behavior. It is sensitive to the object but not thereby conscious. One might reply that a living animal differs from a camera because its object awareness is a genuine mental relation. Yet the blindsight and unconscious-driving cases show that even mental, internally structured, object-directed processing can occur without conscious experience. The mere presence of a mental state does not settle its consciousness.

The HOT theorist can therefore distinguish:

1. **Object awareness as first-order representation:** the subject’s state represents an object and guides behavior.
2. **Object awareness as conscious perception:** the object is presented in experience.
3. **Fact awareness as higher-order representation:** the subject represents itself as being in the first-order state.

If “object awareness” means (1), it is not sufficient. If it means (2), then it is sufficient by definition, but the theory has not explained what makes the object awareness conscious. The HOT theory proposes that the explanation lies in (3). This makes the disagreement partly terminological but also substantively theoretical: what mechanism converts first-order representation into conscious presentation?

## 8. Objections to the HOT account

Although HOT theory has explanatory advantages, it faces several objections.

First, there is the regress problem. If a first-order state is conscious because of a HOT, then perhaps the HOT must itself be conscious in order to make the first-order state conscious. If so, another higher-order thought would be required, and so on indefinitely. The standard response is that the HOT need not be conscious. It is enough that it represents the first-order state. But critics may question how an unconscious representation can generate conscious experience. The answer is functional rather than introspective: the HOT changes the representational status of its target without itself being experienced. Still, this requires a substantive theory of how higher-order representation produces phenomenology.

Second, there is the problem of misrepresentation. A subject may have a HOT that represents it as seeing something when no corresponding first-order state exists, as in hallucination, illusion, or certain forms of metacognitive error. If the HOT alone is sufficient, then a conscious experience could occur without an appropriate first-order state. Rosenthal’s version of HOT theory can accommodate this by allowing a conscious state to be a misrepresented first-order state or by treating the relevant first-order state broadly. But the theory must prevent arbitrary higher-order representations from generating experiences unrelated to the subject’s actual processing.

Third, there is the issue of animal and infant consciousness, already discussed. A conceptual HOT is too demanding, while a nonconceptual HOT requires a careful account of representation. The theory must avoid making consciousness depend on sophisticated self-concepts.

Fourth, the theory may seem to conflict with the transparency of experience. We experience external objects, not inner representations. But this objection is less powerful if the HOT is understood as a background representation that confers conscious status without becoming an object of experience. The transparency of perception concerns the content of the first-order state, not necessarily the absence of higher-order processing.

Finally, there is the empirical question of whether HOTs actually correlate with consciousness. Some theories instead emphasize global availability, recurrent processing, integrated information, or higher-order access. These alternatives may explain why conscious states are reportable, flexible, and integrated without positing a separate thought about the state. The HOT theorist must therefore show not merely that higher-order representations accompany consciousness, but that they are constitutive of it.

## 9. Is consciousness fundamentally a HOT addition?

The thesis that consciousness is fundamentally a HOT addition should be understood in a moderate rather than maximal form. It should not claim that all conscious subjects entertain explicit propositions about their own mental states. Nor should it claim that conscious experience is normally introspective or self-focused. The defensible claim is that a first-order state becomes conscious only when it is represented in a suitable higher-order format as a state of the subject.

This thesis explains the central dissociations. First-order processing without HOTs occurs in blindsight and automatic behavior. Consciousness without explicit reflection occurs in ordinary perception, animal experience, and infant experience. Reportability and memory can vary independently because they are downstream or additional capacities. Hallucination and illusion can occur because the higher-order mechanism can operate over inaccurate or internally generated first-order states. These are significant explanatory gains.

Browne’s alternative has the advantage of ontological simplicity. It treats direct acquaintance as sufficient and avoids adding a further representational layer. But it faces difficulty explaining the difference between unconscious object-processing and conscious perception. It can either deny that blindsight and automatic driving involve object awareness, or it can permit object awareness without consciousness. The first response risks stipulation; the second abandons the sufficiency claim.

The best argument against Browne is therefore not that every conscious subject must explicitly know a fact. It is that first-order object-directed representation does not by itself explain why a state is experienced. A system may represent an object, use information about it, and behave appropriately without anything being present for the subject. The higher-order addition is a plausible way of marking the transition from information processing to experience.

Nevertheless, one should resist saying that consciousness is fundamentally a HOT *thought* if “thought” suggests a proposition with articulated concepts. Consciousness may instead depend on a higher-order *representation*, which can be perceptual, affective, nonconceptual, and implicit. The term “fact awareness” must likewise be used carefully. A primitive organism may not be aware of the fact that it is seeing in the manner of a reflective adult, but it may still possess a representation that places its first-order state within its own perspective.

This moderated thesis also allows for gradations of consciousness. The quality and precision of experience may depend on the richness of both the first-order state and the higher-order representation. A weak higher-order representation might yield vague awareness, as perhaps in blindsight with reported visual “feelings.” A richer representation might support determinate conscious perception and metacognitive confidence. Consciousness would then not be a binary consequence of any higher-order representation, but a structured phenomenon depending on the interaction between first- and higher-order contents.

## Conclusion

The claim that state consciousness requires both object awareness and fact awareness is plausible if fact awareness is interpreted as a suitable higher-order representation of a first-order mental state. It is implausible if fact awareness requires explicit, conceptual, or linguistic knowledge that one is in that state. The essential issue is whether a first-order object-directed state can be conscious merely by representing its object.

Browne’s objection, informed by Dretske’s distinction, rightly emphasizes that object awareness and awareness of facts are different. It also warns against treating ordinary consciousness as if it always involved explicit introspection. Yet the objection does not establish that object awareness alone is sufficient. Blindsight shows that sophisticated object-directed processing can occur without conscious seeing. Unconscious driving shows that perceptual and motor representations can guide complex behavior without entering experience, though the example must be distinguished from failures of attention or memory. In both cases, the relevant first-order processing can occur without a suitable higher-order awareness.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore a qualified HOT thesis. Consciousness is fundamentally a higher-order addition to first-order mental processing, but the addition need not be a reflective judgment or a conceptual thought. What is necessary is a nonaccidental higher-order representation of the subject as being in the relevant first-order state. Direct acquaintance with an object supplies the content of experience; higher-order awareness supplies its conscious status. Object awareness explains what the experience is about, while fact awareness, understood in this minimal higher-order sense, explains why it is an experience for the subject at all.
