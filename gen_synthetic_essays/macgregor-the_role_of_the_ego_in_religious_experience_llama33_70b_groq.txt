The realm of philosophical theology has witnessed a plethora of debates and discussions regarding the nature of religious experience. At the forefront of these discussions are thinkers like Jean-Luc Marion, Paul Ricoeur, and Michel Henry, who have each contributed significantly to our understanding of the relationship between the human subject and the divine. This essay aims to evaluate the central thesis that religious experience is possible precisely because the finite ego does not constitute the phenomenon or master its meaning, but is instead constituted by a higher other. Marion's theory of the saturated phenomenon posits that certain experiences exceed the cognitive faculties of the subject, thereby rendering them incapable of constituting or mastering the meaning of the phenomenon. This excess intuition, as Marion terms it, is characterized by an overwhelming abundance of data that surpasses the subject's capacity for comprehension. The saturated phenomenon is thus not a product of the subject's constitutive powers, but rather an event that imposes itself upon the subject, redefining the subject's relationship with the world. In this context, the subject is no longer the originator of experience, but rather a witness to the phenomenon that unfolds before it.

Ricoeur's concept of call and response offers a complementary perspective on this issue. According to Ricoeur, human existence is characterized by a fundamental responsiveness to the world, which is initiated by a call that emanates from beyond the subject's own being. This call, which Ricoeur terms the "call of conscience," awakens the subject to its own finitude and prompts it to respond in a manner that is both creative and responsible. The call and response structure thus underscores the subject's receptive nature, highlighting its capacity to receive and respond to the world in a way that is both passive and active. In this sense, the subject is not the sole author of its own experience, but rather a participant in a larger dialogue that unfolds between itself and the world.

Henry's distinction between the word of the world and the word of life provides further insight into the nature of religious experience. According to Henry, the word of the world refers to the realm of language and culture that shapes our understanding of reality, whereas the word of life refers to the more fundamental, pre-linguistic experience of existence that underlies all human experience. The word of life is thus not a product of human culture or language, but rather a primal, intuitive awareness of being that precedes all cognitive and linguistic mediation. In this context, the subject's experience of the divine is not a result of its own constitutive powers, but rather an encounter with the word of life that awakens it to its own existence and the existence of the world.
