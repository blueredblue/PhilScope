# Forms as Universals and Forms as Standards: Plato’s Supposed Conflation in the *Phaedo* and *Republic*

## Introduction

Plato’s middle dialogues present the theory of Forms in two apparently different registers. In one register, a Form is a universal: the one intelligible nature that is common to many particular things. Many things are beautiful because they participate in Beauty; many things are equal because they participate in Equality; many acts are just because they participate in Justice. In another register, a Form is a standard or paradigm: the perfect reality by reference to which sensible things are judged defective, approximate, or imitative. Sensible beautiful things are beautiful only imperfectly, whereas Beauty itself is perfectly beautiful; sensible circles fall short of circularity itself; the just city is measured against an intelligible model that no empirical city completely realizes.

These two conceptions are not obviously equivalent. A universal is primarily a logical or metaphysical answer to the question, “What do many things have in common?” A standard is primarily an answer to the question, “By what are things assessed as more or less adequate?” The former is connected with predication and identity; the latter with resemblance, approximation, imitation, and normative deficiency. The claim that Plato conflates two incommensurable theories of Forms—universal Forms (UF) and standard Forms (SF)—therefore deserves serious consideration. Perhaps Plato moves illegitimately from the claim that many things are F to the claim that there exists a perfect F which serves as the model of their imperfect F-ness. Or perhaps the two functions can be integrated within a single metaphysics.

The problem is sharpened by Alexander Nehamas’s Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars Objection, or IA. If Forms are paradigms and sensible particulars merely approximate them, then what determines whether, and in what respect, a particular approximates a Form? A universal can explain why a thing counts as beautiful or equal by supplying the nature predicated of it. But a paradigm seems to require a determinate relation of resemblance. If the relation is left unspecified, the standard theory risks becoming empty; if it is specified in terms of shared properties, it appears to revert to the universal theory it was meant to replace.

R. S. Bluck’s defense of Forms as standards offers an important response. Bluck argues, in effect, that standards need not be conceived as physical exemplars or as objects related to particulars by a simple, measurable resemblance. A Form can be a criterion of intelligibility and excellence without being a model whose properties are copied item by item. This helps explain why sensible particulars “fall short” of Forms while still falling under universal predicates. Yet the success of this defense depends on whether Plato’s texts support a sufficiently precise account of how standardhood and universality are related.

The most plausible conclusion is neither that Plato simply offers two unrelated theories nor that the tension disappears without remainder. The middle dialogues genuinely use Forms in both universal and paradigmatic ways. But the two uses need not be incommensurable. The standard function can be understood as a richer, normative elaboration of the universal function. Plato’s framework does not collapse, though it remains theoretically unstable because he never fully explains the relation between intelligible commonality and sensible approximation.

## I. The Universal Conception of Forms

The universal conception is prominent in the *Phaedo*. Socrates distinguishes sensible things from “the Beautiful itself,” “the Just itself,” “the Equal itself,” and so on. The distinction is not merely between a class and one of its members. The Form is described as what is itself, by itself, and always the same, whereas sensible things are subject to change, mixture, and contrary properties.

The argument from recollection provides one route to the universal character of Forms. In the discussion of Equality, Socrates observes that we encounter sensible objects that are equal to one another, but also recognize that they fall short of equality itself. Two pieces of wood or stones may appear equal, yet they can also appear unequal from another perspective or in relation to other objects. Nonetheless, our judgment that they are equal presupposes some conception of Equality that is not derived simply from any particular pair of sensible objects. We use sensible instances to “recollect” the Equal itself (*Phaedo* 74a–75b).

The logical structure of the argument is universalizing. There are many equal things, but they are equal in virtue of something that is not identical with any one of them. The same applies to beautiful things and just things. The Form answers the question of what it is for diverse things to fall under a common description. If a particular is beautiful, it is not merely associated with other beautiful objects; it is beautiful because Beauty itself provides the relevant nature.

This function is also visible in the so-called affinity argument. Forms are stable, invisible, and intelligible, while sensible particulars are changing, visible, and perceptible (*Phaedo* 78d–80b). The contrast is not only ontological but explanatory. The intelligible Form makes it possible to understand what a thing is despite the flux and variability of its sensible manifestations. A beautiful object may be beautiful in one respect and ugly in another; it may come to be beautiful and cease to be beautiful. Beauty itself, by contrast, is not one beautiful thing among others. It is the intelligible reality in relation to which particular judgments of beauty are possible.

The *Republic* develops the same universal function in the context of knowledge and dialectic. At *Republic* 476a–480a, Socrates contrasts knowledge, which concerns what is, with opinion, which concerns what lies between being and non-being. Beautiful things are many and variable: they may be beautiful in one respect and not beautiful in another. Beauty itself, however, is stable and intelligible. The philosopher is distinguished from the lover of sights and sounds because the philosopher recognizes the Form itself rather than merely encountering its many sensible manifestations.

The famous discussion of the Good in Book VI also presupposes this universal framework. There is one Good itself, and particular goods are intelligible as goods through their relation to it. The Form of the Good is not merely the best item in a class of good things. It is the source of their intelligibility and value. The analogy of the sun presents the Good as what gives truth to objects of knowledge and power to the knower (*Republic* 508e–509b). Even if the Good exceeds ordinary Forms in explanatory rank, it remains connected to the general Platonic question: what makes many things intelligibly what they are?

The universal interpretation thus has strong textual support. Forms are repeatedly described as what many particulars share or participate in. The “one over many” structure is central to Plato’s response to Heraclitean flux, sophistic relativism, and the problem of knowledge. Without something stable and common, there would be no fixed object for definition or scientific understanding.

Still, this is only one aspect of Plato’s account. The Forms do not merely explain predication. They also serve as measures, ideals, and models.

## II. Forms as Standards and Paradigms

The paradigmatic conception is especially evident when Plato speaks of sensible things as deficient copies or imperfect embodiments of intelligible realities. The *Phaedo*’s account of Equality is paradigmatic in precisely this sense. Sensible equal things are not simply instances that possess the same universal property as Equality; they are also inferior approximations to Equality itself. They are “equal” and yet not equal in the strict sense. Their equality is imperfect, unstable, and relative.

The wording of the passage suggests more than ordinary predication. Sensible equals “fall short” of the Equal itself (*Phaedo* 74d–75a). This language naturally evokes a standard. A thing falls short of a standard when it possesses some relevant feature to a lesser degree or in a less complete form. A rough circle falls short of the geometrical circle; a moderately just action falls short of perfect Justice; a beautiful body may be less beautiful than another. In each case, the Form seems to be not simply the common property but the maximal or pure realization of that property.

The *Republic* gives this paradigmatic role an explicitly political and ethical form. The philosopher-rulers are to order the city by looking toward a model or pattern in heaven (*Republic* 592a–b). The model is not a blueprint in the ordinary technical sense. It is an intelligible standard of political order. The empirical city can approximate it more or less successfully, but no sensible regime simply is the Form of the just city. Similarly, the philosopher’s education turns the soul toward intelligible realities that function as measures of what should be believed and done.

The analogy of the divided line also supports a model-like understanding of intelligibles. The objects of thought in the higher sections of the line are treated as realities to which mathematical and dialectical reasoning is directed (*Republic* 509d–511e). In the allegory of the cave, turning the soul toward the intelligible world is a conversion toward what is genuinely real and thereby toward a standard by which the shadows of ordinary experience can be assessed (*Republic* 514a–521c).

The language of imitation becomes explicit in the *Republic*’s critique of art. The painter’s bed is an imitation of the sensible bed, while the sensible bed itself is made in accordance with the Form of Bed (*Republic* 596a–598b). The Form is the one true bed, the basis on which the craftsman makes many beds. Here the Form seems both universal and paradigm. It is what all beds have in common, but it is also the model that guides production. The carpenter does not merely notice a universal property after making a bed; he makes the bed by reference to a conception of what a bed is.

This duality is not accidental. Plato’s theory is partly a theory of explanation. A Form explains why many things are called by one name, but it also explains why those things are better or worse examples of what they are. The Form of Bed explains both the intelligibility of beds as a kind and the standard by which a particular bed can be judged functional or defective. The Form of Justice explains both why diverse actions count as just and why some are more fully just than others.

Nevertheless, the standard interpretation introduces a conceptual difficulty. A universal is not normally something that an individual resembles to a greater or lesser degree. One may resemble Socrates more or less, but one does not normally “approximate” the universal humanity in the same way. A particular either exemplifies a universal or it does not, even if it may exemplify it imperfectly in some broader, evaluative sense. Paradigms, by contrast, are naturally related to degrees of resemblance and adequacy. The question is whether Plato can use both relations without confusing them.

## III. The Charge of Conflation

The claim of conflation can be formulated as follows. Plato begins with a legitimate universalist problem: many particulars are F, so there must be some common nature or Form of F. But he then treats that Form as a perfect F, a paradigmatic object against which particulars are measured. The transition is not logically licensed. From “many things are beautiful because of Beauty” it does not follow that Beauty is itself the most beautiful thing, or that sensible beautiful objects are copies of it. The universal and the standard answer different questions.

The difference can be illustrated by ordinary predicates. The universal “triangle” is not a particularly excellent triangle. It does not have three sides in a way that makes it better than actual triangles; rather, it is the intelligible structure common to triangular figures. A standard of triangularity, however, might be an exact geometrical construction against which drawn triangles are assessed. The exact construction is not simply the universal; it is a criterion of correctness.

The same issue appears in moral cases. If Justice is a universal, then just actions fall under the concept justice. If Justice is a standard, then actions can be more or less just according to their approximation to it. But if the Form is both, Plato must explain how a single entity can function as a logical common nature and as an ideal limit.

There is also a problem concerning negative and relational properties. If sensible things participate in Beauty, is Beauty itself beautiful? If it is, then the Form appears to be an instance of the universal it explains, inviting an infinite regress of Forms. If it is not, then Beauty cannot straightforwardly be the universal property of beautiful things. Plato’s arguments in the *Phaedo* sometimes speak as though the Form itself possesses the property in an especially pure way, while at other times the Form is treated as that by which other things possess the property. This ambiguity contributes to the impression that two accounts have been superimposed.

The standard interpretation may also seem to compromise the explanatory role of Forms. A paradigm explains a particular by resemblance, but resemblance is notoriously symmetrical and often indeterminate. If A resembles B, then B resembles A; yet Plato needs an asymmetrical relation in which the Form explains the particular. Moreover, a particular can resemble a Form in some respects while differing from it in others. What selects the relevant respects?

These concerns motivate Nehamas’s objection.

## IV. Nehamas and the Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars

The Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars Objection challenges the intelligibility of the standard theory. Suppose sensible particulars are imperfect copies or approximations of Forms. What makes a particular an approximation of the Form of Beauty rather than, say, an approximation of the Form of Justice? Or, within one domain, what determines the respects in which a thing is an approximation? If there is no determinate answer, the paradigmatic relation does not explain anything.

The difficulty can be stated more precisely. Let F be a Form and x a sensible particular. On the standard interpretation, x is an imperfect F because x approximates F. But approximation requires a criterion of similarity. There must be some property or structure that x shares with F, though perhaps to a lesser degree. If the relevant shared property is simply F-ness, then the account becomes circular: x is an approximation of F because it is F-like, and it is F-like because it approximates F. If the relevant property is specified independently, then the Form is no longer doing the primary explanatory work. The standard depends on a prior account of what counts as similarity to it.

The problem is especially serious where particulars possess multiple, shifting, or conflicting properties. A beautiful object may resemble Beauty in its proportions, color, harmony, or capacity to produce pleasure. But these dimensions need not coincide. A painting can be beautiful because of color while lacking ideal proportion; a melody can be beautiful through harmony without resembling a body at all. If the Form is a paradigm, which features are essential to resemblance?

The *Phaedo*’s Equality passage makes the issue visible. Sensible things are called equal, yet they fall short of Equality. But in what respect do they fall short? They may be equal in length but unequal in weight; equal relative to one object but unequal relative to another; equal by measurement but not by perceptual appearance. The particular’s “approximation” to Equality is therefore not obvious unless Equality is understood as a determinate standard of exactness. Yet if exactness is simply the universal concept of equality, then the standard interpretation appears to depend on the universal interpretation.

Nehamas’s objection does not merely say that Plato fails to provide a detailed metric of approximation. It raises a more fundamental question about the logical form of the theory. A universal can be predicated of many things without being a model with which they share perceptible features. A standard, by contrast, requires an account of the relation between the standard and the things measured by it. If Plato’s theory moves from predication to approximation without explaining the transition, the metaphysics risks being rhetorically suggestive but philosophically underdetermined.

The objection also challenges the claim that Forms explain the unity of a class. If the members of a class are only approximate copies, then the class may not have a determinate boundary. How approximate must something be in order to count as a beautiful thing, a just action, or a bed? If there is no threshold, then Forms cannot explain ordinary classification. If there is a threshold, Plato must explain whether it derives from the Form, from human conventions, or from some independent structure.

IA is especially forceful against a crude version of SF according to which each Form is a perfect particular, and sensible things are copies that resemble it. A perfect particular would need to possess determinate features, but the Forms in the middle dialogues are often invoked across heterogeneous cases. Beauty applies to bodies, laws, souls, and perhaps intellectual activities. No single sensible-style paradigm can resemble all of them in the same way. Justice is even more obviously structural and normative rather than pictorial. The standard cannot therefore be a simple object whose visible properties are reproduced in its copies.

## V. Bluck’s Defense of Standards

Bluck’s defense is important because it resists the assumption that a standard must be an exemplar in the ordinary sense. A standard may be a criterion, measure, or ideal of correctness rather than a concrete object with a determinate bundle of observable properties. The standard of a meter, for example, need not be something that other objects resemble visually; it establishes a rule for measurement. Likewise, a moral or mathematical Form can determine adequacy without being copied as a sensible image.

On this approach, to say that particulars fall short of a Form is not necessarily to say that they resemble a perfect object imperfectly. It may mean that they instantiate a structure incompletely, or that they realize a value under limiting and compromising conditions. A just action may approximate Justice not because it resembles a single item called “Justice,” but because it realizes, in a partial and context-bound way, the intelligible order that Justice expresses. A beautiful body may fall short of Beauty because its harmony is unstable, dependent, and mixed with contrary features, whereas Beauty itself represents the pure intelligibility of harmonious form.

This interpretation gives sense to Plato’s claims that sensible particulars are both F and not-F. A beautiful object is beautiful insofar as it participates in Beauty, but it is also not beautiful in the strictest sense because its beauty is limited and perishable. The Form is not a member of the class of beautiful sensible objects; it is the standard that makes possible the distinction between more and less adequate instances. The apparent contradiction disappears if “beautiful” is being used in two senses: as a predicate applicable to sensible particulars and as a pure, unqualified perfection belonging to the Form.

Bluck’s defense also helps with the problem of heterogeneous instances. A standard need not resemble all of its instances in the same material or perceptible way. The Form of Justice can be instantiated in laws, institutions, decisions, and dispositions because it is an intelligible order, not a physical shape. The relation between the Form and these instances is functional or structural. Different things approximate the same Form by realizing the relevant order in different media.

This move has an important consequence: approximation is not primitive resemblance. It is derivative from participation in an intelligible standard. To say that a particular falls short of Justice is to say that it realizes the conditions of justice incompletely, not merely that it looks like Justice. The Form supplies the normative content by which the particular is assessed.

Does this answer Nehamas’s IA? It answers the strongest version of the objection, but only by revising what “standard” means. If a standard is merely a perfect exemplar related to particulars by resemblance, IA remains fatal. If a standard is a criterion of intelligible order, approximation can be understood as incomplete realization. Yet the account must still specify the relevant order. Otherwise “incomplete realization” is only a more sophisticated phrase for “approximation.”

Bluck’s defense is most successful in cases where Plato’s Form is clearly normative or structural. The Form of the Good, Justice, or the ideal city can plausibly serve as a standard without being a perfect sensible object. It is less immediately successful in the case of Forms such as Equality or Bed, where Plato’s language may suggest an object-like paradigm. Even there, however, exact Equality can be interpreted as the rule or structure under which sensible equalities are judged approximate. The geometrical circle is not a circular object situated somewhere in heaven; it is the intelligible standard of circularity against which drawn figures are measured.

## VI. Can Standards Also Be Universals?

The key question is whether Bluck’s defense reconciles the standard function with the logical status of universals or merely replaces one theory with another. A successful reconciliation requires showing that a Form can be both what many things are predicated of and the measure by which those things are judged.

One possible answer is that universality and standardhood belong to different explanatory levels. As a universal, the Form explains the unity of predication: many things are called beautiful because they are related to Beauty. As a standard, it explains degrees and deficiencies: they are more or less beautiful according to how fully they realize Beauty. The same Form therefore answers two related questions:

1. Why do these diverse things count as F at all?
2. Why are some more fully or purely F than others?

The first question is classificatory; the second is evaluative. Plato’s mistake would lie only in assuming that every universal is automatically a standard of perfection. But perhaps Plato’s Forms are not ordinary universals. They are universals endowed with normative or ontological priority. They are what sensible instances are trying, so to speak, to be.

This view fits the asymmetry of participation. A particular depends on the Form for its intelligibility, while the Form does not depend on the particular. Participation is not reciprocal resemblance. The Form is the explanatory source; the particular is the dependent realization. Once participation is understood in this asymmetrical way, the standard need not be an independently existing perfect particular.

The same point applies to predication. When Plato says that a thing is beautiful because of Beauty, he need not mean that Beauty is a particular beautiful object. He means that the Form supplies the intelligible basis of the predicate. The Form is “beautiful itself” not by belonging to the class of beautiful sensible things, but by being Beauty in its unqualified and intelligible form. The language is admittedly awkward, but it need not generate a regress if the Form is not treated as an ordinary instance.

This interpretation also clarifies the *Republic*’s account of the Good. The Good is both universal in the broad sense that it explains the intelligibility and value of good things, and paradigmatic in the sense that it is the ultimate standard of rational ordering. The philosopher’s knowledge is not merely knowledge of a universal class; it is knowledge of what makes things good and of how they ought to be arranged. The normative dimension is built into the Form’s explanatory role.

Yet there is a limit to the reconciliation. The concept of a universal by itself does not entail degrees of instantiation. If a universal is a property, its instances possess it or do not. Plato’s language of “more” and “less” therefore requires an additional theory of perfection, realization, or participation. The standard conception supplies that theory, but it cannot be deduced from universality alone. The two functions are compatible, but not identical.

This distinction matters. To say that Plato uses Forms both universally and paradigmatically is not yet to say that he has two incommensurable theories. Two theories are incommensurable only if their central explanatory relations cannot be integrated. UF and SF can be integrated if one understands the Form as an intelligible nature that is also a norm of its complete realization. They remain distinct as concepts, but they are not mutually exclusive.

## VII. Textual Assessment of the Middle Dialogues

The *Phaedo* supports both readings, but it does not present them as systematically separate doctrines. Its Forms are invoked in arguments about recollection, explanation, causation, immortality, and the distinction between intelligible and sensible reality. The Equality passage begins with the epistemic recognition of a universal and moves toward the metaphysical claim that Equality itself exists. The same discussion then characterizes sensible equals as deficient relative to it. The movement from common predication to ideal standard is therefore textual, not merely imposed by modern interpreters.

However, the *Phaedo* does not state a detailed theory of approximation. Participation may be a metaphor for ontological dependence rather than a technical relation. The dialogue itself warns against simplistic causal explanations when Socrates distinguishes the genuine cause of something from the conditions without which it could not occur (*Phaedo* 96a–102a). Forms are not described as mechanical templates stamped onto matter. They function as reasons within an intelligible order.

The *Republic* adds a political and epistemological dimension. The Forms are objects of knowledge, standards of judgment, and principles of rational construction. The philosopher must know not only that there is Justice itself but also how justice orders a soul or city. The ideal city’s relation to the Form is not that of a physical copy to a physical original. It is a project of rational approximation. The model guides construction even if it is never fully realized.

This is particularly evident in the claim that the just city exists “in speech” and perhaps as a pattern in heaven (*Republic* 592a–b). The city is not required to exist as a sensible fact in order to serve as a standard for individual conduct. The paradigmatic role is therefore practical and normative. It does not require a determinate set of sensible properties shared by every instance.

At the same time, the *Republic*’s account of Forms remains universalist. The distinction between the many beautiful things and Beauty itself presupposes the “one over many” structure. The lover of sights and sounds encounters many instances but lacks knowledge of the Form that unifies them. The philosopher’s superiority is precisely an ability to move from multiplicity to intelligible unity.

The textual evidence therefore favors a plural-function interpretation. Plato does not seem to choose between UF and SF. He treats intelligible realities as both common natures and standards of completion. The challenge is not to decide which description is exclusively correct, but to determine whether Plato’s combination can be made philosophically coherent.

## VIII. Does the Framework Collapse?

The charge of collapse is strongest if Plato’s two descriptions are taken in their most literal and simplistic forms. If Forms are universals in the strict logical sense, they cannot automatically be perfect particular exemplars. If Forms are perfect paradigms, then their relation to particulars cannot be explained merely by saying that particulars share their properties. And if approximation is left as an unanalyzed resemblance relation, IA undermines the explanatory value of the theory.

But Plato’s metaphysics need not collapse on these grounds. The middle dialogues can be read as articulating a layered theory. Forms are intelligible structures or natures. Because they are intelligible structures, they can be predicated of many things in the sense that they explain the unity of a kind or property. Because they are complete and unconditioned structures, they also function as standards by which sensible and practical realizations are assessed. Sensible particulars possess Forms in limited, unstable, and mixed ways; hence they can be both genuinely F and deficiently F.

The theory remains philosophically vulnerable, but vulnerability is not collapse. Plato does not fully distinguish “having a universal property” from “realizing a standard to a degree.” Nor does he explain participation with the precision demanded by later metaphysics. But the absence of a complete theory of instantiation does not show that the two functions are incommensurable. It shows that Plato is working with an ontological vocabulary in which logical, epistemic, and normative dimensions overlap.

Indeed, the overlap may be deliberate. For Plato, knowledge of what something is and knowledge of how well it realizes its nature are not wholly separate. To know Justice is to know not only the extension of the term “just” but also the intelligible order that makes justice valuable. To know Beauty is to understand not only a common feature but the form of harmonious excellence. Plato’s universalism is therefore not the thin, value-neutral theory familiar from some later accounts of universals. His Forms are explanatory and normative realities.

Still, Bluck’s defense should not be overstated. Recasting standards as criteria of intelligible order does not completely solve the problem of determination. One still needs to say what makes an action, object, or institution an incomplete realization of the Form. In mathematical cases, the answer may be structural exactness; in ethical cases, it may involve proportion, order, and function. But Plato does not provide a general theory covering all Forms. His examples suggest different modes of participation, and the unity of the theory remains partly programmatic.

Nor does the standard account solve every problem associated with universals. If Forms are transcendent, how can they enter into relations with sensible things? If participation is not resemblance, what is its positive content? If Forms are separate from particulars, how do they explain their properties without becoming merely abstract labels? These are enduring challenges. The universal and standard interpretations illuminate different aspects of the theory, but neither by itself supplies a complete metaphysical account.

## Conclusion

Plato’s middle dialogues do indeed portray Forms in two ways. They are universals insofar as they explain how many diverse things can be called by one name and understood under one intelligible nature. They are standards insofar as sensible particulars are imperfect, changing, and deficient realizations of them. The *Phaedo*’s discussion of Equality and the *Republic*’s contrast between the many and the one, together with the political and ethical function of intelligible models, provide strong textual support for both readings.

Nehamas’s Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars Objection exposes a genuine weakness in any crude standard theory. If Forms are merely perfect exemplars and particulars merely resemble them, Plato has not explained what the relevant resemblance consists in. Approximation becomes indeterminate or circular. The objection is particularly damaging where a Form must apply to heterogeneous particulars and where degrees of realization cannot be reduced to visible similarity.

Bluck’s defense succeeds to the extent that it reinterprets standards as criteria of intelligible order rather than as perfect sensible-like objects. On this account, falling short of a Form means realizing its structure or value incompletely, not copying an item by item. This makes it possible for a Form to be both universal and standard: universal as the intelligible nature shared by many, standard as the complete realization toward which those many are ordered.

The reconciliation is not perfect. Universality does not by itself entail gradations of adequacy, and standardhood requires an account of participation that Plato never fully develops. The relation between predication and approximation remains conceptually distinct. But distinctness is not incommensurability. Plato’s metaphysical framework does not collapse merely because Forms perform both logical and paradigmatic functions. Rather, it reflects Plato’s conviction that what makes a thing intelligibly what it is is also what makes it capable of being more or less fully what it ought to be. His Forms are not neutral universals supplemented externally by ideals; they are intelligible natures whose completeness supplies the measure of imperfect sensible realization.

The cost of this unified interpretation is that Plato’s theory remains architectonic rather than fully analytic. It offers a powerful vision of reality as ordered by intelligible principles, but leaves the precise mechanics of participation, instantiation, and approximation unresolved. The supposed conflation is therefore best understood as an unresolved tension within a single philosophical project—not a fatal contradiction, but a sign that Plato’s theory of Forms occupies the border between logic, ontology, epistemology, and ethics.
