**The Labyrinth of Truth: Evaluating Aquinas’s Distinction Between Human and Divine Truth**

The problem of truth has haunted Western philosophy since its inception, manifesting as an irreconcilable tension between the mutable, fallible nature of human cognition and the immutable, perfect nature of reality. Thomas Aquinas, operating within the Aristotelian tradition yet infusing it with Christian theology, attempts to navigate this labyrinth by positing a fundamental distinction between human truth and divine truth. In the *Summa Theologiae*, particularly in Question 16, Aquinas constructs a truth theory that relies on the correspondence theory of truth, accommodates the relativism of the Pre-Socratics, and differentiates between the finite, changeable propositions of the human mind and the eternal, immutable truth of the divine intellect. However, while this distinction provides a metaphysical framework to resolve the contradiction between human fallibility and divine perfection, it ultimately founders on the rocks of semantic imprecision and an untenable reliance on theology. The distinction between human and divine truth does not successfully resolve the contradictions in Aquinas’s truth theory; rather, it merely relocates them, creating a system where truth is ontologically sound but semantically unstable, and philosophically dependent on a theological foundation that alienates it from purely rational discourse.

To understand the ambition and the failure of Aquinas’s resolution, one must first examine the foundational structure of his truth theory. Aquinas’s truth theory is rooted in the correspondence theory, which he inherits from Aristotle. For Aristotle, truth is the adequation of the intellect to the thing (*adaequatio rei et intellectus*). Truth is not merely a subjective feeling or a linguistic convention; it is an ontological state in which the mind conforms to reality. Aquinas adopts this correspondence theory but radically reconfigures its metaphysical scope. In Question 16 of the *Summa Theologiae*, Aquinas asks whether truth is in the thing, in the intellect, or in both. His answer is definitive: truth is primarily in the thing, but it is also in the intellect insofar as the intellect measures itself against the thing. This correspondence is not a bilateral equivalence between mind and world, but a hierarchical measurement where the finite intellect looks to the eternal form. 

It is here that Aquinas introduces the crucial distinction between human truth and divine truth. Human truth is the truth of the human intellect; it is changeable, contingent, and subject to error because the human intellect is finite, limited in its capacity, and subject to the perturbations of the senses. Divine truth, conversely, is the truth of the divine intellect. Because God is pure actuality and infinite intellect, His truth is eternal, immutable, and identical with the divine essence itself. God does not come to know truth; He *is* truth. The contradiction in Aquinas’s theory arises precisely here: how can a changing human intellect possess truth, which by definition requires a stable correspondence, when the human intellect itself is in flux? If truth is correspondence, and the human intellect changes, then human truth seems inherently unstable, if not illusory.

Aquinas resolves this apparent contradiction by utilizing a metaphysical principle of participation, which resonates deeply with the epistemological anxieties of the Pre-Socratic philosophers. The Pre-Socratics, particularly the likes of Protagoras, famously posited a radical relativism: "Man is the measure of all things." In this framework, truth is entirely relative to the human subject; what is true for you may be false for me, and there is no objective anchor to adjudicate the dispute. Aquinas acknowledges the Pre-Socratic insight that human truth is indeed relative to the human intellect. In Question 16, Article 3, Aquinas argues that truth is in the human intellect in relation to the thing. Because the human intellect is finite, its truth is proportionate to its capacity; it is true *for us* when our intellect adequately conceives of the thing, but this adequacy is always partial and subject to revision. 

However, Aquinas diverges sharply from Pre-Socratic relativism by refusing to let this relativity be the final word. The Pre-Socratics, trapped in the flux of Heraclitus or the one of Parmenides, lacked the metaphysical apparatus to bridge the gap between the relative human perspective and the absolute nature of reality. Aquinas utilizes the Pre-Socratic recognition of human limitation not to validate relativism, but to underscore the necessity of an absolute standard. By grounding truth in the divine intellect, Aquinas provides the metaphysical anchor that the Pre-Socratics lacked. Human truth is relative and changeable because it participates in divine truth, which is absolute and immutable. The contradiction of a changing intellect grasping an unchanging reality is resolved by the distinction between the *mode* of truth in the human mind (which is accidental and dependent) and the *mode* of truth in the divine mind (which is substantial and necessary). Human truth is true because it is a finite, derivative participation in the infinite, essential truth of God.

Yet, while this distinction elegantly solves the metaphysical contradiction of changeable truth, it introduces a severe semantic and epistemological problem: the untenable reliance on theology and the resulting semantic imprecision regarding the multiple applications of truth. The objection that grounding truth in the divine intellect creates an untenable reliance on theology is not merely a modern secular critique; it is a structural flaw within Aquinas’s own system. When Aquinas defines truth primarily as a property of the divine intellect—God knowing all things in their eternal essence—he effectively makes truth a theological category. If divine truth is the ultimate reality, then to understand truth is to understand God. This collapses the boundary between philosophy and theology, reducing epistemology to a branch of revelation. 

The untenability of this reliance becomes apparent when we consider the epistemological circle it creates. For Aquinas, human truth is known through the illumination of the divine intellect. But how do we know the nature of the divine intellect? We know it through revelation and natural reason, both of which are themselves subject to the very human truth we are trying to validate. If the foundation of truth is the divine intellect, and our access to the divine intellect is mediated by human truth, then the distinction between human and divine truth becomes a tautology. We cannot step outside our human truth to verify the divine truth without already presupposing the validity of the very human cognitive faculties whose reliability is in question. The theological grounding thus renders the theory unfalsifiable and circular: truth is true because God says it is true, and we know God is true because the truth He reveals is true.

Furthermore, the grounding of truth in the divine intellect leads to profound semantic imprecision regarding the multiple applications of the word "truth." In Question 16, Article 1, Aquinas carefully delineates that truth applies to the thing, to the intellect, and to the act of understanding. He notes that truth is predicated of God, of the human mind, of things, and of the proposition. This multiplicity of application is not merely a grammatical quirk; it is a semantic chasm. When we say that a proposition is "true," we mean something fundamentally different than when we say that God is "true." In the human context, truth is a relation (the correspondence of mind to world). In the divine context, truth is an essence (the simplicity of God). When Aquinas uses the same word—*verum*—to bridge these radically different ontological categories, he commits a semantic equivocation that undermines the precision his correspondence theory demands.

This semantic imprecision is most acute when dealing with the "multiple applications of truth" to finite, changeable human propositions. Consider a human proposition such as "The sky is blue." This proposition is true when it corresponds to the actual state of the sky, but it is changeable; if the sky becomes overcast, the proposition ceases to be true. Now consider the divine truth that encompasses the proposition. God's truth regarding the sky is eternal and immutable; it does not change when the sky changes. The semantic problem arises when we attempt to unify these two applications under the single banner of "truth." If truth is correspondence, then divine truth cannot be correspondence in the same way human truth is, because God does not "correspond" to the sky; the sky corresponds to God's eternal conception of it. Yet, we speak of both as "true." The distinction between human and divine truth is meant to explain this difference, but it fails to resolve the semantic ambiguity. By using the same term for two fundamentally different logical categories (a relational quality and an essential attribute), Aquinas creates a conceptual fog where the boundary between the mutable and the immutable is obscured by language.

The failure of the distinction to fully resolve these contradictions becomes evident when we ask whether the theory successfully accounts for the *changeable* nature of human truth without reducing it to mere illusion. Aquinas argues that human truth is true *in itself* (secundum se), even if it is not the highest or most perfect truth. But this defense is precarious. If human truth is merely a participation in divine truth, and participation implies a diminution of the formal reality of the participated, then human truth is ontologically lesser. But if human truth is lesser, in what sense can it be considered "true" in a robust, philosophical sense? The correspondence theory requires that the intellect match the reality of the thing. If the human intellect is constantly changing, it cannot maintain a stable correspondence; its truth is always provisional, always subject to the next sensory input or cognitive revision. The distinction between human and divine truth thus isolates human truth as a kind of functional, but not ontological, truth. We treat human truth as true for practical purposes, but strictly speaking, only divine truth is ultimate truth. This renders the human truth of science, ethics, and everyday life epistemologically subordinate, a mere shadow of the divine.

Moreover, the reliance on theology to ground this distinction makes Aquinas’s truth theory vulnerable to the critique that it is not a theory of truth at all, but a theory of God. The philosopher seeking a rigorous account of how propositions relate to states of affairs is forced to adopt a metaphysical commitment to the existence of a divine intellect. If one rejects the theological premise, the entire edifice collapses into Pre-Socratic relativism. Without the divine intellect, there is no absolute anchor for the correspondence of the human mind to reality. The human intellect remains trapped in the flux of Protagoras, where truth is merely what appears to be true to the finite subject. Thus, Aquinas’s resolution is not a resolution at all; it is a relocation of the problem from the epistemological to the theological domain. The contradiction between mutable human truth and immutable reality is not solved; it is simply outsourced to the divine mind.

However, to dismiss Aquinas’s distinction as a failure is to ignore the profound philosophical necessity that drove it. The medieval mind operated in a universe where the finite and the infinite were not separated by the impenetrable abyss of modern secularism. In Aquinas’s framework, the distinction between human and divine truth provides a coherent metaphysical map for a world where change and permanence coexist. The correspondence theory finds its ultimate vindication not in the isolated human mind, but in the mind of God, where all things are perfectly and eternally known. The Pre-Socratic relativism is acknowledged as the human condition, but it is redeemed by the possibility of participation in the divine. Without this distinction, Aquinas’s truth theory would indeed collapse into contradiction: a changing mind could never possess a stable truth, and the correspondence theory would be reduced to a perpetual failure. In this light, the reliance on theology is not a flaw but the necessary foundation of a realistic metaphysics. 

Yet, the evaluative task requires us to judge whether the distinction *successfully resolves* the contradictions. By the standards of analytic philosophy and semantic clarity, it does not. The semantic imprecision regarding the multiple applications of truth—where "true" means a correspondence in the human realm and an essence in the divine realm—creates a theory that is internally equivocal. The untenable reliance on theology transforms truth from an objective, investigable concept into a dogmatic postulate. The contradiction between finite human propositions and eternal divine truth is not resolved so much as it is papered over by a metaphysical hierarchy that renders human truth derivative and ultimately epistemically dependent on an unprovable theological axiom.

In conclusion, Thomas Aquinas’s distinction between human truth and divine truth is a monumental achievement of medieval synthesis. It successfully utilizes the correspondence theory of truth to establish that truth resides in the adequation of intellect and reality, it intelligently incorporates Pre-Socratic insights regarding the relativity of human knowledge, and it provides a metaphysical hierarchy in Question 16 of the *Summa Theologiae* to account for both the finite, changeable nature of human propositions and the eternal, immutable nature of divine truth. By positing that human truth participates in divine truth, Aquinas resolves the immediate metaphysical contradiction of a changing intellect and an unchanging reality. However, the resolution is ultimately unsatisfactory. The grounding of truth in the divine intellect creates an untenable reliance on theology, rendering the theory circular and unfalsifiable from a purely philosophical standpoint. Furthermore, the semantic imprecision arising from the multiple applications of truth—where the term "true" equivocates between a relational correspondence and an essential identity—leaves the theory semantically unstable. The distinction between human and divine truth does not eliminate the contradictions in Aquinas’s truth theory; it merely transforms them into theological mysteries. In doing so, it achieves metaphysical coherence at the expense of semantic precision, leaving the philosopher with a system that is beautiful, comprehensive, and ultimately, philosophically insufficient.
