**The Ambiguity of “Descartes”: Evaluating the Semantic Escape from Van Inwagen’s Reductio**

**I. Introduction**

The Doctrine of Arbitrary Undetached Parts (DAUP) represents one of the most intuitively appealing yet philosophically perilous tenets in contemporary mereology. According to DAUP, for any object, arbitrarily chosen proper parts of that object are also parts of it—and consequently, they constitute a proper part of the whole. The doctrine’s appeal lies in its seemingly innocuous commitment: if a table is made of wood, and we arbitrarily select a subset of its wooden particles, that subset is a part of the table. Yet, as Peter van Inwagen has famously demonstrated, this doctrine harbors a catastrophic logical contradiction. Through a masterful *reductio ad absurdum*, van Inwagen argues that DAUP leads to the absurd conclusion that an object is a proper part of itself. 

Van Inwagen’s reductio typically employs the thought experiment of a human being—René Descartes—losing a leg. If DAUP is true, then the proper part of Descartes consisting of Descartes minus his leg (D-minus-L) is a proper part of Descartes. After the leg is lost, D-minus-L is exactly identical to Descartes. Through the transitivity of identity, van Inwagen concludes that Descartes before the loss is identical to D-minus-L, implying that Descartes is a proper part of himself—a logical impossibility. 

However, recent critical evaluations have proposed that van Inwagen’s reductio fails not on logical grounds, but due to a semantic ambiguity in the term “Descartes.” The claim is that by shifting between a material interpretation of Descartes (the living organism) and an abstract interpretation (the mereological sum of his parts), one manufactures an apparent violation of the transitivity of identity. This essay evaluates this claim by analyzing the four formal identities generated by van Inwagen’s scenario—specifically concerning D-minus and Descartes before and after the loss of a leg. It will determine whether disambiguating the ontological status of the relata dissolves the paradox, and ultimately assess whether maintaining a strict distinction between material objects and abstract objects successfully insulates DAUP from van Inwagen’s rejection.
