**The Absurdity of the Self-Contradicting Speaker: Resolving Moore’s Paradox through Moore, Grice, and Wittgenstein**

Moore’s Paradox has long stood as a fascinating anomaly in the landscape of analytic philosophy. Formulated most famously in the twentieth century, the paradox highlights the peculiar absurdity of first-person assertions such as, “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is raining.” Unlike logical contradictions (such as “It is raining and it is not raining”), Moore’s Paradox involves a seemingly coherent semantic structure that nonetheless strikes the speaker and listener as deeply absurd. The claim that this paradox can be resolved by synthesizing G. E. Moore’s conception of implicative relations with Paul Grice’s framework of conversational maxims offers a compelling pragmatic and pragmatic-linguistic resolution. However, this synthesis faces a formidable philosophical tension when juxtaposed against Ludwig Wittgenstein’s use relation theory, which treats first-person belief utterances as hesitant assertions. This essay evaluates the Moore-Grice resolution, analyzes the tension with Wittgenstein, and argues that characterizing the relation between assertion and belief through conversational implicature successfully captures the pragmatic absurdity of Moore’s Paradox, but ultimately fails to account for its full conceptual depth. Furthermore, it is defended that the distinctive absurdity of these problematic utterances primarily violates practical rationality—the norms governing the act of assertion—rather than theoretical rationality alone.

**Moore’s Implicative Relations and the Structure of Belief**

G. E. Moore’s resolution of the paradox rests on a specific account of the relationship between belief and truth. For Moore, belief is not merely a passive mental state; it carries an inherent implicative relation to the world. To believe a proposition is, in essence, to take that proposition to be true. When a speaker asserts “I believe p,” they are not merely reporting a psychological quirk; they are presenting p as true for themselves. This implicative relation means that belief commits the believer to the truth of the believed content. Moore argued that “I believe p” implicates p in a manner analogous to, though distinct from, logical entailment. If one sincerely asserts “I believe it is raining,” one is thereby taking it to be true that it is raining.

The absurdity of Moore’s Paradox emerges when this implicative relation is combined with a direct denial of the believed content. Consider the utterance: “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is raining.” According to Moore, the first half of this conjunction— “I believe it is not raining”—implicates that the speaker takes “it is not raining” to be true. The second half— “but as a matter of fact it is raining”—explicitly asserts the truth of the contradictory proposition. The absurdity, then, is not merely that the speaker is asserting two contradictory propositions (p and not-p), but that the speaker is simultaneously taking a proposition to be true (in the act of believing it) and asserting that it is false. The implicative relation of belief generates a practical contradiction: one cannot, in the very act of taking something to be true, simultaneously assert that it is not true. The paradox exposes the limits of self-referential speech acts; the speaker attempts to use the concept of belief to distance themselves from a proposition while simultaneously asserting its falsehood, creating a logical and conceptual knot.

**Grice’s Conversational Maxims and the Cooperative Principle**

Paul Grice’s framework of conversational maxims provides a powerful complementary lens through which to view this absurdity. Grice’s Cooperative Principle (CP) dictates that participants in a conversation make contributions that are required by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange. Under this umbrella fall four categories of maxims, the most relevant being the Maxim of Quality, which requires that one do not say what one believes to be false.

When we apply Gricean implicature to Moore’s Paradox, the absurdity is reframed as a pragmatic failure. When a speaker utters “I believe it is not raining,” they generate a conversational implicature. Because asserting “I believe q” implicates that the speaker takes q to be true (and thus, under normal circumstances, that q is true), the hearer is entitled to infer that the speaker is committed to the truth of the believed content. The utterance “I believe it is not raining” therefore implicates “It is not raining.” When the speaker immediately follows with “but as a matter of fact it is raining,” they are explicitly asserting the negation of the implicature. The conjunction of the implicature and the explicit assertion yields a contradiction: the speaker is implicitly asserting “not-p” and explicitly asserting “p.”

The resolution offered by this Moore-Grice synthesis is that the paradox is not a logical contradiction in the strict sense, but a pragmatic absurdity—a violation of the Maxim of Quality. The speaker is effectively asserting a contradiction, which is impossible in a cooperative exchange. The absurdity is located in the clash between the implicature generated by the belief utterance and the explicit factual assertion. The statement is funny or absurd because it forces the hearer to recognize that the speaker has breached the fundamental norms of cooperative communication by implicitly asserting something they simultaneously deny.

**Wittgenstein’s Use Relation Theory and the Tension**

However, this Gricean-Moorean resolution faces a profound philosophical tension when confronted with Ludwig Wittgenstein’s use relation theory. Wittgenstein, particularly in his later work and in notes collected in the *Blue Book* and the *Philosophical Investigations*, argued that first-person belief utterances function differently from ordinary assertions. For Wittgenstein, the expression “I believe p” is not a robust assertion of p, but rather a hesitant assertion or a device of permission. When one says “I believe it will rain,” one is not committing oneself to the truth of “it will rain” in the same way one commits to “It will rain.” Instead, “I believe” serves as a hedge, a way of expressing a proposition without the full assertoric force that would make its denial a direct contradiction.

Wittgenstein’s use relation theory suggests that the meaning and function of “I believe p” are tied to its use in language games. It is a grammatical device that allows a speaker to present a proposition as something they are inclined to accept, without fully staking their epistemic reputation on it. The absurdity of Moore’s Paradox, on this view, is not merely a pragmatic clash of implicatures, but a deeper conceptual absurdity rooted in the misuse of the expression “I believe.” 

The tension between the Gricean-Moorean account and Wittgenstein’s theory is sharp. The Moore-Grice synthesis relies on the premise that “I believe p” carries a robust assertoric force that implicates p. Grice’s Maxim of Quality and Moore’s implicative relations both presuppose that the belief utterance functions as a strong commitment to the truth of the believed content. Wittgenstein, however, denies this robust assertoric force. If “I believe p” is merely a hesitant assertion, then the implicature of p is much weaker, and the contradiction at the heart of Moore’s Paradox cannot be located simply in the clash of conversational implicatures.
