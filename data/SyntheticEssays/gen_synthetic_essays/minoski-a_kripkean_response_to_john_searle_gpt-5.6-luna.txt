# Is Kripke’s Theory of Proper Names Fundamentally Descriptivist?

## Introduction

The philosophical theory of proper names is often presented as a dispute between two competing pictures of reference. According to descriptivism, a proper name refers to an object by being associated with some description, or with a cluster of descriptions, that identifies the object. “Aristotle,” for example, might refer to whoever was Plato’s student, Alexander’s tutor, and the author of the *Metaphysics*. According to Saul Kripke’s causal-historical theory, by contrast, a proper name is introduced in an initial “baptism” and subsequently passed from speaker to speaker through a causal chain of communication. Its reference is preserved even when speakers have little or no descriptive knowledge of its bearer.

John Searle objected that Kripke’s appeal to baptism and causal chains does not eliminate descriptive content. A baptism, Searle argues, must be guided by some identifying intention, and the continuation of a causal chain depends upon speakers’ ability to recognize that they are using the same name for the same object. On this view, causal relations do not by themselves explain reference. They function only in conjunction with descriptions, beliefs, intentions, or other representational contents.

The disagreement is not merely about whether names are associated with descriptions. Kripke himself allows that speakers commonly associate descriptions with names. The deeper issue concerns the role such descriptions play. Do they constitute the semantic mechanism by which a name refers, or do they merely help fix the reference of an expression whose reference is then maintained independently of descriptive content? This distinction is crucial. A description may be involved in introducing or transmitting a name without being what the name means or what ultimately determines its reference in every context.

This essay argues that Kripke’s theory is not fundamentally descriptivist. Searle is right that acts of naming often involve intentions and descriptive information, and he is also right that causal chains cannot be understood as entirely brute physical sequences. Nevertheless, the initial baptism of a name does not require a uniquely identifying description. A demonstrative, perceptual encounter, or communal act may fix a referent without supplying a uniquely identifying property. The hospital twin thought experiment and cases of mistaken identity further show that descriptive content may be incomplete, irrelevant, or false while the name continues to refer to the object originally introduced. The best explanation is therefore that the object, together with the historical practice linking the name to it, is the primary driver of reference. Descriptive content can help establish, preserve, or recover reference, but it is not the semantic basis of proper names.

## I. The Descriptivist Picture

The classical descriptivist theory is associated most prominently with Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell, although it takes several different forms. On one version, a proper name is semantically equivalent to a definite description. The name “Aristotle” might mean “the teacher of Alexander the Great,” or “the author of the *Nicomachean Ethics*.” On another version, developed by Searle, a name is associated not with one description but with a cluster of descriptions. A speaker who uses “Aristotle” may associate with it descriptions such as “a Greek philosopher,” “Plato’s student,” “Alexander’s tutor,” and “the author of many philosophical works.” No single description must be decisive. Rather, the name refers to the object that satisfies enough of the associated descriptions.

Descriptivism has several apparent advantages. First, it seems to explain how speakers can understand names. If someone knows that “Aristotle” means “the author of the *Metaphysics*,” then the person has a route to identifying the name’s bearer. Second, descriptions appear to explain informative identity statements. “Hesperus is Phosphorus” was informative because ancient speakers associated the two names with different descriptions, even though both referred to Venus. Third, descriptions appear important in the introduction of new names. A community cannot begin using a name without some reason for applying it to one object rather than another.

Searle’s version of descriptivism is more flexible than the crude claim that every name has one exact synonymous description. He emphasizes that speakers possess a “cluster” of identifying information. The reference of a name is determined by a network of descriptions and conventions rather than by a single necessary condition. This allows the theory to accommodate error. A person may falsely believe that Aristotle wrote a certain work and still successfully use the name “Aristotle,” provided that the overall cluster points toward the historical philosopher.

Yet the flexibility of the cluster theory generates a problem. If no individual description is necessary, and if no precise number of descriptions must be satisfied, what determines reference? The answer can seem dependent on the speaker’s intentions or on the descriptive content that happens to dominate in a particular context. Kripke’s criticism is that this makes reference unstable and fails to capture the way names actually function in ordinary and scientific discourse.

## II. Kripke’s Alternative: Names as Rigid Designators

Kripke’s central claim is that proper names are rigid designators. A rigid designator designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists. “Aristotle” refers to Aristotle in the actual world, and in counterfactual worlds where Aristotle had not become a philosopher, had not taught Alexander, or had died in infancy, the name still refers to Aristotle. A description such as “the teacher of Alexander” is not rigid in this way. In some possible world, someone else might have taught Alexander, or Aristotle might not have done so. The description can therefore pick out different objects in different worlds.

This distinction does not imply that every use of a name is psychologically independent of description. Speakers may use descriptions to locate an object, learn its name, or determine what another speaker is talking about. Kripke’s point is semantic rather than psychological: the descriptions associated with a name do not constitute the name’s meaning in the way descriptivism claims.

Kripke’s theory has two main components. First, a name is introduced through an initial naming event, or baptism. This may involve directly indicating an object: “Let this ship be called *Vega*.” It may involve ostension, as when a parent points to a child and says, “Her name is Anna.” It may also involve a description, as when a community decides to call “the first settlement beyond the river” by a particular name. Second, after the baptism, the name is transmitted through a causal-historical chain. A later speaker can use the name successfully if that speaker intends to use it as it was used by members of the preceding linguistic community and if the chain ultimately connects the use to the original baptism.

The theory therefore does not deny that descriptions can be involved in naming. It denies that they are the fundamental semantic determinants of reference. A description may play a role in fixing reference on a particular occasion, while the name itself remains a rigid designator whose reference is thereafter secured historically.

## III. Searle’s Challenge: Baptism Requires Descriptive Content

Searle’s most important challenge concerns the initial baptism. Kripke’s appeal to baptism may seem to postpone rather than solve the problem of reference. If a speaker says, “Let this object be called ‘N’,” how does the demonstrative “this object” succeed in identifying one object? If several objects are present, what makes one of them the intended referent? Searle argues that the baptism must be governed by descriptive content. The namer must have some intention such as “I shall use ‘N’ for the person standing before me,” “for the child born at this time,” or “for the object I am pointing to.” Without such content, there is no reason to think that a particular object, rather than another, has been named.

This objection is powerful if “fixing a referent” means selecting one object from a set of possible candidates by means of a uniquely identifying condition. Suppose a ceremony takes place in a room containing two visually indistinguishable objects. Someone announces, “I hereby name this object ‘Alpha.’” If the speaker has no way of distinguishing the two objects and no intention directed toward one rather than the other, it may appear that the baptism has failed. A descriptive or intentional criterion seems necessary to determine which object was named.

However, the conclusion depends on what sort of descriptive content is required. There is a major difference between requiring some content that directs attention and requiring a uniquely identifying description. Kripke can concede the former while rejecting the latter. A baptism may involve the demonstrative “this,” a perceptual relation, a physical gesture, or a causal interaction. These may identify the object in context without attributing to it a unique property known or represented by the namer.

Consider a parent holding a newborn child and saying, “Her name is Lydia.” The parent need not possess a uniquely identifying description such as “the female child with exactly these genetic properties, born at 3:42 p.m. to these parents.” The parent’s relation to the child—holding her, seeing her, and intending to name her—can be enough. The parent may even be unable to distinguish the child from an identical twin by descriptive properties. Still, the parent can name one child rather than the other by directing the naming act toward that child.

The same point applies to naming an animal, a star, or a physical artifact. An astronomer may point toward a newly observed object and introduce a provisional name without knowing its chemical composition, size, origin, or unique location relative to all other objects. The object can be singled out demonstratively, even though no uniquely identifying description is available. The description “the object I am pointing at” may be true in the context, but it is not a substantial identifying description in the descriptivist sense. It does not explain reference by specifying a distinctive property. It works because of the speaker’s perceptual and causal relation to the object.

Searle could reply that the demonstrative itself has descriptive content. “This object” may be analyzed as “the object standing in a certain contextually specified relation to me.” Yet even if this is granted, the analysis does not establish descriptivism about proper names. The fact that an expression used during baptism has descriptive or indexical content does not show that the baptized name inherits that content as its meaning. The demonstrative may perform the pragmatic work of directing attention, while the name thereafter refers to the object independently of the description.

## IV. Must the Initial Baptism Identify One Object Uniquely?

The question of uniqueness deserves careful treatment. There are at least three senses in which a baptism might “identify” an object.

First, the object may be uniquely identifiable by a description known to the namer. For example, a scientific team might name “the planet orbiting the nearest star with a periodic dimming pattern.” If only one object satisfies the description, the description can fix the reference.

Second, the object may be uniquely selected in a context without being uniquely described. A speaker can point to one of two objects, touch one of two cups, or hand one of two children to another person. The object is unique relative to the act, even if there is no property that distinguishes it from all possible objects.

Third, an object may be selected through a causal encounter even when the namer lacks the ability to identify it again. A person may name a mountain seen briefly from a train, even though the person could not later distinguish it from similar mountains. The original encounter can still ground a name.

Searle’s argument requires the first model, or something close to it. He needs baptism to be based on descriptive content that determines which object is intended. But ordinary naming practices often operate according to the second and third models. They depend on attention, demonstration, causal contact, and social coordination rather than on uniquely identifying descriptions.

There is also a distinction between epistemic uniqueness and metaphysical uniqueness. A namer may not know enough to distinguish an object from all others, yet the object can nevertheless be the one actually involved in the naming event. The child held by the parent is one particular child, even if the parent cannot formulate a property possessed only by that child. The absence of a uniquely known description does not entail the absence of a determinate referent.
