## The Unity of the Human Good: Moral Virtue as an Intrinsic Constituent of Aristotelian Eudaimonia

The *Nicomachean Ethics* presents one of the most enduring and perplexing tensions in the history of philosophy: the precise relationship between moral virtue and happiness (*eudaimonia*). On one hand, Book I famously defines happiness as “activity of soul in accordance with complete virtue” (1098a16-18), suggesting a comprehensive life of excellent activity. On the other hand, Book X appears to crown theoretical contemplation (*theoria*) as the sole activity that is “best, most pleasant, and most self-sufficient” (1177a12-18), seemingly relegating moral virtue to a secondary, merely instrumental status. This tension has generated a robust scholarly debate between the *inclusive* and *dominant* (or intellectualist) interpretations of the chief good. The inclusive view, defended with great rigor by J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche, argues that *eudaimonia* is a composite whole comprising both moral and intellectual virtues, where moral virtue operates as an intrinsic constituent of the good life. The intellectualist view, most subtly articulated by John Cooper, posits that while moral virtue is necessary for human flourishing, it ultimately serves as a developmental prerequisite and practical foundation for the *highest* good—divine contemplation—which alone constitutes the *telos* of human existence. This essay will argue that the inclusive interpretation, as defended by Ackrill and Roche, offers the most philosophically coherent and textually faithful reading of the *Ethics*. While Cooper’s hierarchical distinction between human happiness and divine contemplation captures a genuine Aristotelian emphasis on the superiority of *theoria*, it ultimately misidentifies the *locus* of happiness. Moral virtue is not merely a means to a higher end; it is an intrinsic, constitutive element of the human good, because the human function is irreducibly composite. The reconciliation of practical reasoning and pure intellectual activity lies not in separating them into distinct lives, but in understanding *theoria* as the culminating expression of a soul already harmonized by moral virtue—a peak that is part of the mountain, not a separate mountain itself.

### The Textual and Philosophical Problem

The interpretive crux of the *Nicomachean Ethics* hinges on how one reads the relationship between the function argument of Book I and the encomium to contemplation in Book X. In Book I, Aristotle argues that the human function (*ergon*) is “activity of the soul in accordance with reason, or not without reason” (1098a7-8). He concludes that the human good is “activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete” (1098a16-18). This definition seems to leave open the possibility that the “best and most complete” virtue could be singular (theoretical wisdom) or plural (all virtues, both moral and intellectual). The ambiguity is compounded by Aristotle’s subsequent discussion of self-sufficiency, where he claims that happiness is “the most complete (*teleion*)” end because it is pursued for its own sake and is “lacking in nothing” (1097b20-21).

However, Book X appears to resolve this ambiguity in favor of the intellectualist reading. Aristotle states that “happiness extends, then, just so far as contemplation does, and those to whom contemplation more belongs are more truly happy” (1178a8-9). He further argues that the life of contemplation is the highest because it is the most self-sufficient, the most continuous, and the most akin to the divine. He famously advises that “we ought not to listen to those who exhort us, being human, to think of human things, and, being mortal, of mortal things, but ought, so far as possible, to achieve immortality” (1177b31-33). This passage seems to suggest that the ultimate human goal is to transcend our mortal, composite nature and engage in an activity that is purely intellectual, thereby making moral virtue—which concerns the passions, appetites, and human relationships—a mere stepping stone to the true end.

The intellectualist reading, historically championed by figures like W. F. R. Hardie and later nuanced by John Cooper, argues that *eudaimonia* is a *dominant* end, where one activity (contemplation) is the sole or primary constituent of happiness, and all other goods are pursued *for the sake of* this dominant activity. On this view, moral virtue is necessary, but only because it secures the conditions—leisure, freedom from internal conflict, and a stable character—that allow for uninterrupted contemplation. The inclusive reading, by contrast, argues that *eudaimonia* is a *comprehensive* end, comprising a plurality of intrinsically valuable activities, including both moral and intellectual virtues. The debate thus centers on whether moral virtue is an *intrinsic constituent* of the good life or merely a *necessary prerequisite* for the highest good.

### The Inclusive Defense: Ackrill and Roche

J. L. Ackrill’s seminal article, “Aristotle on *Eudaimonia*” (1974), provides the foundational defense of the inclusive interpretation. Ackrill begins by distinguishing between two senses of “complete” (*teleion*). A *dominant* end is one that is complete because it is the sole ultimate goal, and all other goods are pursued for its sake. An *inclusive* end is complete because it contains all intrinsically valuable goods within itself. Ackrill argues that Aristotle’s language in Book I strongly supports the inclusive reading. When Aristotle says that happiness is “lacking in nothing” (1097b21), he is not referring to a single activity that is self-sufficient *in itself*, but to a *whole life* that is self-sufficient because it includes all goods. Ackrill notes that if happiness were merely contemplation, then it would be odd for Aristotle to praise the moral virtues so extensively in Books II through VI. He argues that the moral virtues are not merely instrumental to contemplation; they are activities that are chosen for their own sake. For example, acting justly or courageously is not merely a means to a state of tranquility; it is an expression of our rational nature that is itself valuable.

Ackrill’s key insight is that the *function argument* does not specify a single activity but a *range* of activities. The human function is “activity of the soul in accordance with reason,” and this activity can be expressed in both theoretical and practical domains. The moral virtues are activities of the rational part of the soul insofar as it rules the appetitive part. Thus, the exercise of moral virtue is itself a form of rational activity, and therefore a part of the human function. Ackrill concludes that *eudaimonia* is a complex whole, analogous to a game like tennis, where the pleasure is not a separate goal but is constituted by the various activities of playing (serving, volleying, etc.). Similarly, happiness is constituted by a plurality of virtuous activities, both moral and intellectual. To exclude moral virtue from the definition of happiness would be to ignore the very definition Aristotle provides in Book I.

Timothy Roche, in his essay “*Ergon* and *Eudaimonia* in *Nicomachean Ethics* I” (1988), deepens Ackrill’s argument by focusing on the precise meaning of the *ergon* argument. Roche argues that the intellectualist reading misconstrues the function of a human being. The function of a human is not simply to think, but to live a *human* life, which is a life of rational activity in accordance with virtue broadly construed. Roche points out that Aristotle’s definition of happiness in Book I includes the phrase “in accordance with the best and most complete virtue” (1098a16-18). Roche argues that this phrase is best understood as referring to *all* the virtues, not just the intellectual virtue of theoretical wisdom. He notes that in Book VI, Aristotle explicitly lists *phronesis* (practical wisdom) as an intellectual virtue, and he insists that *phronesis* and moral virtue are mutually dependent. One cannot have moral virtue without practical wisdom, and one cannot have practical wisdom without moral virtue (1144b30-32). Therefore, the “complete virtue” mentioned in Book I must include both the moral and the practical intellectual virtues.

Roche also addresses the apparent conflict with Book X. He argues that when Aristotle says contemplation is the highest activity, he is not saying it is the *only* constituent of happiness. Rather, he is saying that it is the *best* part of a composite whole. Roche points to Aristotle’s statement in Book X that the life of contemplation is “higher than the human level” (1177b26-27). Aristotle explicitly acknowledges that a life of pure contemplation is not a human life but a divine one. Since humans are composite beings (body and soul, passions and reason), their happiness must be a composite activity. Roche argues that the moral virtues are not merely instrumental to contemplation; they are *constitutive* of the human good because they are the activities of the rational soul in its practical capacity. To have a good human life, one must exercise both practical and theoretical reason. The moral virtues are the *sine qua non* of a well-ordered soul, but they are also intrinsically valuable because they are expressions of our rational nature.

### The Intellectualist Challenge: John Cooper

John Cooper’s nuanced defense of the intellectualist position, most fully articulated in his book *Reason and Human Good in Aristotle* (1975), presents a formidable challenge to the inclusive reading. Cooper does not deny that moral virtue is necessary for happiness. Rather, he argues that there is a strict hierarchy within the good life, and that *theoria* is the *sole* ultimate end. Cooper distinguishes between two levels of happiness: the *human* good, which includes moral virtue, and the *divine* good, which is pure contemplation. He argues that while the human good is a composite including moral virtue, the *highest* good—the *telos* towards which all other goods are ordered—is contemplation alone.

Cooper’s argument rests on a careful reading of the self-sufficiency argument in Book I and the encomium in Book X. He argues that Aristotle’s claim that happiness is “lacking in nothing” (1097b21) does not mean that happiness includes all intrinsic goods, but rather that happiness is the *most* self-sufficient activity. Contemplation, Cooper argues, is the only activity that is self-sufficient in the strictest sense, because it does not require external goods or other people. Moral virtue, by contrast, requires other people (as objects of justice or friendship) and external goods (wealth, health). Therefore, contemplation is the only activity that can be the *dominant* end. Cooper argues that moral virtue is necessary for happiness because it secures the conditions for contemplation: it frees the soul from the tyranny of the passions, it provides the leisure and stability needed for study, and it ensures that the contemplator is a good person. However, moral virtue is not itself a component of the *highest* happiness; it is a means to that end.

Cooper’s most significant contribution is his distinction between the *human* and *divine* levels of happiness. He argues that Aristotle’s advice to “immortalize ourselves” (1177b33) is not a call to abandon our human nature, but rather a call to engage in the highest activity of which we are capable—an activity that is analogous to the divine but is still performed by a human being. Cooper suggests that the *human* good is a composite life that includes both moral and intellectual activity, but the *ultimate* goal—the *telos* that gives meaning to the whole—is contemplation. On this view, moral virtue is a *necessary condition* for the good life, but it is not an *intrinsic constituent* of the *chief* good. The chief good is contemplation alone.

### Evaluating the Necessity: Intrinsic Constituent or Prerequisite?

The central question is whether Cooper’s hierarchical model is faithful to Aristotle’s own commitments. I will argue that it is not, and that the inclusive reading of Ackrill and Roche is superior on both textual and philosophical grounds.

First, the textual evidence from Book I strongly supports the inclusive reading. Aristotle defines happiness as “activity of soul in accordance with complete virtue” (1098a16-18). If happiness were merely contemplation, Aristotle would not have used the plural “virtue” or the qualifier “complete” in a way that encompasses all virtues. Moreover, in Book I, Aristotle explicitly states that the happy person is one who is “active in accordance with complete virtue” and that this requires a full life: “for one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy” (1098a18-20). This suggests that happiness is a *temporal* whole, constituted by a variety of activities over a lifetime. A life of pure contemplation, even if it were possible, would be a life of a single activity, not a complete life in the Aristotelian sense.

Second, the *function argument* itself supports the inclusive reading. The function of a human being is not merely to think, but to live a life of rational activity. This activity has two distinct but related aspects: the theoretical (contemplation) and the practical (moral and political action). Aristotle explicitly says that the human function is “activity of the soul in accordance with reason, or not without reason” (1098a7-8). The phrase “or not without reason” is crucial. It acknowledges that some activities (like moral virtue) involve reason in a directive or practical sense, rather than in a purely contemplative sense. The exercise of moral virtue is an activity of the rational soul, and therefore it is a part of the human function. To exclude it from the definition of happiness would be to arbitrarily privilege one aspect of the function over another.

Third, the philosophical coherence of the inclusive view is stronger. Cooper’s model faces a serious problem: if moral virtue is merely a means to contemplation, then it is difficult to see why Aristotle would devote so much attention to the moral virtues as intrinsically valuable. Aristotle repeatedly says that virtuous actions are chosen for their own sake (1105a31-33). For example, the courageous person chooses to act courageously “for the sake of the noble” (1115b13). If courage were merely a means to the tranquility required for contemplation, then it would be chosen for the sake of contemplation, not for its own sake. But Aristotle explicitly rejects this instrumental account of moral virtue. The moral virtues are not merely tools; they are expressions of our rational nature that are valuable in themselves.

Moreover, the relationship between *phronesis* and moral virtue undermines Cooper’s instrumentalism. In Book VI, Aristotle argues that *phronesis* is not merely a means to an end, but is itself a virtue of the rational part of the soul. He states that *phronesis* and moral virtue are inseparable: “it is not possible to be good in the strict sense without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without moral virtue” (1144b30-32). This mutual dependence means that moral virtue is not a mere prerequisite for intellectual activity; it is a constitutive part of the rational soul’s excellence. The person who has moral virtue is not just someone who has prepared the ground for contemplation; they are someone who is actively exercising their rationality in a practical domain. This activity is itself a part of the good life.

Finally, Cooper’s distinction between human and divine happiness is problematic because it creates a dualism that Aristotle himself rejects. Aristotle is a naturalist; he believes that the human good is achievable by human beings in this life. The life of pure contemplation, as he admits, is “higher than the human level” (1177b26-27). To make this divine life the *sole* *telos* of human existence is to make happiness unattainable for most humans, and even for the most virtuous, it would be a life that is only intermittently realized. Aristotle’s ethical theory is practical; it aims at helping human beings live well in the here and now. The inclusive view, which sees happiness as a composite of moral and intellectual activities, is more consistent with Aristotle’s practical orientation. It allows for a happiness that is achievable by human beings, even if the *highest* part of that happiness is contemplation.

### Reconciling Practical Reasoning and Pure Intellectual Activity

If moral virtue is an intrinsic constituent of the good life, how do we reconcile it with the apparent supremacy of contemplation in Book X? The answer lies in understanding the *structure* of the composite good life. The inclusive view does not deny that contemplation is the *best* activity; it merely denies that it is the *only* activity that constitutes happiness. The happy life is a *whole* that is organized hierarchically, with contemplation as its peak. But the peak is part of the mountain. The moral virtues are not merely a foundation upon which contemplation is built; they are the very activities that make contemplation possible *and* meaningful.

The reconciliation is achieved through the concept of *phronesis*. Practical wisdom is not opposed to theoretical wisdom; it is the *human* mode of achieving excellence in the practical domain. The *phronimos* (the practically wise person) is the one who has cultivated the moral virtues in such a way that their rational soul is in harmony. This harmony is not merely a psychological state; it is an active engagement with the world. The *phronimos* exercises practical reasoning in every decision, and this exercise is itself a form of intellectual activity. When the *phronimos* contemplates, they do so *as* a person who has already achieved excellence in practical reasoning. Their contemplation is not a separate, abstract activity; it is the culmination of a life of rational activity.

Aristotle’s advice to “immortalize ourselves” (1177b33) should not be read as a call to abandon our human nature, but rather as a call to engage in the highest activity of which our human nature is capable. The activity of contemplation is the *telos* of the practical life, but it is not a *separate* life. The person who has achieved moral virtue is the person who is best able to contemplate, because their soul is not disturbed by passions, their character is stable, and their rational faculties are fully developed. But more importantly, the moral virtues are not just a means to this state; they are the *expression* of the rational soul in its practical dimension. The happy life is a life in which both practical and theoretical reason are exercised excellently. The practical reasoning of the *phronimos* and the theoretical reasoning of the *sophos* are two aspects of the same rational soul, and both are constitutive of the human good.

Thus, the reconciliation lies in recognizing that *theoria* is not a separate activity that excludes moral virtue, but rather the *culmination* of a life that is already virtuous. The moral virtues provide the *formal* condition for contemplation—they ensure that the rational soul is properly ordered—but they also have intrinsic value because they are activities of the rational soul. The happy life is a *single, complex activity* that includes both moral and intellectual virtues. The intellectualist reading, by isolating contemplation as the sole constituent of happiness, fails to capture the unity of the human soul and the richness of the human good.

### Conclusion

The debate between the inclusive and intellectualist interpretations of the *Nicomachean Ethics* is not merely a scholarly squabble; it goes to the heart of what it means to live a good human life. The intellectualist reading, as articulated by John Cooper, offers a compelling vision of a life ordered towards the highest, most divine activity. However, it ultimately fails to account for the full scope of Aristotle’s ethical theory. The inclusive reading, defended by J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche, provides a more coherent and textually faithful account. By focusing on the concept of *teleion* and the function argument, they demonstrate that *eudaimonia* is a comprehensive end that includes both moral and intellectual virtues. Moral virtue is not merely a necessary developmental prerequisite for theoretical wisdom; it is an intrinsic constituent of the good life. The exercise of moral virtue is an activity of the rational soul, and as such, it is a part of the human function.

The tension between Book I and Book X is resolved not by choosing one over the other, but by understanding the hierarchical structure of the composite good.
