## The Ghost in the Machine: Tugushev’s Rejection of Totality and the Unresolved Problem of Free Will

Leo Tolstoy’s *War and Peace* is not merely a novel of Napoleonic warfare and aristocratic romance; it is a vast philosophical treatise disguised as fiction. In the lengthy Second Epilogue, Tolstoy abandons narrative altogether to grapple with the most stubborn problem in metaphysics: the compatibility of free will with the deterministic laws that govern the physical world. His argument, constructed as a *reductio ad absurdum*, is deceptively simple and devastatingly effective. He posits that if we accept the universe as a closed, causally complete totality—a “totality of being” where every event is the necessary consequence of all preceding events—then human freedom is an illusion. Conversely, if we posit an absolute, uncaused free will, we render history and science incoherent, reducing human action to a chaotic, inexplicable eruption. Reason, Tolstoy concludes, demands the former; consciousness insists on the latter. This antinomy, he argues, is unresolvable by rational means.

In response to Tolstoy’s formidable challenge, the philosopher Nikolay Tugushev offers a critique that is both incisive and strategically brilliant. Tugushev argues that Tolstoy’s *reductio* is not a proof of determinism but a demonstration of the absurdity of its metaphysical presupposition—namely, the totality of being. By rejecting this presupposition, Tugushev aims to break the logical chain that forces the deterministic conclusion. He posits an “open” universe, one not bound by a global, closed causal nexus, thereby creating logical space for genuine human agency. However, Tugushev’s critique, while a necessary corrective to Tolstoy’s monolithic ontology, is ultimately insufficient. The central philosophical question, which this essay will evaluate, is whether Tugushev’s rejection of the totality successfully circumvents the deterministic conclusion *without* reducing free will to a spontaneous, self-caused force arising out of nothingness. A rigorous examination reveals that Tugushev’s negative critique—the mere denial of totality—fails to provide a positive ontology of freedom. In doing so, it leaves free will in a precarious limbo, trapped between the Scylla of incoherent self-causation (*causa sui*) and the Charybdis of brute, meaningless randomness. To truly escape Tolstoy’s dilemma, Tugushev would need to abandon the libertarian framework entirely or develop a robust theory of emergent, top-down causation that he does not supply. Thus, while Tugushev correctly identifies the faulty foundation of Tolstoy’s argument, his solution collapses under the weight of its own metaphysical vacuum, proving that the rejection of the totality is a necessary but profoundly insufficient step towards resolving the problem of free will.

### I. Tolstoy’s Reductio and the Tyranny of the Totality

To understand Tugushev’s critique, one must first appreciate the precise mechanics of Tolstoy’s argument. In the Epilogue, Tolstoy is not merely a novelist but a historian of philosophy, engaging directly with the Enlightenment’s legacy of rational determinism. His argument unfolds in a series of logical steps. First, he observes that history, as a science, seeks to discover the laws that govern human events. Yet, he notes, “the moment we admit the slightest element of free will, the existence of historical laws becomes impossible.” This is the initial premise of his *reductio*. If human actions are truly free—meaning they are not the necessary consequences of prior causes—then they are inexplicable by any scientific law. History would be a sequence of miracles, a series of uncaused events that defy rational comprehension.

Second, Tolstoy identifies the alternative: absolute determinism. If every human action is the necessary product of prior physical, psychological, and social causes, then history is as deterministic as Newtonian mechanics. The actions of Napoleon, Alexander, or Kutuzov are as predictable, in principle, as the trajectory of a billiard ball. Yet, this conclusion is immediately met with a powerful, subjective counter-argument. Our inner consciousness, our immediate experience of deliberation and choice, provides an unshakeable conviction of our own freedom. We feel that we could have done otherwise. Tolstoy writes, “However much we may prove to a man that he is not free, he will never be able to believe it.” This is the core of the antinomy: reason leads infallibly to determinism, but consciousness leads infallibly to freedom.

The crucial pivot in Tolstoy’s argument is his identification of the *metaphysical presupposition* that makes the deterministic conclusion seem inevitable. This is the concept of the “totality of being.” For Tolstoy, the universe is a closed, complete, and unified whole. Every event—from the fall of a leaf to the decision of a general to advance—is a component of this totality. The law of sufficient reason, applied globally, dictates that for every event, there exists a complete set of antecedent conditions that are jointly sufficient to necessitate it. If the whole of the universe at time *t* is a determinate state *S*, then the state at time *t+1* is necessarily determined by *S* combined with the laws of nature. There is no “gap” in this causal nexus, no point where an external or spontaneous force could intervene. Human beings, as parts of this totality, are wholly subject to its laws. Our thoughts, desires, and decisions are merely epiphenomena of the complex physical processes that constitute our brains, which are themselves fully determined by the prior state of the universe.

Tolstoy’s *reductio* is thus constructed as follows: (1) If the totality of being exists, then the universe is a closed causal system. (2) If the universe is a closed causal system, then every human action is causally necessitated. (3) If every human action is causally necessitated, then free will is an illusion. (4) But free will is a datum of consciousness, an undeniable aspect of human experience. (5) Therefore, the totality of being leads to a contradiction with consciousness. Since we cannot deny consciousness, the totality of being must be rejected—but Tolstoy argues that reason cannot reject it, because reason is itself the faculty that constructs the totality. Thus, we are left with an impasse, a “tragedy” of reason. Tolstoy concludes that the problem is insoluble because it arises from the very structure of human cognition, which is split between the rational demand for causal explanation and the intuitive demand for moral responsibility.

### II. Tugushev’s Critique: The Open Universe and the Rejection of Totality

Nikolay Tugushev’s critique enters precisely at this point of impasse. His central claim is that Tolstoy has conflated an *epistemic* ideal with an *ontological* reality. The “totality of being” is not a feature of the world itself, Tugushev argues, but rather a regulative principle of reason—a heuristic device that guides our search for explanation. We *strive* to understand events as part of a complete causal network, but this striving does not entail that such a complete network actually exists. To assert the existence of the totality is to commit a category error, treating a methodological postulate as a metaphysical fact.

Tugushev’s critique is rooted in a process-oriented, or “open,” ontology. He rejects the Newtonian-Laplacian vision of the universe as a deterministic clockwork, where the state of the whole at any given moment contains the complete blueprint of the future. Instead, he posits a universe that is fundamentally incomplete, characterized by genuine novelty and becoming. The future is not contained in the past; rather, it is created through the interaction of entities. Causation, in this view, is not a global, top-down imposition of order but a local, bottom-up affair. Events are not determined by the totality of the universe but by a finite, limited set of local conditions. These conditions provide *constraints* on what can happen, but they do not *necessitate* a single outcome. There is always a degree of indeterminacy, a “slack” in the causal fabric, that allows for the emergence of new forms and new events.

By rejecting the totality, Tugushev aims to dismantle Tolstoy’s *reductio*. The deterministic conclusion only follows if one accepts the premise of a closed causal system. If the universe is open, if the causal nexus is not globally complete, then the argument collapses. The fact that my action is not determined by the totality of the universe does not mean it is uncaused; it simply means it is not *globally* necessitated. There is room for the “event” of human choice to be a genuine addition to the world, a novel synthesis of prior conditions that does not simply repeat the past but creatively transcends it.

Tugushev’s critique is powerful because it shifts the burden of proof. Tolstoy must demonstrate not merely that we *think* in terms of a totality, but that such a totality actually exists. Tugushev points out that we can never empirically verify the existence of a complete causal nexus, since we are always finite observers embedded within the process. The totality is a transcendental illusion, a projection of reason’s demand for completeness onto a reality that is inherently incomplete. By exposing this illusion, Tugushev frees us from the tyranny of the deterministic conclusion. He opens up a space for freedom, a logical space where human agency can operate without violating the laws of physics, because those laws are not seen as a seamless, all-encompassing web but as a patchwork of local regularities with genuine gaps.

### III. The Trap of *Causa Sui* and the Specter of Randomness

However, Tugushev’s critique, while strategically astute, faces a profound ontological challenge. Rejecting the totality is a negative move; it tells us what free will is *not* (not determined by the whole), but it does not tell us what free will *is*. The moment Tugushev opens up a space for freedom, he must provide a positive account of what occupies that space. If the free act is not determined by prior causes, then what is its origin? The answer to this question determines whether Tugushev’s solution is coherent or whether it collapses into the very absurdity he sought to avoid.

The first, and most obvious, trap is the concept of *causa sui*—the self-caused cause. If the free act is not caused by anything external to itself, then it must cause itself. This is the classic libertarian position, famously critiqued by Spinoza, Hume, and countless others. The concept of *causa sui* is logically incoherent. To cause itself, an entity would have to exist prior to its own existence, which is a contradiction. It would have to be both the cause and the effect, the creator and the created. If my choice is self-caused, then what is the “self” that does the causing? If the self is a stable entity, then its choices are determined by its nature, which is a set of prior causes. If the self is not stable, then the choice is not attributable to a unified agent. In either case, the notion of self-causation fails to provide a coherent account of freedom.

The second trap is the reduction of free will to randomness. If the free act is not caused by anything—neither by prior events nor by the agent itself—then it is a brute, uncaused event. This is the position of radical indeterminism. But a random event is not a free act. If my choice to write this essay or to drink a cup of coffee is the result of a quantum fluctuation in my brain, then it is not *my* choice in any meaningful sense. It is something that happens *to* me, not something I *do*. Randomness undermines moral responsibility as thoroughly as determinism does. If my action is random, I cannot be held accountable for it, because I had no control over its occurrence. Tugushev, in rejecting the totality, must avoid both of these traps. He must show that the free act is neither self-caused nor random, but is somehow a genuine product of the agent.

To do this, Tugushev might appeal to the concept of *agent causation*. This is the idea that the agent, as a substance, is a cause of its own actions, but not in the sense of *causa sui*. The agent does not cause itself; rather, the agent, as a persistent entity, causes events. This is an Aristotelian model: the agent is a substance with a nature, and its actions are expressions of that nature. But this model immediately raises the question of the agent’s nature. If the agent’s nature is determined, then its actions are determined. If the agent’s nature is not determined, then it is random. To escape this dilemma, agent causation theorists often posit that the agent has a special kind of power—a power of “teleological” or “final” causation that is not reducible to efficient causation. But this reintroduces a form of dualism, separating the agent from the physical causal order. It creates a “ghost in the machine” that interacts with the physical world without being subject to its laws, which is precisely the kind of metaphysical monstrosity that Tolstoy found absurd.

### IV. The Fundamental Insufficiency of the Negative Critique

The core of my evaluation is that Tugushev’s rejection of the totality, while a necessary first step, is fundamentally insufficient to resolve the problem of free will. It successfully circumvents the *global* deterministic conclusion, but it does so by creating a *local* problem that is equally intractable. Let us examine this more closely.

If Tugushev rejects the totality, he must still account for the causal history of a human action. A human being is a physical organism embedded in a physical environment. My brain is a complex system of neurons, governed by the laws of physics and chemistry. My beliefs, desires, and intentions are (at least partially) realized in physical states of this brain. When I make a choice, there is a physical process occurring in my brain that leads to a physical action. If Tugushev denies that this process is determined by the totality, he must explain how it is determined. Is it determined by the local state of my brain? If so, then we have a form of local determinism, which is just as threatening to free will as global determinism. My choice would be the necessary consequence of my neural state at time *t*, which is itself the consequence of my prior neural states, and so on back to my birth. The fact that this chain is not connected to the state of the entire universe does not make it any less deterministic.

If Tugushev denies that the choice is determined by the local state of my brain, then he must posit a break in the causal chain. This break is either a miracle (an intervention by a non-physical soul) or a brute fact (a random event). Both options are problematic. A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature, which is scientifically and philosophically untenable. A random event is not a free act, as we have seen. Tugushev might try to argue that the break is not a violation of physical law because the physical laws themselves are indeterministic. He might appeal to quantum mechanics, which posits that events at the subatomic level are genuinely probabilistic. But this does not help. The indeterminacy of quantum mechanics is a source of randomness, not of freedom. The decay of a radioactive atom is not a free choice. If my decision is correlated with a quantum event, then it is a random event, not a free one.

The deeper problem is that Tugushev’s critique is purely negative. He has identified the faulty presupposition of the totality, but he has not replaced it with a positive ontology of agency. He has opened a door, but he has not furnished the room. To successfully circumvent determinism without reducing free will to nothingness, Tugushev would need to develop a robust theory of *emergent* causation. This would be a theory that allows for higher-level properties (like consciousness and intentionality) to have genuine causal powers that are not reducible to lower-level physical properties. This is a form of non-reductive physicalism. In this view, the mind is not a separate substance, but it is also not merely an epiphenomenon. It is a complex, emergent system that exercises top-down causal influence on its constituent parts. The whole is more than the sum of its parts, and this “more” is causally efficacious.

But can such a theory avoid the trap of *causa sui*? Let us imagine a specific choice. I am deciding whether to eat a piece of cake. My deliberation involves a complex interplay of desires (for pleasure), beliefs (about health), and values (about self-discipline). These mental states are realized in my brain. According to emergentism, the pattern of these mental states, as a whole, has a causal influence on the outcome of the deliberation. The outcome is not determined by the individual neurons, but by the higher-order pattern they form. This is a compelling idea, but it faces a crucial objection. If the higher-order pattern is itself a physical state, then it is subject to physical laws. If it is subject to physical laws, then it is determined by the prior physical state. If it is not subject to physical laws, then it is a non-physical entity, which returns us to dualism. The emergentist must explain how the higher-order pattern can have causal powers that are not determined by the lower-order physical state, without violating the principle of the causal closure of the physical domain.

This is precisely where Tugushev’s critique fails. He does not provide such an explanation. He simply asserts that the rejection of the totality opens up a space for freedom, but he does not explain what fills that space. The result is that the space is filled by either a *causa sui* (which is incoherent) or a random event (which is meaningless). In both cases, free will is not preserved; it is destroyed. The deterministic conclusion is circumvented, but only at the cost of replacing it with a chaotic or mystical conclusion that is no more palatable.
