# Temporal Flow at the Edge of Existence: Diminishing, Backwards, and Growing Blocks

The Growing Block theory is one of the most intuitively attractive alternatives to presentism and eternalism. According to the Growing Block, the past and present exist, whereas the future does not yet exist. Reality is therefore a temporally extended block that increases as new moments come into being. The present is not merely a psychologically privileged location within an already complete spacetime; it is the objective boundary at which existence expands. This picture appears to vindicate the common human sense that time genuinely passes: the past is fixed and existent, the present is actual, and the future is open.

Yet the phenomenology of temporal passage may not uniquely support the Growing Block. Alternative models—especially the Diminishing Block and the Backwards Block—can assign an objective edge to temporal reality while reversing, relocating, or reinterpreting the relation between that edge and the psychological structures through which temporal flow is experienced. If so, a person situated in one of these alternative temporal orders might have experiences that are introspectively indistinguishable from those of a person in a Growing Block universe. The apparent direction of memory, expectation, change, and causal dependence might all be reproduced by a different metaphysical arrangement.

The central issue is therefore not simply whether a theory contains an objective temporal boundary. It is whether the boundary occupies the same metaphysical and psychological role as the present does in ordinary experience. The relevant questions concern the relationship among three elements: the hierarchy of a subject’s memories, the psychological scaling of temporal importance, and the objective edge of temporal existence. A theory might explain the experience of flow by making memories of the past increasingly remote and less salient, while locating the actual boundary of reality somewhere other than where common sense places it. Conversely, a theory might make the objective boundary central but reverse the direction in which events are generated or eliminated. The resulting experiences could then be phenomenologically indistinguishable even though the underlying temporal ontologies differ radically.

This essay evaluates that possibility. It first distinguishes the Growing Block, Diminishing Block, and Backwards Block models. It then examines how memory hierarchy and psychological importance might generate an experience of temporal flow independently of the metaphysical direction of becoming. It argues that phenomenological indistinguishability is possible in a strong sense, but only if “indistinguishable” is understood as indistinguishability from the first-person perspective rather than indistinguishability under all possible empirical and causal investigation. The essay next assesses Michael Dummett’s critique of backward causation. Dummett’s arguments expose serious difficulties concerning evidence, agency, and the possibility of learning about future causes, but they do not demonstrate that a Backwards Block is logically incoherent. Finally, the essay considers whether the possibility of such indistinguishable alternatives undermines the evidential force of common-sense experience in favor of presentism or the Growing Block. The conclusion is that ordinary experience retains some evidential significance, but not the decisive metaphysical authority often assigned to it.

## I. Three models of temporal becoming

The Growing Block theory begins from a distinction between existence and actuality. The past and present exist, but the future does not. As time passes, the block of existence grows. At one moment, reality includes all events up to that moment; at a later moment, it includes those events plus newly existing events. The present is the edge of the block, and becoming is the process by which the edge advances.

This theory captures several aspects of ordinary temporal thought. The past appears settled because it already exists and cannot be changed. The future appears open because it does not yet exist. Present events occupy a distinctive ontological status: they are the newest additions to reality. The theory also offers a metaphysical basis for temporal asymmetry. The direction from future to past is not merely a direction in which observers happen to remember and deliberate; it is the direction in which existence accumulates.

The principal difficulty for the Growing Block is the “epistemic problem of the present.” If the past and present both exist, then an observer must somehow distinguish the objectively present edge from earlier portions of the block. A person’s experience may seem present-centered even if the person is located in an already-existing region of the block. The theory therefore requires an account of how the present is identified, both metaphysically and phenomenologically. If merely being at the edge is not sufficient to explain the experience of presentness, the theory may need to appeal to special relations between consciousness, memory, and the newest part of reality.

The Diminishing Block, sometimes called the Shrinking Block, reverses the ontological picture. On a standard formulation, the future and present exist, while the past ceases to exist as time passes. The block therefore diminishes from one end. What is currently real will later be removed from reality, leaving behind a smaller block. Depending on the exact version, the future may be a determinate region that already exists, or it may be an ontologically privileged domain extending ahead of a boundary that moves toward it. The essential point is that temporal becoming is modeled as elimination rather than addition.

At first sight, the Diminishing Block seems psychologically implausible. Human beings normally experience the past as especially secure and the future as uncertain. If the past is literally being removed from existence, it is unclear why our memories should be directed toward it or why it should seem more fixed than the future. But this objection assumes that the psychological direction of memory must track the metaphysical direction of becoming. That assumption is precisely what alternative theories challenge. A Diminishing Block could contain subjects whose cognitive architecture is organized so that they retain records of events that are about to disappear, while lacking records of events that remain ahead in the block. The experience of a fixed past and an open future could then be generated by psychological organization rather than by the direction in which existence itself changes.

The Backwards Block is more difficult to characterize because the term can refer to related but distinct proposals. In one version, the block grows in the direction conventionally called past-to-future, but the subject’s temporal perspective is reversed: what we call memories are records of events that, in objective time, lie “ahead” of the remembering state. In another version, the block genuinely grows or diminishes toward what we ordinarily call the past, so that the objective direction of becoming is opposite to the direction suggested by human experience. A third version treats the world as a block with a privileged boundary that is temporally oriented backward: events at the boundary are added or removed in a direction opposite to the ordinary arrow of time.

What unifies these variants is the idea that temporal existence may have an objective edge whose direction does not coincide with the psychological arrow of time. The world may be metaphysically “backwards” while inhabitants experience memory, deliberation, causation, and passage in the familiar direction. The Backwards Block is therefore not necessarily a theory in which people experience time as running backward. Rather, it is a theory in which the objective structure responsible for becoming runs opposite to the direction in which people experience it.

This distinction is crucial. If a theory simply made subjects experience events in reverse order, it would be empirically easy to reject. We would remember what, from our perspective, has not happened; we would deliberate about what we regarded as fixed; and causal explanations would be reversed in ordinary language. But the more sophisticated Backwards Block does not reverse surface phenomenology. It reverses the underlying relation between phenomenology and ontology. It asks whether the familiar experience of flow could be produced by a temporal structure whose objective edge is metaphysically oriented in the opposite direction.

## II. Memory hierarchy and the production of temporal flow

The most important resource available to alternative theories is the distinction between memory as a psychological phenomenon and the existence of the events remembered. A memory is not identical with the past event itself. It is a present mental state, or perhaps a causal trace, representation, disposition, or informational structure. In ordinary life, memory appears to point backward because the events represented by memories precede the acts of remembering. But that relation may depend on the organization of records rather than on a primitive metaphysical direction of time.

A subject’s memory system is hierarchical. At the most immediate level are short-term memories and working-memory contents: what has just been perceived, what one has recently said, and what one is currently doing. At a more extended level are episodic memories of yesterday, childhood, or significant historical events. At still deeper levels are semantic memories, habits, narratives, and socially inherited representations of events no longer personally accessible. This hierarchy gives experience a graded temporal structure. Recent events are often vivid, detailed, and emotionally available; distant events are more schematic, less determinate, and more dependent on reconstruction. The past appears as a field that recedes in psychological accessibility.

The Growing Block can explain this hierarchy naturally. New events are added at the present edge. As they become earlier, they enter memory systems of increasing temporal distance. Their traces may weaken, become abstract, or be overwritten. The objective growth of the block and the subjective recession of memory therefore run in the same broad direction. The present is where new reality arrives; the past is the region in which reality is already established and represented by diminishing traces.

But the correlation is not logically necessary. A Diminishing Block could preserve the same hierarchy by arranging that traces of events remain in subjects precisely as those events become ontologically less secure or disappear from the block. Indeed, the experience of recollection might be especially intense because memory is a record of what has been lost. The subject would not experience the past as absent; it would experience it as represented. The fact that an event no longer exists would not imply that the memory of it no longer exists. A photograph, neural trace, or narrative can survive the disappearance of what it depicts, at least for a time.

This possibility raises an immediate objection. If an event disappears from reality, how can a memory of it remain causally connected to that event? In the ordinary causal picture, the event produces the memory. If the event ceases to exist, perhaps its causal influence also ceases. Yet causal dependence need not require the continued existence of the cause. A past event can produce a present effect even though the event is no longer present. The difficulty is not causal influence but the metaphysical status of past entities. If “no longer exists” means that the event has been annihilated in an absolute sense, one may wonder how it can ground truth, explanation, or causal history. Still, the Diminishing Block can respond that the event existed when it produced the trace, and the trace is now a persisting consequence. The model is unusual, but not immediately self-contradictory.

The Backwards Block can exploit a similar distinction. Suppose what ordinary subjects call memories are, in the objective order, records of events located on the other side of the moving edge. To the subject, a memory has the functional role of representing a prior event: it informs present belief, supports explanation, and constrains action. But at the metaphysical level, the represented event may be “later” relative to the direction in which the block grows. The memory relation would then be psychologically backward-looking but objectively forward-directed.

A critic might say that this merely changes the labels. If the event represented by a memory is objectively later, then it is not a memory but a prediction or anticipation. The Backwards Block seems to confuse these categories. Yet the response is that the categories “memory” and “prediction” are functional and phenomenological categories. A memory is a mental state with a particular role in cognition: it presents content as settled, familiar, and already incorporated into the subject’s history. A prediction presents content as uncertain, hypothetical, and relevant to future action. If the Backwards Block supplies states with the functional and phenomenological profile of memory, then those states are memories in the psychological sense, even if their objective temporal relation to represented events differs from the ordinary one.

This is not merely a terminological point. Human beings do not directly inspect the metaphysical direction of causation. They encounter representations, dispositions, records, and experiences. If all these operate in the familiar way, the subject has no internal standpoint from which to compare psychological “before” and objective “before.” The temporal direction encoded in memory is partly constituted by the relations among mental states, not independently delivered by a metaphysical clock.

Psychological scaling of importance reinforces this conclusion. Human beings do not treat all times as equally significant. The present is typically experienced as urgent, vivid, and action-guiding. The recent past is important because it explains current circumstances. The distant past becomes increasingly schematic, while the future is organized into possibilities with varying degrees of probability and emotional force. This distribution of attention and value can be described as a psychological metric over time. Events nearer to the subject’s current state receive greater cognitive and practical weight.

Nothing in this psychological metric requires that the objective edge of existence be an advancing present. A Diminishing Block could make the current boundary psychologically central because subjects are located there, even if what is being removed from existence lies behind them. A Backwards Block could make its boundary central because all cognitive processing occurs at the boundary, while memories and expectations are arranged in the opposite metaphysical direction. The subject’s sense that “now” is the decisive point would then reflect the architecture of agency and attention, not necessarily the direction in which reality grows.

The notion of an objective edge remains important. A purely static block may explain temporal order and change but not objective becoming. Diminishing and Backwards models retain becoming by positing a privileged limit of existence. The question is whether a subject can tell from experience which side of the edge is ontologically privileged. If the answer is no, then the presence of an edge alone does not determine the phenomenology of passage. What matters is how the edge is connected to memory, causation, agency, and informational accessibility.

One can illustrate this with a simple analogy. Imagine a moving spotlight illuminating a fixed landscape. If the illuminated region expands, observers might experience the world as becoming more complete. If the illuminated region contracts, observers might nevertheless experience a coherent temporal order if their records and expectations are arranged appropriately. The subjective experience depends not only on what the spotlight does but also on the information available to observers within it. An observer cannot infer the direction of illumination merely from the fact that one region is lit and another is not.

The analogy is imperfect because temporal existence is not merely illumination. Nevertheless, it reveals the epistemic underdetermination at issue. The psychological order of memory, expectation, and significance may be compatible with more than one metaphysical direction of becoming.

## III. Can the alternatives be subjectively indistinguishable?

The claim of indistinguishability should be formulated carefully. There are at least three kinds of indistinguishability.

First, there is phenomenological indistinguishability: two theories generate the same experiences for subjects. In both worlds, people feel that time flows from an open future through a vivid present into a fixed past. Their memories have the same content and authority; their expectations have the same uncertainty; and their perception of change is identical.

Second, there is behavioral indistinguishability: subjects act, speak, remember, deliberate, and respond in the same ways. A Backwards Block that reproduces ordinary behavior would be empirically difficult to distinguish from a Growing Block by ordinary observation.

Third, there is complete theoretical or scientific indistinguishability: no possible observation, experiment, or causal intervention could distinguish the models. This stronger claim is much harder to sustain. If the models imply different causal structures, different possibilities for intervention, or different relations between records and events, then a sufficiently comprehensive physics might discriminate among them, even if ordinary human experience could not.

The strongest defensible claim is therefore that Diminishing and Backwards Blocks can be phenomenologically and perhaps behaviorally indistinguishable from the Growing Block. This is enough to challenge an argument from common experience, but it does not establish that the theories are empirically equivalent in every respect.

There is also a conceptual limit to the proposal. If a theory reverses the objective direction of all causal and informational relations, it may be unable to reproduce the ordinary asymmetry between memory and expectation. Memories are not merely representations of events; they are normally produced by those events. Expectations, by contrast, are produced by present states and directed toward possible later events. If the Backwards Block reverses causal order wholesale, then ordinary memory may be impossible. It would require later events to produce earlier traces, which is precisely the controversial backward causation that the model must defend.

The Backwards Block can respond in two ways. The first is to deny that all causal relations must reverse. Perhaps the objective edge is backwards while local causal processes retain the familiar orientation. On this version, the block’s growth direction and the direction of ordinary causation come apart. The theory is metaphysically unusual but not committed to universal backward causation.

The second is to embrace a controlled form of backward causation while preserving internal consistency. Memories can be effects of events that are later in the objective direction, provided the total history is self-consistent. What appears to us as a memory is then a causal trace from an objectively later event. The model must explain why such traces have the same reliability, degradation, and functional role as ordinary memories. But it is not obvious that this explanatory burden is impossible to meet.

A further problem concerns open possibilities. The Growing Block naturally associates the future with nonexistence and therefore with openness. A Diminishing Block in which the future already exists seems to make the future determinate. If the subject nevertheless experiences alternatives as genuinely possible, the theory must explain whether those alternatives are metaphysical possibilities, epistemic possibilities, or merely unrealized representations. A Backwards Block faces the same issue. If the objective future is already part of reality, ordinary deliberation may seem illusory.

However, the Growing Block itself does not automatically solve the problem of freedom or openness. The nonexistence of future events does not entail that more than one future is metaphysically possible; one might combine a Growing Block with determinism. Conversely, an eternalist or Diminishing Block theorist may distinguish the existence of future events from their being knowable or fixed in the sense relevant to deliberation. The phenomenology of openness may depend on limited information and practical uncertainty rather than on the nonexistence of future events.

Thus, alternative models can reproduce the experience of flow if they reproduce a suitable network of functional relations: current perception must differ from stored representation; representations must vary in vividness and authority; deliberation must be directed toward states not currently available for action; and events must be ordered so that explanation and practical reasoning work as they ordinarily do. Whether this network is grounded in a Growing, Diminishing, or Backwards Block is a further metaphysical question.

## IV. Dummett and the challenge of backward causation

Michael Dummett’s discussion of backward causation is relevant because the Backwards Block appears to require causal relations that run opposite to the ordinary temporal direction. Dummett’s critique begins from an epistemic and practical concern. If an event in the future can cause an event in the present or past, then present evidence may be generated by what has not yet occurred relative to us. This threatens to destabilize the ordinary relationship between evidence, action, and causation.

Suppose a person receives a warning that a future event will occur. If the future event is capable of causing the warning, then the person may attempt to prevent it. If the attempt succeeds, the event would not occur and therefore could not have caused the warning. The causal chain appears to undermine itself. The problem becomes especially sharp when the proposed cause is an event that agents can deliberately avoid. If the effect provides information about the cause, then learning about the effect creates an opportunity to interfere with the cause.

Dummett’s argument is powerful because it draws attention to a feature often overlooked by abstract discussions of temporal reversal: causation is embedded in practices of investigation and intervention. Causes do not merely stand in a relation to effects; they explain why evidence exists and guide action. A theory of backward causation must therefore explain why knowledge of a future cause does not normally enable us to prevent it, or why attempts to prevent it are systematically unsuccessful.

The Backwards Block may answer through a consistency condition. If the future event causes a present memory or warning, then the subject’s attempt to prevent it must be one of the conditions that ensures the event occurs. The warning could lead the agent to take precisely the action that brings about the warned-of event. Alternatively, the causal structure may allow only self-consistent histories: events that would generate contradictions simply do not occur. On this model, the fact that an agent tries to prevent a future cause does not show that the cause can be prevented. The attempt is already part of the complete causal pattern.

This response avoids formal contradiction, but it comes at a philosophical cost. It may make the agent’s deliberation seem epiphenomenal or predetermined. If every attempt to prevent the future cause must fail, then the agent is not genuinely open to alternatives. Yet that objection concerns freedom and agency more directly than coherence. A deterministic Growing Block can also imply that deliberation could not have produced a different outcome, even though deliberation remains causally efficacious within the actual history. The mere fact that an action is guaranteed does not show that it is causally irrelevant.

A different response is to distinguish causal dependence from informational dependence. Perhaps a future event can be the cause of a present state without making that state reliable evidence available to agents. Backward causation might occur only in hidden or inaccessible processes. If so, Dummett’s intervention argument would not refute the possibility of backward causation; it would show only that certain forms of observable backward causation are unstable.

Another response is to deny that the Backwards Block requires backward causation in the strong sense. The model may say that the objective edge of existence is reversed while local laws and causal processes retain their ordinary direction. If the model is formulated this way, Dummett’s critique has limited scope. The metaphysical direction of becoming would be backward, but memories could still be produced by events that precede them in the local causal order. The resulting theory would be strange, but it would not posit that future events cause present memories.

Still, the model must then clarify the relation between local causation and global becoming. If causes precede effects in the ordinary direction, but reality becomes existent in the opposite direction, what explains the fact that a newly existing event can have a cause that is not yet part of existence? The theory may need a distinction between the direction in which events become real and the direction in which dependence relations hold. Such a distinction is conceptually possible, but it weakens the intuitive motivation for calling the theory a Backwards Block.

Dummett’s critique also raises a semantic issue. The meaning of “cause” may itself be tied to temporal order. Some theories define causation in terms of counterfactual dependence, intervention, or law-governed production rather than temporal priority. On such accounts, backward causation is not contradictory by definition. But if causal concepts presuppose that the cause is earlier than the effect, then “backward causation” becomes a misuse of the word rather than a genuine metaphysical possibility. The dispute therefore partly concerns the analysis of causation.

Dummett does not, however, establish that backward causation is conceptually incoherent merely by showing that it produces epistemic difficulties. A logically inconsistent theory entails contradictions; a causally strange theory may instead generate unusual constraints on knowledge and action. The fact that a person can learn about a future event and try to prevent it does not by itself show that the event both must and need not occur. A consistent theory can say that the attempt fails, that the warning is unreliable, that the event occurs in a different form, or that the entire history is constrained by a self-consistency principle.

The strongest lesson from Dummett is therefore methodological. A Backwards Block cannot be assessed solely by asking whether its temporal diagrams are mathematically coherent. It must show how memory, evidence, deliberation, and intervention function in a world where objective temporal direction is reversed or where some causal influences run backward. Dummett’s critique exposes a substantial explanatory burden, but it does not conclusively discharge the theory from the realm of coherent metaphysical possibilities.
