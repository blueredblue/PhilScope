### The Absurdity of Self-Knowledge: Moore’s Paradox, Conversational Implicature, and the Norms of Assertion

G. E. Moore’s famous paradox—the striking absurdity of uttering sentences like “It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining,” or its commissive variant, “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is”—has puzzled philosophers for over a century. The utterance is not a logical contradiction in the strict sense; the proposition “p” and the proposition “I do not believe that p” can both be true. Yet the sentence strikes us as deeply incoherent, even pathological, when uttered sincerely by a competent speaker. A prominent attempt to dissolve this paradox combines G. E. Moore’s own insight that asserting “p” *implies* (in a non-logical sense) that the speaker believes “p,” with Paul Grice’s framework of conversational implicature, specifically the Cooperative Principle and its maxim of quality. The claim is that the absurdity arises from a clash between what is explicitly said and what is conversationally implicated, thereby rendering the utterance pragmatically defective rather than logically or epistemically incoherent.

In this essay, I will evaluate this combined Gricean-Moorean resolution. I will argue that while this account offers a powerful *pragmatic* description of why such utterances are odd, it ultimately fails to provide a satisfactory *explanation* of their distinctive absurdity. The tension between this implicative account and Ludwig Wittgenstein’s use-relation theory—which treats first-person belief utterances like “I believe p” not as reports of a mental state but as *hesitant assertions* of p—reveals a deeper problem. The Gricean account presupposes the very norm it seeks to explain: that assertion is constitutively linked to belief. Wittgenstein’s insight shows that the utterance is not merely a clash of implicatures but a *performative contradiction*—a defective move in the language-game that attempts to occupy two incompatible stances simultaneously. Consequently, I will defend the position that these utterances violate *theoretical rationality*—the norm of epistemic consistency—rather than merely practical rationality, although the failure of the speech act itself has derivative practical consequences. The Gricean resolution, therefore, is insufficient; it must be supplemented by a normative account of assertion that grounds the maxim of quality in the very nature of belief and assertion.

#### I. The Paradox and Its Structure

To appreciate the challenge, we must first delineate the exact nature of Moore’s paradox. The standard formulation is the *omissive* form: “p, but I do not believe that p.” The *commissive* form is: “p, but I believe that not-p.” The target utterance in this essay, “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is,” is a variant of the commissive form, where the first conjunct explicitly asserts a belief in not-p, and the second conjunct asserts the truth of p. Both forms share a common feature: they are not logically contradictory. The sentence “p and I do not believe p” can be true if the speaker is mistaken about their own beliefs or is lying. However, the utterance is *absurd* in a way that a mere falsehood or a lie is not. A lie, such as “It is raining” when it is not, is perfectly coherent; it is simply false. The Moorean sentence, however, seems to *self-destruct*—it is not just false, but *incoherent*.

Crucially, the paradox is asymmetric. The third-person analogue, “He believes it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is,” is perfectly fine. It is a mundane report of another’s mistaken belief. The first-person present-tense form is uniquely problematic. This asymmetry points to a special feature of self-knowledge and self-assertion. When I say “I believe p,” I am not merely describing a psychological state of a person; I am performing a specific kind of speech act that has a unique relationship to the content of p. The paradox thus challenges our understanding of the relationship between belief, assertion, and self-knowledge.

#### II. The Gricean-Moorean Implicative Account

The combined resolution under evaluation proceeds in two steps. First, it draws on G. E. Moore’s own observation that asserting “p” *implies* that the speaker believes “p.” This is not a logical implication, for “p” does not entail “I believe p.” Rather, it is a *pragmatic* implication—a relation that holds in virtue of the norms governing assertion. Second, it employs Paul Grice’s theory of conversational implicature to give this pragmatic implication a precise mechanism. Grice’s Cooperative Principle states that participants in a conversation are expected to make their contributions “such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange.” This principle is elaborated by four maxims: Quantity (be informative), Quality (be truthful), Relation (be relevant), and Manner (be clear). The maxim of Quality, specifically, has two sub-maxims: “Do not say what you believe to be false” and “Do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence.”

On this account, when a speaker asserts “p,” they are expected to have adequate evidence for p and to believe p. If a speaker asserts “p” while explicitly denying that they believe p, they are violating the maxim of Quality. In the commissive case, “I believe not-p, but p,” the speaker asserts two things. The first conjunct, “I believe not-p,” is a report of a mental state. The second conjunct, “p,” is an assertion about the world. The assertion of “p” *conversationally implicates* that the speaker believes p. This is because, under the Cooperative Principle, we assume that a speaker who asserts p is being truthful and therefore believes p. Thus, the utterance as a whole generates a *pragmatic contradiction*: the first conjunct explicitly asserts a belief in not-p, while the second conjunct implicates a belief in p. The speaker is, in effect, saying “I believe not-p, and I believe p,” which is a contradiction—not in the logical sense, but in the pragmatic sense of violating the maxim of Quality by implicating something that contradicts what is explicitly stated.

This account has considerable appeal. It explains why the utterance is odd without positing a logical contradiction. It also explains why the utterance can be *cancelled* in certain contexts. For instance, if I say, “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is, although I am suffering from a severe delusion,” the absurdity is reduced, because the added clause cancels the implicature that I am a rational, coherent believer. The Gricean account also explains the asymmetry with third-person reports: when I say “He believes not-p, but p,” I am not implicating that *I* believe p; I am merely reporting his belief. The implicature only arises for first-person assertive utterances.

#### III. Wittgenstein’s Use-Relation Theory: The Hesitant Assertion

The Gricean account treats “I believe p” as a proposition about a mental state, analogous to “He believes p.” It is a report of an internal psychological occurrence. This is precisely the assumption that Ludwig Wittgenstein challenges in his later philosophy, particularly in the *Philosophical Investigations* and *On Certainty*. Wittgenstein argues that first-person psychological utterances like “I believe p” do not function as descriptions of inner states. Instead, they are *expressions* or *avowals* that play a specific role in the language-game. He famously writes: “I say of someone else ‘He seems to believe...’ and of myself ‘I believe...’—the difference is not in what is said, but in the *circumstances* of the utterance.”

Wittgenstein’s key insight is that “I believe p” is not a hesitant report of a mental state but a *hesitant assertion* of p itself. When I say “I believe it is raining,” I am not primarily telling you about my mind; I am telling you that it is raining, but with a reduced degree of commitment. The utterance is a *weakened* version of the assertion “It is raining.” The difference between “I believe p” and “p” is not a difference in the object of assertion (one about the world, one about my mind), but a difference in the *modality* or *strength* of the assertion. In Wittgenstein’s terms, “I believe p” is a move in the language-game that occupies a position on a spectrum of epistemic commitment, ranging from “I am certain p” to “I suspect p.” It is not a report of a mental state that stands alongside the factual claim “p.”

This use-relation theory has profound implications for Moore’s paradox. If “I believe not-p” is not a report of a mental state but a hesitant assertion of not-p, then the utterance “I believe not-p, but p” is not a conjunction of a mental report and a factual claim. It is, rather, an assertion of not-p (with a certain modality) conjoined with an assertion of p (with a stronger modality). This is a *performative contradiction*—a direct contradiction in the speech act itself, akin to saying “p, but not-p” with different levels of confidence. The speaker is simultaneously committing to the truth of p and to the truth of not-p. The absurdity is not a pragmatic clash of implicatures; it is a *grammatical* or *constitutive* violation of the rules of the language-game of assertion. It is as if someone said “I promise to come, but I won’t come”—a performative contradiction that violates the constitutive rules of promising.

#### IV. The Tension and the Failure of the Gricean Resolution

The tension between the Gricean-Moorean account and Wittgenstein’s use-relation theory is thus fundamental. The Gricean account treats “I believe p” as a proposition about a mental state, a report that can be true or false independently of the truth of p. Wittgenstein rejects this. For him, “I believe p” is a *modified assertion* of p, not a report about a belief. This difference is not merely terminological; it determines the nature of the absurdity in Moore’s paradox.

The Gricean account, as we have seen, explains the absurdity as a violation of the maxim of Quality. The speaker implicates a belief in p (from asserting p) while explicitly asserting a belief in not-p. The problem with this explanation is that it *presupposes* the very norm it seeks to explain. Why does asserting p implicate a belief in p? Grice’s answer is that we assume speakers are truthful and have adequate evidence for what they say. But this is a *conversational* norm—a rule of polite, cooperative communication. It is not a *constitutive* rule of assertion. The Gricean account treats the link between assertion and belief as a *default assumption* that can be cancelled or overridden. However, the absurdity of Moore’s paradox is not merely that the speaker is being uncooperative; it is that the speaker is *incoherent*. The utterance is absurd even if we grant that the speaker is perfectly sincere and is trying to be cooperative. The speaker is not lying; they genuinely believe the first conjunct and believe the second conjunct is true. The problem is that the *content* of their utterance is internally inconsistent in a way that a mere violation of a conversational maxim is not.

Moreover, the Gricean account fails to explain why the utterance is *absurd* rather than merely *misleading*. A violation of the maxim of Quality, such as lying, is misleading but not absurd. If I say “It is raining” when it is not, I am being untruthful, but my utterance is perfectly coherent. The Moorean sentence, however, is not just a lie; it is a *self-defeating* utterance. It expresses a state of mind that is *epistemically impossible* for a rational agent. The Gricean account reduces this epistemic impossibility to a pragmatic infelicity, thereby losing sight of the deeper normative issue. The maxim of Quality itself is grounded in the idea that assertion is a speech act that *purports* to express a belief. But this is precisely what Wittgenstein’s use-relation theory makes explicit: assertion is *constitutively* tied to belief. The Gricean account is parasitic on this constitutive tie; it cannot explain it.

Furthermore, the Gricean account has trouble handling the *commissive* form. In the omissive form, “p, but I do not believe p,” the contradiction is between the assertion of p and the denial of a belief in p. The Gricean account says the assertion of p implicates a belief in p, which contradicts the explicit denial. But in the commissive form, “I believe not-p, but p,” the contradiction is between the explicit assertion of a belief in not-p and the implicature from the assertion of p. The Gricean account can handle this, but it requires treating the first conjunct as a report of a mental state. If we adopt Wittgenstein’s view, the first conjunct is a hesitant assertion of not-p. The utterance then becomes a *direct* contradiction between two assertions with different modalities. This is a much more powerful explanation of the absurdity. It shows that the utterance is not merely pragmatically odd but *logically* (in a broad sense) incoherent—it violates the principle of non-contradiction as applied to the speech act itself.

#### V. Theoretical vs. Practical Rationality

The final question to address is whether these problematic utterances violate *theoretical* or *practical* rationality. Theoretical rationality concerns the coherence of our beliefs, the proper response to evidence, and the avoidance of logical inconsistency. Practical rationality concerns the coherence of our actions, intentions, and means-end reasoning. I will defend the position that Moorean utterances violate *theoretical rationality* primarily, and *practical rationality* only derivatively.

Consider the sincere utterance of “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is.” The speaker, by uttering this sentence, expresses a belief in not-p (via the first conjunct) and a belief in p (via the second conjunct, if we accept the Gricean implicature, or via the assertive force of the second conjunct, if we accept Wittgenstein’s view). The speaker is thereby committed to two contradictory beliefs: p and not-p. This is a textbook violation of theoretical rationality—the norm of epistemic consistency. The speaker is holding a logically inconsistent set of beliefs. The absurdity of the utterance is a *symptom* of this underlying epistemic irrationality. The utterance is defective because it *manifests* a failure to satisfy the requirements of theoretical reason.

Is it also a violation of practical rationality? Assertion is a practical act—it is something we do. If there is a norm of assertion that says “assert only what you believe” (or “assert only what you know”), then the speaker is violating a norm governing a practical activity. However, this violation is *derivative* of the theoretical irrationality. The speaker is not failing to take the means to an end (instrumental irrationality), nor are they acting against their better judgment (akrasia). They are not making a practical error in reasoning. Rather, they are *expressing* a state of mind that is theoretically incoherent. The practical failure—the failure to perform a coherent speech act—is a consequence of the theoretical failure. The speaker cannot coherently assert because the *content* of their assertion is epistemically inconsistent.

One might object that assertion is a practical act, and therefore any violation of its norms is a practical failure. But this conflates the *normative source* of the failure with its *locus*.
