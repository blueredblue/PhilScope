# Emptiness Without Nihilism: Causation, the Self, and the Limits of Ontological Explanation in Madhyamaka

## Introduction

Few philosophical doctrines are as easily misunderstood as the Madhyamaka conception of emptiness. To say that all phenomena are empty may appear to imply that nothing exists, that distinctions are merely subjective constructions, or that causal explanation is ultimately arbitrary. Yet Nāgārjuna and Candrakīrti do not present emptiness as a thesis about the nonexistence of the world. Nor do they replace ordinary reality with a hidden metaphysical substance. Their claim is more precise and more radical: phenomena lack *svabhāva*, an inherent, independent, and self-sufficient essence. Things exist, but only dependently—through causes and conditions, parts and wholes, conceptual designation, linguistic practices, and relations of mutual dependence.

This claim creates a philosophical tension. If a phenomenon has no intrinsic nature, how can it possess stable causal powers? If the self is merely designated upon aggregates, who acts, suffers, remembers, and pursues liberation? If all things are empty, are the regularities of nature anything more than conventional projections? And if even emptiness is empty, does Madhyamaka successfully avoid contradiction, or does it dissolve the very basis on which its account of reality depends?

Nāgārjuna’s refutation of causation and Candrakīrti’s Sevenfold Reasoning provide two central routes into these questions. Nāgārjuna argues that production cannot be coherently established as arising from itself, from something other than itself, from both, or from neither. This is not a denial that events occur in causal sequences; rather, it is a denial that causation can be grounded in an inherently existing causal relation. Candrakīrti’s analysis of the chariot similarly shows that a thing cannot be identified with its parts, separated from them, possessed by them, located in them, or reducible to their arrangement. Yet the chariot remains conventionally real. The same reasoning applies to the self.

The philosophical strength of this approach is that it avoids two opposed extremes. It rejects material realism, understood as the view that physical objects possess intrinsic natures independent of relations and conceptual conditions. At the same time, it rejects nihilism, because dependent designation and causal efficacy remain valid within conventional experience. The unresolved question is whether this distinction between conventional and ultimate truth provides a coherent account of reality or merely relocates the problem. Does the emptiness of emptiness resolve the apparent paradox that conceptual designation is necessary for phenomena while the ultimate status of such concepts is denied? Or does the collapse of ultimate ontology leave us unable to explain why nature exhibits order rather than arbitrary regularity?

A defensible assessment is that Madhyamaka succeeds as a critique of metaphysical reification and as an account of the conditions of intelligibility, but it is less successful if required to provide a positive metaphysical foundation for natural laws. It does not show that explanatory arbitrariness is impossible in the strongest realist sense. Instead, it argues that the demand for such a foundation is itself based on a mistaken picture of what explanation must accomplish. Whether that response is satisfactory depends on whether one thinks causal intelligibility requires intrinsic essences or can be grounded in stable, relational, and practice-tested patterns.

## Nāgārjuna’s Refutation of Inherent Causation

Nāgārjuna’s analysis of causation is most famously expressed in his examination of the possible ways in which something might arise. An effect might arise from itself, from something other than itself, from both itself and something other, or from neither itself nor something other. Nāgārjuna argues that none of these four possibilities can establish production as an inherently existing relation.

Production from itself seems incoherent because, if an effect already exists in the very same form in its cause, then production adds nothing. A seed cannot produce a plant if the plant is already fully present as the seed. If the effect is identical with the cause, the relation between cause and effect collapses into identity. There is no genuine arising, since nothing new comes into being.

Production from something wholly other also creates difficulties. If the cause and effect are entirely unrelated, why should that cause produce this effect rather than any other? Fire could produce water, or a seed could produce a stone, if causal difference were absolute otherness. To explain a determinate relation, one must appeal to some connection between cause and effect. But once such a connection is introduced, the relation is no longer one of absolute otherness. Thus, causation from another cannot be understood as arising from an inherently independent other.

Production from both self and other merely combines the problems of the first two alternatives. Production from neither self nor other abandons intelligibility altogether. If an effect arises from no cause and no relation to a cause, then arising is arbitrary. Any event could follow any other, and there would be no reason to distinguish causal processes from random occurrence.

The purpose of this analysis is not to deny that seeds grow into plants, that fire heats, or that actions have consequences. Nāgārjuna explicitly relies on conventional causal practices in order to make his argument intelligible. The point is that none of these causal sequences can be established as possessing an intrinsic mode of existence. Causation operates dependently: a cause is a cause only in relation to an effect, and an effect is an effect only in relation to a cause. Neither term has its causal identity independently of the relation.

This argument exposes a general problem for metaphysical realism. A realist may claim that causal powers belong intrinsically to objects. Fire, for example, might be said to possess the inherent power to heat, while a seed inherently possesses the power to produce a particular plant. But what would “inherently” mean here? If it means that the power exists independently of conditions, then the account is empirically implausible. Fire heats differently depending on fuel, oxygen, distance, and surrounding conditions. A seed requires soil, water, temperature, and a biological environment. If it means merely that the object reliably participates in a network of conditions, then the language of intrinsic power has added little.

Nāgārjuna’s analysis therefore attacks not ordinary causal explanation but the demand that causation be grounded in an independent essence. Causes are not self-sufficient origins. They are nodes in a network of relations. A cause becomes a cause through conditions, and its causal efficacy changes as those conditions change. There is no ultimate starting point at which one finds a causally self-contained entity.

This is also why Madhyamaka connects emptiness with dependent origination. The absence of inherent existence does not make causal relations impossible; it makes them possible. If things possessed fixed essences, they could not interact, change, or enter into new relations. An inherently fixed object would be insulated from the very dependencies that make causal activity intelligible. A thing that could be affected by conditions would thereby show that it was not entirely independent of them.

The Madhyamaka claim is thus paradoxical only if existence and dependence are treated as opposites. From an essentialist perspective, to depend on something is to lack full reality. Madhyamaka reverses this assumption: dependence is precisely the mode through which phenomena exist. The problem is not that phenomena are dependent, but that we mistake dependent phenomena for independently existing entities.

## Candrakīrti’s Sevenfold Reasoning and the Analysis of the Chariot

Candrakīrti’s Sevenfold Reasoning, often presented through the example of the chariot, gives a more detailed analysis of how composite objects exist. The reasoning asks whether the chariot is identical with its parts, different from its parts, possesses its parts, is located in its parts, contains its parts, is the mere collection of its parts, or is the shape or arrangement of its parts.

The first possibility is that the chariot is identical with its parts. But no individual part is the chariot. A wheel is not a chariot; an axle is not a chariot; a seat is not a chariot. If the chariot were identical with the totality of its parts in a strict identity relation, then the terms “chariot” and “parts” would refer to exactly the same thing. Yet they do not function in the same way. We can remove a wheel and still speak of the chariot, even if it is damaged or no longer usable.

The second possibility is that the chariot is entirely different from its parts. This also fails, because the chariot cannot be found apart from them. A chariot without wheels, axle, body, and other components is not an independently existing chariot waiting somewhere beyond its material constituents. The chariot is not an additional entity that stands outside its parts.

The third possibility is that the chariot possesses its parts, as a person possesses property. But this would imply two independently existing entities: the possessor and the possessed. One would then have to explain how the chariot, already established independently, enters into possession of its parts. The same problem recurs if the chariot is said to be located in the parts or if the parts are located in the chariot. These spatial formulations presuppose that both the chariot and the parts already exist as separate entities.

The sixth possibility is that the chariot is simply the collection of its parts. But a collection is not automatically identical with the object designated upon it. A heap of components may be present without there being a functioning chariot. The collection becomes a chariot only through a particular organization, use, and conceptual designation. The final possibility, that the chariot is its shape or arrangement, also fails to provide an essence. Shape depends upon the parts arranged in that way, and the same parts may have different arrangements or functions. Arrangement does not exist independently of what is arranged.

The conclusion is not that no chariot exists. Conventionally, there are chariots. People build them, ride in them, repair them, and distinguish them from carts or piles of parts. What the analysis shows is that the chariot cannot be found as an intrinsically existing entity when one searches for its ultimate identity. It exists dependently upon parts, relations, functions, causes, and designation.

This reasoning is important because it does not merely say that objects are “made up” by language. The chariot is not arbitrary in the sense that any word could successfully designate any collection. Its designation is constrained by material structure, practical function, social conventions, and causal interaction. A random arrangement of sticks does not become a chariot simply because someone calls it one. Conceptual designation operates within a world of causal and functional constraints.

Nevertheless, the chariot is not discovered as a self-identical substance existing independently of these conditions. It is an object of conventional identification. Candrakīrti’s point is therefore neither naïve linguistic idealism nor material reductionism. The object is not reducible to a single material component, but neither is it something over and above the network of conditions in which it is identified.

The Sevenfold Reasoning also applies to the self. One might ask whether the self is identical with the body, feelings, perceptions, intentions, consciousness, or the totality of these aggregates. If the self were identical with the body, it would be difficult to explain the continuity of identity through bodily change. If it were identical with consciousness, which moment of consciousness would it be? Consciousness is a succession of changing episodes, none of which remains permanently present. If the self were entirely different from the aggregates, it would be impossible to identify or locate it. A self separate from all experience would be empty of any recognizable content.

Nor can the self be simply the collection of the aggregates. A collection is not an independent owner or subject standing behind them. The claim that the self possesses the aggregates similarly creates a relation between two entities whose independent existence has not been established. Candrakīrti’s conclusion is that the self exists as a designation upon the psychophysical processes that make up a living person. It is not identical with any one of them, nor completely separate from them.

This analysis has ethical implications. If there were no conventional self, no one could be praised, blamed, trained, harmed, or liberated. But if the self had an unchanging essence, moral and psychological transformation would be impossible. The conventional self is precisely the kind of dependent continuity required for responsibility. The person at one time is not numerically identical in every respect with the person at another time, but neither are the two wholly unrelated. Continuity without strict identity allows for memory, agency, and moral consequence without reifying a permanent soul.

## Beyond Material Realism

Madhyamaka’s rejection of inherent existence is often interpreted as a rejection of physical reality. That interpretation is too simple. The doctrine does not deny that bodies, stones, trees, and planets exist in the ordinary sense. It denies that they exist in a way that makes them independent of conditions, relations, and conceptual practices.

Material realism generally holds that physical objects are fundamentally real in themselves, whether or not anyone perceives or conceptualizes them. A Madhyamaka thinker need not deny that mountains existed before human beings named them. The challenge concerns what it means for the mountain to exist “in itself.” The mountain has a geological history, a chemical composition, a location, and causal effects. But each of these descriptions situates it within relations: tectonic processes, atmospheric conditions, perceptual capacities, scientific classifications, and practical contexts.

One might object that these relations do not create the mountain. They merely reveal or describe a mountain that already exists independently. Madhyamaka can grant that some aspects of the world are not produced by human thought while still denying that they possess an essence independent of all relations. A mountain can be independent of human naming without being independent of geological causes, physical conditions, spatial relations, and conceptual criteria for identifying it as a mountain. The relevant distinction is between independence from a particular observer and independence from all conditions whatsoever.

This distinction is crucial. The denial of inherent existence is not a denial of objectivity. It is a denial that objectivity requires self-subsisting substances. A phenomenon may be objectively constrained by causes and conditions while remaining empty of essence. Scientific inquiry itself often treats entities relationally. An organism is understood through ecological and physiological relations; a particle through interactions and measurable properties; a planet through gravitational and orbital relations. Such descriptions do not necessarily imply that objects are mere constructions.

Madhyamaka’s critique is especially powerful against the idea that physical properties must belong to things as isolated, context-free features. Consider color, temperature, solidity, or mass. These properties are not simply subjective, but their manifestation depends on interactions between objects, measuring conditions, perceptual systems, and theoretical frameworks. To say that a property is relational does not make it unreal. It means that its reality is conditional.

The Madhyamaka position can therefore be read as a form of ontological anti-foundationalism rather than as idealism. It denies that physical phenomena require a final substrate possessing intrinsic characteristics. It does not deny the existence of regular, mind-independent processes. Whether this is sufficient for physical realism depends on how much metaphysical independence realism demands. If realism requires a world of things whose identities and powers are fixed independently of every relation, Madhyamaka rejects it. If realism requires only that experience be constrained by stable processes not reducible to individual preference, Madhyamaka may accommodate it.

## Avoiding Nihilism

The opposite danger is nihilism: the claim that nothing exists, that moral distinctions are meaningless, or that causal regularities have no reality whatsoever. Nāgārjuna repeatedly rejects this conclusion. Emptiness is not equivalent to nonexistence. A thing is empty because it arises dependently, not because it is absent in every sense.

A simple analogy illustrates the distinction. A rainbow is not a solid object existing from its own side, yet it is not nothing. It appears under suitable conditions involving light, water, angle, and an observer. Its lack of independent substance explains its dependence, variability, and disappearance. To call it empty is not to deny its appearance; it is to understand the conditions under which it appears.

Likewise, a person is empty of a permanent self but not absent from the moral and practical world. People feel pain, form intentions, and respond to reasons. Actions have consequences because psychological and social processes continue through causal dependence. The absence of an immortal ego does not imply the absence of agency. Agency can belong conventionally to an organized and temporally extended process.

Madhyamaka’s two truths framework is designed to express this balance. Conventional truth concerns the world of ordinary and scientific practices: persons, objects, causes, effects, language, ethics, and institutions. Ultimate truth is the realization that none of these phenomena exists inherently. These are not two separate worlds. The ultimate truth of a phenomenon is its emptiness; the conventional truth is the phenomenon as dependently designated. A chariot’s emptiness is not an additional fact behind the chariot. It is the absence of an intrinsic chariot within the conventionally functioning chariot.

This formulation avoids nihilism because emptiness preserves dependent functioning. Indeed, Nāgārjuna’s famous identification of dependent origination with emptiness implies that without emptiness there could be no conventional world. If things were fixed by intrinsic essences, they could not arise, change, interact, or cease. Emptiness is not a destructive negation of reality but the condition for flexibility and causation.

The same point applies to ethics. If the self is empty, one might conclude that compassion has no rational basis. Yet Madhyamaka reverses the inference. Because persons are dependent and vulnerable, suffering is possible; because no self is isolated and self-sufficient, the boundary between self-interest and concern for others is less absolute than it appears. Compassion is not grounded in a metaphysical soul but in the causal reality of suffering and the interdependence of living beings.

## The Emptiness of Emptiness

The doctrine of the emptiness of emptiness is Madhyamaka’s safeguard against turning emptiness itself into a new absolute. If emptiness were an inherent property possessed by things, then Madhyamaka would merely replace substance metaphysics with a metaphysics of negation. Things would be intrinsically empty, and emptiness would function as a hidden essence. Nāgārjuna therefore insists that emptiness, too, is dependently designated.

This doctrine addresses a fundamental paradox. Madhyamaka says that things exist only through conceptual designation, yet it also denies that conceptual designations establish ultimate reality. Does this mean that all objects depend on concepts while concepts themselves are illegitimate? If the chariot exists only because it is designated, but the designation is denied as ultimately real, then what remains of the chariot?

The answer is that Madhyamaka distinguishes between denying ultimate validity and denying conventional operation. A concept need not possess an inherent essence in order to function. Words, rules, money, legal institutions, and scientific models are all dependent upon human practices, but their lack of intrinsic existence does not make them ineffective. A currency has no natural essence as money, yet it can purchase goods, structure economies, and generate obligations. Its efficacy depends upon a network of collective recognition and institutional practices.

Similarly, “chariot” is not an arbitrary label imposed upon a wholly undifferentiated world. The designation depends upon a basis—the parts, arrangement, function, and causal history of the object—as well as upon linguistic and practical conventions. Neither the basis alone nor the concept alone establishes the chariot independently. The chariot arises dependently through their relation.

The emptiness of emptiness prevents this relation from being reified. It says that the dependence between concept and basis does not itself have an ultimate, self-standing structure. There is no final metaphysical layer at which one can locate “designation,” “basis,” or “dependence” as independent entities. This is not a defect for Madhyamaka; it is the consistent application of its method. Any alleged foundation would either depend on something else or be inexplicably self-grounding.

Does this resolve the paradox? It resolves one version of it by showing that the paradox depends on an illicit demand. The demand is that either concepts must reveal essences or they must be arbitrary inventions. Madhyamaka rejects this binary. Concepts can be conventionally valid without mirroring intrinsic natures. They can be corrigible, context-sensitive, and constrained by experience. Their validity is practical and intersubjective rather than absolute.

However, this resolution may not satisfy those who seek a robust account of truth independent of conceptual schemes. The claim that conventions are constrained does not by itself explain what makes them correct rather than merely successful. A map may work for navigation while still misrepresenting the terrain in important ways. A scientific theory may predict observations without capturing the underlying structure of reality. If all structures are empty, what distinguishes truth from useful fiction?

The Madhyamaka response would be that the distinction between truth and falsity also functions conventionally. A belief is valid when it withstands conventional scrutiny, coheres with other reliable practices, and successfully engages causal conditions. This is not mere subjective preference. But it does not amount to a correspondence with an intrinsically structured reality independent of all conceptual and causal relations. Madhyamaka therefore offers a pragmatically constrained account of truth rather than a correspondence theory based on metaphysical essences.

The emptiness of emptiness is successful if its aim is therapeutic and dialectical: it prevents the doctrine from becoming an object of attachment and exposes the limits of conceptual reification. It is less decisive if its aim is to provide an ultimate explanation of why conceptual practices succeed. In that stronger sense, the doctrine may appear to suspend rather than solve the problem.

## Does the Collapse of Ultimate Ontology Make Nature Arbitrary?

The most serious challenge concerns natural regularities. If no physical entity possesses an inherent nature, and if causal relations have no ultimate foundation, why should nature display stable patterns? Why does fire generally burn rather than freeze, and why do certain chemical conditions produce repeatable reactions? Is Madhyamaka committed to the conclusion that these regularities are merely conventional?

There are two forms of this objection. The first is epistemological: if concepts do not disclose intrinsic structures, perhaps science cannot give genuine knowledge. The second is metaphysical: if there is no ultimate ontology, perhaps there is no reason why the world should exhibit any particular regularities. Everything might have been otherwise, and no explanation could distinguish order from coincidence.

Madhyamaka can answer the first objection more easily than the second. Scientific knowledge does not require that theoretical entities possess intrinsic essences. It requires reliable observation, reproducibility, predictive success, explanatory integration, and resistance to individual preference. These are conventional criteria, but “conventional” does not mean “arbitrary.” A convention may be constrained by the world and by the consequences of acting within it. Failed predictions and unsuccessful interventions are not simply matters of opinion.

The second objection is more difficult. A realist might say that fire burns because burning is grounded in the intrinsic nature of fire or in fundamental physical laws. Madhyamaka rejects this kind of grounding. But what does it offer instead? It can say that fire burns dependently upon its physical composition and environmental conditions, and that these relations constitute a stable causal pattern. Yet why are these relations stable? The answer seems to be: because the relevant conditions have those dependently arisen characteristics. If one asks again why they do, the Madhyamaka analysis continues indefinitely. There is no ultimate stopping point.

This regress is not necessarily a failure. Many philosophers reject the assumption that explanation must terminate in an unconditioned ground. Scientific explanations often proceed by locating events within broader systems rather than by discovering an essence. A law explains a phenomenon by relating it to other phenomena, and deeper theories may redescribe the law within a larger framework. The fact that explanation remains relational does not make it arbitrary. It may indicate that reality is structured through patterns rather than substances.

Still, relational structure itself needs interpretation. If relations are entirely free-floating, then they cannot explain anything. A causal network without any constraints would be indistinguishable from randomness. Madhyamaka therefore must maintain that dependent origination is not a license for arbitrary stipulation. Conventional reality includes constraints that are discovered rather than invented. The fact that these constraints lack ultimate essence does not erase them. It means only that their identity is inseparable from the conditions in which they operate.

The crucial issue is whether “no inherent essence” entails “no necessary structure.” Madhyamaka generally denies that it does. A relation can be stable without being self-grounding. For example, the rules of a language are conventional and historically contingent, but they are not arbitrary for speakers who participate in the language. A grammatical rule does not possess eternal existence, yet it constrains intelligible speech. Similarly, causal regularities may be contingent in a cosmic sense while still being objective and non-arbitrary within the world.

One might object that linguistic conventions differ from physical laws. Language depends explicitly on human agreement, whereas gravity does not. But the analogy need not claim that nature is invented by agreement. It concerns the form of dependence. A linguistic rule is real through a network of practices; a physical regularity is real through a network of material conditions and interactions. Both can be objective within their systems without possessing an isolated essence.

The Madhyamaka position thus resembles a form of structural realism, though it should not be identified uncritically with modern scientific theories. What matters fundamentally is not an independently existing object with intrinsic properties but a pattern of dependence. The pattern is not reducible to our concepts, because it resists unsuccessful conceptualizations. Yet it is not a metaphysical object existing outside all possible descriptions. Its reality consists in its reliable role within a causal and epistemic network.

The cost of this position is that it cannot provide a final answer to the question “Why this structure rather than another?” But perhaps no philosophical position can do so without appealing to an unexplained primitive. The realist invokes intrinsic nature or fundamental law; Madhyamaka argues that such primitives are conceptually unstable. The choice is not between complete explanation and no explanation, but between different forms of explanatory stopping point.

## A Critical Assessment

Madhyamaka’s refutation of causation is most persuasive as a critique of simplistic foundationalism. Causes and effects cannot be self-sufficient entities because causal relations involve temporal, spatial, modal, and functional dependencies. A cause is not causally sufficient in isolation; it operates within conditions. The refutation also shows that causal language becomes unintelligible if cause and effect are conceived as either strictly identical or absolutely separate.

Candrakīrti’s Sevenfold Reasoning complements this critique by revealing the instability of ordinary objects. The chariot, body, and self cannot be located as independent entities among their parts or beyond them. This analysis explains why composite things are vulnerable to change and why identity is a practical continuity rather than an immutable substance. It also offers a powerful critique of the assumption that conceptual distinctions must correspond to intrinsic divisions in reality.

The doctrine is particularly strong in its treatment of the self. Many theories of personal identity struggle between a permanent soul and a reduction of the person to disconnected psychological events. Madhyamaka offers a middle position: the person is neither an eternal substance nor a mere illusion. The self is a dependently designated continuity with real causal and ethical significance. This view accommodates transformation, responsibility, and liberation without positing an unchanging essence.

The doctrine of emptiness also successfully blocks a common slide from anti-essentialism to nihilism. A phenomenon can be empty and still function. Its emptiness explains its dependence, and dependence explains its capacity to arise, change, and affect other things. The two truths framework is not a compromise between realism and nihilism but an attempt to show why both extremes arise from demanding one mode of existence where two levels of analysis are needed.

Yet Madhyamaka faces genuine philosophical difficulties. Its distinction between conventional and ultimate truth can seem unstable. If ultimate analysis shows that no conventionally posited object can withstand examination, why should conventional knowledge retain authority? The answer that conventions work is plausible, but it risks making truth instrumental. Moreover, the very notion of “conventional validity” requires criteria of reliability that Madhyamaka does not always articulate in detail.

A second difficulty concerns the status of causal necessity. Nāgārjuna shows that causation cannot be inherently existent, but he does not by this argument establish a positive theory of why causal relations are regular. The negative argument undermines essentialist metaphysics, but it does not alone yield an account of natural law. Madhyamaka’s appeal to dependent origination explains how phenomena are related but may not explain why those relations have the particular stability they do.

A third difficulty concerns whether the Sevenfold Reasoning applies equally to all objects. The chariot is an artifact whose identity clearly depends upon human purposes and conventions. Natural entities such as electrons, galaxies, or biological species may not be designated in exactly the same manner. Of course, Madhyamaka can respond that all objects are dependently identified and causally situated, including natural entities. But the artifact example may make conceptual dependence appear stronger than it is in the natural sciences. A mountain can be called many things, but its tectonic processes do not seem to depend on designation.

The best Madhyamaka response is not to insist that human concepts produce natural phenomena. It is to distinguish dependence upon concepts from dependence upon conditions generally. Some phenomena depend upon minds and linguistic practices; others depend primarily upon physical processes; all depend upon multiple conditions. Conceptual designation is one mode of dependence, not the sole source of existence. The chariot illustrates the structure of designation, but it should not be interpreted as proving that every physical process is mentally constructed.

Finally, the emptiness of emptiness can appear self-undermining. If emptiness is empty, is the claim that all things are empty itself merely conventional? If so, why accept it? Madhyamaka’s answer is that the doctrine is like a raft: it is used to cross a river but is not carried after crossing. Emptiness is not a final proposition about reality but a method for loosening attachment to all propositions that claim intrinsic validity. The doctrine is reflexive; it applies to itself.

This reflexivity is philosophically sophisticated, but it also limits what Madhyamaka can claim. It cannot assert, without qualification, that reality is ultimately empty as though emptiness were a metaphysical fact. It can only say that when one searches for inherent existence, none can be found, and that dependent phenomena are therefore empty. The result is less a completed ontology than a disciplined refusal of ontological absolutism.

## Conclusion

Nāgārjuna’s refutation of causation and Candrakīrti’s Sevenfold Reasoning together present emptiness as a critique of the assumption that things must possess independent essences in order to exist or function. Causation cannot be grounded in production from self, other, both, or neither; the chariot cannot be found as identical with its parts, separate from them, possessing them, located in them, or reducible to their mere collection. The self likewise cannot be identified with the aggregates or placed beyond them. Yet causal processes, chariots, and persons remain conventionally real.

This middle way avoids material realism by denying that physical objects possess context-free, self-sufficient natures. It avoids nihilism by affirming the practical and causal efficacy of dependently arisen phenomena. Emptiness is not a hidden substance, and the emptiness of emptiness prevents it from becoming one. Conceptual designation is neither an arbitrary imposition nor a revelation of intrinsic essences. It is a dependent practice constrained by causes, functions, social conventions, and successful engagement with the world.

Does this fully resolve the paradox of dependent designation? It resolves the contradiction if the paradox assumes that a concept must either correspond to an essence or be arbitrary. Madhyamaka offers a third possibility: conventional validity without ultimate foundation. But it does not provide a final explanation of why conventions and causal regularities succeed. Nor does it explain, in the strongest realist sense, why nature exhibits the particular order it does.

That limitation may be deliberate. Madhyamaka holds that the search for an ultimate ontological ground mistakes explanation for possession. To explain a phenomenon is not necessarily to uncover a self-existing substance beneath it. Explanations can remain relational, conditional, revisable, and embedded in practices of inquiry. Nature need not be arbitrary merely because it lacks an intrinsic essence; its regularities can be objective patterns of dependence. Still, this answer asks us to relinquish the hope of an ultimate guarantee that separates order from the possibility of chaos.

Madhyamaka therefore succeeds most clearly as a philosophy of anti-reification and epistemic humility. It shows that the world’s intelligibility does not require a world of self-subsisting things, and that the absence of essence is compatible with causal order, moral responsibility, and knowledge. Whether it succeeds as a complete alternative to metaphysical realism depends on whether one regards the demand for ultimate grounding as indispensable. If one does, Madhyamaka may seem to end in explanatory underdetermination. If one does not, its emptiness doctrine offers a powerful account of how reality can be structured, knowable, and ethically significant without being founded upon anything that exists independently of all relations.
