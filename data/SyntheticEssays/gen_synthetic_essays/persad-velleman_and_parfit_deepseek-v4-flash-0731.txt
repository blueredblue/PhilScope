Here is a philosophical essay evaluating the frameworks of J. David Velleman and Derek Parfit regarding the morality of imprudent self-affecting acts.

---

The ephemeral pleasure of a cigarette, the sharp sting of tobacco smoke upon the throat, constitutes a singular moment of sensory gratification. Yet this fleeting instant, repeated thousands of times, casts a long, malevolent shadow over the decades to come, manifesting as emphysema, cardiovascular disease, or a painful malignancy. The question of whether such an act—volitional, self-directed, and ultimately self-harming—constitutes a *moral* wrong, rather than a mere lapse of prudence, sits at a fraught intersection of metaphysics, ethics, and political liberalism. When we condemn the smoker, do we speak as moralists, or merely as physicians of behavior? Two of the most influential contemporary philosophers, J. David Velleman and Derek Parfit, offer frameworks that purport to carry this act across the threshold of moral culpability. Velleman, working within a Kantian deontology, argues that self-destructive imprudence is a crime against the inherent dignity of rational personhood. Parfit, through his reductionist and relational account of personal identity, suggests that the present self’s risk-taking constitutes an aggression against a psychologically distinct future “other.” However, upon rigorous reconstruction and evaluation, neither framework successfully establishes the *moral* wrongness of smoking without collapsing into a pernicious paternalism that systematically undermines the rightful autonomy of the present self. The central tension remains: any moral condemnation of life-shortening practices tethered to these theoretical frameworks proves either metaphysically unsatisfying or entails overly restrictive, hubristic conclusions that render morality a tyrannical master of ordinary existence.

### Part I: Velleman’s Kantian Dignity and the Unfair Trade

To understand Velleman’s derivation of the immorality of smoking, one must first grasp his reinterpretation of Kant’s core moral insight. Kant famously argued that rational nature, or personhood, possesses an absolute and inherent worth—a *dignity*—which places it in a category distinct from things that have a *price*. A being with dignity may never be used merely as a means to an end, even an end it has set for itself. Velleman extends this principle to the self-relation, arguing that the respect owed to persons is not merely an intersubjective duty but a self-regarding one. Rational nature isn’t just *a* value among others; it is the *source* of all value. It is the capacity to set ends, to evaluate, and to confer worth upon the world. Consequently, to treat one’s own rational nature as a mere tool for acquiring base pleasures is to commit a profound category error—it is to trade what is priceless for what is priced.

The mechanism by which Velleman derives the wrongness of acts like smoking is rigorous. The smoker performs a self-affecting action that brings about a diminution of the capacities required for rational agency—specifically, the physical and cognitive health necessary to pursue, evaluate, and revise a life plan. In this trade, the agent receives a "price" (the hedonic thrill of nicotine, stress relief, or social communion) and pays with a portion of their "dignity" (reduced lifespan, diminished health, impaired cognition). Velleman’s critical point is that this transaction fundamentally misvalues the object of exchange. The dignity of the person is not a fungible good that can be aggregated against potential benefits. It is the standard against which all benefits are measured. By choosing immediate sensory pleasure over the long-term maintenance of her rational capacities, the smoker implicitly commits a performative contradiction—she uses her rational agency to authorize a decision that undermines the very source of that authorization’s value.

Applied specifically to smoking, Velleman’s account treats the act as a violation of the Categorical Imperative, specifically the Formula of Humanity. The smoker treats her rational nature as something to be sacrificed or degraded for a mundane utility. She does not respect her own humanity as an end in itself. In this view, the refusal to engage in self-harm is not a matter of mere self-concern; it is a moral injury to the dignity that she embodies. The degradation of one’s health is not incidental to the person but is constitutive of the person’s ability to be a rational agent. Damaging that biology is, therefore, an attack on the very ground of one’s own moral standing. The long-term pain and incapacity of the future self are not merely consequential harms; they are accelerations of the dissolution of personhood itself.

Yet, a crucial weakness emerges when we hold Velleman’s framework up to the sun of real-world moral deliberation. The Kantian apparatus, as interpreted by Velleman, depends on a robustly normative conception of what rational personhood *requires*. It assumes that the preservation of one’s biological integrity is *always* a precondition for—rather than a competing end within—rational agency. But how does one weigh the dignity of a 25-year-old who intentionally engages in risky mountaineering against the dignity of a smoker? Both risk long-term physical harm and even death. The mountaineer might argue that the activity itself is constitutive of a meaningful life, an expression of courage and self-transcendence that enriches her rational nature, rather than diminishes it. The smoker could make an analogous claim: smoking is part of her social ritual, her literary identity, her way of coping with the overwhelmingly suffocating burdens of modern existence. Velleman’s framework struggles to distinguish between choosing a risk that *enhances* the story of one’s rational life and choosing a risk that *demeans* it. The judgment concerning what constitutes a "price" for a benefit is intensely subjective and evaluative.

The result is that Velleman’s theory, when applied to smoking, appears to conflate "prudent rationality" with "moral rationality." It suggests that any act that is imprudent—in the sense of sacrificing long-term well-being for short-term satisfaction—is thereby a betrayal of one’s dignity. This leads directly to the "overly restrictive conclusions" objection. If smoking is immoral because it trades future capabilities for present pleasure, then we must also condemn eating sugary desserts, driving a car (which carries a non-negligible risk of fatal accident), or spending excessive hours at one’s desk, all of which trade a statistical degree of future health for present satisfaction or career advancement. Do we condemn marathon runners who risk permanent joint damage? Velleman would likely draw a line between risks intrinsic to a flourishing rational project and risks that are merely indulgent. But the line is, at best, blurred, and at worst, a version of aesthetic or perfectionist paternalism that the liberal Kantian tradition explicitly rejects. If a person says, "I value the ritual of smoking over five to ten years of my life," Velleman’s framework provides no neutral, non-question-begging way to prove this valuation is *wrong* without appealing to a substantive conception of the good life that the individual does not share. Thus, while Velleman attempts to ground a special duty to oneself, his mechanism for doing so inevitably usurps the very autonomy it purports to respect.

### Part II: Parfit’s Successive Selves and Harm to the Future “Other”

Derek Parfit offers a radically different metaphysical starting point. In *Reasons and Persons*, Parfit advances a reductionist account of personal identity. He argues that the fact of a person’s identity through time consists in nothing more than the holding of certain psychological connections—memories, intentions, beliefs, and desires—linked by causal chains. Crucially, these connections are not all-or-nothing but come in degrees. Over time, as a person’s psychology shifts, these connections weaken. The 70-year-old is connected to the 20-year-old by a chain of overlapping connections (the 21-year-old remembers the 20-year-old’s thoughts; the 22-year-old remembers the 21-year-old’s), but the 70-year-old is *not* directly connected to the 20-year-old in terms of strong memories or beliefs. Identity, for Parfit, is not what matters; what matters is *Relation R*—psychological connectedness and/or continuity. From this, Parfit draws his famous conclusion that the notion of a single "self" persisting over time is an illusion. A person is more accurately a "successive self," a series of overlapping mental states.

The mechanism by which Parfit equates risks to future selves with harms imposed on other people flows directly from this reductionism. If the present self is not strictly identical to the future self, then the moral weight of harming that future self cannot be based on a concept of *self-interest* or prudence. The present smoker, at age 20, is harming a future individual at age 70. Because the 20-year-old and the 70-year-old lack strong psychological connectedness, the relation between them is structurally similar to the relation between two distinct people. Consequently, Parfit argues, we can apply the impersonal principle of beneficence: we have a moral reason not to make the future person worse off, just as we have a moral reason not to make a contemporary stranger worse off. The moment I light a cigarette, I am committing a risk of harm against a future person—a person who will experience the pain of breathlessness, the fear of death, and the indignity of physical decline. If harming an innocent person is wrong, then harming my future "successor" self is equally wrong.

This framework has an intuitive, distributive appeal. It forces us to view time not as a unified stage for a single protagonist, but as a sequence of disparate individuals, each deserving of equal moral consideration. It breaks the egoistic discounting rate that allows us to say "I’ll quit later" because it shows that "later" is a different individual who has no say in the decision made by the "earlier" one. The wrongness of smoking, in Parfit’s view, is not that I am betraying my own future (an internal, self-regarding flaw) but that I am imposing a risk of grave harm on a third party. This makes the problem not one of mere prudence but one of justice—a duty of non-maleficence toward a vulnerable other who cannot consent.

However, a deep internal tension threatens to break Parfit’s mechanism apart. The theory’s strength—the severing of identity—simultaneously destroys the grounds for generating a moral duty *from* the present self *to* the future self. If my future self is essentially a different person, then why should I, the present self, have any special obligation to that future person’s welfare? The moral duty of non-maleficence typically applies to others *because they are others*, and we recognize that our individual freedom ends where another’s right to non-interference begins. But in the case of a future self, if that future self is morally distinct from me, I might legitimately ask: "What is that person to me?" I have no more obligation to preserve the future person’s health than I do to preserve the health of a random stranger in a distant country. If I am not identical to the smoker at 70, I can treat the risk of his lung cancer as a merely contingent externality, like a carbon emission, rather than a violation of my own autonomy.

Parfit explicitly argued against this, claiming that we ought to care about our future selves because of the strong psychological continuity, but he conceded that the *rationality* of this concern is not based on identity. However, this introduces a subtle shift—from a *moral* duty to a *rational* preference. Parfit argues that we should be rationally impartial between our own present experiences and our future experiences (and the experiences of others), which is why future pains matter. Yet this impartiality is a strong moral prescription. It demands that the present self adopt a God’s-eye view, treating its own imminent pleasures and the future distress of its successor with equal weight. But this is an extraordinarily demanding ethical stance, one that is indistinguishable from a form of utilitarianism applied across time. If we fully adopt Parfit’s framework, the present self becomes a mere custodian or steward for a succession of futuristic strangers. This does not just *restrict* the present self; it fundamentally *alienates* the present self from its own life trajectory. The present self is stripped of any authoritative say over its own future, because its future is not "its own."

Furthermore, the argument proves too much. Parfit’s mechanism, if taken to its logical conclusion, would render nearly every risky activity a violation of the rights of a future person. Driving, skiing, eating a high-fat diet, even staying up late working—all impose a statistical risk of harm on one’s future successors. If such actions are morally wrong, then the moral fabric of human life becomes an absurd gauntlet of risk-aversion. The present self is reduced to a mere instrument for maximizing the welfare of a series of disconnected future beings, a role that no rational agent could accept without resigning their own agency. Parfit’s reductionism successfully highlights the distributive injustice of temporally distant harms, but it fails to bridge the gap between "this harms a future person" and "the present person has a moral obligation to refrain." The bridge requires a normative principle, such as "we should maximize impersonal welfare," which is itself too broad and controversial to ground a specific moral prohibition on smoking, and which wholly undermines the prerogative of the present self to author its own life according to its own values.

### Part III: The Autonomy Objection and the Tyranny of Everyday Risk

The objection that both theories entail overly restrictive conclusions regarding everyday activities is not a mere pedantic footnote; it strikes at the heart of their moral plausibility. Consider the half-cup of coffee in the morning, which carries mild carcinogenic risks, or the casual hamburger, linked to coronary disease. If Velleman’s maxim is “do not trade your dignity for price,” then the coffee drinker is trading a modicum of future health (a price-paying reduction in dignity) for a fleeting pleasure. If we universalize Parfit’s principle, then the hamburger consumer is imposing a risk of harm on their future successors. To be consistent, both philosophers must either condemn virtually all creature comforts, or they must posit a threshold of harm below which morality does not intervene. Velleman might argue that the dignity of the person is only violated when the harm is *severe* or *systematic*, such as chronic addiction. Parfit might argue that the risk becomes immoral only when the probability of harm is high enough or the harm is grave enough. But these thresholds are ad hoc and unprincipled. Where exactly does the line between a moral and an amoral health risk lie? One cigarette? A pack a day? A 1% risk of cancer? A 10% risk? The inability to draw a non-arbitrary line exposes the flaw in deriving a *moral* verdict from these metaphysical frameworks.

This leads directly to the central tension articulated in the prompt: the conflict between condemning life-shortening practices and preserving individual autonomy. The smoker, regardless of the imprudence of her choice, is exercising her capacity for self-governance. She is weighing the goods of her life—pleasure, sociality, instant relief—against the good of longevity, and she has rendered a verdict. For a moral theorist to override that verdict, they must claim a superior epistemic and moral authority over the agent’s own life. Velleman’s account does this by positing an objective, metaphysical value (dignity) that supposedly trumps the agent’s subjective values. Yet, in doing so, it fails to recognize that the value of dignity, as experienced, is partially constituted by the agent’s own projects and commitments. A person might see quitting smoking as a denial of her dignity because it represents a surrender to social pressure and a morbid fear of death. Parfit’s account does this by essentially making the present self a slave to the future self.

The "rightful role of the present self" that both theories undermine is precisely the role of the *author*. We are not merely investors in a future asset, nor are we merely custodians of an abstract dignity. We are, first and foremost, agents who make temporal trade-offs. The present self has a legitimate authority to define the meaning of its own life, including the acceptance of existential risk. To choose to smoke, understanding the risks, is to make a tragic choice—a choice between the certainty of present gratification and the probability of future suffering. The moral wrongness of such a choice cannot be established merely by showing that the future self will suffer, anymore than a soldier’s choice to fight (knowing he may die or be maimed) is morally wrong because it harms his future body. The soldier’s allegiance to a larger cause, or even a personal desire for adventure, makes the risk *constitutive* of a meaningful life. The smoker’s choice may be less heroic, but it is still an exercise of the same fundamental capacity for self-direction.

The Kantian principle of Autonomy, ironically, cuts against Velleman’s conclusion. The Categorical Imperative ultimately requires us to respect the capacity for rational agency in ourselves and others. But respecting that capacity may, in some instances, mean *tolerating* its misguided exercises. The smoker’s choice, though perhaps irrational from a hedonic calculus, is a manifestation of her rational will. To declare it *morally wrong* is to treat her not as an end in herself but as a means to some abstract conception of "human flourishing" that she does not endorse. Similarly, for Parfit, the present self’s right to make decisions that affect its future is a foundational element of personal autonomy. If we deny this right, we deny the very possibility of a stable, responsible agent. We would be required to constantly second-guess every choice that might impact a future "other," making us perpetual hostages to their putative rights.

### Part IV: The Primacy of the Present Self and the Limits of Morality

Ultimately, the forceful conclusion is that neither Velleman’s Kantian dignity nor Parfit’s successive-selves model successfully establishes that imprudent self-affecting acts such as smoking are morally wrong. Their failure is instructive. They fail precisely because they attempt to reduce the complex, first-personal phenomenon of living a life to a third-personal, objectifiable set of harms and values. They flatten the temporal texture of human existence, either by elevating a single abstract value (dignity) to a tyrannical position over all others, or by breaking the self into a collection of strangers, thereby destroying the unity that grounds prudential concern in the first place.

Does this mean that smoking is morally permissible? It might mean that it is *prudentially* foolish, a violation of self-interest, but it does not follow that it is a *moral* wrong. The domain of morality principally governs our interactions with other agents in a way that respects their reciprocal autonomy. When my actions harm *myself*, the moral community has an interest insofar as it prevents me from becoming a burden, but the primary harm is to my own life project. The moral law, rightly understood, does not compel us to be perfect stewards of our future health; it compels us to be respectful, just, and non-exploitative in our dealings with others. The duty we owe to ourselves is more akin to an aesthetic or existential duty—a duty to craft a life we can look back on with some degree of satisfaction, whatever that requires.

The autonomy objection is decisive. A moral system that prohibits smoking because it violates one's dignity, or because it harms a future self, necessarily treats the present self as a mere instrument for achieving a state of affairs it does not value. It denies the present self the authority to define its own good, which is the essence of autonomy. The smoker might be *ignorant*, briefly *weak-willed*, or even *tragically short-sighted*, but calling her a *moral criminal* (or a violator of justice) is to abandon the crucial distinction between morality and prudence.

The rightful role of the present self is to be the locus of decision-making, the author of the narrative. This includes the authority to make mistakes, to gamble with one’s future, and to imbue one’s life with risks that others might deem irrational. To preserve this role, we must resist the temptation to moralize imprudence. We might offer the smoker evidence, we might appeal to her own stated goals of living long and healthy, but we must respect her final choice as *hers*.
