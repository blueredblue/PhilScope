# The Pragmatic Grammar of Absurdity: Resolving Moore’s Paradox Through Gricean Implicature and Wittgensteinian Use

## Introduction

At the heart of G. E. Moore’s famous paradox lies a peculiar species of linguistic and cognitive dissonance. To utter a sentence of the form "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining," or its doxastic cousin, "I believe that it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is," is not to commit a logical contradiction. The state of affairs described in the proposition is logically possible; it is entirely conceivable that the world is one way while my mental inventory stubbornly registers it as another. 

Yet, to voice this state of affairs in the first person present indicative is to produce an utterance that feels profoundly irrational, semantically crippled, and practically incoherent. It strikes the ear not as a false claim, but as a performance that undermines its own conditions of intelligibility. 

How are we to account for this distinctively first-person absurdity? The traditional landscape of philosophical reaction divides broadly into semantic, epistemic, and pragmatic camps. Among these, an enticing, synthetic strategy proposes that Moore’s Paradox can be neatly untangled by fusing G. E. Moore’s own early insights regarding implicative relations with Paul Grice’s pragmatic framework of conversational maxims. On this view, asserting "p" does not logically entail "I believe p," but it conventionally or conversationally *implicates* it via the Cooperative Principle—most notably, the Maxim of Quality. 

However, this pragmatic harmonization runs headlong into a formidable rival: Ludwig Wittgenstein’s "use relation" theory of meaning, as elaborated in *Philosophical Investigations*. Wittgenstein treats first-person present-tense belief utterances not as descriptions of internal psychological states, but as expressions or "hesitant assertions" inextricably bound to the grammar of human agency and practice. 

This essay evaluates the claim that Moore’s Paradox can be resolved by combining Moorean implicative relations with Gricean pragmatics. I will argue that while the Gricean-implicative account provides a rigorous diagnostic tool for isolating *why* the utterance feels absurd, it ultimately falters by treating the assertion-belief relation as a mere calculation of conversational intent. 

To redeem the phenomenon of Moorean absurdity, we must incorporate Wittgenstein’s use-theoretic insights, recognizing that the absurdity is not merely a violation of conversational efficiency, but a breakdown in the very grammar of first-person authority. Finally, I will defend the position that these problematic utterances constitute a fundamental violation of *practical rationality*—specifically, the constitutive norms of agency—rather than a mere failure of theoretical consistency.

## I. The Anatomy of Moore’s Paradox and the Limits of Logical Analysis

To appreciate the proposed synthesis of Moore and Grice, we must first map the contours of the paradox itself. G. E. Moore originally formulated the puzzle not primarily as a linguistic curiosity, but as an illustration of the bizarre asymmetry between the first-person and third-person perspectives on belief and assertion. 

Consider the following two formulations:
*   **The Conjunctive Form:** "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining."
*   **The Doxastic Form:** "I believe that it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is."

If we subject these formulations to standard truth-functional logic, we find no contradiction. Let $p$ be "It is raining." The conjunctive form is symbolized as:
$$p \land \neg Bap$$
where $Bap$ stands for "Agent $a$ believes that $p$." 

Can $p$ be true while $\neg Bap$ is true? Manifestly, yes. It can be raining outside while I am entirely agnostic, oblivious, or even stubbornly skeptical about the meteorological facts. The world does not alter its weather patterns simply to match my doxastic states. Similarly, for the doxastic form:
$$Bap \land \neg p$$
I can easily believe that the moon is made of green cheese ($\sim p$) while the moon is, in reality, composed of silicate rock ($p$). 

Thus, the logical propositions expressed by these sentences are mutually compatible. There is no formal contradiction of the form $p \land \neg p$. 

Yet, if a speaker stands before us and asserts, "It is raining, but I do not believe it," we do not accuse them of uttering a logical falsehood; rather, we suspect them of semantic incoherence or psychological derangement. The absurdity is located in the *act of assertion*. As Moore observed, when someone asserts $p$, they imply—or commit themselves to—the belief that $p$. To assert $p$ while simultaneously asserting that one does not believe $p$ is to perform a speech act that cuts its own throat. 

Historically, philosophers attempted to resolve this by positing a hidden logical or semantic relation: perhaps "I assert that $p$" logically entails "I believe that $p$." If assertion *entails* belief, then asserting $\neg Bap$ while asserting $p$ *does* generate a contradiction. 

However, this semantic move faces severe difficulties. If assertion entails belief, then whenever a liar asserts $p$ (while believing $\neg p$), they would be uttering a contradiction. Yet liars are coherent, if dishonest; they do not speak nonsense, they merely speak falsely. Consequently, philosophers abandoned strong semantic entailment accounts in favor of pragmatic explanations. This sets the stage for the convergence of Moorean implicative relations and Gricean pragmatics.

## II. The Gricean-Implicative Synthesis: Saving the Phenomenon via the Maxim of Quality

The strategy of combining G. E. Moore’s conception of implicative relations with Paul Grice’s framework of conversational maxims aims to bridge the gap between logical consistency and pragmatic absurdity. 

### Moorean Implication vs. Entailment
Moore himself sensed that the connection between asserting $p$ and believing $p$ was not a matter of strict entailment, but of what he termed "implication." In his later writings, Moore noted that when a person says "p," they *imply* that they believe $p$, even though the proposition "I believe $p$" is not part of the meaning of $p$. If I say, "The cat is on the mat," I am not stating that I believe the cat is on the mat; I am stating something about a feline and a piece of textile. Nevertheless, my utterance conventionally or conversationally conveys my doxastic state.

### The Gricean Machinery
Enter Paul Grice. In his seminal lectures on *Logic and Conversation*, Grice argued that talk is a cooperative enterprise governed by a general **Cooperative Principle**: 
> "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged."

This principle is operationalized through various maxims, most notably the **Maxims of Quality**:
1. Do not say what you believe to be false.
2. Do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence.

When a speaker asserts a proposition $p$ in a conversational context, the hearer assumes the speaker is obeying the Cooperative Principle, and specifically the Maxim of Quality. By making the assertion "It is raining," the speaker represents themselves as satisfying the condition of the Maxim of Quality—namely, that they possess adequate evidence for, and actually hold, the belief that it is raining ($Bap$). 

Therefore, the Gricean account explains Moore’s Paradox through the mechanism of **conversational implicature**. To assert $p$ is to conversationally implicate that one believes $p$. 

When a speaker utters the Moore-paradoxical sentence, "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining," they perform a split-level communicative act:
1. They explicitly state $p$.
2. By stating $p$, they conversationally implicate that they believe $p$.
3. In the very same breath, they explicitly deny that they believe $p$ ("I do not believe that it is raining").

The resulting absurdity is thus *pragmatic*. The speaker has not violated the rules of formal logic, but they have flouted the pragmatic presuppositions of conversation. They have asserted something whose explicit content directly contradicts the conversational implicature of their own speech act. It is the communicative equivalent of handing someone a gift while simultaneously announcing that you are refusing to give it to them.

This Gricean-implicative synthesis appears exceptionally powerful. It preserves the logical independence of $p$ and $Bap$ while fully diagnosing why the conjunction feels toxic to rationality. It locates the failure not in the abstract structure of propositions, but in the pragmatics of interpersonal communication.

## III. Wittgenstein’s Use Relation Theory and the "Hesitant Assertion"

Despite its neatness, the Gricean-implicative synthesis encounters deep resistance when confronted with Ludwig Wittgenstein’s later philosophy of language, particularly his "use relation" theory developed in *Philosophical Investigations* and *Remarks on the Philosophy of Psychology*.

### Against the Picture of Inner States
Gricean pragmatics still operates within a broadly Cartesian framework: it treats beliefs as inner psychological states or mental representations that exist independently of language, which utterances then *report*, *describe*, or *implicate*. Language is a vehicle for conveying states of mind.

Wittgenstein violently rejects this picture. For Wittgenstein, the meaning of a psychological term (like "believe") is not derived from introspection directed toward a private mental theater, but from its *use* in the public language-game. When someone says, "I believe it is raining," they are not peering into their mind, spotting a mental entity labeled *belief*, and then reporting its presence to an interlocutor. 

Instead, Wittgenstein treats first-person present-tense psychological utterances as functioning analogously to avowals or expressions. Crucially, in *Philosophical Investigations* (Part II, section xi), Wittgenstein analyzes the asymmetry between first-person and third-person psychological attributions:
> "‘I believe...’ is a parenthesis, or: its function is similar to that of the assertion sign; it is not the assertion of a probability."

When I say "I believe $p$," my utterance often functions not as a description of an internal event, but as a **hesitant assertion** of $p$. To say "I believe it will rain" is a way of venturing the claim "It will rain" while acknowledging a slight lack of dogmatic certainty. 

### The Use Relation vs. Conversational Implicature
If Wittgenstein is right, the Gricean-implicative account gets the grammar of first-person belief backwards. The Gricean assumes that $p$ is asserted first, and from that assertion, a separate mental state of belief is conversationally implicated. 

Wittgenstein, by contrast, argues that in the first-person present indicative, the avowal of belief is bound to the assertion of the fact itself. The utterance "I believe $p$" is governed by a completely different set of language-game rules than "He believes $p$." 
*   **Third-person ("He believes $p$"):** Empirical hypothesis about his internal psychological state, verifiable by observing his behavior.
*   **First-person ("I believe $p$"):** An expression of my orientation toward the world, possessing what philosophers call **transparency**. To look out at the world and ask "Is it raining?" is, for all practical intents and purposes, identical to asking "Do I believe it is raining?"

Because first-person belief utterances function as expressions of the agent’s commitment to reality, treating them as mere Gricean implicatures trivializes their role. An implicature can be cancelled ("It is raining—though, mind you, I don't necessarily believe it"). Yet a Moorean utterance cannot be cancelled without linguistic incoherence. You cannot say, *"It is raining, but I do not believe it—just kidding about the belief part."* 

The impossibility of cancellation reveals that the connection is deeper than a conversational implicature. It is structural; it is woven into the very grammar of what it means to make an assertion. As Wittgenstein points out, if someone says "P, but I don't believe it," they are treating their own avowal as if it were an empirical observation about a stranger, thereby abdicating their subjectivity. They are speaking *about* themselves as if they were a thermometer rather than an agent.

## IV. Evaluating the Gricean-Implicative Account Against Wittgensteinian Critique

Can the Gricean-implicative synthesis survive Wittgenstein’s use-theoretic critique? To answer this, we must evaluate whether conversational implicature is robust enough to account for the distinctive absurdity of Moorean utterances, or if it reduces a deep grammatical feature to a superficial breach of etiquette.

### The Limits of Grice in First-Person Avowals
Gricean maxims are fundamentally *teleological* and *social*. They presume a speaker engaged in a cooperative exchange with a hearer, calculating what needs to be said to achieve communicative goals. But Moore’s Paradox can be generated in solitude. 

Imagine a person alone in a room, writing in a diary or muttering to themselves: *"It is raining outside, but I don't believe that it is."* 

In this solitary context, there is no interlocutor to cooperate with, no conversational goals to subvert, and no audience to mislead. Yet the utterance remains profoundly absurd. It feels just as deranged whispered to an empty room as it does shouted in a crowded debate. 

If the absurdity were purely a violation of Gricean conversational maxims (which regulate interpersonal communication), then solitary Moorean utterances should be perfectly coherent. The fact that they are not demonstrates that Gricean pragmatics misses the foundational target. The absurdity is not a failure of *conversation*; it is a failure of *expression*.

### The Wittgensteinian Diagnosis of the Absurdity
Wittgenstein’s use relation theory explains the solitary absurdity effortlessly. To assert "It is raining" is to participate in the language-game of making a claim about the world. In that language-game, the speaker endorses the state of affairs. 

To tack on "...and I do not believe it" is to simultaneously withdraw that endorsement from within the same breath. It is a linguistic self-stultification. The speaker uses the grammatical sign for commitment while simultaneously deploying a phrase that annuls that very commitment. 

Therefore, the attempt to resolve Moore’s Paradox *solely* through Gricean implicative relations fails. Gricean pragmatics treats the connection between assertion and belief as contingent and communicative—something derived from the cooperative intent to convey information. But Wittgenstein’s use relation shows that the connection is internal to the grammar of assertion itself. To assert is to express belief; the two are inextricably bound by the rules of the language-game, not tethered by the loose ropes of conversational maxims.

## V. Theoretical vs. Practical Rationality: Where Does the Violation Lie?

Having dismantled the pure Gricean-implicative resolution in favor of a Wittgensteinian use-theoretic grounding, we must confront a final, crucial normative question: *What kind of rationality is violated in Moore’s Paradox?* 

Is a person who utters a Moore-paradoxical sentence guilty of a failure of **theoretical rationality** (consistency of beliefs, logic, and doxastic alignment) or **practical rationality** (agency, intentional action, and the constitutive norms of what it is to act as a rational subject)?

### The Case for Theoretical Rationality
Many epistemologists (such as those working in the epistemic logic tradition) locate Moore’s Paradox within theoretical rationality. On this view, belief is governed by a norm of truth. To believe $p$ is to take $p$ to be true. 

If theoretical rationality demands that one’s doxastic states form a coherent, truth-tracking set, then holding $\neg Bap$ while $p$ is the case (or asserting it) represents a fracture in the agent's epistemic economy. Proponents of this view argue that the paradox reveals an internal tension in the agent's cognitive architecture: the agent is epistemically fragmented, unable to bring their meta-beliefs into alignment with their first-order tracking of reality.

### The Case for Practical Rationality
However, an evaluation of the speech act reveals that the primary violation is not theoretical, but **practical**. 

An assertion is an *action*. It is something an agent *does*. When an agent asserts $p$, they are performing an intentional speech act that carries normative responsibilities: they are staking their credibility, offering a warrant, and committing themselves to the truth of $p$. 

Practical rationality governs intentional agency—the domain of what we *do* and how our actions cohere with our identity as agents. Consider the practical analogue of Moore’s Paradox:
*   *"I am going to open this window, but I do not intend to open this window."*

This is known as practical Moore’s Paradox (first noted by Sydney Shoemaker). Just as theoretical Moore’s Paradox pairs an assertion with a denial of belief, practical Moore’s Paradox pairs an intentional action (or avowal of intention) with a denial of the intention to perform it. 

You cannot logically deduce a contradiction from "I will open the window and I do not intend to." Yet, to execute this combination is to break down as an agent. An intentional action requires an agent to stand behind their action. To declare an action while disavowing the intention behind it is to paralyze one's own agency.

### Defending the Practical Rationality Violation
I defend the position that Moore’s Paradox—both theoretical and practical—constitutes a fundamental violation of **practical rationality**. 

The absurdity does not stem from a miscalculation in the theoretical domain of pure belief, but from a collapse in the practical architecture of *self-presentation and agency*. When a speaker asserts $p$, they are engaging in a normative practice where they present themselves as a unified subject endorsing a claim. 

To say "p, but I don't believe it" is to perform an action that actively destroys the normative authority required to perform that very action. It is an act of self-undermining agency. The speaker attempts to use the tools of assertion while shedding the volitional skin of the assertor. 

This is why the paradox feels so deeply irrational: it is a practical self-contradiction inherent in the exercise of first-person authority. The agent is fighting against the grammar of their own subjectivity. They are attempting to occupy two incompatible practical stances simultaneously—the stance of the one who claims the world is thus-and-so, and the stance of the observer who disowns any stake in that claim.

---

## Conclusion

The attempt to resolve Moore’s Paradox by combining G. E. Moore’s implicative relations with Paul Grice’s framework of conversational maxims offers a seductive linguistic diagnosis. It correctly identifies that asserting $p$ conversationally implicates the presence of belief via the Maxim of Quality, explaining why the conjunction of the two feels pragmatically jarring. 

However, this Gricean-implicative synthesis ultimately proves insufficient. By treating belief as an inner mental state and assertion as a vehicle for its Gricean conveyance, it misses the deeper, foundational insights of Ludwig Wittgenstein’s use relation theory. Wittgenstein reveals that first-person present-tense belief avowals are not empirical reports or conversational add-ons, but expressions of the agent’s commitment woven into the very grammar of assertion. 

When we push the analysis to its normative core, we see that Moore’s Paradox is not merely a breach of conversational etiquette or a minor glitch in theoretical consistency. It is a profound collapse of **practical rationality**. It exposes the fragility of first-person authority when an agent attempts to exercise the power of assertion while disavowing the unifying stance of the self. In the end, Moore’s Paradox cannot be dissolved by calculating conversational implicatures; it must be understood as the limit-case where agency stumbles over its own grammar, paralyzed by the impossibility of speaking from nowhere while standing squarely in the first person.
