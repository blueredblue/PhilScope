# From Free Choice to Predestination: Augustine on Divine Sovereignty and Human Responsibility

Augustine’s theology of the will is marked by a profound shift. In his early polemics against Manichaeism, he presents free choice as the decisive explanation for moral evil. Human beings sin because they freely turn away from the higher good, not because evil is an independent substance or because God has created them with an evil nature. In *On Free Choice of the Will*, this account serves an apologetic and theodical purpose: it protects God’s goodness and justice by locating the origin of sin in the creature’s voluntary misuse of a good gift. In his later anti-Pelagian writings, however, Augustine gives far greater weight to divine grace, predestination, and the necessity of God’s initiative in salvation. In *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*, he insists that even the beginning of faith and the perseverance of the saints depend on God’s gratuitous election rather than on an antecedent human decision.

At first glance, these positions appear contradictory. The early Augustine seems to affirm an autonomous, even uncaused, human will capable of determining its own moral direction. The later Augustine seems to subordinate the will so completely to divine causality that human freedom risks becoming merely nominal. Yet the development can also be understood as a coherent reordering of questions. Augustine’s early theory addresses the origin of evil and the justice of divine judgment; his later theory addresses the origin of saving faith and the limits of human merit. The will remains significant throughout, but its condition changes from an originally free capacity to a fallen and divided power that requires grace. Augustine therefore does not simply abandon freedom for determinism. He transforms the meaning of freedom: from the capacity to choose between alternatives to the capacity to love and enjoy the good, a capacity that is perfected rather than destroyed by grace.

Whether this transformation succeeds depends on how one evaluates Augustine’s understanding of divine sovereignty. If the sovereignty of God requires that every saving movement of the will originate in God, Augustine achieves a powerful theological unity. Evil is attributable to the creature, salvation to grace, and no human being can claim ultimate credit for righteousness. But if moral responsibility requires that a person could have chosen otherwise in an unconditioned sense, Augustine’s later theology appears to compromise the very agency his early work defended. His mature position is therefore neither a simple contradiction nor an unproblematic synthesis. It is a coherent theological response to the pressures of his controversies, but one that achieves unity by revising human freedom so substantially that the ordinary meaning of responsibility becomes difficult to preserve.

## I. The Manichaean Problem and the Invention of Moral Agency

Augustine’s early account of free will must be understood in relation to Manichaeism. Manichaeism presented reality as a cosmic conflict between two principles, one good and one evil. Human moral experience could then be explained by the presence of evil substance or power within the person. Sin was not fundamentally the act of a free subject who turned away from God; it was the expression of a darker material principle entangled with the soul. Augustine was attracted to Manichaeism in part because it seemed to explain the conflict he experienced within himself. His desire for what he knew to be wrong could be interpreted as the effect of an alien substance rather than as the responsibility of his own will.

This solution, however, threatened the sovereignty and goodness of the Christian God. If evil is an independent substance, then God is not the creator of all that exists. If human beings sin because an evil nature compels them, then divine judgment appears unjust. The Manichaean worldview therefore solved the problem of evil by limiting God’s power, but at the cost of Christian monotheism and moral accountability. Augustine’s response was to deny that evil has positive being. Evil is a privation, a defect or corruption of a good nature. Everything that exists insofar as it exists is good because it derives from God; evil is the failure of a good will or nature to possess the order and perfection appropriate to it.

This argument required a new account of the will. In *On Free Choice of the Will*, Augustine argues that moral evil cannot be attributed to God, since God gives human beings the capacity for rational and voluntary action. The will is a good gift. Its misuse, not its existence, is the source of sin. A person becomes morally blameworthy not merely because an act occurs through him or her, but because the person consents to a lower good in preference to a higher one. The will turns away from immutable divine truth toward mutable and lesser goods. Evil is therefore not a substance implanted in the soul; it is the disorder produced when the will abandons the proper hierarchy of love.

The philosophical structure of this argument depends upon assigning causal priority to the will. If the will’s movement toward evil were itself forced by another cause, then the sinner would not be responsible in the relevant sense. Augustine therefore treats the will as a source of action. It is not merely an instrument through which external forces operate. The person who sins is the person who wills to sin. Augustine’s famous claim that nothing makes the will sinful except the will itself should not be read as a theory of an absolutely self-created soul. The will is created by God and exists within a providential order. Yet, in the context of the Manichaean controversy, it must be “uncaused” in the morally relevant sense: its sinful choice cannot be traced to an evil substance or coercive external necessity. The will is the origin of its own disorder.

This position serves a clear strategic objective. Augustine is not initially trying to solve every problem concerning divine foreknowledge, grace, and predestination. He is trying to exonerate God from responsibility for evil. The crucial distinction is between the creation of a good capacity and the corruption of that capacity through voluntary misuse. God creates free will, and free will is good; the creature turns away from the good, and that turning is evil. God is therefore not the author of sin.

The argument also preserves divine justice. God can justly command, reward, and punish because human beings possess the power to obey or disobey. If sin were caused by an evil principle or by a necessity imposed upon the will, punishment would be arbitrary. But if the will chooses its disorder, judgment is intelligible. The moral order of the universe reflects the structure of free agency: creatures are accountable because they are sources of their actions.

Still, Augustine’s early account contains tensions that would later become increasingly difficult to ignore. If the will is free in the sense that it can initiate its own sinful movement, why does it not equally initiate the movement toward God? If sin originates in an uncaused choice, then perhaps righteousness also originates in an uncaused choice. But if a person can begin to turn toward God independently of grace, then salvation may seem to depend ultimately on human initiative. Augustine’s anti-Manichaean defense of responsibility thus contains the seed of the later Pelagian controversy.

## II. Freedom, Foreknowledge, and the Limits of the Early Position

A further difficulty concerns divine foreknowledge. Augustine wants to maintain both that human choices are free and that God knows all future events. The fact that God foreknows an act does not, he argues, cause the act. Knowledge is not coercion. God knows what the will will choose, but divine knowledge does not force the will to choose it. This distinction allows Augustine to preserve freedom while affirming providence.

Yet the distinction does not fully settle the philosophical problem. If God infallibly knows that a person will perform a particular action, can that person genuinely do otherwise? Augustine’s early solution is largely conceptual and moral: the action is free because it proceeds from the agent’s will, not because it is metaphysically undetermined in every sense. A person acts voluntarily even if God knows the action beforehand. The early Augustine therefore tends to define freedom by the absence of compulsion and by the presence of rational consent, rather than by a strict requirement of alternative possibilities.

This point is important for assessing the later development. Augustine never simply moves from libertarian freedom to a completely unrelated doctrine of coercive determinism. Even in *On Free Choice*, freedom is not absolute independence from God. God is the source of the will’s existence, the source of the moral order, and the object toward which the will ought to be directed. The will is free within a created order. What changes later is not that divine causality appears for the first time, but that Augustine comes to emphasize the depth of divine causality in every movement that leads to salvation.

The early position also presupposes that the will is capable of orienting itself toward the good. Augustine distinguishes between having free choice and possessing the freedom that consists in righteousness. A person may have the power to choose but be morally defective in its use. Freedom is therefore already an ordered concept. The will is free in one sense simply by being voluntary, but it is more fully free when it is rightly ordered by love of God. This distinction enables Augustine later to argue that grace does not destroy freedom. Grace restores and strengthens the will so that it can become what it was created to be.

Nevertheless, the anti-Manichaean framework places explanatory emphasis on the creature. The origin of evil is located in the will’s self-directed turn. Augustine’s God is vindicated because the creature is an autonomous source of defect. The more independent the will’s causal role, the more successfully Augustine avoids making God responsible for sin. At this stage, the theological priority is theodicy: the defense of God’s goodness in the face of evil.

## III. The Pelagian Challenge and the Reinterpretation of Freedom

The Pelagian controversy shifted the question. Pelagius and his followers did not generally deny God’s grace. They emphasized, however, the goodness of creation, the reality of human freedom, and the justice of divine commands. If God commands perfection, they reasoned, human beings must possess the capacity to obey. Pelagian moral theology rejected the idea that inherited guilt or an incapacitating corruption of nature makes sin unavoidable. Human beings are responsible because they are free, and they can choose righteousness through the proper exercise of their natural powers, assisted by divine instruction, forgiveness, and example.

Julian of Eclanum sharpened this challenge. Julian objected that Augustine’s account of original sin and irresistible grace made God’s judgment arbitrary. If human beings are born burdened by Adam’s sin and unable to will the good without a special divine intervention, then those who are not granted that intervention seem condemned for a condition they did not personally choose. Julian also feared that Augustine’s doctrine undermined moral exhortation. Why command people to be righteous if their willingness to obey depends entirely on an antecedent divine gift?

The Pelagian challenge exposed the unresolved implication of Augustine’s early theology. If the will is the source of its own moral direction, then why should the sinner’s will be described as unable to return to God? If God’s commands correspond to human capacity, then perhaps nature itself contains sufficient resources for righteousness. Augustine’s mature response was to insist that the human will after the Fall is not destroyed but radically wounded. It remains a will, and therefore remains capable of voluntary action, but it is enslaved by disordered desire. Human beings can choose particular goods, perform socially admirable acts, and even recognize moral truths. What they cannot do by their natural powers is produce the rightly ordered love of God that constitutes true righteousness and merits eternal life.

This account introduces a distinction between natural freedom and liberating grace. The fallen person still chooses voluntarily; he or she is not externally compelled. Yet the will is internally bound by sin. Augustine’s image is not of a body physically chained, but of a will whose loves have been deformed. People do what they want, but what they want is shaped by concupiscence, pride, fear, and attachment to temporal goods. The absence of external compulsion is therefore insufficient for salvation. A person must be healed at the level of desire.

In this context, grace becomes more than divine assistance added to an essentially sufficient human power. Grace is the cause of the will’s recovery. It gives not merely instruction but delight in the good; not merely an external command but an inward transformation. Augustine’s later theology often presents God as making the will willing. Grace does not force a reluctant person in opposition to his or her will. Instead, it changes the will so that it freely embraces what it previously resisted. The liberated will is therefore not an uncaused will but a healed will.

This position responds directly to Julian’s moral perfectionism. Julian’s view, as Augustine understood it, made human righteousness depend on the integrity of nature and the autonomous exercise of choice. Augustine feared that such a system would allow human beings to boast before God. If one person believes and another does not, and if the decisive difference lies in their independent use of free choice, then salvation ultimately depends on something the human being supplies. Divine grace becomes a supplement rather than the foundation.

Against this conclusion, Augustine insists that no one can claim ultimate merit for believing. The beginning of faith is itself a gift. The distinction between the believer and the unbeliever is not explained by a superior natural will that God merely rewards. It is explained by God’s merciful election. This is the central movement of *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*.

## IV. Predestination in *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*

In *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*, Augustine presents predestination as God’s foreordination of those whom he intends to save. He rejects the idea that God chooses people because he foresees that they will choose him through their own unaided freedom. Such a view would make divine election depend on a human act that precedes grace. For Augustine, the good will that turns toward God is itself produced by grace. God does not elect people because they are already righteous; election makes them righteous.

This is a decisive reversal of explanatory priority. In the anti-Manichaean account, the will is emphasized as the origin of evil so that God is not blamed for sin. In the anti-Pelagian account, God is emphasized as the origin of faith so that human beings cannot claim credit for salvation. The asymmetry is crucial. Augustine does not say that God causes sin in the same way that he causes grace. Sin is a privation and defect arising from the creature’s disordered will; grace is a positive gift that restores the will. Divine sovereignty is therefore not a symmetrical determinism in which God is equally responsible for good and evil. God’s causality is universal with respect to the existence and restoration of creatures, but evil remains a privative failure rather than a positive divine production.

Augustine’s doctrine of predestination also entails that the number and identity of the elect depend on God’s hidden judgment. Human beings cannot discover the entire pattern of election through ordinary moral observation. Some who appear far from God may be converted; some who seem righteous may not persevere. The decisive gift is perseverance to the end, and this too is given by God. A person may begin well and later fall away, demonstrating that initial faith and final perseverance are not identical gifts.

This doctrine directly challenges Julian’s concern with moral equivalence. Augustine accepts that individuals are judged for their own sins, but he denies that human beings stand before God with equal natural capacities to attain saving righteousness. The Fall has introduced a condition in which all are implicated in sin and none can demand grace as a right. If God saves some and permits others to remain in their fallen condition, he is not unjust, because no one is owed mercy. Mercy is, by definition, gratuitous. The scandal of predestination is therefore not that God denies people what they deserve, but that he gives some what none deserve.

The argument is powerful within Augustine’s theological premises, but it produces severe philosophical difficulties. If God’s grace is necessary and efficacious, and if God chooses whom to save, then what role remains for human agency? Augustine’s answer is that grace operates through the will, not against it. The elect believe freely because God causes them to will the good. Their choices are voluntary, pleasurable, and expressive of their renewed character. They are not puppets. Yet their willing is not independent in the sense defended by a libertarian theory of freedom. The difference between the saved and the unsaved ultimately lies in divine election, not in an unconditioned act of self-determination.

This is why calling Augustine’s mature position “deterministic” is both illuminating and misleading. It is deterministic if determinism means that the decisive conditions of salvation are established by divine causality rather than by an autonomous human initiative. It is misleading if it suggests that Augustine imagines God mechanically forcing choices upon unwilling subjects. His model is closer to theological compatibilism: an action is free when it proceeds from the agent’s own will, even if that will has been decisively shaped by grace. Freedom is self-expression, but the self is understood relationally and teleologically. A will is not most free when it is undetermined; it is most free when it rests in the good.

## V. Contradiction or Development?

Does Augustine’s evolution constitute a fundamental contradiction? The answer depends on which definition of freedom is treated as essential.

If freedom means the power of contrary choice grounded in an uncaused or self-originating will, then there is a genuine contradiction. The early anti-Manichaean argument requires the will to be the ultimate source of sinful movement. The later anti-Pelagian argument denies that the will can initiate the movement toward salvation without grace. One might respond that Augustine needs two different standards: he makes the creature sufficiently autonomous to explain evil but insufficiently autonomous to explain good. This asymmetry can appear ad hoc. Why is the will credited with originating evil but not with originating faith?

Augustine’s answer is that evil and good are metaphysically different. Evil is a privation, a falling away from the good, whereas grace is a positive restoration that exceeds fallen nature. A will can defect through its own disorder, but it cannot heal itself. The analogy is that a person can fall into sickness without possessing the power to cure himself. The fact that the illness is self-induced does not imply that recovery is possible without external aid. This analogy gives Augustine a coherent structure, but it does not eliminate the concern that the fall itself may have required an autonomous capacity that later disappears.

Moreover, Augustine’s early language about the will’s self-causation is less absolute than a modern libertarian reading suggests. The will is not independent of God’s creative causality. It is a creaturely cause within a divine order. If Augustine can distinguish between God’s creating the will and the will’s causing its own sin, he can similarly distinguish between God’s grace and the will’s willing the good. The later doctrine does not necessarily deny all creaturely causality; it denies that fallen human causality can produce saving righteousness without grace.

On this reading, Augustine’s theology develops rather than simply contradicts itself. The early doctrine answers the question, “Why is God not the author of evil?” The later doctrine answers the question, “Why is anyone saved?” The two answers are not identical because the two problems are not symmetrical. Evil is explained as the creature’s privation and rebellion; salvation is explained as God’s healing and gift. The asymmetry is not a logical accident but the center of Augustine’s doctrine of grace.

There is also continuity in Augustine’s conception of freedom. Even in his mature writings, he does not define freedom as mere choice among options. The will is oriented by love. To be free is to act from one’s own deepest desire, and the deepest desire of a rightly ordered person is God. Sinful people are not free because they lack all choice; they are unfree because their choices arise from a disordered love. Grace liberates them by changing what they love. Thus divine causality does not compete with human freedom as though God and the creature were rival agents operating in the same conceptual space. God is the source of the creature’s power to act, while the creature remains the proximate agent of its own choices.

This synthesis is coherent if freedom is defined teleologically and compatibilistically. It is less convincing if freedom requires ultimate alternative possibility. Augustine’s mature theology sacrifices the latter in order to preserve the priority of grace.

## VI. Divine Supremacy and the Problem of Moral Responsibility

The most difficult issue concerns moral responsibility. Augustine wants to maintain that sinners are responsible for sin, while also asserting that saving righteousness depends entirely on grace. Can responsibility survive if human moral agency is subordinate to divine supremacy?

One answer is that responsibility concerns the source of an action within the agent’s character, not the ultimate origin of that character. A person is responsible for an action if the action expresses what the person wants and is not produced by external compulsion. On this account, the sinner who loves temporal goods more than God is responsible because that love is genuinely his or hers. The fact that the fallen condition has a historical and inherited origin does not erase personal participation in it. Similarly, the saint is responsible for acts of love and obedience because grace operates through a will that truly consents.

This view allows Augustine to maintain both condemnation and praise. God condemns sin because sinners desire and perform it. God rewards righteousness because the righteous desire and perform it. Yet the final source of the distinction between the two remains grace. Human beings are responsible as agents, but not sovereign as ultimate causes. Augustine’s hierarchy is therefore: God is the first cause of all good; human beings are secondary causes of their choices; sin is a privative defect in those choices.

The difficulty is that responsibility is often linked to the ability to do otherwise. If the sinner’s will is so bound that it cannot turn toward God without a grace not given to all, then punishment may seem unfair. The sinner does what he wants, but perhaps he could not have wanted otherwise. Augustine’s answer rests on the distinction between inability and excuse. The fallen person cannot do the good required for salvation, but this inability is not an external constraint imposed upon an otherwise innocent subject. It is the expression of a corrupt will. The sinner is not forced to sin against his desires; he sins because he desires wrongly. Augustine therefore treats bondage to sin as culpable bondage.

This response is philosophically controversial. An inherited condition can be described as culpable only if one accepts Augustine’s account of solidarity in Adam and the transmission of original sin. Without that theological premise, the responsibility of individuals for a condition they did not initiate appears doubtful. Julian’s criticism remains powerful: if grace is selectively given, and if those without grace cannot achieve the righteousness God demands, then divine judgment seems to evaluate people according to a standard that God alone makes attainable.

Augustine’s theology also risks weakening the practical meaning of moral exhortation. If God must grant the will both to believe and to persevere, then exhortations, warnings, and disciplines might appear unnecessary. Augustine rejects this conclusion by arguing that preaching and admonition are among the means through which God gives grace. The command does not imply that human beings possess independent power; it is an instrument by which God awakens and transforms the will. The fact that God causes an outcome through exhortation does not make the exhortation meaningless. It remains part of the causal order through which the will is renewed.

Yet this answer changes the function of moral discourse. In a Pelagian framework, a command addresses an agent presumed capable of compliance by nature. In Augustine’s framework, a command may reveal guilt, expose inability, and become an occasion for grace. Moral responsibility is preserved, but not in the form of autonomous self-mastery. It becomes dependence: the creature is responsible precisely because it is accountable to God, while its restoration depends on God’s mercy.

## VII. Does Divine Supremacy Unify Augustine’s Theology?

Subordinating human moral agency to divine supremacy does unify several major strands of Augustine’s theology. It offers a consistent account of creation, sin, grace, and salvation. Creation is good because it comes from God. Sin is not a substance but a privation arising from the will’s disorder. Human beings remain responsible because sin is voluntary. Salvation is impossible without grace because the will is wounded and cannot heal itself. Grace does not abolish freedom but restores it by redirecting love toward God. Predestination ensures that salvation is truly a gift rather than a reward for an independent human achievement.

This structure also protects divine sovereignty from two opposite threats. Against Manichaeism, Augustine denies that evil is a rival power capable of limiting God. Against Pelagianism, he denies that the human will is a rival power capable of determining salvation independently of God. In both controversies, Augustine’s theology moves toward monotheistic supremacy: there is no evil principle alongside God, and there is no autonomous human principle above or alongside grace.

This unity is intellectually impressive. Augustine can explain why God is not responsible for sin without conceding that God lacks control over creation. He can affirm that grace is decisive without reducing the will to a physical mechanism. The same doctrine of ordered love explains both bondage and freedom. Human beings are free when their loves are rightly ordered, and enslaved when they are disordered. God’s sovereignty is therefore not merely juridical or causal; it is the sovereignty of the highest good over the creature’s final end.

At the same time, the unity is purchased at a price. Augustine’s mature theology leaves a sharp asymmetry between the explanation of damnation and the explanation of salvation. Salvation is positively willed and efficaciously produced by God. Damnation is attributed to sin, but the persistence of sin among those not granted saving grace remains within a providential order. Augustine can say that God does not cause their evil, yet God’s decision not to heal them becomes difficult to distinguish from an intentional permission of their ruin. The doctrine of predestination thus generates the problem it seeks to solve: divine sovereignty is protected, but divine goodness becomes harder to reconcile with the unequal distribution of grace.

The same tension affects human dignity. Augustine’s early defense of free will gives human beings a striking moral independence. They are genuine sources of action whose choices matter to the structure of the universe. His later emphasis on predestination places them in a more dependent position. They are still agents, but their most important act—the act of turning toward God—cannot originate from them. Human beings are morally responsible, but their ultimate destiny is not under their control. This can inspire humility and gratitude, yet it can also produce anxiety, passivity, or a sense that moral striving has no independent significance.

The philosophical judgment therefore depends on the desired conception of human agency. Augustine’s theology is coherent if human freedom is fundamentally a capacity for right love and if divine grace is understood as the condition of that capacity. It is less coherent if freedom requires that the agent be the ultimate source of the motives and choices by which salvation is determined. His later doctrine does not preserve the early meaning of free will unchanged. It preserves responsibility by redefining freedom in relation to desire, grace, and final fulfillment.

## Conclusion

Augustine’s shift from *On Free Choice of the Will* to *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum* reflects the changing strategic demands of his polemical context. Against the Manichees, he needed to establish that evil does not come from God or from an evil substance. He therefore emphasized free will as the creaturely source of sin. The will’s turn toward evil had to be sufficiently independent to preserve divine goodness and human accountability. Against the Pelagians and especially the moral perfectionism associated with Julian of Eclanum, Augustine needed to establish that salvation cannot arise from the autonomous exercise of natural human powers. He therefore emphasized the priority, efficacy, and gratuitousness of divine grace, extending predestination even to the beginning of faith and the gift of perseverance.

This development is not simply a contradiction, because Augustine maintains important continuities. He consistently distinguishes voluntariness from external compulsion, understands freedom through the orientation of love, and treats evil as a privation rather than a positive substance. His mature theology can therefore be read as a form of compatibilism: grace determines and heals the will, but the renewed will genuinely desires and chooses the good. Divine causality and human agency operate at different levels rather than as mutually exclusive forces.

Nevertheless, the development is not a seamless reconciliation. Augustine’s early account gives the will a stronger role as an originating cause than his later theology allows in the sphere of salvation. His mature position protects divine supremacy by denying that humans can take ultimate credit for righteousness, but it complicates the fairness of judgment toward those who do not receive efficacious grace. Human responsibility survives only after being detached from ultimate self-determination. People are responsible because they act from their own desires, yet the transformation of those desires belongs entirely to God.

Augustine thus unifies his theology by making divine sovereignty the paramount principle of both creation and redemption. But the resulting unity is tragic rather than uncomplicated. It preserves God’s goodness against Manichaean dualism and God’s supremacy against Pelagian moral autonomy, while leaving human beings free in a narrower, more dependent sense. The will remains morally significant, but it no longer stands as an uncaused origin of its own destiny. Augustine’s final position is therefore best understood as a coherent defense of divine sovereignty that exposes, rather than fully resolves, the tension between grace and responsibility. It gives priority to God at the cost of redefining what it means for a human being to be free.
