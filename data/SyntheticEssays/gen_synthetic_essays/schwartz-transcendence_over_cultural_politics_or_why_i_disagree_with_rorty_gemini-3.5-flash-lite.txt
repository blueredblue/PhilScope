# The Immanent Horizon: Rorty, Brandom, Heidegger, and the Transcendence of the Social

## Introduction

The trajectory of late-twentieth-century Anglo-American and Continental philosophy has been largely defined by a singular, persistent anxiety: the dread of the foundational. Whether framed as the "God-trick" of the view from nowhere, the metaphysical illusion of the unconditioned, or the onto-theological legacy of Western philosophy, thinkers of various stripes have sought to purge discourse of its lingering dependencies on things-in-themselves, absolute perspectives, and transcendental structures. 

At the vanguard of this movement stands Richard Rorty. In works such as *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* and *Consequences of Pragmatism*, Rorty executed a sweeping maneuver: the displacement of ontology by cultural politics. Where tradition sought to align thought with the deep, invariant structures of reality—to map the furniture of the universe sub specie aeternitatis—Rorty argued that truth, reality, and goodness are nothing more than the temporary, contingent outcomes of historicized conversational practices. Epistemology and ontology, on this view, are not failed sciences waiting for a better method, but defunct vocabularies. They are symptoms of a culture’s desire to anchor its parochial projects in something higher than itself.

Yet, this pragmatic substitution of cultural politics for ontology raises an urgent philosophical question: Does Rorty’s deflationary gesture successfully excise the metaphysical impulse, or does it merely drive it underground? To answer this, one must interrogate the mechanisms by which "social practice" is granted sole legislative authority over the real and the true. This requires an engagement with Robert Brandom’s reading of Martin Heidegger’s foundational categories—*Zuhandensein* (readiness-to-hand), *Vorhandensein* (presence-at-hand), and *Dasein*—which Brandom marshals to demonstrate that all epistemic and ontological authority reduces to normative social practices. 

However, Brandom’s and Rorty’s immanentism—the claim that "it’s language all the way down" or "practices all the way down"—faces a profound structural tension. On the one hand, Rorty explicitly rejects any "background" of all possible practices, insisting that there is no overarching horizon or nature against which our vocabularies can be measured. On the other hand, Rorty consistently appeals to William James’s standard of human happiness and solidarity as a regulative or structural end for social action. If there is no background, from whence does this standard derive its normative weight? Furthermore, when we turn to Thomas Sheehan’s radical interpretation of Heidegger’s *Kehre* (the turning) and the *Ereignis* (the event of appropriation), we must ask whether the sheer *givenness* of entities requires a *tertium quid*—a dimension of *Sache selbst* that exceeds the discursive and pragmatic mastery of the social. 

This essay will evaluate Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics, utilizing Brandom’s Heideggerian taxonomy and Sheehan’s reading of the later Heidegger to test the limits of immanentism. I will argue that while Rorty successfully deconstructs traditional metaphysics, his attempt to reduce all ontology to cultural politics collapses into an unacknowledged metaphysical reliance on a pragmatic telos. Moreover, through Sheehan's lens, we will see that the givenness of entities resists total social domestication, pointing toward a non-metaphysical excess that escapes the linguistic-pragmatic grid.

## Part I: Rorty’s Pragmatic Turn and the Elimination of Ontology

To understand Rorty’s replacement of ontology with cultural politics, one must first grasp his diagnosis of the philosophical enterprise. For Rorty, the history of philosophy from Plato through Kant and into the twentieth-century analytical tradition is a sustained attempt to find a *tertium quid* between human vocabularies and the world—a medium of representation that guarantees truth by mirroring reality. This "mirror of nature" presupposes that the mind or language stands in a special relation to things, such that our epistemic success is measured by the adequacy of our representations to an antecedent, mind-independent reality.

Rorty’s pragmatism, heavily indebted to Wilfrid Sellars, Willard Van Orman Quine, and Thomas Kuhn, dissolves this representationalist problematic. With Quine, Rorty rejects the analytic-synthetic distinction and the "dogmas of empiricism," arguing that our beliefs face the tribunal of experience not as isolated atomic units, but as a vast, web-like fabric where no strand is immune to revision. With Sellars, Rorty rejects the "Myth of the Given"—the idea that there are non-inferential, unconceptualized inputs from reality that serve as the indubitable foundation of knowledge. There is no unmediated contact with the real. Every encounter with the world is already inscribed within a historically contingent vocabulary.

Consequently, Rorty urges us to abandon the very questions that animate ontology and traditional epistemology. Questions like "What is the nature of reality?" or "Do numbers, moral values, or physical objects *really* exist independently of our language?" are diagnosed as pseudo-questions. They are linguistic artifacts generated by the confusion between the causal interactions we have with the world and the justificatory practices within our conversational communities. 

Objects cause us to have sensations—a brute causal fact that Rorty does not deny. A heavy stone falling on my foot causes pain and triggers the belief "That stone is heavy." But causation is one thing; justification is another. Justification is a normative status conferred within a community of language-users. As Rorty famously asserts, nothing can serve as a justification for a belief except another belief. Therefore, to ask whether our beliefs correspond to reality *as it is in itself* is to commit a category mistake. We can step from one vocabulary into another, but we can never step outside of vocabulary altogether to compare our language with an un-language-framed reality.

From this diagnosis flows Rorty’s programmatic replacement: cultural politics takes the place of ontology. If ontology asks what *is*, cultural politics asks *what we should do*. It is an ongoing, open-ended conversation about which vocabularies, metaphors, and social arrangements will best serve human flourishing, freedom, and solidarity. Instead of seeking truth as correspondence with an intrinsic nature of reality, Rorty defines truth in a purely deflationary, pragmatic sense: truth is simply what is good for us to believe. It is an expression of praise for beliefs that have passed the tests of our current conversational practices and proved useful in achieving our communal goals.

By shifting the register from truth-as-correspondence to solidarity-as-utility, Rorty aims to banish the specter of metaphysical transcendence. There is no longer any need for a "beyond"—no divine mind, no noumenal realm, no deep metaphysical structure of the cosmos. There are only human beings cobbling together tools, institutions, and stories to cope with pain, death, and each other.

## Part II: Brandom’s Heidegger: From Social Practice to Ontological Authority

Yet, Rorty’s casual dismissal of ontology often leaves analytical philosophers and phenomenologists unsatisfied. How can we speak of "practices," "vocabularies," and "communities" without making implicit ontological commitments? What is the structure of the domain that remains when ontology is cleared away? To bridge the gap between Rorty’s pragmatism and the rich tradition of Continental philosophy, we must turn to Robert Brandom’s monumental work on normativity and his radical reading of Martin Heidegger’s *Being and Time*.

In works like *Making It Explicit* and his subsequent engagements with the history of philosophy, Brandom develops an inferentialist, norm-based pragmatism that shares Rorty’s anti-representationalist impulses while providing a rigorous systematic foundation. In his essays on Heidegger (such as "Heidegger’s Categories in *Being and Time*"), Brandom undertakes a daring interpretive translation, reading Heidegger’s existential analytic not as a piece of speculative ontology in the traditional sense, but as a proto-pragmatist account of normative social practice.

Brandom approaches Heidegger through the categories of *Zuhandensein* (readiness-to-hand), *Vorhandensein* (presence-at-hand), and *Dasein*. In traditional readings, these categories are understood as strata of being: entities are first encountered as equipment ready-to-hand in a pragmatic web of involvements (*Verweisungssatz*), and they are transformed into mere present-at-hand objects when our practical engagement breaks down or is interrupted by theoretical contemplation. 

Brandom radicalizes this by reinterpreting these categories through the lens of deontic scorekeeping and normative pragmatics. For Brandom, *Zuhandensein* is not merely a description of how we use hammers and pens; it is the fundamental structure of normative engagement. To treat something as ready-to-hand is to master a practice—to know how to navigate a normative space of commitments and entitlements. The hammer is not a bundle of properties (mass, extension, hardness) to which we attach a utility; rather, the hammer *is* its place in the pragmatic-inferential network of practical engagements. Its identity is entirely constituted by its role in social practices.

What, then, of *Vorhandensein* (presence-at-hand)? In Brandom’s reading, *Vorhandensein* is not a more fundamental ontological layer (as modern naturalism assumes, taking the physical object as the primary reality), nor is it a mere falling away from authentic practice. Instead, *Vorhandensein* is a *derivative* status produced by a specific kind of discursive practice: the practice of making explicit what was previously implicit in our practical coping. When a tool breaks, or when we step back to theorize, we do not drop out of social practice into a non-social, mind-independent reality; rather, we engage in a *new* set of discursive practices. We move from practical mastery (knowing-how) to explicit assertion (knowing-that). The present-at-hand object is an artifact of this discursive shift. It is what things look like when we subject them to the game of giving and asking for reasons.

At the center of this architecture is *Dasein*—the entity that we ourselves are, characterized by *In-der-Welt-sein* (being-in-the-world) and *Sorge* (care). Brandom interprets *Dasein* not as a privileged metaphysical substance or a transcendental ego, but as a *social node*—a locus of normative appraisal where practical engagement and linguistic articulation intersect. For Brandom, *Dasein* is fundamentally social (Mitsein). The authoritative normative status that entities possess—whether they are hammers, electrons, or moral norms—is conferred by the communal practices of *Dasein*.

Through Brandom’s reading, Heidegger’s ontology is completely domesticated by pragmatism. Ontology does not reveal the deep, mind-independent structure of the cosmos; rather, ontology is *hermeneutic disclosure*, and disclosure is nothing more than the unfolding of normative social practices. All epistemic and ontological authority reduces to social practice. To say that an entity exists or has certain properties is just to say that it occupies a specific node in a socially articulated network of practical commitments and entitlements. There is no appeal to a reality outside of or prior to these practices. Brandom thus provides Rorty with the systematic machinery he lacked: an explicit demonstration that even the most rarefied categories of being are downstream from the normative dynamics of linguistic communities.

## Part III: The Tension in Rorty: The Rejection of Background vs. The Teleology of Human Happiness

Despite the theoretical consonance between Rorty’s cultural politics and Brandom’s norm-conferring pragmatism, a deep structural tension persists at the heart of Rorty’s project—a tension that exposes the vulnerability of trying to dispense with all forms of transcendence.

This tension crystallizes around two seemingly incompatible commitments in Rorty’s work:
1. His vehement rejection of any "background" of all possible practices, foundational horizons, or transcendental frameworks.
2. His persistent, unavoidable reliance on a substantive normative standard—specifically, William James’s standard of human happiness, freedom, and the minimization of cruelty—as the structural end for social action.

Throughout his critique of foundationalism, Rorty repeatedly attacks the idea that there is a deep background against which all vocabularies are measured, or a single human nature that our cultural politics must reflect. Influenced by historicism and pragmatism, Rorty insists that there is no "nature" of man, no universal reason, and no ultimate horizon within which human history unfolds. We are entirely historical all the way down. There is no overarching framework that could adjudicate between competing vocabularies from a neutral standpoint. Vocabularies simply succeed one another through redescription, irony, and the shifting tides of cultural fashion, much like biological species undergo evolutionary drift.

Yet, when Rorty turns to politics and ethics—particularly in *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity*—he cannot maintain this pure, deflationary historicism without collapsing into a paralyzing moral relativism. To avoid the charge that his pragmatism reduces to an endorsement of any prevailing social consensus (whether liberal democracy or fascism), Rorty anchors his cultural politics in a normative aspiration: the reduction of cruelty and the maximization of human solidarity. 

Here, he explicitly invokes William James. For James, truth and goodness are tested by their capacity to satisfy human needs, foster human flourishing, and harmonize human desires. Rorty adopts this Jamesian metric as the ultimate arbiter of cultural politics. A vocabulary is better than another if it results in less cruelty, more freedom, and richer possibilities for human solidarity.

The tension is acute: If there is no background of all possible practices, no universal human nature, and no transcendental horizon, *from whence does this standard of human happiness derive its authority?* 

If "human happiness" and "the minimization of cruelty" are themselves just local, contingent products of a particular Western, liberal, bourgeois vocabulary, then why should anyone outside that historical lineage care about them? Rorty wants to treat liberalism as an ungrounded, contingent historical achievement ("our" project, as opposed to "foundational truth"), but he simultaneously relies on the universalistic moral resonance of "human suffering" and "cruelty" to give his cultural politics its bite. When Rorty argues that cruelty is the worst thing we do, he is appealing to a deeply entrenched normative intuition that feels remarkably like a metaphysical absolute, even if he dresses it up in historicist garb.

Furthermore, by making human happiness the structural end for social action, Rorty smuggles a teleology back into his pragmatism. A teleology requires a horizon. To measure whether social practices are successfully reducing cruelty, one must presuppose a coherent understanding of what constitutes human flourishing and what counts as a violation of it. But if all standards are immanent to the practices that generate them, then there is no independent standpoint from which to judge whether our current cultural politics is actually achieving "happiness" or merely reinforcing a localized form of hegemony.

Rorty attempts to evade this by retreating to irony: the ironist is someone who uses vocabularies while knowing their contingency, never taking their own final vocabulary as ultimate. But a society of pure ironists cannot sustain the robust moral solidarity that Rorty’s liberal utopia requires. Solidarity demands conviction; it requires the belief that the suffering of *others* matters, not merely as a local conversational preference, but as something that makes a non-negotiable claim upon us.

Thus, Rorty’s rejection of the background founders on the rock of his own ethical commitments. By relying on William James’s standard of human happiness as the structural end of social action, Rorty reintroduces a quasi-transcendental demand—a normative horizon that cannot be fully dissolved into the shifting currents of cultural politics without losing its critical force.

## Part IV: The Given and the *Tertium Quid*: Thomas Sheehan’s Reading of the *Kehre* and *Ereignis*

To push this critique further, we must ask whether the immanentist reduction of ontology to social practice—shared by Rorty and Brandom—adequately accounts for the *givenness* of entities. Does the pragmatic-discursive grid exhaust what it means for something to be encountered, or is there an ontological excess that resists social domestication?

To answer this, we turn to Thomas Sheehan’s radical interpretation of Martin Heidegger’s later philosophy, specifically his reading of the *Kehre* (the turning) and the *Ereignis* (the event of appropriation). Standard readings of Heidegger, including Brandom’s, tend to remain anchored within the framework of *Being and Time*, focusing on *Dasein* and its worldly practices. But Sheehan argues that this early framework is itself subject to a profound transformation in Heidegger’s later work—a transformation that breaks decisively with any anthropocentric or pragmatist reduction.

Sheehan’s central thesis is that Heidegger’s entire philosophical trajectory is an attempt to think *Anwesenheit* (presence). But what does "presence" mean? In traditional metaphysics, presence is attributed to entities as a property they possess independently of humans. In early Heidegger, presence is tied to *Dasein’s* understanding of Being and its pragmatic clearing (*Da*). However, Sheehan contends that the later Heidegger realizes an even more radical truth: *Dasein* is not the master of the clearing, nor is the clearing merely a product of human social practices. Rather, human beings—*Dasein*—are *themselves* thrown into an antecedent clearing that they neither created nor control.

This brings us to the *Ereignis* (often translated as the event of appropriation or event-co-belonging). In Sheehan’s reading, *Ereignis* is not a mystical, theological entity or a new foundational ground. It is the irreducible fact that *there is* an opening, a clearing (*Es gibt*), in which things can show up as what they are *before* and *condition for* any human linguistic or pragmatic engagement with them. 

Crucially, Sheehan emphasizes that entities are given to us *as resisting* our practices, our vocabularies, and our normative categories. The sheer *thereness* of the real—what Heidegger calls *Sache selbst* (the matter itself)—exceeds our conceptualizations. When a scientist encounters an anomaly, or when a person experiences physical pain, or when the earth shakes in an earthquake, these events are not mere "projections" of a social practice, nor are they exhaustively accounted for by Brandom’s deontic scorekeeping. They are manifestations of an ontological givenness that erupts *into* and *disrupts* our normative spaces.

This points directly to the necessity of a *tertium quid*—not a representational mirror of nature in the old metaphysical sense, but an ontological dimension that transcends the linguistic-pragmatic primacy of the social. 

What is this *tertium quid*? It is the sheer *self-showing* of entities out of a non-human, non-social horizon (the *Es gibt*). It is the ontological difference understood not merely as the difference between entities and *Dasein’s* understanding of them, but as the irreducible givenness of the real that precedes, conditions, and survives every historical vocabulary. 

If we follow Brandom and Rorty, we are asked to believe that because we can only *speak* of things within a vocabulary, and because we can only *justify* beliefs with other beliefs, therefore the very structure of reality is exhausted by linguistic practices. This is what Quentin Meillassoux and other speculative realists term "correlationism"—the philosophical belief that we only ever have access to the correlation between thinking and being, and that to think of a reality outside of this correlation is a self-contradiction.

Sheehan’s reading of the later Heidegger provides a powerful antidote to this correlationist closure. The *Kehre* signals the realization that human Dasein is *claimed* by Being, not the other way around. We are derivative of the clearing; the clearing is not derivative of us. Therefore, cultural politics cannot be the ultimate horizon of philosophy. Cultural politics—the negotiation of norms, vocabularies, and institutions—takes place *within* a prior ontological clearing that is given to us as a gift (*Es gibt*), a gift whose source is radically non-social and non-human.

## Part V: Evaluating Rorty’s Replacement: Does It Avoid Metaphysical Transcendence?

We are now equipped to evaluate the central question of this essay: Does Richard Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics successfully avoid the necessity of metaphysical transcendence?

The answer is complex and demands a twofold judgment. 

**First, in terms of *traditional* metaphysics, Rorty’s project is an unqualified success.** Rorty successfully dismantles the architecture of onto-theology, foundationalism, and representationalism. He liberates philosophy from the illusion that we must ground our ethical and epistemic claims in a God’s-eye view, an immutable human nature, or a mind-independent mirror of reality. By showing that vocabularies are historical tools and that justification is a social practice, Rorty closes the book on the metaphysical quest for absolute certitude. In this respect, his displacement of ontology by cultural politics is a liberating, anti-authoritarian maneuver that redirects philosophical energy away from sterile scholastic debates and toward concrete human problems.

**Second, however, in terms of *absolute* immanentism, Rorty’s project fails.** Rorty’s attempt to reduce everything to cultural politics and social practice ultimately founders on two fronts: the internal structural tension of his own pragmatic teleology, and the ontological reality of the given as illuminated by Sheehan’s Heidegger.

1. **The Teleological Trap:** As demonstrated in Part III, Rorty cannot sustain his cultural politics without smuggling in a substantive, universalistic normative standard—William James’s standard of human happiness and the minimization of cruelty. By making this standard the structural end of social action, Rorty covertly reinstates a transcendental horizon. He treats human suffering not merely as one local conversational preference among others, but as a non-negotiable moral imperative. When Rorty insists that solidarity is our highest duty, he is speaking the language of secularized theology. He has expelled metaphysics through the front door, only to let it back in through the back window disguised as humanistic ethics.

2. **The Ontological Excess:** As demonstrated through Brandom’s taxonomy and Sheehan’s critique, the reduction of all epistemic and ontological authority to social practice suppresses the dimension of *givenness*. Brandom attempts to show that even *Vorhandensein* is a social-discursive achievement, but this leaves open the nagging question: Why do our practices break down in the ways they do? Why does the hammer break? Why do our best scientific vocabularies run up against anomalies that force us to revolutionize our conceptual frameworks? 

The pragmatist answers: "Because it is instrumentally useful to do so." But this is circular. The resistance of the real—the sheer brute fact that things push back against our desires, our theories, and our social projects—cannot be fully explained as a mere internal artifact of normative scorekeeping or linguistic fashion. There is an ontological excess, a *tertium quid*, that transcends the social. As Sheehan’s reading of the *Kehre* shows, *Dasein* is thrown into a clearing that it does not own. The *Es gibt*—the "there is" of givenness—precedes every human vocabulary, every community of language-users, and every cultural-political negotiation.

To reduce this ontological excess entirely to cultural politics is to commit a new form of dogmatism: an *anthropocentric dogmatism* or *sociological reductionism* that treats the human social sphere as the absolute horizon of all possible meaning. Ironically, this makes Rorty vulnerable to the very critique he leveled against foundationalism. Just as traditional metaphysics hypostatized God or Substance into an absolute ground, pragmatic immanentism hypostatizes "the social conversation" into an absolute ground. It replaces the God of onto-theology with the conversational community of liberal pragmatists.

---

## Conclusion

Richard Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics was one of the most provocative and culturally significant philosophical gestures of the late twentieth century. By wedding pragmatism to historicism, Rorty sought to cure philosophy of its metaphysical nostalgia and to reorient thought toward the urgent, practical tasks of human freedom and solidarity.

Yet, as we have seen through our engagement with Robert Brandom’s normative reading of Heidegger and Thomas Sheehan’s radical interpretation of the *Kehre*, Rorty’s immanentist horizon is inherently unstable. Brandom brilliantly demonstrates how ontological authority can be translated into the grammar of social practices, but in doing so, he exposes the peril of losing the world to the community. Rorty’s simultaneous rejection of a background and his reliance on William James’s standard of human happiness reveals a deep structural contradiction: an unacknowledged dependence on a transcendental normative telos that cannot be justified by historicist pragmatism alone.

Finally, Sheehan’s reading of Heidegger’s *Ereignis* reminds us that the sheer givenness of entities—the irreducible *Sache selbst* that erupts into our lives, resists our vocabularies, and conditions our practices—cannot be dissolved into cultural politics. There is a *tertium quid*, an ontological excess that transcends the social and reminds us that human beings are not the masters of the clearing, but its guests.

In the end, Rorty’s cultural politics does not successfully avoid the necessity of metaphysical transcendence; rather, it displaces the transcendent from the heavens to the community, and from the nature of things to the pragmatic horizon of human flourishing. But the real—in its recalcitrance, its givenness, and its silent refusal to be completely spoken for—continually breaches the boundaries of our cultural politics, whispering that the conversation is never quite as all-encompassing as we pretend it to be.
