# The Spatiotemporal Knot: Tugushev, Tolstoy, and the Metaphysics of Freedom in *War and Peace*

## I. Introduction: The Epistemological Impasse of Historical Necessity

In the second epilogue of *War and Peace*, Leo Tolstoy attempts nothing less than the execution of the traditional concept of free will, substituting for it an uncompromising metaphysics of historical determinism. Armed with a *reductio ad absurdum* argument, Tolstoy seeks to demonstrate that the attribution of human action to an uncaused, spontaneous will—an absolute freedom detached from physical and historical antecedents—leads to logical incoherence. For Tolstoy, to posit a free act is to sever the causal chain, thereby rendering history unintelligible and reducing human agency to a miraculous disruption of the fabric of reality. 

Yet, Tolstoy’s deterministic architecture rests upon a foundational metaphysical presupposition: the "totality of being"—a seamless, continuous block universe wherein every event is inexorably bound by immutable laws of cause, time, and space. Against this monistic totality, contemporary philosopher Nikolay Tugushev has launched a sophisticated critique. Tugushev argues that Tolstoy’s *reductio* is not a devastating strike against human freedom, but rather an artifact of a flawed metaphysical imagination—specifically, the conflation of determinism with the totality of being. By rejecting this absolute totality, Tugushev attempts to clear a conceptual space for freedom. 

However, this maneuver invites an acute philosophical peril. If one rejects the totality of being to escape determinism, how does one avoid falling into the abyss of indeterminism? How can freedom be rescued from the clutches of physical necessity without collapsing into a mystical, self-caused force—a *causa sui*—that emerges ex nihilo, defying both reason and natural law? 

This essay will evaluate Tugushev’s critique of Tolstoy’s *reductio ad absurdum* argument. I shall argue that while Tugushev successfully exposes the limits of Tolstoy’s mechanistic ontology, his attempt to circumvent the deterministic conclusion remains perpetually vulnerable to the specter of absolute spontaneity. To resolve this tension, we must look beyond the crude binary of Tolstoy’s block universe and Tugushev’s fractured ontology, proposing instead a relational, emergent model of agency that locates freedom not in an escape from the totality of being, but in the internal articulation of its differentiated structures.

## II. Tolstoy’s *Reductio Ad Absurdum*: The Mechanics of Inevitability

To understand Tugushev’s intervention, we must first reconstruct the architecture of Tolstoy’s deterministic assault in the second epilogue of *War and Peace*. Tolstoy approaches the problem of free will not as an abstract metaphysical puzzle, but as an empirical and historical crisis. If historians continue to attribute the movements of nations to the free decisions of individual commanders like Napoleon or Alexander, history remains an unscientific collection of miraculous accidents. To elevate history to a science, Tolstoy insists, we must abandon the chimera of free will and recognize that human actions are subject to invariable laws.

Tolstoy’s *reductio ad absurdum* proceeds in stages, targeting the common-sense intuition that we are conscious of being free. He concedes that if we isolate a single human action and view it through the lens of subjective consciousness, it *appears* to be free. I can raise my arm, walk to the window, or choose to write a sentence. In this micro-perspective, no external constraint forces my hand. 

However, Tolstoy argues that this subjective experience of freedom is an optical illusion born of epistemic limitation. We experience freedom only because our ignorance of the true causes of our actions prevents us from perceiving the causal chain. If we expand our field of vision—incorporating the preceding psychological states, physiological conditions, environmental stimuli, and historical context—the illusion of spontaneity begins to dissolve. 

The *reductio* reaches its apex when Tolstoy applies this logic to the macro-level of history. Let us assume, for the sake of argument, that human beings possess absolute free will. If a single individual possesses the capacity to act entirely independently of physical causes, hereditary dispositions, and environmental pressures, then that individual's actions are utterly unpredictable and lawless. Scale this up to millions of people interacting in a historical event, such as the invasion of Russia in 1812. If each soldier, general, and peasant acts through an uncaused, spontaneous exercise of free will, historical events become a chaotic aggregate of miraculous interruptions. 

Tolstoy writes:
> "To suppose that man can be free while his action is subject to a law is to suppose that two and two make not four, but five."

For Tolstoy, freedom and law are mutually exclusive. If human actions are lawful, they are not free; if they are free, they are lawless and unintelligible. Because human actions *are* intelligible and exhibit regular patterns when viewed at scale, they must be governed by laws. Therefore, the concept of free will is a logical absurdity—a superstitious remnant of a pre-scientific age that must be discarded in favor of historical necessity.

Crucially, Tolstoy’s argument relies on a specific metaphysical substrate: the totality of being. For Tolstoy, reality is an exhaustive, closed continuum. There are no ontological gaps, no loose threads, and no empty spaces where an uncaused will could insert its causal leverage. Every event is inextricably linked to an antecedent cause, stretching backward into the infinite past, and bound to a consequent effect stretching forward into the future. Within this seamless web of being, an uncaused act of free will is not merely an impossibility; it is an ontological monstrosity—an event without a parent, a breach in the inviolable continuity of the cosmos.

## III. Tugushev’s Critique: Dismantling the Totality of Being

It is precisely this metaphysical substrate—the totality of being as a closed, deterministic continuum—that Nikolay Tugushev targets in his critique. Tugushev argues that Tolstoy’s *reductio* is not a universally valid logical demonstration, but a rhetorical sleight-of-hand that derives its persuasive power entirely from its initial, unexamined premise: the assumption that reality constitutes a singular, all-encompassing totality wherein every part is rigidly predetermined by the whole.

Tugushev contends that Tolstoy commits a profound category mistake by treating the ontological totality of the universe as a closed, mechanical system that dictates the behavior of all its constituents in a strictly linear, downward fashion. In doing so, Tolstoy reduces human consciousness and agency to mere epiphenomena—passive reflections of an underlying causal machinery. 

To dismantle Tolstoy’s argument, Tugushev challenges the coherence of the "totality of being" itself. Drawing upon contemporary developments in ontology and philosophy of mind, Tugushev argues that being is not a monolithic block, but a stratified, plurivocal domain characterized by ontological discontinuity and emergence. When Tolstoy asserts that every event must have an antecedent cause within the totality, he assumes that causality operates uniformly across all levels of reality in a deterministic register. 

Tugushev counters that causality is pluralistic. Physical, biological, psychological, and social strata of reality possess their own distinct modes of organization and causal efficacy. More importantly, Tugushev rejects the idea that the totality of being is a completed, closed book. By introducing the notion of ontological openness—an idea resonant with the philosophies of Henri Bergson, Nikolai Berdyaev, and process philosophy—Tugushev argues that the future does not pre-exist in the past like a coiled spring. The totality of being is continually coming into being; it is an unfinished symphony rather than a static crystal.

By rejecting Tolstoy’s presupposition of the totality of being, Tugushev seeks to undermine the foundation of the *reductio ad absurdum*. If reality is not a closed, deterministic continuum, then the claim that an uncaused or self-determined act disrupts the causal fabric of the universe loses its sting. An act of freedom does not need to shatter the laws of a rigid totality if those laws themselves are emergent, relational, and incomplete. 

Furthermore, Tugushev argues that Tolstoy’s deterministic framework suffers from a performative contradiction. Tolstoy writes *War and Peace* as a moral and artistic appeal to his readers, urging them to abandon their illusions of pride and recognize the humility of historical necessity. Yet, if determinism is absolute, the reader’s apprehension of Tolstoy’s argument, and Tolstoy’s own act of writing the book, are merely predetermined outcomes of physical and historical forces. To argue for determinism is to presuppose a rational subject capable of evaluating evidence, weighing arguments, and freely altering its beliefs—an intellectual freedom that Tolstoy’s own thesis explicitly denies. Tugushev exposes this paradox: Tolstoy must smuggle in a clandestine form of cognitive agency in order to persuade us that agency does not exist.

## IV. The Metaphysical Precipice: The Specter of *Causa Sui*

Yet, while Tugushev’s critique successfully exposes the dogmatic underpinnings of Tolstoy’s monistic determinism, it brings us to a perilous philosophical precipice. The central challenge facing any critique of determinism is the avoidance of the *causa sui*—the self-caused cause that arises ex nihilo. 

If we reject Tolstoy’s totality of being, and with it the rigid causal continuity that binds every event to its antecedents, we are immediately confronted with a terrifying metaphysical void. If an act is not determined by prior causes within the totality, what determines it? If nothing determines it, then the act appears to be random, arbitrary, and disconnected from the agent’s character, desires, and reasons. This is the classic dilemma of free will, often framed as the incompatibility problem: 
1. If determinism is true, an action is necessitated by prior causes, and we are not free.
2. If indeterminism is true, an action is uncaused (or probabilistic), and we are not the authors of our actions; it is merely a matter of chance.

In both horns of the dilemma, moral responsibility and genuine agency evaporate. If Tugushev rejects Tolstoy’s determinism by dissolving the closed totality of being, does he not fall directly onto the second horn of the dilemma? Does his rejection of the causal continuum force him to embrace an atomistic, spontaneous will that erupts out of nothingness like a ghost in the machine?

Tugushev is keenly aware of this danger. He recognizes that substituting a crude indeterminism for Tolstoy’s rigid determinism is no victory at all. An agent whose choices are the result of random quantum fluctuations or inexplicable spontaneous eruptions is no more free, in any meaningful moral or psychological sense, than an automaton bound by clockwork laws. Both models reduce human agency to a non-rational event—either the blind execution of a mechanical program or the equally blind roll of cosmic dice.

The crux of the evaluative question is this: **Can Tugushev’s rejection of the totality of being successfully circumvent the deterministic conclusion *without* reducing free will to a spontaneous, self-caused force arising out of nothingness?**

To answer this in the affirmative, Tugushev must articulate a middle way—an ontology of freedom that avoids both the totalizing closure of Tolstoy’s determinism and the atomistic void of libertarian indeterminism. If Tugushev fails to provide this middle way, his critique remains merely negative, a deconstruction of Tolstoy’s errors that leaves us stranded in a philosophical vacuum.

## V. Reconstructing Tugushev’s Middle Way: Relational Emergence and Agency

To rescue Tugushev’s critique from the charge of lapsing into a mystical *causa sui*, we must reconstruct and extend his implicit ontology. Tugushev’s rejection of the totality of being does not entail a universe of disconnected, chaotic fragments. Rather, it entails a shift from a *substantivalist* ontology (where reality is made of pre-existing, immutable building blocks governed by external laws) to a *relational and emergent* ontology.

In an emergentist framework, higher-level properties and capacities—such as human consciousness, deliberation, and intentionality—arise from lower-level physical and biological processes, but they are not reducible to them. Once these higher-level capacities emerge, they possess genuine causal efficacy of their own (*downward causation* or *emergent causation*). 

Applied to the problem of free will and Tolstoy’s determinism, this emergentist ontology fundamentally alters the terms of the debate. Tolstoy’s *reductio* assumes a flat, reductionist universe where an action must either be traced down to physical necessity (determinism) or treated as a miraculous exception to it (indeterminism). Emergence shatters this flatland. 

When a human agent makes a choice in *War and Peace*—such as Kutuzov deciding to abandon Moscow, or Pierre deciding to remain in the burning city—that choice is not an uncaused event arising out of nothingness, nor is it a mere epiphenomenal gear turning within a pre-calculated mechanical clockwork. It is the synthesis of a complex, stratified history of biological, psychological, social, and moral interactions. 

Crucially, this choice is *free* not because it lacks causes, but because the primary locus of its causation is the integrated, reflective self of the agent, acting as a unified emergent totality. The agent gathers the dispersed threads of memory, desire, moral conviction, and environmental constraint, and *synthesizes* them into a novel course of action. The act is caused, but it is caused by *the agent as a whole*, rather than by external physical forces acting upon the agent as a passive conduit.

This dissolves Tolstoy’s objection that freedom requires an impossible rupture in the causal chain. Freedom does not require a break in causality; it requires a *transformation in the type of causality* at play. Physical causation is external, transitive, and deterministic; personal, agentic causation is internal, reflective, and teleological. 

By rejecting Tolstoy’s closed totality of being, Tugushev opens the door to this non-reductive, stratified universe. In this universe, the future is not a pre-written script, nor is it a chaotic void. It is a field of genuine possibilities that are actualized through the participatory agency of conscious beings. The agent does not create actions out of nothingness (ex nihilo); rather, the agent crafts new configurations of meaning and action out of the raw materials of their embodied existence.

## VI. Dialogue with Tolstoy: The Case of Kutuzov and Pierre

To test the viability of Tugushev’s anti-totalizing critique against Tolstoy’s own literary universe, we must examine how *War and Peace* itself quietly subverts Tolstoy’s explicit philosophical pronouncements. It is one of the great ironies of Tolstoy’s masterpiece that his artistic practice continually rebels against his dogmatic determinism. 

Consider the contrasting figures of Napoleon and Kutuzov, whom Tolstoy uses to illustrate his theory of history. Napoleon, consumed by rationalistic hubris, believes he is the absolute master of events, issuing commands that direct the grand machinery of war. Tolstoy mercilessly exposes Napoleon’s belief as an absurdity; Napoleon is a puppet dancing on strings he cannot see, his grand strategies thwarted by the chaotic, emergent reality of battle. 

Kutuzov, on the other hand, embodies Tolstoy’s ideal of historical wisdom. Kutuzov does not issue grand, deterministic commands; he waits, listens, observes, and aligns himself with the subterranean current of events. He recognizes that battles are not won by abstract plans, but by the elusive, incalculable spirit of the troops—the *morale* of the army. 

Tolstoy interprets Kutuzov’s behavior as proof of determinism: Kutuzov is merely a passive instrument of historical necessity, wise enough not to swim against the current. Yet, a closer reading through Tugushev’s lens reveals a profound misinterpretation on Tolstoy’s part. Kutuzov’s passivity is not mechanical submission; it is a refined form of *active receptivity* and strategic discernment. Kutuzov exercises a high degree of practical wisdom (*phronesis*). He weighs complex, ambiguous situations, makes deliberate choices about when to engage and when to retreat, and takes moral responsibility for the outcomes. 

When Kutuzov makes the fateful decision to abandon Moscow without a fight—a decision condemned by the court, the generals, and standard military logic—he is not acting as a mindless gear in a deterministic machine. He is performing an act of radical moral courage that runs counter to the prevailing pressures of his environment. If Tolstoy’s determinism were absolute, Kutuzov’s decision would be entirely predictable from physical and historical antecedents. Yet, to contemporaries, it appeared as an astonishing, singular act of individual intuition that altered the course of European history. 

Similarly, consider Pierre Bezukhov’s spiritual transformation. Pierre’s journey from a bewildered, dissolute intellectual to a man of grounded, ethical agency is a narrative of breaking free from the paralyzing grip of deterministic despair. In the prison camp, Pierre encounters Platon Karataev, the embodiment of unreflective unity with the natural world. But Pierre does not remain a Karataev-like automaton; he synthesizes Karataev’s instinctive wisdom with his own intellectual self-consciousness, emerging as an agent capable of moral choice, sacrifice, and love. 

In both Kutuzov and Pierre, Tolstoy’s narrative portrays agents who are neither puppets of a totalizing determinism nor miraculous creators of actions ex nihilo. They inhabit the exact middle ground that Tugushev’s ontology demands: agents whose freedom is forged within a relational, open-ended world through reflection, moral struggle, and participation in the unfolding of history.

---

## VII. Conclusion: Toward an Open Ontology of Freedom

Nikolay Tugushev’s critique of Leo Tolstoy’s *reductio ad absurdum* argument marks a crucial intervention in the philosophy of action and historical determinism. By identifying and rejecting Tolstoy’s foundational metaphysical presupposition—the closed, monistic totality of being—Tugushev successfully unmasks the dogmatic core of absolute determinism. He demonstrates that Tolstoy’s rejection of free will is not a self-evident logical necessity, but the consequence of a flattened, mechanistic ontology that reduces human agency to epiphenomenal illusion.

However, Tugushev’s critique also brings us face-to-face with the ultimate peril of anti-deterministic thought: the specter of the *causa sui*, the irrational plunge into spontaneous indeterminism. To prevent this collapse, we have argued that Tugushev’s metaphysical rejection of the totality of being must be completed by an emergentist, relational ontology. Freedom is neither a miraculous disruption of the cosmos nor a mechanical output of physical laws. It is an emergent capacity of embodied, conscious agents who navigate an open, unfinished universe.

In the final analysis, *War and Peace* remains a magnificent battlefield where these philosophical forces clash. Tolstoy the philosopher may demand that we bow before the altar of historical necessity, but Tolstoy the novelist paints a world alive with the agonizing, beautiful, and irrepressible reality of human freedom. By dismantling the totalizing cage of determinism, Tugushev allows us to step back into that open world—not as gods creating ex nihilo, nor as puppets dancing on strings, but as genuine authors of our own shared history.
