# Reason in the Service of the Passions: Evaluating Humean Sentimentalism through the Aristotelian Lens

## Introduction

Moral philosophy has long wrestled with the tension between cognition and affect—between the cool, calculating deliverances of reason and the warm, immediate pulls of feeling. In the modern philosophical landscape, this tension manifests vividly in the debate between rationalist accounts of morality (which trace their lineage from Plato and Kant) and sentimentalist accounts (which find their most formidable articulation in the writings of David Hume). Hume’s revolutionary inversion of the traditional hierarchy of the mind—famously declaring that "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them"—dislodged rationality from its throne as the arbiter of right and wrong. For Hume, moral distinctions are not derived from abstract contemplation of eternal verities or logical deductions concerning fitness and unfitness, but arise from an internal sentiment of approbation or disapprobation produced by an imaginative mechanism he calls "sympathy."

Yet, this sentimentalist framework invites a battery of rigorous objections. Among the most pressing is the charge that sympathy, as a purely affective and imaginative conduit, lacks intrinsic value judgments and fails to generate the universal, overriding obligations characteristic of genuine moral experience. Without objective rational constraints, pure sentiment risks sliding into subjectivism, cultural relativism, or—more dangerously—validating harmful inclinations and malicious desires. A sentiment-based ethics appears vulnerable to distortions of perspective, parochial biases, and pathologies of feeling, such as a misguided sympathy that valorizes acts of vengeance or cruel retribution. 

This essay will evaluate David Hume’s sentimentalist account of moral reasoning against these formidable objections. It will analyze how the absence of rational constraints within pure sentiment exposes moral judgment to arbitrariness and malicious motivational capture. Finally, it will assess whether incorporating Aristotle’s notion of *ethos* (habituated character) and rational control (*phronesis*) can resolve the vulnerabilities of emotion-based ethics, while simultaneously maintaining that affective feeling remains an indispensable, non-negotiable motivational engine of moral action. By weaving together Humean psychology and Aristotelian virtue theory, we can construct a robust meta-ethical framework that preserves the motivational vitality of the passions while anchoring moral obligation in a normative reality disciplined by reason and habituation.

## Part I: The Architecture of Humean Sentimentalism

To understand the force of the objections against Hume, one must first grasp the elegant and radical architecture of his moral philosophy, primarily set forth in Book III of *A Treatise of Human Nature* and later refined in *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*. Hume initiates his inquiry with a direct assault on moral rationalism, which he targets using his famous fork and his arguments concerning motivation. 

Hume observes that actions can be laudable or blameworthy, but they cannot be said to be *true* or *false*. Truth and falsehood consist in the agreement or disagreement of ideas either to real relations of ideas or to real existence. Morality, however, has an intrinsic and powerful influence on human actions and affections. Reason alone, being merely the discovery of truth or falsehood, can never inspire or prevent an action; it is entirely inert. As Hume writes, "Reason is perfectly inactive, and can never be the source of so active a principle as conscience, or a sense of morals." Therefore, because morals have an influence on the actions and affections, it follows that they cannot be derived from reason alone. The active force that moves us to act must stem from the passions—those internal impressions that arise immediately from the soul, or from the secondary impressions derived from them, such as desires, aversions, hope, fear, grief, and joy.

If reason does not discover moral qualities, where do they come from? Hume’s answer is that moral distinctions are derived from a sentimental internal response. An action, trait of character, or mental quality is deemed virtuous if it excites in a suitably positioned spectator a particular kind of pleasure, and vicious if it excites a pain. This sentiment of approbation or disapprobation is not a cold intellectual judgment, but an affective impression.

However, a critical problem immediately confronts any purely subjective theory of sentiments: human beings are notoriously self-interested, fickle, and partial. If my moral approval of an action were entirely dependent upon how it affects *me* personally, morality would disintegrate into a chaotic war of subjective preferences. My enemy’s misfortune would be my joy; my neighbor’s prosperity might be my envy. To rescue morality from this solipsistic mire, Hume introduces the crucial mechanism of **sympathy**.

Sympathy, for Hume, is not a synonym for modern compassion or pity; rather, it is a psychological and communicative mechanism by which we bridge the epistemological gap between our own minds and the minds of others. Hume argues that all human beings share a similar fundamental mental and emotional constitution. When we observe the signs of a passion in another person—their facial expressions, tone of voice, posture, or the surrounding circumstances—the idea of that passion is enlivened within our own minds by a species of conversion, transforming the idea into an impression. We literally come to *feel* a faint echo of what the other person is feeling. 

Yet, if sympathy were unfettered, it would remain hopelessly parochial. We naturally feel greater sympathy for those who are close to us—physically, temporally, or relationally—than for total strangers on the other side of the globe. To correct for this natural partiality and to achieve stable, communicable moral judgments, Hume argues that we must adopt what he terms a **"general point of view"** (or the "common point of view"). 

When we evaluate characters and actions morally, we abstract from our particular, transient situation and project ourselves into a general perspective. We consider the quality of a person's character not with respect to how it affects *us* here and now, but with respect to its general tendency to promote the happiness or utility of the agent and those associated with them. Through the operation of sympathy combined with this general perspective, we feel a calm, disinterested sentiment of approbation toward traits that are useful or agreeable (such as benevolence, justice, industry, and discretion).

## Part II: The Objections from Internal Value Judgments and Universal Obligation

Despite the sophisticated scaffolding of the general point of view and the mechanism of sympathy, Hume’s sentimentalism faces severe, persistent objections. Two of the most damaging are that **sympathy lacks internal value judgments**, and that **it fails to provide universal, overriding obligations**.

### The Absence of Internal Value Judgments
The first objection targets the cognitive poverty of mere sentiment. When Hume reduces moral approbation to an "impression" or "feeling" of pleasure or pain, critics argue that he evacuates moral claims of their semantic and normative content. To say "Charity is good" or "Cruelty is wrong," on a strict sentimentalist reading, is ultimately to say nothing more than "Charity causes a pleasant feeling in my breast when viewed from a general point of view, whereas cruelty causes an unpleasant one." 

This formulation encounters the famous **Euthyphro dilemma** recast for sentimentalism: Is an action virtuous because it causes a sentiment of approbation, or does it cause a sentiment of approbation because it is virtuous? If the former, morality becomes completely arbitrary. There is no independent standard of moral rightness external to the shifting, contingent emotional responses of human observers. If human emotional constitutions were wired differently—if, say, torturing the innocent caused universal delight—then, under a pure sentimentalist framework, torture would *be* virtuous. 

Critics like Richard Price and, later, intuitionists and rationalists, argue that moral judgments possess an *internal truth-value* and claim a mind-independent reality. When we judge an act to be unjust, we do not merely register an internal visceral tickle of displeasure; we perceive a normative reality that commands our assent. Because sympathy is merely an affective conduit—a psychological mechanism for transmitting passions—it is blind. It transmits joy, grief, anger, or ambition indifferently, without evaluating the rational legitimacy of those passions. Sympathy makes us *feel* with others, but it does not tell us *whether* we ought to feel with them, nor does it provide an internal standard to distinguish between a noble passion and a degraded one.

### The Failure of Universal Obligation
Closely allied to the first objection is the problem of obligation. A normative moral system must be able to answer the Socratic question: *Why should I be moral?* Or, more precisely, why should moral requirements override my personal desires, whims, or prudential calculations?

For Kant and rationalist philosophers, moral obligations are categorical. They bind rational agents universally and unconditionally, simply in virtue of their rationality. If an action is morally required, it commands obedience regardless of whether the agent happens to feel sympathetic toward the affected parties.

Hume’s sentimentalism struggles to ground such universal, overriding obligations. Because moral distinctions are founded upon sentiments of pleasure and pain, and these sentiments are variable across individuals, cultures, and epochs, the resulting "obligations" possess no absolute stringency. Hume attempts to bridge this gap by arguing that virtue is generally in an agent’s long-term interest and that vice brings internal turmoil and social ostracism. Yet, as critics from his own day (such as Henry Home, Lord Kames) to the present have noted, this reduces moral obligation to a species of enlightened self-interest or aesthetic preference. 

What happens when an individual lacks the requisite sympathetic sensitivity? What if a callous person derives no pleasure from the happiness of others and feels no pain at their oppression? Within a Humean framework, one cannot logically *compel* this person through reason to be moral, because reason is inert and cannot generate a new passion. One can only appeal to their existing passions or attempt to cultivate their sentiments. But if they remain impervious, the moral claim loses its grip. Sympathy, being a contingent psychological capacity rather than a rational requirement of agency, cannot secure universal moral obligation. It binds only those who happen to be so constituted as to feel its pull.

## Part III: The Pathology of Sentiment – Malicious Desires and Misguided Sympathy

The vulnerabilities of a purely sentiment-based ethics are nowhere more apparent than in its susceptibility to emotional distortions, partisan biases, and the validation of harmful inclinations. Without the rigorous architectonic constraints of objective reason, pure sentiment can easily be hijacked by dark, destructive passions.

### Misguided Sympathy and Vengeance
Consider the phenomenon of **misguided sympathy** or partisan emotional capture. Sympathy is notoriously discriminatory. As noted earlier, human beings naturally sympathize more intensely with those who share our tribal identity, our religious affiliations, our political factions, or our cultural narratives. 

When a group is gripped by collective rage, grievance, or a desire for retribution, sympathy operates in a viciously insular loop. Imagine a community inflamed by a perceived insult or crime committed by an out-group member. An individual within this community feels the burning anger and desire for vengeance of their peers. Through Hume’s mechanism of sympathy, this passion is rapidly amplified and transmitted throughout the collective. The members of the mob feel an intense, cohesive solidarity; they experience a powerful emotional resonance—a collective "sentiment of approbation"—toward acts of violent retaliation against the offender.

From the internal perspective of the participants, this dynamic matches many of the formal criteria of Humean moral approval: it generates a shared feeling of harmony, fortifies social cohesion within the in-group, and satisfies the psychological passions of the actors. Yet, viewed from an objective, rational standpoint, it is a catastrophic moral failure—a lynching or a blood feud fueled by perverted affect. 

Can Hume’s "general point of view" rescue the system here? Hume would argue that when the spectators adopt the general point of view, they abstract from their immediate factional passions and evaluate the long-term utility of vengeance, realizing that blood feuds undermine social stability, commerce, and security. Therefore, the general point of view would condemn the lynching.

However, critics argue that the general point of view is itself infected by the psychological limitations of the observers who construct it. Who defines what counts as "utility" or "general welfare"? In deeply prejudiced societies, the "general point of view" of the dominant class routinely excludes marginalized groups from the calculus of sympathy. For centuries, slaveholders, colonizers, and patriarchal societies maintained internal "general points of view" that justified slavery, subjugation, and systemic cruelty because their sympathies were culturally conditioned to register the humanity only of their own caste. If sympathy is the ultimate foundation, and sympathy is plastic, historically contingent, and easily manipulated by ideology, then the general point of view becomes a mirror reflecting the prejudices of the age rather than an independent tribunal of justice.

### The Validation of Harmful Inclinations
Furthermore, pure sentiment lacks the internal conceptual tools to declare an existing desire *irrational* or *baseless*, provided it does not directly conflict with immediate social utility. If an individual possesses a subtle, refined inclination toward aesthetic cruelty or manipulative dominance—traits that might be masked with charm and thus elicit pleasure in social interactions—Humean sentiment struggles to condemn them unless their destructive social consequences are glaringly obvious.

More profoundly, human beings frequently harbor deep-seated psychological drives toward self-destruction, domination, and malice. Kant rightly argued that inclinations are blind and variable; to allow them to dictate the moral law is to abandon morality to the vagaries of nature. If our emotional machinery is corrupted by trauma, ideology, or evolutionary misfires, a sentimentalist ethics risks canonizing pathology as virtue. Without a rational standard that stands *over and against* our current emotional states—a standard that can interrogate, critique, and veto our feelings—we are left defenseless against the tyranny of unexamined passions.

## Part IV: The Aristotelian Correction – Character, Habituation, and Rational Control

Are the flaws of Humean sentimentalism fatal, or can they be remedied by integrating insights from other ethical traditions? To rescue emotion-based ethics from its vulnerabilities without sacrificing the vital motivational role of the passions, we must turn to **Aristotle**.

Aristotle’s virtue ethics offers a powerful bridge between the affective psychology prized by Hume and the rational constraints demanded by critics. For Aristotle, morality is neither a purely intellectual exercise nor a chaotic storm of unguided feelings; it is the cultivation of a stable **character (*ethos*)** through habituation, guided and structured by practical wisdom (**phronesis**).

### The Harmony of Reason and Passion
Unlike Kant, who often treats reason and emotion as locked in perpetual warfare (where the passions are recalcitrant rebels that must be forcibly subjugated by the categorical imperative), Aristotle views the soul as an integrated whole. The soul is divided into rational and irrational parts, but the irrational part is further subdivided into the vegetative (which is non-rational) and the **appetitive or desiderative (*orexis*)**, which is capable of *listening* to and obeying reason.

In the Aristotelian framework, a virtuous person does not experience a painful internal conflict between what they *want* to do (desire) and what they *ought* to do (reason). Rather, their passions have been educated and aligned with reason. As Aristotle famously writes in the *Nicomachean Ethics*:

> "Virtue, then, being of two kinds, intellectual and moral, intellectual virtue owes its birth and its growth to teaching (for which reason it requires experience and time), while moral virtue comes about as a result of habit... Neither of the moral virtues, then, arises in us by nature; for nothing that exists by nature can form a habit contrary to its nature."

Herein lies the profound solution to the Humean dilemma. Hume rightly noted that reason alone is inert and that passions are necessary for action. Aristotle agrees: mere knowledge of the good (Platonic intellectualism) is insufficient to make a person good. We must *feel* the appropriate emotions at the right times, toward the right objects, for the right motives, and in the right manner. This is what Aristotle calls having the passions *well-conditioned*.

### Habituation (*Ethos*) and Rational Oversight (*Phronesis*)
How are the passions brought into this harmonious state? Through **habituation (*ethos*)**. Just as a person becomes a builder by building or a harpist by playing the harp, so we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, and courageous by doing courageous acts. 

In the early stages of moral development, children and novices do not act from a fully mature, rational understanding of the Good. They act under the guidance of parents, teachers, and laws. They perform virtuous actions repeatedly until these actions become second nature—habits encoded into the affective structure of their personality. 

However, habituation alone is not enough, for blind habit can degenerate into dogmatism or cultural conformity. This is where **phronesis** (practical wisdom) enters. Phronesis is an intellectual virtue of the practical soul. It is the capacity to deliberate well about what is conducive to the good life as a whole, to perceive the salient moral features of concrete, particular situations, and to determine the appropriate mean between excess and deficiency.

Crucially, **phronesis provides the objective rational constraint that pure Humean sentiment lacks.** While Humean sympathy responds fluidly to whatever passions happen to be present, Aristotelian practical wisdom actively evaluates, shapes, and directs the emotions. Reason, in the Aristotelian tradition, is not a mere "slave" serving whatever master passion happens to dominate; rather, it is a statesman ruling over free citizens (the passions) who have a voice and must be persuaded, but whose ultimate direction is guided by a rational vision of human flourishing (*eudaimonia*).

## Part V: Synthesizing Hume and Aristotle – A Robust Ethical Framework

By bringing Hume and Aristotle into constructive dialogue, we can formulate a comprehensive meta-ethical synthesis that corrects the vulnerabilities of pure sentimentalism while retaining its profound psychological insights.

### 1. Reclaiming the Motivational Necessity of Feeling
Hume’s greatest contribution to moral philosophy is his unassailable insight regarding human motivation: cognition alone cannot move us. To know that an action is mathematically optimal or logically consistent does not provide a reason to perform it unless one possesses a antecedent desire or care for that outcome. 

Incorporating this into an Aristotelian-Humean synthesis means recognizing that **emotions are cognitive-affective appraisals**. Unlike Hume, who sometimes treats passions as raw, blind impressions, or modern empiricists who view them as mere visceral reactions, contemporary virtue ethicists (following Aristotle and modern cognitive psychologists like Martha Nussbaum) view emotions as containing *judgments* or *beliefs* about the world. When I feel anger, my emotion embodies the belief that I have been unjustly wronged. When I feel compassion, it embodies the belief that someone is undeservedly suffering.

Thus, feelings are not external to moral reasoning; they are its vehicle. Sentiment is the way we *register* value. Rationality without sentiment is empty; sentiment without rationality is blind.

### 2. Imposing Normative Constraints on Sympathy
How does this synthesis solve the problem of misguided sympathy, such as the vengeance mob or culturally conditioned prejudice? 

Under a pure Humean model, if the community’s sympathetic resonance aligns in favor of vengeance, the action risks receiving moral approbation. But under our **synthesized Aristotelian-Humean framework**, sympathy is subjected to the tribunal of *phronesis* and universalizable norms of human flourishing (*eudaimonia*).

1. **Educated Perception:** Sympathy is not left as a raw, untutored instinct. Through moral education and habituation, our capacity for sympathy is trained to respond to genuine human needs rather than tribal animosities or superficial markers of identity. We learn *who* and *what* to sympathize with.
2. **Rational Oversight:** When a passionate impulse—such as the desire for vengeance—arises, practical wisdom intervenes. Phronesis asks: *Does this action promote genuine human flourishing? Is it consistent with the virtues of justice and temperance?* If the emotion is disproportionate, unjust, or destructive to the objective requirements of a well-ordered life, it is judged to be defective, and the agent resists it. The agent’s affective response is brought into alignment with the objective mean.
3. **Universality and Objectivity:** Unlike pure sentimentalism, which struggles to escape relativism, this synthesis anchors moral obligation in the objective teleology of human nature. While cultures and individuals differ in their emotional expressions, the constitutive conditions of human flourishing—rational agency, social cooperation, friendship, health, and emotional stability—provide an objective, non-arbitrary standard against which sentiments and practices can be critically evaluated. Slavery is not wrong merely because it happens to cause an unpleasant feeling in contemporary observers; it is wrong because it systematically thwarts the rational agency and flourishing proper to human beings, a truth that reason apprehends and habituation learns to honor.

### 3. Re-evaluating Obligation
What about the objection that sentimentalism fails to provide universal, overriding obligations? 

By integrating Aristotelian character and rational oversight, we provide a robust answer to the Socratic question. Why should I be moral? Because morality is not merely a matter of satisfying transient emotional preferences or securing social approval; it is the realization of my highest potential as a rational, social animal. 

Obligation in this framework is both **internal** and **external**. It is internal because the virtuous person *wants* to do what is right—their passions are harmonized with reason, making moral action a source of deep joy and fulfillment (hence preserving Hume’s insight that virtue is agreeable). It is external and objective because the standards of virtue (courage, justice, temperance, wisdom) are grounded in the objective conditions of human flourishing, preventing morality from collapsing into subjective whim or cultural consensus.

---

## Conclusion

David Hume’s sentimentalist account of moral reasoning performed an invaluable service to philosophy by shattering the cold abstractions of hyper-rationalism and placing human emotion at the very center of the moral life. He demonstrated with unassailable clarity that reason alone is inert and that without the vital pulse of the passions, morality would be a dead letter, incapable of moving human beings to action.

Yet, as we have evaluated, pure sentimentalism bears significant vulnerabilities. By resting moral distinctions upon an unguided mechanism of sympathy and affective impressions, it struggles to furnish internal value judgments, risks collapsing into subjectivism or cultural relativism, and leaves itself dangerously exposed to pathologies of feeling—such as misguided sympathy for acts of vengeance or malignant desires. Without rational constraints, sentiment can validate the darkest inclinations of the human heart.

By turning to Aristotle, however, we find the necessary medicine for these ailments. Incorporating the notions of habituated character (*ethos*) and rational oversight (*phronesis*) rescues emotion-based ethics from its arbitrariness without sacrificing its motivational soul. In this synthesized vision, reason and passion are not adversaries locked in combat, nor is reason the abject slave of arbitrary desires. Rather, reason, disciplined by practical wisdom and nurtured by habituation, guides, educates, and elevates the passions. Sympathy becomes an enlightened, expansive capacity; feelings remain the indispensable motivational engine of moral action; and moral obligation finds its anchor in a shared, objective human flourishing. Through this integration, we honor Hume’s insight into the warmth of human sentiment while securing it with the steadfast anchor of rational character.
