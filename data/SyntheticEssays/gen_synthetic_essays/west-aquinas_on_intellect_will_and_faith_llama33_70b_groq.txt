The philosophical theology of St. Thomas Aquinas has long been a subject of fascination and debate among scholars, with his concept of faith being a particularly contentious issue. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether Aquinas advances an indirect descriptive volitionalism regarding the acquisition of Christian faith. This essay will delve into the complexities of Aquinas' thought, examining the role of the will in moving the intellect to assent to propositions whose evidence is insufficient to compel belief. We will also investigate the metaphysical identity between being and goodness, which Aquinas invokes to defend his position against charges of epistemic unjustifiedness and the absence of certainty.

To begin with, it is essential to understand the distinction between direct and indirect descriptive volitionalism. Direct descriptive volitionalism posits that the will directly influences the intellect's judgment, effectively forcing assent to a proposition. In contrast, indirect descriptive volitionalism suggests that the will plays a more nuanced role, inclining the intellect toward a particular proposition without coercing it. Aquinas' account of faith, as outlined in his Summa Theologica, appears to align with the latter position. According to Aquinas, faith is a virtue that involves the will's movement of the intellect to assent to propositions that are not demonstrable by reason alone.

The question arises, however, as to how the will can move the intellect to assent to such propositions without compromising the intellect's autonomy or succumbing to epistemic unjustifiedness. Aquinas addresses this concern by invoking the metaphysical identity between being and goodness. He argues that being and goodness are convertible, meaning that they are ultimately the same thing. This identity is rooted in the notion that existence itself is a manifestation of God's goodness, and therefore, all being is inherently good. As a result, the will's inclination toward the universal good is, in effect, an inclination toward being itself.

This metaphysical framework provides the foundation for Aquinas' defense against charges of epistemic unjustifiedness. By positing that the will moves the intellect toward the universal good, Aquinas can argue that faith is not a matter of arbitrary or unjustified assent, but rather an inclination toward the very essence of reality. In this sense, the will's movement of the intellect is not a coercive force, but rather a gentle inclination that allows the intellect to apprehend the truth of the proposition.

However, this position generates a philosophical tension, as it appears to propose that faith operates through the will's inclination toward the universal good rather than through direct intellectual demonstration. This raises questions about the nature of faith and its relationship to reason. If faith is not grounded in rational demonstration, does it not become a matter of blind or arbitrary assent? Furthermore, if the will's inclination toward the universal good is the primary driver of faith, does this not undermine the role of reason in the acquisition of knowledge?

To resolve this dilemma, it is essential to consider the theological and moral implications of Aquinas' position. For Aquinas, faith is not merely a matter of intellectual assent, but rather a transformation of the human agent's heart and desires. The will's inclination toward the universal good is not a rejection of reason, but rather a completion of it. By inclining the intellect toward the universal good, the will enables the human agent to apprehend the truth of the proposition in a way that transcends mere rational demonstration.

This transformation of the human agent's heart and desires is rooted in the concept of charity, which Aquinas sees as the form of faith. Charity is the virtue that enables the human agent to love God and neighbor, and it is through charity that the will's inclination toward the universal good becomes a living and active principle in the human agent's life. In this sense, faith is not a static or abstract concept, but rather a dynamic and relational reality that involves the transformation of the human agent's very being.

The implications of this position are far-reaching. If faith is a transformation of the human agent's heart and desires, rather than merely an intellectual assent, then it follows that the acquisition of faith is not a solitary or individualistic endeavor. Rather, it is a communal and relational process that involves the participation of the entire person, including the will, intellect, and emotions. This understanding of faith highlights the importance of spiritual practices, such as prayer, contemplation, and service, in the formation of the human agent's heart and desires.

Furthermore, this position underscores the importance of moral and theological virtues in the acquisition of faith. For Aquinas, the virtues are not merely abstract concepts, but rather habits of being that enable the human agent to participate in the life of God. The virtues of faith, hope, and charity are particularly important, as they enable the human agent to incline toward the universal good and to participate in the divine life.

The will's inclination toward the universal good is not a rejection of reason, but rather a completion of it, enabling the human agent to apprehend the truth of the proposition in a way that transcends mere rational demonstration.

The philosophical tension generated by this position is resolved by considering the theological and moral implications of Aquinas' thought. Ultimately, Aquinas' account of faith presents a nuanced and dynamic understanding of the human agent's relationship with God, one that emphasizes the importance of the will, intellect, and emotions in the acquisition of knowledge and the transformation of the human heart.
