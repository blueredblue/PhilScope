### The Unsystematic Self: Why Rationality Does Not Demand the Subsumption of Desires

**Introduction**

The question of what it means to deliberate rationally is among the most fundamental in practical philosophy. At its core lies a tension between two competing visions of the rational agent. On one hand, we have the psychological realist, for whom rationality is a procedural filter applied to the messy, contingent material of human desire. On the other, we have the normative idealist, for whom rationality is a substantive force that shapes desires into a coherent, unified, and ultimately justifiable whole. This essay evaluates a specific, powerful version of the latter demand: that making desires systematically justifiable—that is, subsuming specific desires under general, higher-order principles—is a necessary component of rational deliberation. To do so, I will pit Bernard Williams’s account of the subjective motivational set (SMS) and internal reasons against Michael Smith’s account of the fully rational agent. My central thesis is that the stronger sense of coherence—the systematic unification of desires into a unified desiderative profile—is *not* a necessary component of rational deliberation. The weaker sense of coherence, which merely requires the avoidance of practical conflicts, is sufficient for an agent to be rational. The seductive analogy between theoretical and practical reasoning ultimately fails because theoretical reasoning aims at a single, truth-apt description of reality, while practical reasoning aims at action within a world of irreducibly plural values. Consequently, internal reasons are fundamentally relativistic; they are relative to the agent’s subjective motivational set, and the demand for systematic justification cannot, and need not, render them non-relativistic.

To frame this argument, I will first articulate Williams’s internalism, emphasizing the minimal, procedural nature of his rationality requirement. I will then reconstruct Smith’s more demanding ideal, which posits that a fully rational agent would possess a maximally coherent and unified set of desires. Next, I will critically analyze the analogy between theoretical and practical reasoning, arguing that the demand for unification in the former does not translate to the latter. This will lead to a defense of the weaker sense of coherence, demonstrating that an agent can be perfectly rational while retaining a plurality of underived, particularistic desires. Finally, I will conclude that because systematic justification is not a constitutive requirement of rationality, internal reasons remain irreducibly relative to the agent’s SMS, and any attempt to force non-relativism through a demand for systematization imports a substantive normative theory that rationality itself does not mandate.

#### I. Williams’s Internalism and the Subjective Motivational Set

Bernard Williams’s seminal essay “Internal and External Reasons” provides the foundational framework for understanding practical rationality in a psychologically realistic manner. Williams’s central claim is that an agent A has a reason to φ only if there is a sound deliberative route from A’s existing subjective motivational set (SMS) to a desire to φ. The SMS is broadly conceived; it includes not just simple desires, but also dispositions of evaluation, patterns of emotional reaction, personal loyalties, and various projects—what Williams calls the agent’s “commitments.” Crucially, the SMS is the psychological ground from which all practical reasoning must proceed. It is not a neutral, rational starting point; it is the contingent, historical, and often messy set of motivations that constitute who the agent is.

For Williams, rationality is a matter of how one deliberates *from* this set. The “sound deliberative route” includes several operations: finding means to satisfy existing desires, identifying the constituent parts of a desired end, and, importantly, eliminating inconsistencies. If an agent desires both to φ and to not-φ, they are in a state of practical irrationality. The deliberative process must resolve this conflict, perhaps by weighing the relative strength of the desires, or by discovering that one desire is based on a false belief. The role of imagination is also crucial; an agent might come to desire something by vividly imagining what it would be like to have it, thereby expanding their SMS through a deliberative route. However, the key point is that rationality is a *filter* on the SMS, not a *source* of new, externally imposed content. There is no rational requirement to adopt a desire that is not already a potential output of the agent’s own SMS.

This account gives rise to Williams’s famous rejection of external reasons. An external reason statement claims that A has a reason to φ regardless of whether φ-ing is connected to A’s SMS. Williams argues that such statements are false or incoherent. If a reason is to genuinely motivate a rational agent, it must be able to survive the agent’s own deliberative scrutiny. If it cannot connect to the SMS, it is merely a piece of moralizing or persuasion, not a reason. Consequently, the normativity of reasons is internal to the agent’s psychology.

Within this framework, what does coherence require? Williams’s account demands the *weaker* sense of coherence: the avoidance of *practical* contradictions. An agent cannot rationally will two incompatible states of affairs simultaneously, because this would paralyze action. The deliberative process must adjudicate such conflicts. However, Williams does *not* require that the agent’s desires be systematically unified under a general principle. An agent can have a desire for wine, a desire for philosophical clarity, a desire to help a specific friend, and a desire to climb a specific mountain. These desires might be entirely unrelated. They do not need to be subsumed under a higher-order desire for, say, “maximizing personal flourishing” or “acting according to universalizable maxims.” As long as they do not conflict in a way that prevents action, the agent is perfectly rational. The SMS can be a patchwork of particularistic commitments, and rationality does not demand that we stitch it into a single, seamless garment.

#### II. Smith’s Fully Rational Agent and the Demand for Systematic Justification

Michael Smith’s project in *The Moral Problem* offers a starkly contrasting picture. Smith seeks to solve the apparent tension between the objectivity of moral judgments and their practical, motivating force. His solution posits a conception of the fully rational agent whose desires are not merely conflict-free, but are *systematically justified* and unified. For Smith, a fully rational agent is one who has no false beliefs, possesses all relevant true beliefs, and has a maximally coherent and unified desiderative profile. This is not a descriptive claim about actual human beings, but a normative ideal against which our actual deliberations are measured.

The crucial feature of Smith’s account is the demand for *systematic justification*. A desire is justified, in Smith’s sense, if it can be derived from or subsumed under a more general desire. For example, a specific desire to help this person in front of me is justified if it is an instance of a general desire to help those in need. That general desire is itself justified if it is an instance of a desire to be benevolent, and so on. The fully rational agent’s desires form a hierarchy, culminating in a set of ultimate, general principles (which, for Smith, converge on the moral law). Specific desires that cannot be subsumed under any general principle are considered “brute” or arbitrary. Such desires would not be present in a fully rational agent, because they would represent a failure of coherence and unity.

Why does Smith think this is necessary? His argument rests on an analogy with theoretical reasoning. In the epistemic domain, a fully rational agent would not merely hold a set of consistent beliefs; they would seek a *coherent theory*. They would prefer a set of beliefs that is unified, explanatory, and systematic over a mere list of isolated facts. Fragmented beliefs are epistemically deficient because they lack explanatory power and are vulnerable to error. Similarly, Smith argues, a fully rational agent would prefer a set of desires that is unified and systematic over a mere list of independent preferences. Fragmented desires are practically deficient because they lack normative justification. If I desire X but cannot explain why I desire X except by appealing to the brute fact of my desiring it, then my desire is arbitrary. To be fully rational, I must be able to justify my desire by showing that it is an instance of a more general, rationally acceptable desire.

This leads to Smith’s famous claim that moral judgments express beliefs about what a fully rational agent would desire. The content of morality is determined by the desires of this idealized agent. Since the fully rational agent is defined by possessing a maximally coherent and unified desiderative profile, the moral requirements that fall out of this profile are necessarily non-arbitrary and objective. They are the same for all rational agents, because any deviation from this ideal would constitute a failure of rationality. Thus, for Smith, rationality *mandates* the stronger sense of coherence: the subsumption of specific desires under general ones, culminating in a unified desiderative profile.

#### III. The Disanalogy Between Theoretical and Practical Reasoning

The crux of the debate lies in whether Smith’s analogy between theoretical and practical reasoning is sound. I argue that it is fundamentally flawed, and that the disanalogy reveals why the demand for systematic justification is not a necessary component of rational deliberation.

The goal of theoretical reasoning is *truth*. We seek to form beliefs that accurately represent the world. In this domain, unification is indeed a constitutive goal. A set of beliefs that is merely consistent but fragmented—knowing that water boils at 100°C, that the Earth orbits the Sun, and that my cat is on the mat—is epistemically impoverished compared to a set of beliefs that is unified under explanatory laws, such as thermodynamics or Newtonian mechanics. The reason for this is that the world itself is a unified system. There is a single reality, and our beliefs aim to map that reality. Unification is instrumental to truth because it increases explanatory power, predictive success, and our ability to identify and correct errors. The demand for systematic coherence in theoretical reasoning is driven by the nature of its subject matter: a single, law-governed universe.

The goal of practical reasoning, however, is not truth but *action*. We deliberate to decide what to do. The subject matter of practical reasoning is not a single external reality, but the agent’s own will and the plurality of values that exist in the human world. Unlike beliefs, desires do not aim to fit the world; they aim to make the world fit them (the classic “direction of fit” distinction). Because desires are not truth-apt, the epistemic reasons for unification do not directly apply. We do not ask whether a desire is “true” or “false”; we ask whether it is good, or whether it is worth pursuing. And the question of what is good is not settled by discovering a single, unified reality.

This is where value pluralism becomes decisive. Philosophers like Isaiah Berlin have argued that fundamental human values—liberty, equality, justice, love, creativity, security—are not only plural but often incommensurable. They cannot be ranked on a single scale, and they frequently conflict in ways that admit of no rational resolution that would satisfy all parties. Forcing these values under a single general principle (e.g., “maximize utility” or “act only on universalizable maxims”) either distorts the nature of these values or requires an arbitrary ranking principle that is itself unjustified. A rational agent might deeply value both artistic freedom and social stability. These values can conflict. The agent must make a choice, but the choice need not be justified by subsuming both under a higher-order principle. The agent might simply decide, in a particular context, that one value is more salient or more urgent. This is a rational decision, but it is not a *systematic* one.

Furthermore, consider the nature of particularistic desires. My desire to care for *this* specific child, my love for *this* particular person, my appreciation for *this* specific painting—these desires are often irreducible. They are not instances of a general desire to care for all children, or to love all people, or to appreciate all art. They are directed at the particularity of the object. If I attempt to subsume my love for my child under a general principle of “benevolence towards all,” I lose the specific character of that love, which is precisely what makes it meaningful to me. Smith’s demand for systematic justification would require me to either abandon such particularistic desires as “brute” and irrational, or to distort them by subsuming them under generalities. Both options are deeply counterintuitive. A life filled with such particularistic attachments is not a life of irrationality; it is a life of rich, human engagement.

The theoretical analogy fails because it conflates explanatory coherence with practical coherence. In theoretical reasoning, we unify beliefs because the world is unified. In practical reasoning, we do not need to unify desires because the self, and the values it holds, is not a single, monolithic entity. The self is a locus of diverse commitments, and rationality’s job is not to erase this diversity but to manage it effectively. The weaker sense of coherence—ensuring that our desires do not contradict each other in a way that prevents action—is sufficient for this task.

#### IV. The Necessity of the Weaker Sense and the Sufficiency of Particularism

Let us now directly address the question of whether a rational agent must value the stronger sense of coherence. I contend that they must not. The weaker sense of coherence—avoiding conflicts within practical reasoning—is a necessary condition for rationality, but the stronger sense—subsuming specific desires under general ones—is not.

The necessity of the weaker sense is obvious. If an agent desires to go to the party and desires to stay home, and the time to act arrives, they cannot satisfy both. They are in a state of practical paralysis. To be rational, they must resolve this conflict. However, the resolution does not require a general principle. The agent might weigh the strength of the desires, consider the specific circumstances (e.g., I promised to go, but I am very tired), and make a contextual judgment. They might decide to go for an hour and then leave. This is a perfectly rational decision, but it is not derived from a general rule like “always keep promises” or “always prioritize health.” It is a particularistic judgment about this specific situation.

Jonathan Dancy’s moral particularism provides a robust philosophical defense of this view. Particularists argue that moral reasons are context-dependent and cannot be captured by general principles. What counts as a reason in one situation might not count as a reason in another, or might even count as a reason *against* an action in a third. For example, the fact that an action causes pleasure is usually a reason to do it, but if the pleasure is sadistic, it is a reason against. The particularist claims that we can make rational judgments in each case without appealing to a master principle. We simply weigh the reasons as they present themselves in the context.

Smith would object that such contextual weighing is arbitrary. If the agent has no general principle, then their choice between conflicting desires is nothing more than a brute preference for one over the other, which is irrational. But this objection conflates “arbitrary” with “not systematically justified.” A choice can be non-arbitrary if it is based on a careful consideration of the specific features of the situation. The agent who chooses to go to the party for an hour because they promised, but leaves early because they are exhausted, is not being arbitrary. They are responding to the specific reasons present in the situation. The fact that they cannot articulate a universal rule does not make their decision irrational. It makes them a sensitive, context-aware deliberator.

Moreover, demanding systematic justification imposes a specific normative theory onto the agent. It assumes that rationality requires generalism—that all reasons must be instances of general principles. But this is a substantive meta-ethical claim, not a neutral requirement of rationality. A particularist can be perfectly rational while denying generalism. They simply hold that reasons are context-dependent. Since rationality does not dictate which meta-ethical theory is correct, it cannot mandate the stronger sense of coherence. Rationality is a procedural virtue; it governs *how* we deliberate, not the *content* of the principles we use.
