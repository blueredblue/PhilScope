# Context, Skepticism, and the Fragility of Knowledge

Philosophical skepticism poses a distinctive problem for the concept of knowledge. In ordinary life, people confidently claim to know many things: that there is a table in front of them, that they have hands, that they attended a lecture yesterday, or that their car is parked outside. Yet skeptical arguments appear to show that such knowledge is impossible. The skeptic asks us to consider hypotheses that are incompatible with our ordinary beliefs but consistent with our experiences: perhaps we are dreaming, deceived by an evil demon, brains in vats, or inhabitants of an elaborate virtual reality. If our evidence would be the same whether these hypotheses were true or false, then, the skeptic argues, we do not know the ordinary propositions after all.

Contextualism offers an influential response. According to epistemic contextualism, the truth conditions of knowledge ascriptions vary with context. The sentence “I know that I have hands” may be true in an ordinary conversational context but false in a philosophical context in which skeptical possibilities are being taken seriously. Contextualism therefore seeks to preserve ordinary knowledge without denying the force of skeptical arguments. It does so by arguing that “know” expresses a context-sensitive relation: in ordinary contexts, relatively modest epistemic standards apply, while in skeptical contexts, more demanding standards become relevant.

This strategy has considerable appeal. It explains why ordinary knowledge claims seem true while also explaining why skeptical arguments seem compelling. Nevertheless, contextualism faces serious difficulties. It is not always clear how a conversational context determines the relevant epistemic standards, how speakers recognize that a context has shifted, or why merely mentioning a skeptical possibility should alter the truth conditions of knowledge claims. Further, contextualism may appear to beg the question against the skeptic by treating ordinary knowledge as already secure and then explaining away skeptical doubts as merely context-generated. The success of contextualism therefore depends on whether its account of context is independently motivated and sufficiently precise. Contextualism can illuminate important features of epistemic language, but it does not by itself dissolve the skeptical problem. At best, it offers a partial reconciliation between ordinary knowledge and skeptical doubt.

## The skeptical challenge

The skeptical problem can be formulated in several ways. A simple closure-based argument runs as follows:

1. I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat.
2. If I know that I have hands, and I know that having hands entails that I am not a brain in a vat, then I know that I am not a brain in a vat.
3. Therefore, I do not know that I have hands.

The first premise expresses the skeptic’s central challenge. My perceptual experience of having hands may be exactly the same whether I am an ordinary embodied person or a brain receiving artificially stimulated experiences. If knowledge requires ruling out all alternatives incompatible with what one believes, then I lack knowledge of ordinary propositions.

The argument exploits a plausible principle known as epistemic closure. Roughly, if a person knows proposition *p*, and knows that *p* entails *q*, then the person knows *q*. If I know that this is a hand and know that the presence of a hand entails that I am not a handless brain in a vat, then it seems I should know that I am not a brain in a vat. But if I cannot know the latter, perhaps I cannot know the former either.

The skeptic can also appeal to an underdetermination argument. My evidence does not distinguish between ordinary reality and a skeptical scenario. Since knowledge requires evidence that favors the truth over relevant alternatives, and since the same evidence is compatible with both possibilities, I do not know the ordinary proposition. The skeptic thus attempts to show that our everyday epistemic practices rest on standards that cannot withstand philosophical scrutiny.

There are several possible responses. One can reject closure, deny that skeptical alternatives are relevant, defend a form of foundationalism, or argue that knowledge requires only reliable belief rather than the elimination of every logically possible alternative. Contextualism takes a different route. Rather than directly denying the skeptic’s argument, it claims that the argument changes the conversational standards governing “know.” The skeptic and the ordinary speaker may be using the same word but operating with different standards. The skeptic’s conclusion may be true in the skeptical context without implying that ordinary knowledge claims were false in everyday contexts.

## The contextualist proposal

Contextualism about knowledge is primarily a semantic thesis. It holds that the truth conditions of knowledge ascriptions depend partly on the context in which those ascriptions are made. The proposition expressed by “S knows that p” is not fixed solely by the subject, the evidence, and the proposition *p*. It also depends on a contextually supplied epistemic standard.

This proposal resembles contextualism about other expressions. The word “tall,” for example, may be true of a person in a basketball context but false of the same person in a context involving professional basketball players. “Flat” may be true of a road relative to ordinary purposes but false in a discussion of precision engineering. Similarly, “know” may demand a relatively low level of epistemic justification in daily conversation and a much higher level in a courtroom, scientific investigation, or philosophical discussion of skepticism.

Important versions of contextualism are associated with authors such as Stewart Cohen, Keith DeRose, and David Lewis. Although their theories differ, they share the basic thought that the skeptic raises the standards for knowledge. In an ordinary context, the possibility that I am a brain in a vat may be ignored. In a skeptical context, that possibility becomes relevant, and I can no longer truly say that I know I have hands. The ordinary claim is not necessarily refuted; instead, the context has changed.

This strategy appears to preserve two intuitions:

- In ordinary life, people genuinely know many ordinary propositions.
- Skeptical arguments can make it inappropriate or false to claim knowledge of those propositions.

Contextualism thus attempts to explain the apparent conflict between common sense and skepticism without simply dismissing either side. It does not say that the skeptic is making a straightforward mistake. Nor does it say that ordinary speakers are confused when they claim knowledge. Rather, it says that both parties may be correct relative to different contexts.

## Is knowledge genuinely context-sensitive?

The first issue is whether ascriptions of knowledge are in fact context-sensitive. There is strong linguistic and philosophical motivation for thinking that they are. The appropriateness of “know” often varies with practical stakes, conversational purposes, and the alternatives under discussion. Consider someone who says, “I know the bank will be open on Saturday.” In an ordinary situation, this may be acceptable if the person has seen the bank’s regular hours. But if the person is deciding whether to drive several hours to deposit an urgent payment, the same evidence may no longer justify the assertion. The stakes and practical context appear to matter.

Likewise, in a courtroom, a witness may be required to rule out possibilities that would be ignored in casual conversation. A doctor may be said to know that a patient has a particular disease only after excluding serious alternatives. A scientist may be required to possess evidence that would not be demanded in ordinary conversation. These examples suggest that “know” is not governed by one fixed standard in every context.

Contextualism also explains disagreements that otherwise seem puzzling. Suppose one speaker says, “I know the animal in the field is a zebra,” while another says, “You do not know that; it could be a cleverly disguised mule.” The disagreement may not concern the person’s visual evidence alone. It may concern which alternatives are relevant. In an ordinary zoo context, the disguised-mule possibility is ignored. In a context emphasizing deception, it becomes relevant. Contextualism captures this difference by making the relevant alternatives part of the context.

However, the existence of context-sensitive uses of “know” does not establish full-blown epistemic contextualism. It may show only that knowledge attributions are pragmatically flexible. A speaker can use “know” loosely or strictly without the truth conditions of knowledge itself changing. For example, “I know the restaurant is open” may be conversationally appropriate because the speaker does not need to mention minor uncertainty, even if the underlying epistemic state remains the same. The distinction between semantic context-sensitivity and pragmatic variation is therefore crucial.

If contextualism is merely a thesis about conversational propriety, it may not solve skepticism. The skeptic’s argument concerns whether the subject genuinely possesses knowledge, not merely whether it is appropriate to assert knowledge in a particular conversation. To answer skepticism, contextualism must maintain that the truth of “I know that p” itself changes with context. Otherwise, the skeptic can say that ordinary speakers are simply speaking loosely while the underlying fact remains that they do not know.

The contextualist is therefore committed to a substantive semantic claim. The same subject, with the same evidence, can know *p* relative to one context and fail to know *p* relative to another. This is a coherent position, but it requires an account of what context contributes and why that contribution affects knowledge rather than merely assertion.

## The force of the skeptical context

The contextualist’s central explanatory idea is that skeptical arguments introduce unusually demanding standards. In ordinary contexts, people take many alternatives for granted. When I look at a table, I need not rule out the possibility that I am dreaming, hallucinating, or being deceived by a powerful computer. These alternatives are not part of the practical conversational background. My visual experience, together with ordinary assumptions about perception, is enough for the claim that I know there is a table.

The skeptical philosopher deliberately brings one of these alternatives into focus. Once the possibility of dreaming or being a brain in a vat is explicitly raised, it becomes relevant to the discussion. The standards for knowledge are raised, and ordinary perceptual evidence is no longer sufficient. The skeptical conclusion then follows: given the newly relevant alternative, I do not know the ordinary proposition.

This model explains why skepticism can feel both compelling and artificial. The skeptic does not necessarily discover an ordinary defect in our perceptual evidence. Instead, the skeptic changes what counts as an adequate epistemic performance. The ordinary speaker does not typically need to establish that she is not dreaming. The philosopher asks her to do so. The resulting inability is then interpreted as a failure of knowledge.

Yet this explanation immediately generates a difficult objection. Does the mere consideration of a skeptical hypothesis really introduce a legitimate epistemic standard, or does it merely impose an arbitrary one? The skeptic can describe indefinitely many possibilities: dreams, demons, simulations, false memories, deceptive testimony, and so on. If simply mentioning an alternative makes it relevant, then knowledge becomes extremely fragile. Any person could destroy another person’s knowledge by inventing an increasingly remote possibility. This seems wrong. The fact that someone says, “Perhaps you are a brain in a vat,” does not obviously make it rationally necessary for me to investigate that possibility before claiming to know that I have hands.

The contextualist may reply that not every mentioned alternative becomes relevant. Context shifts are constrained by conversational salience, practical importance, and standards already in play. A random assertion by an eccentric stranger should not automatically raise the epistemic bar. But once this qualification is introduced, the theory needs a principled explanation of which alternatives count and why. Without such an explanation, contextualism risks replacing skepticism with arbitrariness.

## Does the skeptical hypothesis dissolve ordinary knowledge?

The objection that skeptical consideration dissolves ordinary knowledge can be put more sharply. If ordinary knowledge disappears whenever a skeptical hypothesis is mentioned, then contextualism seems to make knowledge depend on an unstable feature of conversation. I may know that I have hands while walking to work, cease to know it when attending a philosophy seminar, and regain knowledge when leaving the seminar. Yet nothing about my evidence, cognitive capacities, or the world has changed. Only the topic of conversation has changed.

This result is not necessarily incoherent. Other context-sensitive predicates behave similarly. A person may be tall relative to one comparison class and not tall relative to another, even though the person’s height remains constant. But the analogy may be misleading. “Tall” is explicitly comparative, whereas “know” appears to describe a cognitive relation between a subject and a proposition. It is not immediately obvious why the truth of that relation should fluctuate with what people happen to be discussing.

Moreover, ordinary knowledge is often used to guide action outside the conversation in which it is attributed. If I know that there is a table in front of me, then I can safely walk around it, place an object on it, or warn someone else about it. If my knowledge disappears merely because a philosopher mentions a skeptical hypothesis, it becomes difficult to explain why my practical abilities remain unchanged. The contextualist can distinguish knowledge-relative-to-context from practical certainty, but this may make the theory increasingly complicated. It would need to say that the subject retains some epistemic achievement while no longer satisfying the strict truth conditions of “know” in the new context.

There is also a concern about the direction of explanation. Skepticism appears to show that ordinary evidence is insufficient to exclude skeptical possibilities. Contextualism responds that such possibilities become relevant only in a special context. But why should relevance determine knowledge? The skeptic may claim that the possibility was always epistemically relevant, whether or not anyone mentioned it. If the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis is a genuine defeater, its force should not depend on conversational attention. The contextualist appears to treat a change in our talk as a change in the epistemic facts.

Contextualists sometimes answer that they are not claiming that the world or the subject’s evidence changes. They are making a claim about the semantics of the knowledge attribution. The sentence “I know that I have hands” can have different truth conditions in different contexts. The subject’s underlying evidence remains unchanged, but the relation expressed by “know” varies. This response clarifies the position, but it also shows its limits. Contextualism may explain why knowledge ascriptions vary while leaving untouched the skeptic’s deeper question: whether the subject has a kind of epistemic justification that deserves to be called knowledge independently of linguistic context.

## How does context determine epistemic standards?

A central weakness of contextualism is the difficulty of explaining how context determines the relevant standard. Context is a broad and vague notion. It may include the speaker’s intentions, the hearer’s expectations, the practical stakes, the conversational topic, social roles, institutional norms, and salient alternatives. But which of these factors matters, and how do they combine?

One possibility is that the relevant standard is determined by the alternatives that are salient in conversation. On this view, I know *p* when my evidence rules out all alternatives to *p* that are relevant in the context. The skeptic makes the skeptical hypothesis relevant by explicitly raising it. This account has intuitive appeal, but it faces a problem of salience. An alternative can be salient because someone mentions it, but mere mention cannot be sufficient. Otherwise, knowledge would be hostage to arbitrary conversational behavior.

A second possibility is that practical stakes determine the standard. When much depends on being right, more evidence is required. This view resembles subject-sensitive invariantism more than traditional contextualism. It also raises questions about whether high stakes alter knowledge or merely alter justified assertion. If I am deciding whether to undergo a risky medical procedure, I should investigate more carefully than when casually discussing symptoms. But it does not follow that the truth of “I know I have this disease” changes simply because the stakes change.

A third possibility is that social or institutional norms determine standards. Courts, laboratories, and ordinary conversations may possess different rules for knowledge attribution. This can explain variations in language, but it risks relativizing knowledge to local practices. If one community permits a weak standard and another demands an exceptionally strong one, are the same beliefs knowledge in one community but not the other? Contextualism can accept this, but it must then explain why knowledge remains a truth-oriented epistemic notion rather than a label governed by social convention.

The problem becomes especially pressing when context shifts are gradual or contested. In a philosophical conversation, exactly when does the skeptical context begin? Does the context change when the skeptical hypothesis is first mentioned, when the speaker endorses it as a serious possibility, when the audience takes it seriously, or only when the discussion adopts a demand for ruling it out? Different answers produce different predictions. Contextualism needs a theory of these transitions rather than simply pointing to “the context” as an explanatory placeholder.

## How are shifts in context recognized?

Relatedly, contextualism must account for how speakers recognize changes in epistemic standards. Ordinary speakers often respond to skeptical challenges with something like, “Of course I cannot prove that I am not dreaming, but I still know that I am sitting here.” This reaction suggests that they do not automatically accept the contextualist shift. They may understand the skeptical argument while rejecting its demand as irrelevant or excessive.

If a context shift occurs whenever an alternative is made salient, speakers should normally become aware that their knowledge claims are now subject to stricter conditions. But this does not always happen. People can discuss a skeptical possibility while continuing to use “know” in its ordinary sense. Philosophers may say, “I know that I have hands, even though I cannot rule out the brain-in-a-vat scenario,” treating the skeptical hypothesis as an objection to knowledge rather than as a change in the meaning of “know.”

This creates a dilemma. If context shifts are automatic, contextualism makes knowledge too vulnerable to casual mention. If shifts require conscious recognition or acceptance, then the theory must explain what mental or conversational event produces the change. It cannot simply assume that a skeptical hypothesis is relevant because it has appeared in the dialogue.

The contextualist might say that conversational presuppositions change. At the beginning of an ordinary discussion, everyone presupposes that ordinary perceptual conditions obtain. Once the possibility of deception becomes the focus, that presupposition is suspended. Yet the skeptic can challenge the presupposition itself. Why is it permissible to presuppose that I am not a brain in a vat in ordinary contexts? If the answer is that ordinary life would be impossible without such presuppositions, then contextualism has moved toward a pragmatic defense of common sense rather than a semantic solution to skepticism.

The lack of a clear account of context recognition also threatens the theory’s explanatory ambition. A theory that says “knowledge is relative to context” must explain the mechanisms by which contexts acquire their epistemic standards. Otherwise it merely redescribes the phenomenon. The skeptic raises the standard; the ordinary speaker lowers it. But why is one move legitimate and the other not? Without an answer, contextualism cannot determine whether a skeptical challenge exposes a genuine epistemic limitation or merely attempts to manipulate the conversation.

## The charge of begging the question

Another major criticism is that contextualism begs the question against the skeptic. The skeptic argues that ordinary evidence does not establish the falsity of skeptical hypotheses. The contextualist replies that ordinary contexts do not require such establishment, so ordinary knowledge remains intact. But this response may simply assume that ordinary standards are the correct standards.

The skeptic’s challenge is not merely that people sometimes use “know” strictly. It is that, given the possibility of deception and the apparent inability to rule it out, ordinary beliefs do not qualify as knowledge. If the contextualist declares that skeptical alternatives are irrelevant in ordinary contexts, the skeptic can ask why. The answer cannot simply be that ordinary speakers do not normally consider them, because that confuses psychological attention with epistemic relevance. Nor can the answer be that ordinary language treats such beliefs as knowledge, because the skeptic is precisely questioning whether ordinary language has set the standard correctly.

In this sense, contextualism may appear to protect common sense by stipulation. It builds ordinary knowledge into the semantic rules of ordinary contexts and then claims that skepticism arises only when those rules are replaced. But the skeptic can insist that the higher standard is not optional. If knowledge requires a sufficiently well-grounded true belief, and if skeptical alternatives cannot be excluded, then ordinary claims fail regardless of how people talk.

The contextualist has several possible replies. First, the skeptic also relies on contestable assumptions, such as the principle that knowledge requires ruling out skeptical alternatives or that closure transfers knowledge across all relevant entailments. Contextualism need not be the only position accused of begging the question. Second, all epistemic theories must identify some standards as legitimate. Foundationalists, reliabilists, and common-sense philosophers also begin from assumptions about what counts as adequate justification. Contextualism is not uniquely vicious merely because it takes ordinary epistemic practices seriously.

Third, contextualism can argue that the skeptic changes the subject. The skeptic’s conclusion may be true under an artificially elevated standard, but it does not answer the ordinary question of whether I know that I have hands. This is not necessarily question-begging; it may be a diagnosis of ambiguity in the word “know.” The skeptic is entitled to ask whether I can rule out the skeptical possibility, but the answer is that I cannot meet that special standard. It does not follow that I lack knowledge in every legitimate sense.

Still, this reply will satisfy only those who already accept that multiple standards of knowledge are legitimate. The skeptic can reject the semantic diagnosis and maintain that there is one objective epistemic standard. On that view, ordinary discourse may be mistaken, just as ordinary visual appearances can be mistaken. Contextualism therefore cannot refute skepticism without defending its semantic premises. It can show how skeptical and ordinary claims might coexist, but it cannot establish that coexistence is philosophically acceptable merely by describing it.

## Contextualism and closure

The relationship between contextualism and closure is especially important. In ordinary contexts, contextualism often preserves closure: if I know that I have hands, I know that I am not a brain in a vat, provided the relevant standards remain fixed. But once the skeptical alternative becomes salient, I may no longer know either proposition. The argument from closure remains valid within each context, but the truth values of the premises and conclusion shift as the context changes.

This allows contextualism to explain why the skeptic’s reasoning seems valid without accepting its unrestricted conclusion. In the ordinary context, the premise “I do not know that I am a brain in a vat” may be false, because the skeptical alternative is not relevant. In the skeptical context, the premise becomes true, and the conclusion “I do not know that I have hands” also becomes true. Closure is preserved, but knowledge attributions become context-dependent.

The cost is that contextualism produces what may be called a “shifting truth” problem. A speaker may assert, truly, “I know that I have hands,” then later truly say, “I do not know that I have hands,” without acquiring or losing evidence. This is not an outright contradiction if the contexts differ, but it is philosophically unsettling. The ordinary speaker may reasonably ask: do I know or do I not? Contextualism answers: both, relative to different standards. The skeptic may regard this as evasive, while the ordinary speaker may regard it as an unnecessary complication.

Furthermore, contextualism can struggle with embedded knowledge claims. Suppose I say, “I knew yesterday that I had hands, but today I realize that I did not know it.” Does the skeptical context today retroactively alter the truth of yesterday’s claim? If knowledge is context-sensitive, the theory must distinguish the context of the original attribution from the context in which it is now discussed. Such cases reveal that contextualism needs a sophisticated account of indexicality and semantic evaluation. It is not enough to say that context matters; one must specify whose context, at what time, and under which standards.

## Comparison with alternative responses

The limitations of contextualism become clearer when compared with competing theories.

A strict invariantist may argue that “know” has one standard, but that the standard is not as demanding as skepticism assumes. Reliabilists, for example, may say that a belief counts as knowledge when produced by a reliable cognitive process, even if the subject cannot rule out far-fetched alternatives. This approach preserves ordinary knowledge without making truth conditions context-sensitive. Its weakness is that skeptical hypotheses may challenge the reliability of the relevant process. If I am a brain in a vat, my experiences may be systematically unreliable with respect to the external world.

A relevant-alternatives theory holds that knowledge requires ruling out only relevant alternatives, but it may treat relevance as an objective epistemic matter rather than a conversational one. On this view, the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis is not relevant in ordinary perceptual contexts because it is too remote or because it does not arise from the evidence. This approach resembles contextualism but avoids making truth itself depend on conversation. Its difficulty is explaining why the skeptical hypothesis becomes relevant in philosophical contexts if its remoteness remains unchanged.

A Moorean response begins from ordinary knowledge and rejects the skeptical argument. Someone may argue, “I know that I have hands; therefore I know that I am not a brain in a vat.” This refuses to give the skeptic the epistemic priority that contextualism grants. The Moorean position has the virtue of directness, but it can seem dogmatic because it asserts ordinary knowledge without explaining why the skeptical possibility should be ignored.

A subject-sensitive invariantist maintains that knowledge may depend on practical stakes, but not on the context of the knowledge attribution. The same belief can count as knowledge for one person and not another because their decisions make different possibilities relevant. This view captures the intuition that high stakes matter while preserving a stable semantic meaning of “know.” Yet it faces its own question-begging concerns: why should practical stakes affect knowledge rather than merely justified action?

Contextualism is therefore not uniquely troubled. Every anti-skeptical theory must explain why ordinary evidence is enough for knowledge despite the availability of skeptical alternatives. Contextualism’s distinctive contribution is to locate the solution in language and conversational standards. Its distinctive liability is that this linguistic solution may not address the skeptic’s epistemological concern.

## Can contextualism reconcile ordinary knowledge and skepticism?

Contextualism succeeds to a significant extent as a theory of epistemic discourse. It explains why people ordinarily say “I know” without engaging in elaborate investigations. It explains why skeptical arguments create a feeling of pressure: they activate alternatives that ordinary contexts suppress. It also explains how apparently conflicting judgments might both be appropriate. The ordinary speaker and the skeptic may be speaking truly relative to different conversational standards.

This is a genuine philosophical achievement. Skepticism often depends on moving between contexts without acknowledging the transition. It begins with ordinary claims, introduces an extreme alternative, insists on ruling it out, and then returns to ordinary claims to declare them unjustified. Contextualism exposes the possibility that the argument’s apparent force derives from a shift in the meaning or standards of “know.” It therefore provides a useful diagnosis of why skeptical reasoning is rhetorically powerful.

But contextualism does not fully reconcile ordinary knowledge with the skeptical hypothesis. It does not show that ordinary evidence discriminates between the ordinary world and the skeptical world. It shows only that ordinary knowledge claims need not be evaluated by a standard requiring such discrimination. Whether that standard is legitimate remains contested. If the skeptic’s demand is epistemically mandatory, then contextualism has not answered the skeptic; it has merely explained why ordinary speakers resist the conclusion. If the skeptic’s demand is conversationally optional, contextualism has a persuasive answer, but that claim requires independent support.

The theory is strongest when understood as a moderate, pluralist account of epistemic language. There are different standards for different purposes, and “know” is flexible enough to reflect them. It is weakest when presented as a complete theory of knowledge that dissolves skepticism simply by changing the context. Semantic variation cannot by itself establish that one has the epistemic right to rely on ordinary beliefs.

## Conclusion

The contextualist solution to skepticism rests on an attractive insight: knowledge ascriptions are sensitive to context. Ordinary conversation, scientific inquiry, legal judgment, and skeptical philosophy may impose different demands on what counts as knowing. When a skeptical hypothesis is introduced, the relevant standards may become unusually restrictive, so that a claim previously true becomes false or inappropriate. This enables contextualism to preserve ordinary empirical knowledge while explaining why skeptical arguments seem compelling.

Yet the strategy is not decisive. The mere consideration of a skeptical hypothesis should not automatically dissolve knowledge. If arbitrary mention raises the standard, knowledge becomes too fragile; if only certain mentions do so, contextualism must explain which ones and why. The theory also needs an account of how context determines epistemic standards and how speakers recognize shifts in context. Without such an account, “context” risks functioning as an unexplained label for changes that the theory is supposed to explain.

Finally, contextualism risks begging the question against the skeptic when it treats ordinary standards as legitimate without defending them. It can reply that the skeptic also relies on contestable assumptions and that skeptical contexts change the subject. But this response is persuasive only if one already accepts that knowledge has context-sensitive standards. Contextualism therefore offers a powerful diagnosis of the linguistic and conversational dimensions of skepticism, but not a conclusive refutation of the epistemological challenge.

The most defensible conclusion is consequently qualified. Ascriptions of knowledge are plausibly context-sensitive, at least in many ordinary and institutional uses. Contextualism successfully explains why ordinary claims and skeptical doubts can coexist in our practices of assertion. It does not, however, demonstrate that ordinary empirical knowledge survives every skeptical challenge. To accomplish that stronger task, contextualism would need an independent theory of relevance, standards, and context shifts, together with a non-question-begging justification for privileging ordinary epistemic practices. Until then, contextualism remains an illuminating partial solution: it shows how skepticism can alter what we can truthfully say, but it does not fully establish what we genuinely know.
