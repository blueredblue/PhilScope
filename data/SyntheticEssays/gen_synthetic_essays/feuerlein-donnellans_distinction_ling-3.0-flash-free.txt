**The Semantic Import of Donnellan’s Distinction: Reference, Belief, and Truth**

The philosophy of language has long grappled with the seemingly simple act of using a definite description to refer to an object or individual. At the heart of this inquiry lies a fundamental question: does the meaning of a sentence, and thus its truth conditions, depend solely on the semantic content of its words, or does it inevitably intertwine with the speaker’s intentions, beliefs, and the communicative context? This question becomes particularly acute when examining the use of definite descriptions, such as “the F.” Bertrand Russell provided the foundational semantic analysis of such descriptions, arguing that they carry no referential import but are instead disguised quantifiers. However, Keith Donnellan’s seminal 1966 paper, “Reference and Definite Descriptions,” shattered this consensus by arguing that definite descriptions can be used in two fundamentally different ways: attributively and referentially. The ensuing philosophical debate centers on a critical evaluation of whether Donnellan’s distinction carries semantic import or remains solely a pragmatic feature of speech acts. To properly evaluate this, one must first rigorously analyze Russell’s theory of definite descriptions, specifically his treatment of truth conditions for denoting sentences where nothing fits the description. One must then examine Saul Kripke’s critique concerning the difference between speaker’s reference and semantic reference, which attempts to relegate Donnellan’s distinction to pragmatics. Finally, by examining the role of speaker belief—particularly through the lens of Donnellan’s famous “Smith’s murderer” example—one can resolve the tension between Russell’s entailment criteria and the intuitive truth values of referential utterances, ultimately demonstrating that Donnellan’s distinction carries profound semantic import.

Russell’s theory of definite descriptions, articulated in his 1905 paper “On Denoting,” was designed to solve two major philosophical problems: the problem of non-referring expressions and the problem of embedded contexts. Russell argued that sentences containing definite descriptions are not atomic statements about a single object, but rather complex logical propositions comprising three distinct claims. For a sentence of the form “The F is G” to be true, Russell argued, three conditions must be met: first, there must exist at least one thing that is F (existence); second, there must be at most one thing that is F (uniqueness); and third, that unique F must be G (predication). Logically, this can be formalized as: (∃x)(Fx • (y)(Fy ⊃ y=x) • Gx). 

Crucially, Russell’s analysis provides a precise account of truth conditions for denoting sentences where nothing fits the description. If there is no F, or if there are multiple Fs, the existential uniqueness condition fails. Consequently, the entire proposition “The F is G” is false. This was a radical departure from earlier theories, such as that of P.F. Strawson, who argued that such sentences are neither true nor false, but rather lack a truth value altogether because they presuppose existence. Russell insisted that “The present king of France is bald” is simply false, because the existential claim is false. Furthermore, Russell’s theory provides strict entailment criteria: if “The F is G” is true, it entails that there exists a unique F, and that this unique F is G. The semantic content of the sentence is entirely captured by these quantified logical forms. There is no room in Russell’s theory for a distinction between how a speaker uses a description to describe a class of things versus how they use it to point to a specific individual; the description functions identically in both cases, as a criterion of identity that must be satisfied for the sentence to be true.

It is precisely this rigid, truth-conditional framework that Keith Donnellan challenged in his 1966 paper. Donnellan argued that Russell’s theory, while capturing the attributive use of definite descriptions, fails to account for the referential use. In an attributive use, the speaker uses the description to attribute a property to whoever fits the description. The truth conditions are strictly Russellian: the sentence is true if and only if there is exactly one person who fits the description, and that person has the attributed property. In a referential use, however, the speaker uses the description as a device of abstraction—a kind of linguistic pointing device—to pick out a specific individual, regardless of whether the description actually applies to that individual. 

Donnellan illustrated this distinction with the now-famous example: “Smith’s murderer is insane.” Consider two scenarios. In the first, a speaker believes that Jones killed Smith, and that Jones is insane. The speaker utters the sentence attributively. Under Russell’s theory, the sentence is true if and only if Jones is the unique killer of Smith and Jones is insane. In the second scenario, the speaker knows full well that Jones did not kill Smith; the actual murderer is someone else. However, the speaker is speaking about Jones, perhaps to highlight his bizarre behavior, and uses the description “Smith’s murderer” as a label for Jones. Intuitively, the speaker is attributing insanity to Jones, not making a claim about the actual killer. Yet, under Russell’s strict truth conditions, because Jones is not Smith’s murderer, the sentence “Smith’s murderer is insane” is false. The tension, therefore, lies between Russell’s entailment criteria—which demand that the unique killer be insane for the sentence to be true—and the intuitive truth value of the referential utterance, which seems to be perfectly true if Jones is indeed insane, precisely because the speaker is talking about Jones. 

The central question that arises from this tension is whether the difference between the attributive and referential uses is a matter of semantics or pragmatics. If it is merely pragmatic, then the semantic content of the sentence remains Russellian in both cases, and the referential use is simply a case of conversational implicature or pragmatic enrichment. The speaker would be semantically saying something false (that the actual killer is insane), while pragmatically communicating something true (that Jones is insane). However, a compelling case can be made that Donnellan’s distinction carries semantic import, fundamentally altering the truth conditions of the utterance in a way that cannot be reduced to pragmatic inference.

Saul Kripke’s influential critique in “Speaker’s Reference and Semantic Reference” (1979) provides the most robust attempt to argue that Donnellan’s distinction is purely pragmatic. Kripke draws a sharp distinction between semantic reference and speaker’s reference. Semantic reference, according to Kripke, is governed by the meaning of the words and the descriptions associated with them. It is the semantic content of the expression that determines what it semantically refers to. Speaker’s reference, on the other hand, is an act of intention performed by the speaker, which may or may not align with the semantic reference. Kripke argues that just as the word “I” semantically refers to the speaker, but the speaker’s intention might be to refer to someone else in a different context (though this is a slightly different analogy), definite descriptions can be used to refer to individuals via speaker’s reference, even when the semantic content of the description fails to pick out that individual. 

For Kripke, this distinction mirrors the difference between the semantic content of an expression and the pragmatic act of using it. He argues that speaker’s reference is a pragmatic phenomenon, akin to ostension or pointing. Just as a speaker can point to an object and thereby refer to it, overriding the semantic reference of a word, a speaker can use a definite description referentially. On this view, Donnellan’s distinction does not change the semantic truth conditions of the sentence. Semantically, “Smith’s murderer is insane” remains the Russellian quantified statement. Pragmatically, the speaker intends to refer to Jones, and the audience understands this intention. Thus, the intuitive truth value of the referential utterance is not a property of the sentence itself, but of the speaker’s communicative intention. The tension between Russell’s entailment criteria and intuitive truth values is resolved by recognizing that the sentence says one thing (the Russellian proposition), but the speaker means another (the referential act). If Kripke is correct, Donnellan’s distinction is entirely a pragmatic feature of speech acts.

However, while Kripke’s framework elegantly separates semantic and pragmatic levels, it ultimately fails to account for the deep role that speaker belief plays in determining whether a description functions referentially or attributively, and how this role fundamentally alters the truth conditions of the token utterance. To understand why Donnellan’s distinction carries semantic import, one must closely examine the role of speaker belief in the referential use of definite descriptions.

Consider again the referential use of “Smith’s murderer is insane,” where the speaker intends to refer to Jones, who did not kill Smith. Under a purely pragmatic view, the speaker’s belief that Jones is Smith’s murderer is irrelevant to the semantic truth conditions of the sentence; the sentence is semantically false, and the speaker’s intention merely generates a pragmatic implicature that Jones is insane. But this view creates an intolerable problem when we consider the nature of speaker belief and its relationship to the truth of the utterance. When a speaker uses a description referentially, they are not merely implicating a proposition; they are using the description as a rigid designator for the individual they have in mind, based on their belief that the description applies to that individual. The speaker’s belief is not merely a psychological accompaniment to the utterance; it is constitutive of the referential act itself. 

If the speaker believes that Jones is Smith’s murderer, and they use the description to refer to Jones, then for that specific utterance, the description functions as a singular term for Jones. The truth of the utterance, therefore, depends not on the Russellian quantification over all killers of Smith, but on whether the individual the speaker intends to refer to (Jones) has the attributed property (insanity). If Jones is insane, the referential utterance is true. If Jones is not insane, it is false. The truth conditions of the utterance have shifted from the Russellian “There is a unique F who is G” to the referential “The individual I am referring to via this description is G.” 

This shift in truth conditions is not a pragmatic matter; it is a semantic one. Pragmatics deals with what is implied beyond what is said, but here, what is said—the content of the utterance—changes depending on the speaker’s belief and intention. The difference between the attributive and referential uses is not merely a difference in what the speaker means in a Gricean sense, but a difference in the semantic content of the token utterance itself. When a speaker uses a description referentially, the semantic value of the description in that context is the object of the speaker’s reference, not the object that uniquely satisfies the description in the world. This is precisely the insight that Kripke’s distinction between semantic and speaker’s reference struggles to accommodate. Kripke suggests that semantic reference is fixed by the description, and speaker’s reference is an act of intention that points elsewhere. But if the semantic content of the utterance remains Russellian while the speaker points elsewhere, then the speaker is semantically saying something false while pragmatically saying something true. Yet, intuitively, the speaker is not making a false statement about the actual killer; they are making a true statement about the person they are referring to. The truth of the utterance tracks the referent of the speaker’s intention, not the denotation of the description. 

This is why speaker belief is the linchpin in resolving the tension between Russell’s entailment criteria and the intuitive truth values of referential utterances. Russell’s entailment criteria apply strictly to the attributive use, where the description is used to characterize an individual within a class. But when a speaker believes that a description successfully denotes a specific individual, and uses it referentially, the belief licenses a different semantic interpretation. The speaker’s belief that Jones is Smith’s murderer transforms the function of the description from a quantificational predicate to a singular term. The sentence “Smith’s murderer is insane” is no longer evaluated against the actual world where Jones is not the killer; it is evaluated against the speaker’s epistemic state where Jones is the intended referent. The truth of the utterance is therefore contingent on the speaker’s belief and the properties of the believed referent. 

This does not mean that the referential use is entirely divorced from the semantic structure of the language; rather, it means that the semantic content of a token utterance is sensitive to the speaker’s epistemic and intentional states in a way that Russell’s purely logical analysis fails to capture. Donnellan’s distinction carries semantic import because it dictates which logical form governs the truth conditions of the utterance. In the attributive use, the logical form is existential and quantificational; in the referential use, the logical form is singular and referential. The difference between these forms is not a difference in what is pragmatically implied, but a difference in what is semantically asserted. If the distinction were merely pragmatic, then a speaker could make a false Russellian assertion while sincerely communicating a true proposition, and the truth of their communicative act would depend entirely on the audience’s ability to infer the speaker’s intention. But the referential use of a definite description is often transparent and direct; the audience understands the speaker to be talking about Jones, and the truth of the statement is judged by whether Jones is insane, not whether Jones killed Smith. 

Furthermore, the pragmatic view struggles to explain the phenomenology of referential misdescription. When a speaker referentially misdescribes an individual—using a description that fails to uniquely pick them out—the utterance still succeeds in communication. The speaker says “Smith’s murderer is insane,” intending to refer to Jones, and the audience understands them. If the semantic content remained Russellian, the audience would have to perform a complex pragmatic maneuver to extract the truth that Jones is insane from the false Russellian proposition that the actual killer is insane. However, the audience does not typically engage in such complex inference; they simply take the speaker at their word, treating the description as a label for Jones. This transparency suggests that the semantic content of the utterance has already shifted to accommodate the referential use. The speaker’s belief that Jones is the murderer functions as a semantic operator that shifts the reference of the description from the actual world to the speaker’s epistemic world. 

In conclusion, while Russell’s theory provides the indispensable baseline for understanding the truth conditions of denoting sentences—particularly in establishing that non-referring descriptions render sentences false—Donnellan’s distinction between referential and attributive uses cannot be dismissed as merely a pragmatic feature of speech acts. Kripke’s critique, which separates semantic reference from speaker’s reference and frames the latter as a pragmatic act of intention, offers a powerful framework for containing the distinction within pragmatics. However, this framework ultimately fails to account for the constitutive role of speaker belief in determining the truth conditions of referential utterances. When a speaker believes a definite description successfully denotes a specific individual and uses it referentially, that belief alters the semantic content of the utterance, shifting the truth conditions from Russellian quantification to singular reference. The “Smith’s murderer is insane” example illuminates this point: the intuitive truth value of the referential utterance depends on the properties of the intended referent, not the actual denotation of the description. Because the speaker’s belief fundamentally changes what is semantically asserted—and thus what must be true for the utterance to be true—Donnellan’s distinction carries genuine semantic import. The boundary between semantics and pragmatics is not as impermeable as Kripke’s framework suggests; in the realm of definite descriptions, the speaker’s intentional act of reference reshapes the semantic landscape itself.
