# Aesthetic Universality and Environmental Obligation: A Critical Evaluation of Matthew Faulconer’s Kantian Defense of Nature

## Introduction

Immanuel Kant’s explicit moral framework, anchored in the categorical imperative, notoriously restricts direct moral duties to rational agents. Because non-rational entities—such as ecosystems, non-human animals, and non-sentient nature—lack autonomous will and the capacity for moral legislation, they cannot serve as ends-in-themselves under the strict definitions of Kant’s *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* and the *Metaphysics of Morals*. Consequently, traditional Kantian environmental ethics has often been relegated to the instrumental domain: we have indirect duties regarding nature (e.g., not torturing animals because it hardens the human heart toward our fellow humans), but no direct duties *to* nature itself. This anthropocentric bottleneck appears to disqualify Kantianism from offering a robust foundation for contemporary environmentalism, which demands that nature possess moral standing independent of its utility or emotional resonance for human subjects.

In this context, Matthew Faulconer’s intervention offers a provocative and sophisticated re-reading of Kant. Faulconer argues that Kant’s third *Critique*—the *Critique of the Power of Judgment*—provides a legitimate, non-consequentialist foundation for environmental obligations that bypasses the limitations of the categorical imperative. By locating the bridge between nature and freedom in reflective judgment and judgments of beauty, Faulconer suggests that our aesthetic encounters with nature demand a universal assent that structurally mirrors moral obligation, thereby establishing a categorical demand to protect and preserve natural beauty.

This essay critically evaluates Faulconer’s argument. It investigates the dialectical tension between Kant’s ontological insistence that nature lacks inherent, objective value and the peculiar phenomenological claim that aesthetic judgments simultaneously lay claim to universal validity. Furthermore, it addresses the specific objection that consequence-based assessments of environmental policy are inherently arbitrary, evaluating whether Faulconer’s aesthetic transition can successfully resolve the crisis of non-arbitrary environmental decision-making without collapsing into utilitarian calculations. Ultimately, I argue that while Faulconer successfully uncovers a profound phenomenological link between aesthetic appreciation and ecological valuation, his attempt to transform this link into a binding moral obligation introduces deep structural strains within Kantian orthodoxy, ultimately revealing an unresolved tension between the disinterestedness of taste and the interestedness of ecological preservation.

## 1. The Kantian Impasse: Rational Agency and the Exclusion of Nature

To appreciate the necessity and boldness of Faulconer’s project, one must first map the boundaries of the Kantian impasse regarding the environment. In the *Groundwork*, Kant famously defines the moral community—the "Kingdom of Ends"—as a systematic union of different rational beings through common laws. Morality, for Kant, is inextricably tied to autonomy: the capacity of a rational will to give law to itself. Because moral worth requires an agent to act *from* the conception of law, and because non-rational entities are governed exclusively by physical causality rather than normative self-legislation, they fall outside the sphere of direct moral consideration.

In the *Metaphysics of Morals*, Kant directly addresses humanity’s relation to non-human nature, specifically animals and minerals. Here, he reaffirms that we have no immediate duties to non-rational beings:
> "Beings contained under the objects of mere experience, if they are not rational beings, have only a relative value as means, and are therefore called things, whereas rational beings are called persons, because their nature already marks them out as ends in themselves."

Crucially, Kant acknowledges that we have duties *regarding* these things. To violently destroy a beautiful landscape or ruthlessly mistreat a faithful dog is morally impermissible, but not because the landscape or the dog possesses intrinsic rights or moral standing. Rather, such acts violate our *duty to ourselves*. Cruelty to animals dulls the capacity for human sympathy, which is "so useful to morality in relations with other human beings." Similarly, the wanton destruction of beautiful botanical formations or majestic geological formations works against the cultivation of moral dispositions.

This "indirect duty" view has long frustrated environmental philosophers. It renders our obligations to nature entirely contingent upon human psychology. If a society could systematically bulldoze old-growth forests or eradicate endangered species without eroding human moral sensitivity—perhaps through psychological compartmentalization or desensitization training—Kant’s framework would have to condone it. For contemporary environmental ethics, which insists that the loss of a species or an ecosystem is an objective moral tragedy regardless of whether human observers are emotionally or morally damaged by it, Kantianism appears hopelessly impoverished.

It is precisely this impasse that Faulconer seeks to dismantle. By turning away from the practical philosophy of the first two *Critiques* and toward the teleological and aesthetic framework of the third, Faulconer asks whether the transition from nature to freedom can be enacted via the faculty of judgment (*Urteilskraft*), thereby generating a distinctively Kantian environmental obligation that is neither merely instrumental nor anthropocentrically solipsistic.

## 2. The Dialectical Tension: Disinterestedness vs. Universal Assent in Aesthetic Judgments

At the heart of Kant’s aesthetics lies a paradox that Faulconer must navigate: the judgment of taste is simultaneously entirely subjective and yet claims universal validity. In the *Critique of the Power of Judgment*, Kant defines the beautiful as that which pleases universally without a concept. This definition contains two vital moments that generate a profound dialectical tension when applied to nature:

1. **Disinterestedness:** To judge an object as beautiful, our pleasure must be entirely free from any interest in the object’s existence or utility. If I judge a forest to be beautiful because it provides timber, cleans the air, or generates revenue, my judgment is not pure; it is bound up with an empirical desire or concept of the useful. True aesthetic pleasure is contemplative, indifferent to whether the object actually satisfies our physical needs or moral goals.
2. **Universal Assent (*Sensus Communis*):** Despite this radical subjectivity—where the judgment is grounded merely in the harmonious play of the cognitive faculties (imagination and understanding)—the judging subject speaks with a universal voice. When I declare, "This alpine valley is beautiful," I do not merely mean that it pleases *me*; I demand that *everyone* ought to agree with my assessment, presupposing a common human faculty of judgment called the *sensus communis*.

Herein lies the dialectical tension for environmental philosophy. On one hand, Kant insists that aesthetic judgments do not attribute an intrinsic property or objective value to the object itself. Beauty is not a concept inherent in the mountain, the river, or the forest; it is a relational reflection of the subject’s internal cognitive state. Nature, in itself, lacks inherent moral or aesthetic value; it is merely a canvas upon which our cognitive faculties achieve a pleasurable equilibrium. 

On the other hand, the demand for universal assent treats the aesthetic experience as if it were objective. It binds the subject to a normative community, insisting that failure to perceive the beauty of nature is a failure of subjective refinement, a privation of the proper exercise of cognitive freedom.

Faulconer’s insight is to seize upon this universal validity and weaponize it against the strictly anthropocentric reading of Kant. If a judgment of beauty regarding a natural formation demands universal assent, and if the destruction of that natural formation forecloses the very possibility of this universally demanded experience for future subjects, then the act of destruction is not merely an aesthetic misfortune; it is a normative breach. 

However, this move creates a profound internal friction: How can an aesthetic judgment, which explicitly abstracts from the *existence* of the object (disinterestedness), ground an *obligation* to preserve that object's existence? If my pleasure in a sunset or an old-growth forest does not depend on the forest actually existing (since, phenomenologically, the pure judgment of taste concerns only the form of the object as presented to the imagination), why should I care if the forest is clear-cut tomorrow?

To bridge this gap, Faulconer must establish that while the *pleasure* is disinterested, the *experience of natural beauty* is inextricably bound up with the presupposition of real, existing natural forms that reveal a profound, non-human teleology.

---

## 3. Teleology, Natural Beauty, and the Bridge to Morality

To resolve the tension between disinterestedness and existence, Faulconer must move from the analytic of the beautiful to Kant’s treatment of the *fine art of nature* and the *teleology of nature*. 

In the second half of the third *Critique*, Kant addresses the way living organisms and nature as a whole exhibit a purposiveness *without a purpose*. When we look at a complex ecosystem, a crystal, or a flower, we cannot explain its form merely through mechanical efficient causality. We are compelled to view it teleologically—as if it were designed to be beautiful and harmonious. Yet, because we cannot cognize any intentional designer (without overstepping the bounds of theoretical reason into dogmatic metaphysics), this teleology remains *reflective* rather than *determinant*.

Crucially, Kant singles out *natural beauty* as having a privileged status over *artistic beauty*. Art is beautiful when it looks like nature; nature is beautiful when it looks like art. But nature’s art is unconscious. In §42 of the third *Critique*, Kant writes:
> "A beauty of nature is a beautiful thing; art is a beautiful representation of a thing."

When we contemplate natural beauty, we are not merely enjoying symmetrical lines or pleasing colors; we are encountering a profound attunement between the forms of nature and the requirements of our cognitive faculties. Nature seems to meet our capacity for understanding halfway. It presents us with an infinite, inexhaustible array of forms that stimulate the free play of imagination and understanding.

Faulconer argues that this systematic meeting of nature and mind is the linchpin for environmental obligations. Because natural beauty cannot be mechanically reproduced or artificially substituted in the way a painting can be forged or replaced, the destruction of natural beauty represents an irreparable loss to the *sensus communis*—the shared human community of judgment. 

Moreover, Kant links the appreciation of natural beauty directly to morality in §29 ("Methodology of Taste"). He writes that "the propension of the mind to [moral feeling] is bound up with the enjoyment of natural beauty." While this sounds dangerously close to the old instrumental argument (nature as a training ground for morality), Faulconer reads it with greater ontological weight. The experience of natural beauty is the *symbol* of morality. When we take a disinterested pleasure in the form of nature, our mind experiences a transition from sensory receptivity to intellectual freedom. Nature’s purposiveness prefigures, in a sensible mode, the kingdom of ends.

Thus, the dialectical bridge is constructed:
1. Nature presents forms that spontaneously solicit our cognitive faculties into a harmonious free play, distinct from any conceptual or utilitarian interest.
2. This judgment of beauty lays claim to universal human assent, grounding a normative community of subjects attuned to natural form.
3. The existence of these natural forms is therefore a necessary condition for the actualization of this universal aesthetic community and its symbolic attunement with moral freedom.
4. Therefore, to destroy natural beauty is to undermine the sensible conditions under which human beings can experience the harmony of nature and freedom, generating a non-instrumental, categorical obligation to preserve it.

## 4. The Problem of Arbitrariness and Environmental Policy

At this juncture, we must confront a major methodological objection that plagues both environmental ethics and public policy: **the problem of arbitrariness**. 

When modern environmental policy relies on consequence-based assessments—such as Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA), ecosystem services valuation, or utilitarian preference aggregation—critics routinely charge that these methodologies are inherently arbitrary. How does one quantify the economic value of an endangered species, the spiritual serenity of an arctic wilderness, or the atmospheric stability of a rainforest? Utilitarian and consequentialist frameworks reduce these incommensurable values to shadow prices, contingent human preferences, or subjective hedonic calculus. Consequently, environmental decisions fluctuate wildly based on economic whims, political cycles, and shifting consumer preferences. If a developer can prove that a shopping mall will generate more net human happiness (measured in jobs and tax revenue) than an undisturbed wetland, utilitarianism must, in principle, pave over the wetland.

Does Faulconer’s Kantian aesthetic framework successfully resolve this problem of arbitrariness, or does it merely substitute one form of subjectivity for another?

The skeptic’s immediate challenge to Faulconer is devastatingly direct: **Aesthetic judgments are notoriously subjective and variable.** If beauty is in the eye of the beholder—if my judgment that an old-growth forest is sublime is merely a contingent feature of my idiosyncratic psychological conditioning—then grounding environmental policy in aesthetic judgments seems to enshrine radical arbitrariness at the very foundation of law. If person A finds a clear-cut industrial zone aesthetically compelling (perhaps symbolizing human mastery and technological sublime) while person B finds it repulsive, how can Kantian aesthetic universality adjudicate between them? Does not "universal assent" in Kantian aesthetics collapse into a pious wish rather than a binding normative standard?

Faulconer’s defense against this charge rests on a rigorous unpacking of Kant’s distinction between *agreeable* (*das Angenehme*) and *beautiful* (*das Schöne*). 
* The agreeable is strictly subjective and private: "Everyone has his own taste (of sense)." If someone says champagne is agreeable, I do not dispute their subjective sensation.
* The beautiful, by contrast, lays claim to *necessity*. When I judge something to be beautiful, I do not say, "It is beautiful *for me*." I presuppose a voice of universal agreement. If others disagree, I do not assume my taste is merely idiosyncratic; I assume they have failed to abstract from their private desires, interests, or conceptual biases to properly engage the object's form.

Therefore, the universality demanded by aesthetic judgment is *not* empirical universality (which would be proven by taking a poll of what people like). It is *a priori* universality—a normative demand rooted in the shared structure of human cognitive faculties. 

By anchoring environmental policy in the *a priori* requirement of the *sensus communis*, Faulconer argues that we escape the contingent, subjective arbitrariness of utilitarian preference aggregation. We are no longer asking, "How much money are people willing to pay to save this species?" (a question fraught with wealth disparities, cognitive biases, and adaptive preferences). Instead, we are asking a normative, transcendental question: "Does the systematic destruction of this natural form violate the structural conditions under which human beings can experience the harmonious attunement of nature and freedom?"

## 5. Critical Assessment: Can Aesthetic Universality Replace Utilitarian Calculations?

Having outlined Faulconer’s proposed resolution, we must now rigorously assess whether this transition from aesthetic universality to moral obligation successfully resolves the problem of making non-arbitrary environmental decisions without relying on utilitarian calculations. I will argue that while Faulconer’s model provides a brilliant critique of utilitarian environmentalism, it faces three fatal structural hurdles within Kantian orthodoxy and practical policy formulation.

### A. The Inadequacy of Formalism for Ecological Complexity
The first problem is Kantian formalism. Kantian aesthetics is strictly formal; it concerns the *form* of purposiveness, abstracting from the material existence and biological complexity of the object. A pile of rubble or an oil slick on the ocean might occasionally present striking formal lines and colors that trigger the free play of imagination. Conversely, a deeply degraded, ecologically sterile monoculture pine plantation might look impeccably neat, orderly, and formally pleasing to the human eye, whereas a vital, biodiverse, and ecologically critical wetland might appear muddy, chaotic, and aesthetically displeasing to an untrained observer.

If environmental obligations are derived strictly from *judgments of beauty*, the Kantian framework struggles to capture the hidden, systemic realities of ecological science. Ecosystem health, trophic cascades, genetic diversity, and biogeochemical cycles are not matters of immediate aesthetic intuition; they require empirical, scientific cognition. 

Faulconer attempts to mitigate this by invoking Kant’s teleological judgment (the third *Critique*'s treatment of organized beings), but this introduces a dangerous slippage. Aesthetic judgment is immediate and formal; teleological judgment of organisms is conceptual and systemic. When we judge an organism as a natural purpose, we apply a heuristic concept ("it is organized and self-organizing"). But Kant explicitly denies that we can have theoretical knowledge of ecological wholes as moral ends. 

If we rely on aesthetic judgments to dictate environmental policy, we risk preserving nature solely for its picturesque qualities—favoring majestic mountains, charismatic megafauna, and postcard-ready sunsets—while ignoring ecologically vital but aesthetically mundane environments like deep-sea vents, peat bogs, or microscopic soil biomes. Far from eliminating arbitrariness, aesthetic formalism substitutes *picturesque bias* for utilitarian calculation.

### B. The Slippage from Aesthetic Universality to Moral Obligation
The most precarious step in Faulconer’s argument is the inferential bridge spanning the chasm between *aesthetic universality* and *moral obligation*. 

In Kant’s system, the faculties of cognition (First *Critique*), aesthetics (Third *Critique*), and morality (Second *Critique*) are rigorously demarcated. Aesthetic universality is a *subjective* universality. It commands assent, but it does not legislate action. I can recognize that you *ought* to find a sunset beautiful, but if you remain cold and unmoved, I cannot charge you with a moral transgression or invoke the coercive power of the state to compel your agreement. Taste cannot be commanded; it must be cultivated.

To leap from "this natural form ought to be judged as beautiful by all rational subjects" to "therefore, we have a binding moral/legal obligation to prevent its destruction" is to commit a category mistake. Kant himself is acutely aware of this boundary. In the *Metaphysics of Morals*, when he discusses our duties regarding nature, he explicitly grounds them in *moral psychology* (self-cultivation of the human heart), not in the intrinsic rights of nature or the direct demands of aesthetic taste. 

If Faulconer insists that aesthetic universality generates a *categorical* duty to preserve nature, he must explain how a subjective judgment of the free play of the imagination can generate a practical law that binds the will. Kant’s categorical imperative derives its normative force from the structure of rational agency and universalizable maxims. Aesthetic judgments derive their force from the free harmony of cognitive faculties. To treat an aesthetic demand as a moral imperative is to collapse the distinction between the agreeable, the beautiful, and the good—a collapse that Kant explicitly warns against in §14 of the third *Critique*.

### C. The Inability to Adjudicate Policy Conflicts Without Teleological Consequentialism
Suppose we grant Faulconer his premise: that the destruction of natural beauty violates the *sensus communis* and breaches our indirect or quasi-moral obligations. How does this framework operate when environmental policy demands hard trade-offs?

Consider a classic environmental dilemma: constructing a renewable energy installation (e.g., a massive wind farm or a geothermal plant) in a pristine wilderness area. 
* On one hand, the wind farm is necessary to mitigate global climate change—a moral imperative under Kantian duties of beneficence and ecological preservation on a planetary scale. 
* On the other hand, the wind farm will permanently alter and aesthetically degrade the pristine wilderness, violating the *sensus communis* of natural beauty.

How does Faulconer’s aesthetic Kantianism adjudicate this conflict? Kantian deontology struggles profoundly with conflicts of duties (*collatio officiorum*), and introducing aesthetic judgments into the mix exacerbates the paralysis. If aesthetic universality demands the preservation of the landscape, and the categorical imperative (via duties to humanity's future) demands carbon reduction, we are left with incommensurable imperatives. 

Without a consequentialist or aggregative metric (such as weighing the magnitude of aesthetic loss against the magnitude of ecological disaster), the decision collapses into either dogmatic absolutism or arbitrary judicial fiat. Faulconer’s rejection of utilitarian calculations leaves policymakers with no rational mechanism to weigh competing ecological and aesthetic values.

## 6. Toward a Reconfigured Kantian Environmental Ethic

Despite these critical limitations, Faulconer’s project performs an invaluable service for environmental philosophy. It forces us to re-examine the rigid silos of Kant’s architectonic and exposes the deep phenomenological affinities between aesthetic experience and ecological preservation. Even if Faulconer’s attempt to derive strict moral obligations directly from aesthetic universality ultimately stumbles over Kant’s structural boundaries, it points toward a more capacious, non-reductive Kantian environmental ethic.

To salvage what is viable in Faulconer’s thesis while avoiding its logical traps, we must reconstruct the Kantian argument not as a deduction of *moral duties to nature*, but as an expansion of **moral anthropology and environmental character**. 

Kant was right that we cannot have direct moral duties to non-rational things because duties require a reciprocal subject capable of being bound by a law. However, Faulconer helps us see that our duties *regarding* nature are far more robust than traditional anthropocentrism allows. If the capacity to appreciate natural beauty is, as Kant asserts, the "propension of the mind to moral feeling," then the systematic destruction of nature is not merely a localized property crime or an imprudent squandering of resources; it is an assault on the sensuous conditions of human morality itself.

When a society permits the unbridled devastation of its natural environment, it does not merely deplete its natural capital (the utilitarian concern); it systematically de-sensitizes its citizenry, foreclosing the reflective experiences that remind human beings of their place in a teleological order larger than mechanical efficiency. It produces an impoverished moral imagination, trapped in the hyper-rationalized, instrumentalized cage of what Kant called the *mechanics* of nature, entirely blind to its *teleology*.

Thus, while Faulconer may be overzealous in trying to convert aesthetic universality into a binding moral obligation akin to the categorical imperative, his diagnosis is spot on: **Utilitarian calculations and economic cost-benefit analyses are structurally incapable of capturing the normative weight of nature.** They reduce the awe of an ancient forest to a contingent consumer preference, rendering environmental policy perpetually vulnerable to market fluctuations and anthropocentric short-sightedness.

Kant’s third *Critique* offers a powerful antidote to this reductionism. It insists that human experience is not exhaustible by theoretical cognition and technical utility. There is a realm of reflective judgment—of beauty, sublimity, and purposiveness—where the human mind encounters nature not as raw material to be exploited, but as a resonant partner that awakens our highest cognitive and moral capacities.

---

## Conclusion

Matthew Faulconer’s reading of Kant represents a bold and imaginative attempt to bridge the notorious chasm between Kantian rationalism and environmental ethics. By confronting the dialectical tension between the disinterestedness of taste and the universal validity of aesthetic judgments, Faulconer attempts to forge a non-utilitarian, non-arbitrary foundation for environmental obligations that bypasses the anthropocentric restrictions of the categorical imperative.

This essay has argued that while Faulconer’s project successfully exposes the poverty of consequentialist and utilitarian environmental policy, his transition from aesthetic universality to binding moral obligation encounters severe structural obstacles within Kantian orthodoxy. Aesthetic judgments remain formal, subjective in their genesis, and categorically distinct from moral commands. Attempting to deploy them as direct policy drivers risks falling into a picturesque bias that ignores invisible ecological complexities while failing to provide a mechanism for adjudicating complex environmental trade-offs.

Nevertheless, Faulconer’s work succeeds in shifting the conversation. It demonstrates that Kant’s philosophy contains resources for environmental thought that far exceed the cramped boundaries of "indirect duties to humanity." By revealing how natural beauty acts as the sensible symbol of morality and the anchor of the *sensus communis*, Faulconer reminds us that our relation to the natural world is not merely economic or mechanical, but constitutive of what it means to be fully human, morally attuned, and capable of freedom. In an age of ecological crisis, recovering this Kantian vision of reflective judgment may be precisely what saves us from the tyranny of utility.
