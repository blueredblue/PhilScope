## The Grammar of Fear: Wittgenstein, Radford, and the Dissolution of the Paradox of Fiction

### Introduction

Why do tears well up in our eyes when Anna Karenina throws herself beneath the train? Why does our pulse quicken and our breath catch as Michael Myers silently rises from behind Laurie Strode? These are not merely rhetorical questions; they constitute the core of a persistent philosophical puzzle. The paradox of fiction, most famously articulated by Colin Radford, presents a seemingly intractable logical inconsistency: (1) we experience genuine, profound emotions for characters we know to be fictional; (2) we do not hold beliefs in the existence of these characters; and (3) genuine emotional responses logically entail a belief in the existence of their objects. The conclusion appears to be that our emotional engagement with fiction is profoundly irrational, a kind of cognitive and affective schizophrenia.

This essay will argue that Radford's paradox is not a genuine puzzle about human psychology, but rather a philosophical artifact generated by a deeply flawed, representationalist metaphysics of mind. Specifically, the paradox rests upon an untenable metaphysical distinction between mind-dependent fictional states of affairs and mind-independent reality, a distinction that presupposes emotions are inner, propositional states directed at objects whose existence must be independently verifiable. I will assess the claim that adopting Ludwig Wittgenstein's later philosophy, particularly his use-theory of meaning and the framework of language-games, successfully dissolves the paradox by dismantling the requirement that genuine emotional responses imply belief in the existence of their objects. Ultimately, I will contend that Wittgensteinian quietism—the refusal to theorize beyond the description of actual linguistic practices—reframes emotional expressions toward characters such as Michael Myers not as irrational psychological states, but as rule-governed, meaningful practices embedded within specific cultural and narrative contexts. By shifting our focus from the internal "what" of emotion to the external "how" of its expression, Wittgenstein provides a decisive dissolution of Radford's paradox, revealing it as a grammatical confusion rather than a substantive philosophical problem.

### Section 1: Radford's Paradox and Its Cartesian Inheritance

To understand the force of Radford's paradox, we must first appreciate its precise logical structure. In his seminal paper "How Can We Be Moved by the Fate of Anna Karenina?" (1975), Radford sets out a trilemma. First, we *are* moved—we feel pity, fear, or sadness—for fictional characters. This is an undeniable phenomenological fact; the tears, the racing heart, and the lump in the throat are real physiological and experiential events. Second, we do not believe that these characters exist. We do not, upon leaving the cinema, search for Michael Myers to warn him of his impending doom, nor do we call the police to report a murder in a Midwestern suburb. We possess a robust, unshakeable knowledge that the events depicted are not real. Third, it is a conceptual truth that being genuinely moved by something requires a belief in its existence. One cannot, for instance, genuinely grieve for a person one knows to be alive and well, nor feel terror at a threat one knows to be a harmless illusion. Since these three premises cannot all be true, Radford concludes that our emotional responses to fiction are fundamentally irrational—we are moved by things we simultaneously know to be nothing.

The apparent cogency of this paradox rests upon a specific, albeit often unacknowledged, metaphysical framework. This framework is a descendant of Cartesian dualism and Lockean representationalism. In this view, the mind is a private, internal theatre populated by mental states—beliefs, desires, emotions—which are distinct from the external, public world. Emotions are conceived of as inner episodes, typically propositional attitudes. To feel fear, for example, is to have a mental state directed at a specific intentional object (e.g., the dangerous bear, the oncoming car). The rationality of this emotion is then judged by its correspondence to the external world. If the intentional object exists in mind-independent reality, the emotion is rational; if it does not, the emotion is irrational, a mere phantasm of the mind.

Within this Cartesian schema, the fictional world is relegated to the realm of the "mind-dependent"—a shadowy, subjective domain of representations, ideas, or imaginings. The real world is the "mind-independent" realm of objective facts. Radford's paradox emerges precisely at the intersection of these two realms. The emotional response is a genuine, mind-internal state, but its object is a mind-dependent fiction. The logic of the paradox demands that a genuine emotion must have a mind-independent object to latch onto. Since the fictional character lacks this ontological weight, the emotion is left dangling, attached to a mere nothing. The paradox, therefore, is not a discovery about the nature of emotion, but a direct consequence of an inherited metaphysical picture that bifurcates reality into the internal (subjective, mental) and the external (objective, physical). It assumes that the intentional object of an emotion must be a *thing* in the world, rather than a node in a network of meaning.

### Section 2: The Unviability of the Metaphysical Distinction

The first step in evaluating the claim that Radford's paradox rests on an untenable distinction is to expose the fragility of this metaphysical bifurcation. The strict dichotomy between "mind-dependent" fictional states and "mind-independent" reality is not as clean as the paradox requires. Fictional entities, such as Michael Myers, are not merely private, solipsistic hallucinations confined to an individual's skull. They are intersubjective, culturally constructed artifacts. They exist within a shared social reality governed by conventions, narratives, and genres. The character of Michael Myers has a stable identity, a history, a set of properties (he is relentless, silent, nearly indestructible) that are publicly accessible and agreed upon by a community of viewers and critics. He is a figure in a language-game, not a private mental image.

Furthermore, the distinction between "real" and "fictional" is itself a rule-governed distinction *within* our language, not a fundamental ontological divide that language merely mirrors. When we say "Michael Myers is fictional," we are not making a claim about a peculiar kind of non-existent object; we are participating in a specific linguistic practice. We are indicating that the name "Michael Myers" functions according to the rules of a narrative discourse, not a historical or empirical one. The distinction is pragmatic and contextual. We know that the events of *Halloween* did not happen in the actual world, but we also know that they "happen" within the fictional world of the film. The problem with Radford's paradox is that it treats the fictional world as a mere absence, a void, rather than as a structured domain of meaning that has its own internal logic and coherence.

However, merely pointing out the fuzziness of the metaphysical distinction is insufficient to dissolve the paradox. One could concede that fictional entities have some kind of cultural existence, yet still insist that they lack the *existence* required to justify a genuine emotional response. The paradox's third premise—that genuine emotion requires belief in existence—seems to demand a specific kind of ontological commitment. To fully dismantle the paradox, we must attack this third premise directly, and this requires a radical shift away from the representationalist picture of the mind and toward a Wittgensteinian understanding of language and meaning.

### Section 3: Wittgenstein's Use-Theory and the Framework of Language-Games

In his *Philosophical Investigations*, Wittgenstein famously rejects the Augustinian picture of language, wherein words function as names that label objects, and the meaning of a word is the object it stands for. Instead, he proposes that "the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (PI §43). This seemingly simple dictum has profound implications. It shifts our attention away from the referential relationship between words and things and toward the complex, rule-governed activities in which words are embedded. These activities, which Wittgenstein calls "language-games," are not merely linguistic exchanges but are interwoven with actions, gestures, and social practices. They constitute what he calls "forms of life."

For Wittgenstein, the meaning of an emotion word—"fear," "sadness," "pity"—is not given by a private, inner sensation to which the word refers. Instead, its meaning is determined by its role in various language-games. We learn the meaning of "fear" not by being taught to introspect, but by learning when it is appropriate to use the word, how it is expressed (trembling, screaming, running), and what it justifies (seeking help, avoiding danger). The language-game of fear includes a vast array of contexts: the fear of a real threat, the fear of a fictional monster, the fear of public speaking, the fear of God. These are not all instances of a single, identical inner state; they are family-resemblance concepts, connected by overlapping similarities in their use, expression, and consequences.

This framework provides the key to dissolving Radford's paradox. The paradox assumes that "genuine emotion" must be a singular, uniform phenomenon—an inner state that is either rational or irrational based on its object's existence. But Wittgenstein shows that "genuine emotion" is not a monolithic category. The criteria for what counts as a "genuine" emotion are not internal but external—they are the public, observable behaviors, utterances, and contextual appropriateness that constitute the language-game. Crying at a funeral and crying at a movie are both instances of crying, but they belong to different language-games. The former is part of the language-game of mourning, which involves beliefs about the deceased's death and the social rituals of grief. The latter is part of the language-game of aesthetic or narrative engagement, which involves a different set of rules and expectations.

### Section 4: Dissolving the Belief Requirement

The central claim of Radford's paradox is that genuine emotion implies belief in the existence of its object. Wittgenstein's use-theory dismantles this requirement by demonstrating that "belief" itself is a family-resemblance concept with diverse uses. We say "I believe it will rain," "I believe in democracy," and "I believe in you." These uses share a grammatical form, but they do not share a single, essential psychological state. Crucially, the kind of "belief" required for the language-game of fiction is entirely different from the kind of "belief" required for empirical claims.

When we watch *Halloween*, we do not form the propositional belief "Michael Myers is currently stalking me." We do, however, engage in a complex set of rule-governed responses: we gasp, we tense our muscles, we grip the armrest, we feel a rush of adrenaline. These responses are not predicated on a belief in Michael's physical existence; they are the appropriate responses *within the language-game of horror cinema*. The rules of this game dictate that we are supposed to be frightened by the killer, that we are supposed to suspend our ordinary empirical judgments (though "suspension of disbelief" is a misleading term—we are not suspending a belief so much as adopting a different set of criteria for relevance), and that we are to treat the narrative events as if they had a certain kind of significance.

Wittgenstein's insight is that the criteria for "genuine emotion" are not internal states but outward expressions and behaviors. The question "Is the fear genuine?" does not ask whether there is a particular inner quale present, but whether the behavioral and linguistic expressions are appropriate to the context. A person who screams and runs out of the cinema during a horror film is expressing genuine fear, even if they know the monster is fictional. The genuineness of the fear is evidenced by the scream and the flight, not by a hidden mental state that must correspond to an external reality. The paradox arises from a category mistake: it applies the rules of one language-game (the game of empirical belief and physical danger) to another (the game of narrative fiction). It is like asking whether the move "checkmate" in chess is a valid move in a game of checkers. The rules are different, and the criteria for validity are internal to each game.

Thus, the requirement that genuine emotion implies belief in the existence of its object is a grammatical illusion. It is a confusion born from the representationalist assumption that every meaningful expression must correspond to a fact in the world. But within the language-game of fiction, the emotional expression does not need to correspond to a fact in the physical world; it needs to correspond to the rules of the narrative. Our fear of Michael Myers is not a belief that he exists, but a rule-governed response to a particular configuration of sounds, images, and narrative structures. It is a response that we have learned to make, and it is meaningful precisely because it is embedded in a shared form of life.

### Section 5: Wittgensteinian Quietism and the Case of Michael Myers

To fully appreciate the dissolution of Radford's paradox, we must turn to what is often called Wittgensteinian "quietism." This is the methodological stance that philosophy should not construct theories or offer explanations, but rather describe the actual workings of our language. As Wittgenstein writes, "Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language" (PI §109). The task is not to solve problems but to dissolve them by showing that they arise from a misuse of language. In the case of the paradox of fiction, the quietist response is not to offer a theory of emotion that reconciles the three premises, but to show that the premises themselves are based on a misunderstanding of how emotion words function.

Applying this to Michael Myers, we can see that the "irrationality" of our fear is not a psychological defect but a feature of the language-game. The horror film is a specific cultural practice with its own internal logic. The startle reflex, the suspense, the catharsis—these are not irrational responses to a non-existent threat; they are the *appropriate* responses to the film's formal qualities. The quietist does not ask "Why do we fear Michael Myers when we know he isn't real?" because this question presupposes a flawed picture of fear as an inner state requiring a real object. Instead, the quietist describes the practice: "We watch a film. We see a masked figure. We hear ominous music. We feel a rush of adrenaline. We say 'I was so scared!'" This description is complete. There is no further philosophical work to be done.

The quietist stance reframes emotional expressions toward characters like Michael Myers as rule-governed linguistic practices. The rules of the horror genre dictate that we are supposed to feel suspense, that we are supposed to identify with the protagonist, that we are supposed to react with fear to the villain's appearance. These rules are not arbitrary; they are learned through cultural immersion. We know how to watch a horror film just as we know how to play a game of chess. The fear we feel is not a failure of rationality but a successful performance of a social script. It is a form of life.

This reframing has a profound consequence: it removes the stigma of irrationality that Radford attaches to the viewer. The viewer is not suffering from a cognitive delusion; they are participating in a meaningful cultural activity. The fear of Michael Myers is not a mistake; it is a skill. It demonstrates our mastery of the language-game of horror. We know when to be scared, how to express that fear, and what to do with it. The paradox of fiction, therefore, is not a problem for the viewer but a problem for the philosopher who tries to impose a monolithic, representationalist grammar onto a diverse set of human practices.

### Section 6: Addressing Objections and Clarifying the Quietist Solution

A common objection to the Wittgensteinian dissolution is that it reduces emotions to mere behavior, collapsing the rich inner life of feeling into a set of outward gestures. This is a misunderstanding of Wittgenstein's position. He does not deny that there are inner experiences; he denies that these inner experiences are the *criteria* for the meaning of emotion words. The inner life is not a private theatre that gives meaning to our public expressions; rather, our public expressions, learned through social training, give shape and meaning to our inner life. The fear we feel while watching *Halloween* is real, but its reality is not established by introspection. It is established by the coherence of our responses—the trembling, the shouting, the subsequent conversation about the film—within the context of the horror genre. The inner sensation is a shadow cast by the outer practice, not the other way around.

Another objection is that this quietist approach simply sidesteps the paradox rather than solving it. But this is precisely the point. Wittgensteinian philosophy does not aim to *solve* problems in the traditional sense; it aims to *dissolve* them by revealing them as pseudo-problems. The paradox of fiction is a pseudo-problem because it is generated by a false picture of language and mind. Once we see that "emotion" and "belief" do not have fixed, essential meanings but are used in a variety of language-games, the paradox loses its grip. There is no logical contradiction in being moved by a fictional character, because the rules of the fiction language-game do not require a belief in existence. The contradiction only appears if we insist on importing the rules of the empirical language-game into the fictional one.

Finally, one might argue that the quietist solution is too permissive, that it licenses any kind of emotional response as "genuine" as long as it follows some arbitrary rules. But this is not the case. The rules of language-games are not arbitrary; they are rooted in shared forms of life. The fear of Michael Myers is meaningful because it is part of a widespread, culturally established practice of horror cinema. It is rule-governed in the sense that it has a normative dimension: there are better and worse ways to experience horror, and we can be criticized for getting it wrong (e.g., laughing during a serious scene). The rules are intersubjective and public, not private and capricious. The quietist is not saying that anything goes; they are saying that what goes is determined by the grammar of the practice, not by an external metaphysical standard.

### Conclusion

Colin Radford's paradox of fiction is a compelling puzzle, but its power derives entirely from an unexamined metaphysical inheritance. It presupposes a Cartesian split between the mind and the world, a representationalist theory of emotion as an inner propositional state, and a monolithic conception of belief. These presuppositions constitute an untenable metaphysical distinction between mind-dependent fictional states and mind-independent reality. The fictional world is not a mere nothing; it is a structured, intersubjective domain of meaning, and our emotional responses to it are not irrational anomalies but coherent, rule-governed practices.

By adopting Wittgenstein's use-theory of meaning and the framework of language-games, we can successfully dissolve the paradox. The requirement that genuine emotional responses imply belief in the existence of their objects is a grammatical confusion. "Emotion" and "belief" are family-resemblance concepts whose meanings are determined by their use in specific contexts. The language-game of fiction has its own internal rules, and within that game, the fear of Michael Myers is a perfectly rational, genuine response. It is not a belief in his physical existence but a mastery of the conventions of horror.

Wittgensteinian quietism, in particular, offers a liberating reframing. It asks us to stop looking for hidden mental mechanisms or metaphysical justifications and instead to look at the public, observable practices that constitute our emotional lives. The tears we shed for Anna Karenina and the fear we feel for Michael Myers are not evidence of irrationality; they are evidence of our deep immersion in the rich tapestry of human culture. The paradox of fiction is not a problem to be solved but a spell to be broken. Once we see that the meaning of our emotional expressions lies in their use, the spell is broken, and we are free to enjoy the movie—or the novel—without philosophical anxiety. The only irrationality lies in the philosopher who insists on applying the wrong grammar to the right practice.
