# Unknown Defeaters, Defeaters of Defeaters, and the Limits of Klein’s Fourth Condition

## Introduction

The Gettier problem arises from the apparent failure of the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief. Edmund Gettier’s cases show that a person may possess a belief that is true and justified while nevertheless lacking knowledge, because the truth of the belief depends upon an epistemically accidental connection between the believer’s evidence and reality. The familiar lesson is that justification and truth are not sufficient for knowledge. Something more is required: an appropriate relation between the believer’s grounds and the truth of the proposition believed.

Peter Klein’s proposed solution is a defeasibility condition. In broad outline, Klein holds that propositional knowledge requires not merely truth, belief, and justification, but also the absence of a relevant defeater. A person knows that \(p\), roughly, only if there is no true proposition \(q\) such that, were the person justified in believing \(q\), that justification would defeat the person’s justification for \(p\). This fourth condition is designed to exclude Gettier cases. In a standard stopped-clock case, for example, the proposition that the clock has stopped is a true proposition that would undermine the subject’s justification for believing the time. In a fake-barn case, the proposition that most nearby structures are convincing facades would defeat the subject’s ordinary visual justification for believing that the structure before her is a barn.

Klein’s proposal is attractive because it explains the epistemic defect in Gettier cases without abandoning justification as a necessary condition for knowledge. Nevertheless, the fourth condition generates a serious difficulty. It appears to exclude knowledge whenever there exists some unknown true proposition that would have undermined the agent’s justification had the agent been aware of it. This problem becomes especially acute when the allegedly defeating proposition is itself defeated by another proposition. If an unknown defeater has a further defeater, it is unclear whether the original defeater should continue to prevent knowledge. An account that counts every true potential defeater, regardless of whether it is itself epistemically defeated, may make knowledge depend upon the totality of hidden facts rather than upon the agent’s actual epistemic position.

Klein’s “felicitous-coincidence” principle helps expose the motivation for the fourth condition. The principle is roughly that a belief is not knowledge when its truth is a fortunate or felicitous coincidence relative to the believer’s grounds. The cases involving an unexecuted threat to the State Capitol Building and a delayed visit from a sister-in-law are intended to show that merely true beliefs can fail to be knowledge when their truth depends on circumstances the agent has not properly ruled out. Yet these cases also raise the question whether Klein’s standard is too strong. If every hidden circumstance that would have affected an agent’s justification destroys knowledge, then ordinary knowledge claims may become nearly impossible to sustain. People ordinarily know many things despite being unaware of remote possibilities, background circumstances, and facts that could have altered their reasoning if they had discovered them.

This essay argues that Klein’s fourth condition identifies a genuine feature of Gettier cases: knowledge cannot be based on a justificatory route that is vulnerable to an undefeated defeater. However, the condition is not plausible if “defeater” includes every true proposition that would have undermined the agent’s justification had the agent possessed additional evidence. The distinction between a defeater and an undefeated defeater is essential. A proposition that would defeat the subject’s justification but is itself defeated should not ordinarily deprive the subject of knowledge. Moreover, the existence of a defeater for a defeater can preserve genuine knowledge even when the agent is unaware of both propositions, provided that the higher-order defeating relation is objective rather than dependent on the agent’s recognition of it. Klein’s principle is therefore most defensible when understood as a condition excluding epistemically operative, undefeated defeaters—not all unknown truths capable of hypothetical epistemic disruption.

## 1. The Gettier Problem and the Appeal of Defeasibility

The traditional analysis of knowledge has three components:

1. \(p\) is true;
2. the subject believes that \(p\);
3. the subject is justified in believing that \(p\).

Gettier cases demonstrate that these conditions can be satisfied even though the subject does not know. Suppose Smith has excellent evidence that Jones will receive a job offer. Smith also sees ten coins in Jones’s pocket and infers that the person who will receive the job has ten coins in his pocket. Unknown to Smith, Jones will not receive the job, but Smith himself will, and Smith happens to have ten coins in his pocket. Smith’s proposition is true, believed, and justified, but its truth is accidental from Smith’s epistemic perspective.

The problem is not simply that Smith is ignorant of some fact. All ordinary knowledge is compatible with ignorance. I can know that there is a book on the table while being ignorant of the book’s publisher, its printing history, and the intentions of the person who placed it there. What matters in the Gettier cases is that the unknown fact breaks the explanatory connection between the evidence and the truth. Smith’s evidence points toward Jones, while the truth is secured by Smith. His belief is true for a reason different from the reason that justifies it.

Defeasibility theories try to capture this difference. A defeater is a proposition that would undermine the subject’s justification if the subject were justified in believing it. In a stopped-clock case, the subject looks at a clock and forms the belief that it is 2:00. The clock stopped at 2:00, but it happens to be 2:00 now. The subject’s belief is true and supported by apparently good evidence. Yet the proposition that the clock stopped is a defeater: if the subject learned that fact, the visual evidence would no longer justify the belief. The subject’s belief is true, but its truth is coincidental relative to the evidence.

Klein’s fourth condition aims to exclude precisely this kind of coincidence. It does not require that the subject actually possess all relevant information. Rather, it asks whether there is some true proposition that would defeat the subject’s justification if the subject were justified in believing it. This formulation is stronger than a purely internal requirement. It allows a subject to fail to know because of a true but unknown fact. In that respect, it resembles externalist theories of knowledge, such as safety and sensitivity theories, which assess the connection between belief and truth partly independently of the subject’s reflective awareness.

The attraction of this approach is clear. Gettier cases are often cases in which something is true that would rationally undermine the subject’s grounds, even though the subject does not know that it is true. If the fourth condition required an actual awareness of the defeater, many Gettier cases would pass as knowledge. Smith does not know that the job proposition is false, and the stopped-clock subject does not know that the clock has stopped. Yet those hidden facts seem relevant to whether the beliefs count as knowledge. Klein’s condition gives them a role.

The difficulty is that the same strategy threatens to make knowledge excessively fragile. It is one thing to say that a known defeater destroys knowledge. It is another to say that any true fact that would have defeated justification if known destroys knowledge, even where the fact is remote, epistemically irrelevant in context, or itself defeated by further evidence.

## 2. What Is the Fourth Condition Supposed to Require?

Klein’s fourth condition can be interpreted in at least two ways.

On a weak interpretation, it says that the subject must have no actual undefeated defeater. A defeater is “actual” when it is part of the subject’s epistemic situation in a way that genuinely undermines the subject’s justification, even if the subject is not consciously aware of its significance. On this reading, an unknown fact can defeat knowledge, but only if it is not neutralized by other epistemic facts.

On a strong interpretation, the condition says that there must be no true proposition that would defeat the subject’s justification if the subject were justified in believing it. This interpretation treats the mere existence of a potential defeater as sufficient. The subject need not have any reason to suspect the defeater, and the defeater need not be psychologically operative. Its truth and counterfactual capacity to undermine are enough.

The strong interpretation is what generates the most troubling cases. It appears to imply that knowledge is sensitive to indefinitely many hidden background circumstances. Consider a simple proposition such as:

> The State Capitol Building will not be bombed today.

Suppose a person believes this on the basis of ordinary background knowledge: no credible warning has been issued, no suspicious activity has been observed, and the building is functioning normally. The proposition is true. Now suppose, unbeknownst to the person, someone has issued a threat to bomb the building but has not carried it out. The threat is a true proposition. If the person became justified in believing that a threat had been made, the person’s ordinary grounds for believing that the building would not be bombed would presumably be weakened or defeated. Under the strong version of Klein’s fourth condition, the person does not know that the building will not be bombed.

But this result seems to depend heavily on how the example is described. If the threat was credible, specific, and recent, then it is understandable that learning of it would defeat the person’s justification. The belief may indeed be a lucky one. If the threat was publicly known but the subject happened not to hear about it, the subject’s claim to knowledge is especially doubtful. The subject’s confidence rests upon an epistemically defective background picture.

Yet the case becomes less compelling if the threat was frivolous, anonymous, immediately discredited, or made by someone with no capacity to act. In those circumstances, the proposition that “someone issued a threat” might not defeat the justification at all. It may be true that the subject would reconsider upon learning of the threat, but mere reconsideration is not the same as defeat. A person can rationally suspend judgment when presented with weak or ambiguous evidence without thereby losing knowledge of the proposition previously known.

This distinction is crucial. A defeater should not be defined simply as any fact that would cause a psychologically cautious person to hesitate. Epistemic defeat concerns the loss of justification, not merely a possible change in confidence. If a proposition is sufficiently weak, irrelevant, or itself undermined, its discovery may require investigation without destroying knowledge.

The Capitol example therefore does not establish that every hidden threat defeats knowledge. It establishes, at most, that some hidden circumstances can undermine an otherwise justified belief. The challenge is to specify which ones.

## 3. Felicitous Coincidence and the Capitol Building Case

Klein’s felicitous-coincidence principle expresses the intuition that knowledge excludes certain kinds of epistemic luck. A belief is felicitously true when it is true in a way that fits the believer’s grounds. A belief is merely coincidentally true when its truth is secured by circumstances disconnected from those grounds. The principle is valuable because it identifies what is wrong with many Gettier cases without requiring that all luck be eliminated. Ordinary knowledge is compatible with a great deal of luck. I may know that a tossed coin landed heads even though it could easily have landed tails. What knowledge excludes is not every form of contingency, but a mismatch between the basis of belief and the fact that makes the belief true.

The Capitol case can be understood as an attempt to show such a mismatch. Imagine that the subject believes the building will not be bombed merely because nothing unusual appears to be happening. Unknown to the subject, a serious threat has been made. The threat is not carried out, so the belief is true. Its truth, however, is not explained by the subject’s evidence. The subject is right only because the threat fails to materialize. The belief therefore looks like a lucky true belief rather than knowledge.

The example is persuasive if the threat would have made the subject’s evidence unreliable. But it is not persuasive merely because there exists a true proposition that would have been relevant had it been known. For example, suppose a person somewhere once jokingly mentioned bombing the Capitol, and the remark never came to the attention of anyone capable of acting on it. The proposition that the remark occurred is true. If the subject learned of it, the subject might briefly become concerned. But the remark does not necessarily defeat the subject’s justification. To treat it as a defeater would be to confuse relevance in a broad practical sense with epistemic defeat.

There is also a temporal issue. If the threat is made and then withdrawn, or if the relevant authorities conclusively establish that it was a hoax, the resulting epistemic situation may support knowledge that the building will not be bombed. The original threat is not simply absent; it is defeated by later information. This suggests that the right object of evaluation is not the set of all true propositions capable of initially undermining confidence. It is the set of live, undefeated considerations remaining after the evidence is properly assessed.

Suppose the subject does not know either that the threat was made or that it was canceled. Does the cancellation preserve knowledge? If the defeater-defeater is objective—if the threat really was canceled, and the cancellation genuinely neutralizes the threat—then there is a strong case that it does. The subject may be ignorant of an entire chain of facts, but the original threat does not make the belief unsafe because it no longer creates a genuine possibility of the contrary. The truth of the proposition is not lucky in the relevant sense. The threat’s failure is not an accidental rescue of a belief that remains epistemically vulnerable; it is a fact that removes the threat’s capacity to undermine.

However, an internalist might resist this conclusion. If the subject has no awareness of either the threat or its cancellation, then the subject’s actual reasons remain exactly as they were. The subject cannot cite the cancellation as a reason. If knowledge requires justification accessible to the subject, then the existence of a defeater-defeater may not help. This reveals a deeper disagreement about what justification is. On an internalist conception, justification depends on reasons available from the subject’s perspective. On an externalist conception, a subject can know through a reliable or appropriately connected process even without recognizing all the facts that make the process reliable.

Klein’s condition appears to occupy an unstable middle position. It is externalist in allowing unknown defeaters to matter, but it describes them in counterfactual terms involving what would happen if the subject were justified in believing them. This makes it unclear whether the relevant defeat is a fact about the world, a fact about the subject’s rational perspective, or a fact about the relation between the two.

## 4. The Sister-in-Law Case and the Problem of Hidden Background Conditions

The delayed-visit case raises similar issues in a more ordinary setting. Suppose a person believes:

> My sister-in-law is not visiting today.

Perhaps the person believes this because the sister-in-law normally announces visits, no plans have been discussed, and the person has received no indication that she is coming. In fact, the sister-in-law intended to visit, but her journey was delayed. She will not arrive today. The belief is true. Yet the subject’s grounds may seem defective because the truth depends on an unrecognized circumstance: the sister-in-law’s delayed arrival.

The case is meant to suggest that the subject’s belief is true only by coincidence. The subject’s evidence supports the proposition that no visit was planned or expected, but the actual reason the sister-in-law is not present is that her visit has been delayed. If the subject had known of the intended visit, the ordinary evidence would no longer support the belief that she was not coming. Thus the hidden fact appears to be a defeater.

But again, the force of the case depends on what exactly the proposition is. “My sister-in-law is not visiting today” may mean that she will not be physically present, that she has no intention of visiting, or that no visit has been arranged. These are different propositions. If the subject means “she will not be physically present today,” then the delay may make the belief true in a way that is not connected to the evidence. If the subject means “there is no scheduled visit today,” then the fact that she intended to visit but was delayed may be irrelevant or even compatible with the belief’s evidential basis.

The example also illustrates the danger of demanding too much informational completeness from ordinary knowledge. In ordinary life, people know that someone is not coming without knowing all the reasons why that person is not coming. I can know that a colleague is not in the office without knowing whether she is sick, traveling, working from home, or delayed by traffic. The fact that one of these hidden explanations would have altered my expectations does not automatically defeat my knowledge of her absence.

The relevant distinction is between a hidden fact that makes the belief true for the wrong reason and a hidden fact that merely supplies an unknown explanation. If I see that the office is empty, and my colleague is in fact absent, my visual evidence may justify my belief that she is not there even though I do not know why she is absent. The delayed-visit case is different only if my evidence was supposed to establish a future-oriented proposition about what she would do, and the delay makes the truth independent of that evidence.

Consequently, the case cannot be resolved by asking simply whether there is a true proposition that would have defeated the subject’s justification if known. We must ask whether that proposition is genuinely incompatible with the evidential basis, whether it would actually undermine the subject’s reason, and whether it remains undefeated in the complete epistemic situation.

## 5. Defeaters for Defeaters

The most serious problem for a simple no-defeater condition is the possibility of higher-order defeat. Let \(p\) be the proposition believed, \(q\) a defeater for the justification of \(p\), and \(r\) a proposition that defeats \(q\). The question is whether \(q\) still defeats \(p\).

Consider the structure:

- The subject believes \(p\) on the basis of evidence \(E\).
- \(q\) is true and would undermine \(E\) if the subject were justified in believing \(q\).
- \(r\) is true and would undermine the subject’s justification for believing \(q\).
- The subject is aware of neither \(q\) nor \(r\).

If every defeater automatically defeats, then the existence of \(q\) prevents knowledge of \(p\), regardless of \(r\). But that seems wrong if \(r\) genuinely neutralizes \(q\). The threat to the Capitol may have been canceled. The sister-in-law’s intended visit may have been based on a mistaken message. A report that the clock stopped may itself be based on an unreliable source. In each case, the lower-level defeater is not epistemically decisive once the higher-level information is taken into account.

One response is to distinguish a defeater from an undefeated defeater. The proposition \(q\) is a prima facie defeater for \(p\), but it is not an all-things-considered defeater if \(r\) defeats it. Knowledge requires the absence of undefeated defeaters, not the absence of defeaters simpliciter. This approach reflects ordinary reasoning. We routinely encounter evidence that initially undermines a belief and then receive further evidence that restores the belief. The final epistemic status depends on the balance of reasons, not on the mere existence of an initially negative consideration.

A second response is to distinguish rebutting and undercutting defeaters. A rebutting defeater provides reason to believe that \(p\) is false. An undercutting defeater attacks the connection between the evidence and \(p\). A further proposition may defeat an undercutting defeater by restoring the reliability of the evidence. In the stopped-clock case, evidence that the clock was regularly checked and recently repaired may undercut the claim that the clock stopped. In the Capitol case, evidence that the threat was a known hoax may prevent the threat from undermining the subject’s ordinary grounds.

A third response is to treat defeat as a relation that is sensitive to the total evidence. On this view, \(q\) defeats \(p\) only relative to a specified evidential background. Once \(r\) is added, the relation may disappear. This avoids the strange result that a defeated defeater continues to operate indefinitely merely because it once had the potential to undermine.

The more difficult question is whether the subject must be aware of \(r\) for it to defeat \(q\). Suppose the threat was in fact canceled, but the subject does not know this. Does the subject know that the Capitol will not be bombed? An externalist answer can be affirmative, assuming the subject’s belief was formed through a reliable process and there is no actual remaining threat. An internalist answer may be negative, because the subject has no accessible reason that neutralizes the threat. The subject’s actual perspective remains vulnerable to the information that a threat existed.

There is no purely formal solution to this dispute. It depends on the intended role of justification in the theory of knowledge. If justification is a condition on rationally defensible belief, then awareness of the defeater-defeater may be required. If justification is a condition concerning the objective reliability or appropriateness of the evidential connection, then an unknown defeater-defeater can preserve knowledge.

Klein’s framework is best understood as favoring the latter approach, but only if his fourth condition is modified. The condition should not ask whether there is any true proposition that would defeat the subject’s justification if believed. It should ask whether there is any true, undefeated proposition that would defeat the subject’s justification, where the undefeated status is determined by the relevant epistemic facts rather than by the subject’s awareness alone.

## 6. Does Klein’s Principle Make Knowledge Impossible?

A natural criticism of Klein’s position is that it defines knowledge so strongly that ordinary knowledge claims become almost impossible. There are countless facts about any situation that the subject does not know. Some of these facts would change the subject’s beliefs if discovered. If each such fact counts as a defeater, then virtually no one knows anything.

Suppose I know that my car is parked outside. Unknown to me, a sensor has malfunctioned, a passerby has reported a possible theft, and a friend has received an unreliable message claiming that the car was moved. If I learned of these circumstances, I might investigate rather than continue to rely on memory. Do these facts mean I do not know that my car is outside? Surely not. They may create practical reasons for checking, but they do not necessarily defeat my justification. The mere possibility of error, or the existence of information that would rationally prompt inquiry, is not enough.

Likewise, I may know that a friend is at home even though there is an unknown possibility that she has briefly stepped outside, or that she plans to leave later, or that a visitor has arrived. Knowledge does not require that my evidence remain conclusive under every conceivable addition to the background. It requires a sufficiently strong connection between the evidence and the truth under normal conditions.

Klein could reply that his condition does not count every possible fact. It counts only true propositions that would defeat justification, not propositions that merely raise doubt. This reply is important, but it shifts the burden to the concept of defeat. If defeat is defined strongly enough, the theory avoids skepticism but risks becoming circular: a proposition is a defeater when it would defeat justification, and it matters only when it is an undefeated defeater. The theory then needs an independent account of what makes defeat genuine.

The Capitol and sister-in-law examples illustrate this pressure. If the hidden circumstances are stipulated to be serious and epistemically relevant, then it is plausible that they defeat knowledge. But if the cases are ordinary and underspecified, the examples do not show that knowledge fails. They may simply show that the subjects’ beliefs are not maximally secure, or that the subjects lack knowledge of why the proposition is true. A person can know that an event did not occur without knowing every background fact explaining its nonoccurrence.

The danger of an overly strong definition is especially apparent when the theory treats hypothetical justification as equivalent to actual epistemic defeat. Suppose \(q\) would defeat my justification if I became justified in believing \(q\), but I have no reason to believe \(q\), and \(q\) is itself defeated by \(r\). It would be implausible to say that I fail to know \(p\) merely because a counterfactual route to rational doubt exists. Knowledge normally tolerates unknown possibilities and does not require the absence of every potential defeater. Otherwise, the gap between knowledge and certainty would collapse.

A successful account must therefore distinguish at least three conditions:

1. **Possibility of defeat:** some proposition could, under some circumstances, undermine the belief.
2. **Actual defeat:** a true proposition in the subject’s epistemic situation undermines the belief.
3. **Undefeated defeat:** the undermining proposition remains effective after relevant counterevidence is considered.

Only the third condition is a plausible necessary condition for knowledge. The first is far too weak to matter; the second may be too strong unless carefully specified; the third best captures the Gettier intuition.

## 7. Can an Unknown Defeater for a Defeater Preserve Knowledge?

The central issue can now be stated directly: does the existence of a defeater for a defeater preserve genuine knowledge in the absence of agent awareness?

The answer should be qualified but generally affirmative. If the defeater-defeater is genuine, then the original defeater should not prevent knowledge. Awareness is not always required because knowledge is not identical with the subject’s ability to articulate every fact that makes the belief reliable. A person may know that a bridge is safe without knowing that a reported crack was repaired, provided the bridge is in fact safe and the evidential situation is not presently undermined. The repair need not be consciously represented if the person’s belief is based on an objective, reliable connection to the safety of the bridge.

This conclusion does not entail that all unknown higher-order facts preserve knowledge. The defeater-defeater must actually neutralize the defeater. A mere countervailing possibility is not enough. If there is a serious threat to the Capitol and the subject merely hopes that it was canceled, the threat remains an undefeated defeater. If the sister-in-law intended to visit and there is no reason to think the plan was abandoned, the subject’s belief that she will not visit may remain epistemically lucky even if she is delayed.

Nor does the conclusion imply that the subject is justified in believing the defeater-defeater. The subject may possess knowledge without possessing knowledge of the conditions that make that knowledge secure. This is a familiar feature of externalist epistemology. A person can know through a reliable perceptual process without knowing that the process is reliable. Similarly, a person can have a belief protected from defeat by an objective fact without being able to identify that fact.

Still, there is a limit. If the subject’s belief is based on evidence that is internally misleading, and the unknown defeater-defeater exists only somewhere outside the subject’s cognitive situation, calling the belief knowledge may seem wrong. Imagine that the subject sees a highly realistic fake barn, but, unknown to the subject, the fake-barn region has been closed and the visible structure happens to be the only genuine barn for miles. Does the existence of this background fact restore knowledge? Probably not. The subject’s visual belief remains unsafe in the relevant environment, even if some further fact happens to make the particular belief true. A defeater-defeater must not merely rescue the truth; it must restore the epistemic connection.

This point returns us to the Gettier problem. The issue is not whether the belief has some true explanation. It is whether the truth of \(p\) is secured in a way that is appropriately connected to the subject’s grounds. A defeater-defeater preserves knowledge only when it removes the threat to that connection, not when it merely supplies another accidental reason why the subject happens to be right.

## 8. A Revised Felicitous-Coincidence Principle

Klein’s felicitous-coincidence principle should therefore be retained but reformulated. The principle should not say that knowledge is absent whenever the truth of a belief depends on circumstances unknown to the subject. Nor should it say that knowledge is absent whenever there is a true proposition that would have caused the subject to revise the belief if learned. Both formulations are too demanding.

A more plausible version is:

> A subject does not know that \(p\) when the truth of \(p\) is accidental relative to the subject’s evidential route because an undefeated defeater disconnects the subject’s justification from the truth of \(p\).

This formulation preserves the anti-Gettier insight. In the standard cases, the subject’s evidence is undermined by an actual, undefeated fact. Smith’s evidence about Jones does not connect with the truth of the job proposition. The stopped-clock subject’s visual evidence is disconnected from the current time. The fake-barn observer’s visual experience is unreliable in the relevant environment.

At the same time, the revised principle permits ordinary knowledge. Unknown facts that merely explain the truth, or that would have prompted further inquiry without defeating justification, do not automatically undermine knowledge. Nor do defeaters that are themselves defeated. The principle recognizes epistemic hierarchy: a reason against a belief can be neutralized by a reason against that reason.

This revision also clarifies the role of awareness. Awareness is relevant to internal rationality, but it is not necessarily required for the objective preservation of knowledge. If the subject is unaware of both a defeater and its defeater, the subject may still know, provided the total epistemic situation contains no undefeated defeater and the subject’s belief-forming process is appropriately connected to the truth. If, by contrast, the theory defines knowledge as a form of reflectively accessible justification, then the subject may lack knowledge until the higher-order information becomes available. That is a coherent position, but it is a more demanding internalist theory than Klein’s defeasibility proposal by itself establishes.

## Conclusion

Peter Klein’s fourth condition is motivated by a powerful insight from the Gettier problem: a justified true belief does not amount to knowledge when an unrecognized fact disconnects the believer’s grounds from the truth. The concept of defeat captures this disconnect better than a simple appeal to justification or truth. Klein’s felicitous-coincidence principle is therefore a valuable expression of the idea that knowledge excludes certain forms of epistemic luck.

Nevertheless, the principle becomes implausible if it treats every unknown true proposition that would have affected the subject’s reasoning as a defeater. The examples involving the unexecuted threat to the State Capitol Building and the delayed visit from the sister-in-law do not by themselves show that the subjects lack knowledge. Their force depends upon whether the hidden facts genuinely undermine the evidential connection, rather than merely providing unknown explanations or reasons for temporary caution. If every such circumstance were sufficient to defeat knowledge, ordinary knowledge claims would indeed become virtually impossible.

The problem is resolved, at least in part, by distinguishing defeaters from undefeated defeaters. A proposition that initially undermines justification may itself be neutralized by a further proposition. When that happens, the original proposition should not continue to block knowledge merely because it once had defeating power. The existence of a genuine defeater for a defeater can preserve knowledge even in the absence of agent awareness, provided that the higher-order fact objectively restores the connection between the subject’s evidence and the truth. Awareness may be required for reflective justification, but it is not necessarily required for knowledge understood as an externally successful epistemic relation.

Klein’s fourth condition should therefore be weakened and clarified. Knowledge requires the absence of undefeated defeaters, not the absence of all unknown potential defeaters. The felicity of a coincidence is not destroyed by every hidden fact; it is destroyed when the truth of the belief remains dependent upon an epistemically accidental route that an undefeated defeater exposes. Understood in this way, defeasibility remains a compelling response to Gettier, while avoiding the skeptical consequence that no ordinary belief can survive the infinite complexity of the hidden world.
