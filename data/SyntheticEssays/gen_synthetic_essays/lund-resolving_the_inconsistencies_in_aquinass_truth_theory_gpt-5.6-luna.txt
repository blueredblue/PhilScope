# Human Truth, Divine Truth, and the Limits of Aquinas’s Theory of Truth

Thomas Aquinas’s account of truth appears to contain several tensions. On the one hand, truth is commonly described in correspondence-theoretical terms: an intellect is true when it conforms to reality, and a proposition is true when what it asserts corresponds to the way things are. On the other hand, Aquinas identifies God with truth itself and treats divine truth as eternal, necessary, and immutable. Human judgments, however, are finite, historically situated, and capable of changing from truth to falsity. A statement such as “Socrates is sitting” may be true at one moment and false at another, while divine truth cannot undergo alteration. Aquinas must therefore explain how truth can be both changeable and immutable, both many and one, both dependent on created reality and grounded in the divine intellect.

The distinction between human truth and divine truth offers a powerful way of organizing these apparently conflicting claims. It distinguishes different orders of truth rather than treating “truth” as a univocal property operating identically in every case. Human truth is the conformity of a finite intellect to things; divine truth is the perfect conformity, or more precisely the identity, of the divine intellect with its own essence and with the principles of all created reality. In this framework, propositions can change without implying that truth itself changes in God. The distinction also allows Aquinas to preserve the correspondence theory of truth while situating it within a broader metaphysical structure: created things are measured by divine knowledge, while human knowledge is measured by created things.

Nevertheless, the distinction does not eliminate every difficulty. It risks making truth depend too heavily on theological premises that are not independently established. If the ultimate standard of truth is the divine intellect, then the theory may seem unable to explain truth without first assuming the existence and nature of God. Moreover, Aquinas uses “truth” in several related but non-identical senses—truth in God, truth in created things, truth in human intellects, and truth in propositions. His account explains the relations among these senses, but it may not always provide sufficient semantic precision concerning how one and the same term applies to them. The distinction between human and divine truth therefore successfully resolves some contradictions in Aquinas’s truth theory, especially the tension between finite change and divine immutability, but it does so by relocating rather than completely abolishing the deeper problems of metaphysical dependence and analogical language.

## Correspondence, Relativism, and the Problem of Change

The most familiar element of Aquinas’s account is its correspondence theory of truth. In a general form, correspondence theory holds that a judgment is true when it agrees with reality. Aquinas formulates this idea through the language of conformity: truth consists in the conformity of intellect and thing. The intellect is true when it says of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not. This formula preserves the ordinary intuition that truth is not created simply by assertion, belief, or linguistic convention. A proposition is true because reality is as the proposition represents it.

The theory responds directly to the problem of relativism associated especially with Protagoras’s claim that “man is the measure of all things.” If human beings are the measure of truth, then what appears true to one person could be true for that person, while what appears otherwise to another could be true for someone else. Truth would become reducible to subjective appearance. Aquinas’s position resists this conclusion by insisting that the human intellect is measured by things rather than being the ultimate measure of them. A person’s belief does not make a thing to be what it is. Instead, the mind must conform itself to an independently existing reality.

Pre-Socratic philosophy provides a useful background for understanding this issue. The relativistic tendencies associated with Protagoras threaten the distinction between truth and appearance. If perception or judgment is the measure of being, no stable standard remains by which competing beliefs can be evaluated. Heraclitean reflections on flux create a related difficulty. If everything is continually changing, then knowledge may seem impossible, since the object known would never remain sufficiently stable to be known. By contrast, the Eleatic emphasis on unchanging being suggests that genuine knowledge requires an immutable object, but at the cost of making the changing world of experience difficult to explain.

Aquinas’s response does not simply choose between an unchanging Parmenidean reality and a radically changing Heraclitean world. He accepts that created things undergo change while maintaining that their changing existence remains intelligible. A thing can change from one state to another without becoming unintelligible. The truth of a proposition concerning the thing may also change, but this does not mean that truth is arbitrary. If a proposition concerns a contingent fact, then its truth depends upon the fact at the relevant time. “The tree is green” may be true in summer and false after the tree has lost its leaves. The change lies not in an alteration of some eternal truth but in the changing relation between a finite proposition and a changing state of affairs.

This point is crucial. The correspondence theory does not require every truth to be immutable. It requires only that truth be determined by the relevant reality. The proposition “Socrates is sitting” is true when Socrates is sitting and false when he stands. The proposition has not become meaningless, and the standard of truth has not become subjective. Rather, the object to which it refers has changed. Aquinas can therefore preserve both correspondence and the reality of change.

Yet this solution generates a further question. If human truth depends on changing things, what guarantees that the changing world remains intelligible? Why should finite objects possess determinate natures and relations that the intellect can know? Aquinas’s answer appeals to divine intellect. Created things are not self-explanatory units of intelligibility; they derive their forms and ordered existence from God. The world can serve as a measure for human knowledge because it is itself measured by divine knowledge. This introduces a hierarchy of truth: human intellect is measured by things, things are measured by the divine intellect, and the divine intellect is not measured by anything outside itself.

## Truth in Human Intellects and Propositions

Aquinas’s account is more precise than the simple claim that truth is “agreement” between a thought and an object. Strictly speaking, truth belongs primarily to the intellect. Things are called true only in a derivative sense. A stone does not make a judgment, and therefore it is not true in exactly the same way that a proposition is true. The intellect is the primary bearer of truth because truth involves knowing or asserting reality as it is.

This emphasis helps distinguish between a thing’s being and the truth of a judgment about it. A thing exists independently of anyone’s knowing it, but a proposition about that thing is true only when the proposition conforms to it. Thus, the proposition “snow is white” is true because snow is white; snow does not become white because the proposition is asserted. The direction of dependence in human knowledge runs from reality to the intellect.

At the same time, Aquinas also speaks of truth in things. A created thing is true insofar as it fulfills the intention or idea according to which it was made. A well-made artifact is true to its design, and a natural thing is true insofar as it possesses the form proper to its nature. In this sense, a thing’s truth is its conformity to the divine intellect. The natural world is intelligible because it has been produced according to divine ideas. Truth in things is therefore not primarily a relation between a created object and a human observer. It is a relation between the object and the divine intellect.

This creates two directions of conformity. Human truth is a conformity of the human intellect to things. Truth in things is a conformity of things to the divine intellect. These relations are not identical, but they are ordered. A human judgment is true because it conforms to a created reality, and that created reality is itself what it is because it conforms to divine knowledge. The theory thus incorporates correspondence at the human level while giving correspondence a theological foundation at the metaphysical level.

The distinction also clarifies why human propositions can be finite and changeable. A proposition is a linguistic expression formed by a human intellect. Its truth depends on what it signifies and on the state of the world to which it refers. Since human language and human acts of judgment are temporal, they can vary. A person may move from ignorance to knowledge, or from error to truth. A community may revise its scientific theories. A proposition about a contingent object may shift in truth-value as the object changes.

None of this implies that divine truth changes. Human truth is not divine truth in the same mode. Human truth is acquired, discursive, and dependent. Divine truth is immediate, complete, and identical with the divine act of knowing. The changing status of human judgments reflects the limitations of created intellects and the contingency of created things, not a fluctuation in the ultimate standard of intelligibility.

## Question 16 of the *Summa Theologiae*

Question 16 of the *Summa Theologiae* provides the central framework for this account. Aquinas first asks where truth is found and argues that truth is primarily in the intellect. Truth is the conformity of the intellect to the thing known. He then distinguishes the truth of the intellect that knows from the truth of things known. A thing is called true because it is apt to produce a true understanding of itself or because it conforms to a divine idea.

This distinction is already a response to an apparent contradiction. If truth is only in the intellect, then truth might seem subjective. If truth is only in things, then truth would seem to exist even without any act of understanding and would no longer be a property of judgment. Aquinas avoids both conclusions by assigning truth primarily to intellect while recognizing a derivative truth in things. Truth is neither mere private appearance nor an entirely mind-independent object in exactly the same sense. It is a relation of intelligibility whose primary realization is intellectual but whose foundation lies in being.

The question then turns to God as the first and highest truth. God is not true merely because divine beliefs happen to correspond to an external reality. God does not stand before an independent world and form hypotheses about it. Rather, God is the source of the world and knows creatures through knowing the divine essence and the divine ideas. The created order is measured by divine knowledge. In God, truth is not an acquired relation between a mutable mind and an independently changing object. Divine truth is inseparable from God’s own being.

This is the point at which Aquinas’s theory expands beyond a straightforward correspondence theory. At the human level, correspondence means conformity to things. At the divine level, it cannot mean conformity to something external to God. If God were measured by a reality beyond God, then God would not be the first principle. Aquinas therefore treats divine truth as the conformity of divine intellect and divine being, or as the perfect actuality of divine self-knowledge. Because God is simple and identical with the divine essence, the divine act of knowing cannot undergo accidental modification. God’s knowledge is eternal and immutable because God’s being is eternal and immutable.

Aquinas next addresses whether there is only one truth. The answer depends on the level under consideration. There is one first truth in God, but there are many created truths because there are many intellects and many things known. Human beings may possess different true judgments about different objects, and even several true judgments about the same object under different descriptions. These truths are not independent ultimate standards. They participate in and derive from the first truth.

This structure permits Aquinas to affirm both unity and plurality. There is one ultimate source of truth, but many finite realizations of truth. The plurality of human truths does not entail relativism because they are not self-authorizing. Their validity depends upon their conformity to reality, and reality ultimately depends upon divine intellect. Conversely, the unity of divine truth does not erase the diversity of created knowledge. Human beings know different aspects of a complex world through different concepts and propositions.

The question also considers whether created truth is eternal and whether truth is immutable. Aquinas’s answer distinguishes divine truth from created truth. Created things and created acts of knowing are not eternal in the same way that God is eternal. A human proposition can become false, and a created state of affairs can cease to exist. Thus, created truth is not immutable simply by virtue of being truth. Its stability depends upon the stability of its object and the meaning of the proposition.

This distinction is particularly important for temporal propositions. Consider “the door is open.” If the door is open at one time and closed at another, the proposition may be true at one time and false at another. There is no contradiction because the temporal conditions of the proposition differ. The proposition’s truth-value changes because its correspondence relation changes. Aquinas can explain this without conceding that truth itself is merely subjective or relative. What changes is the creaturely situation to which the proposition refers, not the divine truth by which all created reality is intelligible.

## Does the Distinction Resolve the Contradictions?

The distinction between human and divine truth successfully resolves several apparent contradictions in Aquinas’s theory.

First, it explains how truth can be both changeable and immutable. Human truth changes because human cognition and created objects change. Divine truth is immutable because it is identical with the unchanging divine intellect and essence. The word “truth” applies to both, but not in precisely the same mode. There is no contradiction unless one assumes that anything called true must possess identical properties in every context.

Second, the distinction explains how truth can be both dependent on reality and prior to reality. Human truth is dependent on created things: the human intellect must conform to what exists. Divine truth, however, is prior to created things because divine knowledge is the measure according to which they are produced. The apparent reversal is not inconsistent once the levels are distinguished. What is measured at one level can be the measure at another.

Third, the distinction allows Aquinas to reject Protagorean relativism without denying the plurality of human perspectives. Human judgments are finite and situated, but they are not therefore arbitrary. Their standard is not the individual subject’s appearance but the nature of things. Since different intellects can grasp different aspects of reality, there can be many true propositions without many incompatible ultimate truths.

Fourth, the distinction addresses the tension between the world of change and the demand for stable knowledge. Created things change, but their change is ordered according to intelligible natures. The divine intellect provides the ultimate ground for those natures. Human knowledge is therefore possible even though its objects are not immutable in every respect. One need not know an object as absolutely unchanging in order to know a true fact about it at a particular time.

Still, the success of the distinction is conditional. It resolves contradictions only if Aquinas is permitted to appeal to a robust metaphysics of God, creation, essence, and divine ideas. Without those premises, the distinction may appear less like a solution than a theological restatement of the problem. Saying that human truth is grounded in divine truth does not by itself demonstrate that such a ground exists or explain how finite knowers gain access to it. The distinction clarifies the architecture of Aquinas’s theory, but its persuasiveness depends on accepting the architecture’s theological foundations.

## The Objection from Theological Dependence

The most serious objection is that grounding truth in the divine intellect creates an untenable dependence on theology. A philosophical theory of truth should presumably explain what makes propositions true without requiring prior belief in God. Scientific, mathematical, and ordinary empirical judgments are often assessed through evidence, argument, and observation. If Aquinas says that their ultimate truth depends upon God’s intellect, one might object that he has not explained truth but simply placed it inside a theological system.

This objection has two forms. The first is epistemological. How can human beings know that their judgments are true if the ultimate standard is a divine intellect that they do not directly perceive? If our knowledge is measured by created things, perhaps we can discuss correspondence without invoking God at all. The additional theological claim seems unnecessary for ordinary truth.

The second is metaphysical. Even if God exists, why should divine knowledge be regarded as the ground of truth rather than merely one being among others that possesses perfect knowledge? To say that God knows all truths does not obviously entail that God makes propositions true. The relationship between knowledge and truth requires argument, not assertion.

Aquinas can answer that his appeal to God is not intended to replace ordinary justification. Human beings do not ordinarily verify the truth of “the tree is flowering” by inspecting the divine intellect. They observe the tree. The theological account addresses a different question: not how a particular person knows a proposition, but what makes the world knowable and what grounds the ultimate order of intelligibility. Aquinas distinguishes the immediate standard of human judgment from the ultimate metaphysical ground of that standard. Created things are the proximate measure of human knowledge; divine intellect is the first measure of created things.

This reply gives the theory philosophical significance, but it does not entirely defeat the objection. A critic may accept the correspondence theory at the human level while rejecting its theological grounding. The claim that reality is intelligible because God creates it according to divine ideas is a metaphysical explanation, not a neutral analysis of the concept of truth. It may be coherent and explanatory for someone within a theistic worldview, but it is not compulsory for every theory of truth.

There is also a danger of circularity. Aquinas’s argument may seem to run as follows: truth is conformity to reality; reality is intelligible because it conforms to divine intellect; divine intellect is true because it conforms to itself. But why trust the account of reality as conforming to divine intellect? If the answer is that this is what divine revelation or theology teaches, then the theory may appear to presuppose precisely the theological authority it is meant to justify.

The circularity is not necessarily formal. Aquinas does not have to claim that we first know divine truth and then infer ordinary truths from it. He can begin from contingent and intelligible features of the world and argue toward a first cause or ultimate measure. Yet the argument remains controversial. The transition from “human knowledge presupposes an intelligible reality” to “that intelligibility is grounded in a divine intellect” is not forced by the correspondence theory alone. It is a substantive metaphysical inference.

Therefore, the theological objection limits the scope of Aquinas’s achievement. The distinction between human and divine truth provides a unified explanation within a theistic metaphysics, but it does not establish that metaphysics independently of theological or philosophical arguments for God. It is best understood as a theory of the ultimate ground of truth rather than as a minimal account of truth that all philosophical positions must accept.

## The Problem of Semantic Imprecision

A second objection concerns the several applications of the word “truth.” Aquinas says that truth is found in intellects, in things, in propositions, and supremely in God. This raises a semantic question: Is “truth” being used univocally, equivocally, or analogically?

If the term is univocal, then truth must mean the same thing in God, in a human judgment, and in a created thing. But this seems impossible. Human truth involves conformity to an external object, whereas divine truth cannot involve conformity to anything outside God. A proposition is true by being correctly related to a state of affairs, while a created thing is true by conforming to a divine idea. These are not identical relations.

If the term is equivocal, then the theory loses unity. “Truth” in God and “truth” in human judgments would have entirely unrelated meanings. In that case, saying that human truth derives from divine truth might be rhetorically attractive but conceptually empty.

Aquinas’s likely answer is analogy. The meanings are neither exactly the same nor wholly unrelated. The term “truth” applies to different beings according to ordered relations. Human truth is the conformity of intellect to thing; the truth of a thing is conformity to divine intellect; divine truth is the perfect actuality of divine intellect and being. Created truths resemble divine truth because they derive from it, but they do not possess it in the same mode.

Analogy helps preserve both continuity and difference. It explains why one can meaningfully call God “truth” without making God merely a larger human knower. It also explains why created propositions can participate in truth without being eternal. However, analogy can seem to function as a mechanism for avoiding precise definition. Whenever a contradiction emerges, one may simply say that the terms apply analogically. Unless the relations are carefully specified, analogy risks becoming semantic imprecision rather than philosophical clarification.

The concern is especially acute in discussions of truth in things. To say that a thing is true because it conforms to a divine idea may mean that the thing has the nature it was intended to have. But “truth” here begins to overlap with being, goodness, intelligibility, and formal perfection. Aquinas himself connects truth and being as convertible notions, while distinguishing them conceptually. Every being is in some sense true because it is intelligible, but not every being is true in the same way that a proposition is true. The conceptual distinction is defensible, yet it places considerable pressure on the reader to track several related meanings.

Question 16 provides some structure, but perhaps not complete semantic precision. It establishes an order of priority: truth is primarily in intellect, secondarily in things; divine truth is first; created truth depends upon it. What it does not provide is a simple definition applicable without qualification to every use. Its method is metaphysical and analogical rather than strictly semantic. Aquinas is less interested in reducing “truth” to one unambiguous formula than in mapping the relations among the different realities called true.

Whether this is a defect depends on what one expects from a theory of truth. If the aim is a formal semantic theory, Aquinas’s account may seem insufficiently exact. It does not offer a modern account of reference, proposition structure, linguistic context, or truth conditions. If the aim is a metaphysical account of why truth appears in multiple forms, analogy is an appropriate tool. It allows Aquinas to maintain that all truth ultimately belongs to one ordered reality without pretending that divine knowledge and human assertion are identical activities.

## The Relation to Pre-Socratic Relativism

The contrast with Pre-Socratic relativism further reveals both the strength and the limits of Aquinas’s position. Against Protagoras, Aquinas denies that the individual human subject is the ultimate measure of reality. Human beings can be wrong because reality places an independent constraint on their judgments. This is a decisive anti-relativist point.

Against a radical interpretation of Heraclitean flux, Aquinas maintains that change does not destroy intelligibility. A changing thing can possess a stable nature through different actualizations. The intellect can know what changes by understanding both the thing’s present state and the principles governing its change. Truth need not require that every object be frozen in an immutable condition.

At the same time, Aquinas recognizes something important in the Heraclitean challenge. Human truths about contingent reality are often indexical or temporal. Their truth depends upon circumstances. The proposition “this person is healthy” can become false when the person becomes ill. Aquinas does not deny the variability of such truths. He denies only that variability entails subjectivism. Temporal relativity is not the same as epistemic relativism.

The divine-human distinction gives this answer metaphysical depth. If created things were merely flux without stable forms, correspondence would have no reliable object. If human beings were the measure of things, disagreement would lack an objective resolution. But if created reality is ordered by divine intellect, then change occurs within an intelligible structure. Human truth is finite because it grasps that structure incompletely, but it is truth because the structure is not constituted by human awareness.

This is a sophisticated alternative to both Protagorean subjectivism and a purely static conception of knowledge. The world need not be immutable in order to be knowable; it must instead possess sufficient determination and order. Divine truth explains why such order exists, while human truth explains how finite intellects participate in it.

## Final Assessment

The distinction between human truth and divine truth is one of the most effective features of Aquinas’s theory. It allows him to preserve the correspondence theory at the level of human knowledge while explaining why truth is not reducible to subjective appearance. Human propositions are true when they conform to finite realities. Those realities are themselves intelligible because they conform to divine intellect. Divine truth is not another proposition competing with human propositions; it is the ultimate measure and source of the order within which propositions can be true.

This framework successfully addresses the apparent contradiction between finite, changeable human truths and eternal, immutable divine truth. A proposition can change from true to false because its object or temporal circumstances change. Divine truth remains immutable because it is not an acquired relation to a changing external world but the perfect actuality of divine knowledge and being. Similarly, there can be many human truths without many ultimate standards, because finite truths participate in one first truth.

The theory also provides a strong response to Pre-Socratic relativism. Against the claim that human beings are the measure of all things, Aquinas argues that the human intellect is measured by reality. Against the thought that flux makes knowledge impossible, he maintains that changing beings remain intelligible through their forms and ordered relations. Truth is neither private appearance nor the possession only of an absolutely static realm. It is an ordered relation between intellect and being.

Yet the resolution is not complete in a way that would satisfy every philosophical standpoint. Aquinas’s ultimate grounding of truth in divine intellect depends upon a theological and metaphysical account of God that the correspondence theory itself does not establish. The theory therefore risks appearing sectarian or circular when presented as a universal solution to the problem of truth. It explains why truth has an ultimate ground only within the broader argument for divine creation and divine simplicity.

Likewise, Aquinas’s multiple uses of “truth” are unified through analogy, but analogy does not eliminate all semantic ambiguity. Truth in a human intellect, truth in a proposition, truth in a created thing, and truth in God are related but structurally different. Aquinas explains their order of dependence, yet the term remains flexible enough to generate confusion. The account is more successful as a metaphysics of truth than as a sharply delimited semantic theory.

The most balanced conclusion is therefore that the human-divine distinction is a qualified success. It does not dissolve every tension in Aquinas’s theory, but it shows that the tensions need not be contradictions. Human and divine truth can coexist because they operate at different but connected levels. Human truth is temporal, discursive, and measured by created things; divine truth is eternal, immediate, and the measure of those things. The distinction preserves objectivity without denying change and preserves divine immutability without making human knowledge impossible.

Its cost is that truth becomes inseparable from Aquinas’s larger theological vision. For those who accept that vision, Question 16 offers a coherent hierarchy in which being, intelligibility, and truth are unified. For those who do not, the divine grounding may seem unnecessary or insufficiently justified, and the analogical use of “truth” may seem imprecise. Aquinas’s theory therefore succeeds not by providing a neutral definition that settles all debates, but by offering an integrated account in which human correspondence, anti-relativism, created intelligibility, and divine eternity are parts of one metaphysical order.
