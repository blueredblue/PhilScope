### The Lookup Table and the Ghost in the Machine: A Dialectical Critique of Block's Rejection of the Turing Test

Alan Turing’s 1950 proposal of the “imitation game” was a radical philosophical gambit. By shifting the question from the metaphysical essence of thought (“Can machines think?”) to a behavioral performance (“Can a machine adequately imitate a human in a text-only conversation?”), Turing sought to dissolve a notoriously intractable debate into an empirically testable criterion. The resulting Turing Test (TT) has since become a cornerstone of artificial intelligence discourse, often treated not merely as a necessary condition for intelligence but as a sufficient one: if a machine passes the test, it *is* intelligent. Ned Block’s seminal 1981 paper, “Psychologism and Behaviorism,” presents a formidable challenge to this sufficiency claim. Through the construction of two hypothetical machines, M1 and M2—massive lookup tables containing exhaustive databases of conversational responses—Block argues that a system can pass the TT while being utterly devoid of intelligence. The argument is logically valid, but its soundness rests entirely on the intuitive force of its second premise: that M1 and M2 are not intelligent. This essay will evaluate Block’s argument, assessing whether his database-driven counterexamples can successfully pass the test under standard time limits, particularly in light of Daniel Dennett’s observation that the TT permits judges to ask *any* question. I will argue that while Block’s counterexamples can indeed pass under finite time limits, the dialectical foundation of his argument—the intuition that a lookup table is not a mind—is profoundly unstable. When we push the counterexample to its logical extreme, imagining a machine with unbounded sensible conversational abilities, the attribution of intelligence ceases to be intuitively clear and becomes a genuinely arguable philosophical thesis. This erosion of intuitive certainty undermines the soundness of Block’s rejection of the TT’s sufficiency, revealing that his argument, though powerful, does not decisively refute the behavioral criterion.

#### I. The Architecture of Block’s Challenge
