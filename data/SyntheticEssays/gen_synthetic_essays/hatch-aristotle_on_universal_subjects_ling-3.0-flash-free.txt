**The Ontological Weight of the Universal: Evaluating Gyekye, Englebretsen, and Aristotle’s Anticipation of Predicate Logic**

The problem of universals has remained a central axis of Western metaphysics since antiquity, pitting the reality of general terms against the ontological primacy of individuals. In the twentieth century, the analytic tradition frequently approached this problem through the lens of modern predicate logic, treating universal terms as linguistic variables devoid of independent ontological status. Kwame Gyekye, in his engagement with Aristotelian ontology, proposes a strikingly similar thesis: that Aristotle anticipates modern predicate logic by treating universal subjects as incomplete symbols dependent on primary substances. Yet this provocative reading has been forcefully challenged by George Englebretsen, who insists that Gyekye commits a fundamental category mistake by conflating a sentence’s semantic meaning with its ontological truth conditions. This essay evaluates Gyekye’s thesis through the critical lens of Englebretsen, ultimately arguing that while Gyekye correctly identifies the logical dependence of universals on primary substances, his reduction of universals to incomplete symbols collapses the rich metaphysical architecture of Aristotle’s theory of definitions and the distinction between synonymous and homonymous predication. By examining Aristotle’s prohibition of non-substantial subjects in the *Posterior Analytics*, we can resolve the dialectical tension between ontological reductionism and the necessity of universal knowledge, concluding that the prohibition functions as an epistemological constraint on demonstration rather than an ontological elimination of universals.

Kwame Gyekye’s thesis rests on the premise that Aristotle’s ontology anticipates the structural insights of modern predicate logic. In contemporary predicate logic, a subject term is not a complete ontological entity but a variable that only acquires semantic content and truth value when bound to a constant within a domain of discourse. Gyekye argues that Aristotle’s distinction between primary and secondary substances mirrors this logical structure. Primary substances—the individual man, the individual horse—are the ultimate ontological subjects; they are the "this-ses" (*tode ti*) that alone exist in fullness and serve as the ultimate subjects of predication. Secondary substances, by contrast, are the universals (such as "man" or "animal") that are predicated of primary substances. Gyekye contends that these universals are, in effect, "incomplete symbols." Like the variable *x* in the logical formula *Fx*, the universal "man" cannot stand alone as a subject of existence; it only becomes a meaningful, ontologically operative term when it is predicated of a primary substance. 

For Gyekye, this reading dissolves the traditional problem of universals by aligning Aristotle with a form of ontological reductionism. If universal terms are merely incomplete symbols, they do not possess the independent, floating reality of Platonic Forms. Instead, they are parasitic on the primary substances that instantiate them. The universal "man" is nothing apart from the individual men that bear the name; it is a logical tool that captures the commonality of form across instances without positing a third entity. Gyekye’s interpretation is compelling because it explains how Aristotle avoids both Platonic realism and nominalist elimination. Aristotle can affirm the necessity of universal knowledge—since science must proceed through universals—while maintaining that the ultimate furniture of the universe consists solely of individual substances. In this view, Aristotle’s ontology is not a reduction of universals to nothingness, but a logical reduction of their ontological status: they are real, but only as incomplete symbols whose reality is constituted entirely by their relation to primary substances. 

However, George Englebretsen’s critique cuts to the very foundation of Gyekye’s project. Englebretsen argues that Gyekye commits a category mistake by conflating the semantic structure of a proposition with its ontological truth conditions. To treat a universal as an "incomplete symbol" is to view it primarily through the lens of syntax and semantics—how a term functions within a sentence to generate meaning and truth. But for Aristotle, the ontological status of a term is not exhausted by its semantic function. 

Englebretsen’s objection centers on the fact that in Aristotle’s metaphysics, secondary substances are not merely semantic placeholders; they are the actual forms (*eidos*) and essences (*to ti ēn einai*) of primary substances. When we say "Socrates is a man," the semantic meaning of "man" requires a subject to form a complete proposition, but the ontological truth condition of the sentence is not merely the syntactic binding of a variable to a constant. The truth of the proposition depends on Socrates actually instantiating the form of humanity. The universal "man" is the actual essence that makes Socrates what he is. By reducing this essence to an incomplete symbol, Gyekye strips the universal of its ontological weight, transforming a substantial form into a mere linguistic device. 

This conflation of semantic meaning with ontological truth conditions leads Englebretsen to argue that Gyekye misrepresents Aristotle’s fundamental commitment to the reality of forms. In Aristotle’s ontology, the universal is not just a concept in the mind or a rule for language; it is the actual actuality of the substance’s matter. An incomplete symbol, by definition, lacks the full ontological standing of a complete subject; it is dependent on another term to exist. But Aristotle’s secondary substances, while dependent on primary substances for their instantiation, are still the actual essences that constitute what the primary substance *is*. Gyekye’s predicate-logical reading effectively collapses Aristotle’s hylomorphic metaphysics into a purely semantic theory of reference, thereby losing the ontological depth that makes Aristotle’s account of predication distinct from both Platonic realism and modern analytic nominalism.

The dialectical tension produced by this critique becomes most acute when we turn to Aristotle’s prohibition of non-substantial subjects in the *Posterior Analytics*. In this work, Aristotle establishes the strict requirements for scientific demonstration (*apodeixis*). A valid syllogism for scientific knowledge requires that the middle term be either a substance or in a substance. More specifically, Aristotle prohibits non-substantial subjects from serving as the ultimate subjects of demonstration, a rule often interpreted as an attempt to prevent an infinite regress of predication. 

Gyekye’s framework provides a straightforward explanation for this prohibition: if non-substantial subjects (universals treated as incomplete symbols) are permitted to serve as ultimate subjects, the chain of predication would never terminate in an ontologically grounded reality.
