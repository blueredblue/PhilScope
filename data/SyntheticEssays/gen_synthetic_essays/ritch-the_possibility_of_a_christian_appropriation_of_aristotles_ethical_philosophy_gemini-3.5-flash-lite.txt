# Immanence, Transcendence, and the Divine Spark: A Re-Evaluation of Nussbaum’s Aristotle and the Bridge to Christian Eudaimonia

## Introduction

The interpretation of Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* (NE) has long been a battleground for philosophical anthropology, caught between the immanent demands of civic and moral life and the seemingly radical, otherworldly claims of the contemplative ideal. At the center of this modern debate stands Martha Nussbaum. Through her seminal works, including *The Fragility of Goodness* and her numerous essays on Aristotelian practical reason, Nussbaum offers a resolutely anthropocentric, immanent, and pluralistic reading of Aristotle. For Nussbaum, Aristotle’s ethics is an investigation into *human* flourishing (*eudaimonia*), anchored in species-specific vulnerabilities, the incommensurability of a plurality of intrinsic goods, and an anti-foundationalist conception of practical wisdom (*phronesis*) where universal principles serve merely as provisional summaries of good decisions. 

Yet, this reading faces a formidable historical and textual challenge, most notably epitomized by the architecture of the *Nicomachean Ethics* itself: Book X. In Book X, chapters 7 and 8, Aristotle appears to elevate the contemplative life (*bios theoretikos*) far above the domain of practical virtue, describing it as something divine (*theios*), immortalizing (*athanatizein*), and exceeding the measure of the merely human. Critics of Nussbaum’s anthropocentric harmonization argue that this text introduces a transcendent hierarchy that shatters the unity of the treatise and anticipates the radical eschatology of New Testament theological doctrines, wherein the ultimate human good is found not in civic balance or rational self-realization, but in assimilation to the divine.

This essay evaluates the claim that Nussbaum’s interpretation of Aristotle misconstrues the relationship between the practical moral virtues of Books I–IX and the contemplative ideal of Book X. I will argue that while Nussbaum provides a profoundly illuminating defense of human agency, her attempt to domesticate Book X within an exclusively immanent, species-relative framework ultimately strains against the internal teleology of Aristotle’s metaphysics. However, rather than viewing Book X as a proto-Christian departure from Aristotelian immanence, or viewing Nussbaum’s reading as a fatal distortion, we can locate a deeper structural continuity. The tension between human vulnerability and the pursuit of the divine life in Aristotle reveals a profound metaphysical paradox that, far from merely anticipating New Testament theology, provides a coherent rational grammar capable of structuring—and being transfigured by—Christian doctrine. By examining Nussbaum’s core pillars—species-relative goods, the incommensurability of values, and provisional principles—we can map the precise fault lines where ancient Greek ethics meets the theological transformation of the soul.

## Part I: Nussbaum’s Anthropocentric Framework and the Immanent Good

To understand Nussbaum’s reading of Aristotle, one must first grasp her intervention against both Platonic foundationalism and modern utilitarian/Kantian reductionism. Nussbaum approaches the *Nicomachean Ethics* as a defense of "the fragility of goodness"—the tragic recognition that a truly good human life is exposed to the vicissitudes of fortune, loss, bodily decay, and relational dependence. 

### 1. Species-Relative Goods and the Internal Architecture of Human Flourishing
Nussbaum’s Aristotelianism begins with the concept of the "human being" as a specific form of life with characteristic functions, capacities, and vulnerabilities. Rejecting both a cosmic, external teleology that anchors human value in the divine order of the stars and a relativistic subjectivism that reduces value to mere desire, Nussbaum posits a form of *internal essentialism*. In her essay "The Discourses of Biological Vulnerability," she argues that Aristotle derives his account of the good from an inquiry into what it means to flourish *as a human being*.

Within this framework, *eudaimonia* is not an abstract state of bliss achieved by escaping our nature; it is the realization and exercise of our characteristic capacities—rationality, sociability, emotional responsiveness, and bodily existence—over a complete life. The goods constitutive of *eudaimonia* (friendship, political activity, philosophical contemplation, courage, justice) are valued precisely because they are good *for us*, given our biological and social constitution. They are species-relative goods. 

This anthropocentric orientation allows Nussbaum to read the practical virtues of Books I–IX as exhaustive of the ethical domain. Courage, temperance, and justice are not merely instrumental strategies for securing a separate, transcendent salvation or divine contemplation; they are intrinsic components of a well-lived human life. They are ways in which human beings structurally organize their shared vulnerability and mutual dependence.

### 2. The Incommensurability of a Plurality of Ethical Values
A cornerstone of Nussbaum’s reading of Aristotelian practical reason is her insistence on the radical *incommensurability* of the plurality of intrinsic goods. In her extensive engagement with Plato’s *Protagoras* and Aristotle’s ethics, Nussbaum argues against any single, master-metric of value (such as utilitarian pleasure or a Platonic Form of the Good). 

For Nussbaum, the good life consists of a multitude of distinct, irreducible spheres of human experience—fear, bodily appetite, distribution of resources, speech, humor, social association, political action, and contemplation. Each sphere demands its own corresponding virtue. Because these values are qualitatively distinct and frequently come into tragic conflict, practical wisdom (*phronesis*) cannot operate as a deductive techne that applies a universal rule to calculate the optimal outcome. Instead, *phronesis* is a perceptive, flexible capacity to discern the salient features of a complex, concrete situation. It is the ability to navigate a world where commitments collide, where one cannot simultaneously maximize all valuable ends, and where tragic choices—situations where any course of action involves moral residue or violation—are an unavoidable feature of human fragility.

### 3. Principles as Provisional Summaries of Good Decisions
Consistent with her anti-foundationalist and anti-deductivist stance, Nussbaum interprets Aristotle’s use of universal principles and rules not as absolute, foundational axioms from which moral duties are derived, but as *provisional summaries* of good practical decisions. 

In her reading of *Nicomachean Ethics* VI and VII, as well as the *Rhetoric*, principles are generalizations distilled from the accumulated wisdom of reflective agents (*phronimoi*). They serve as heuristic guides, pointing the agent in the right direction, but they must always remain flexible (*epieikeia*—equity) and subservient to the nuanced demands of the particular situation. The ultimate seat of moral authority is not the abstract law or universal rule, but the perception of the virtuous agent confronting the unique, unrepeatable texture of lived experience.

## Part II: The Book X Challenge – The Specter of Transcendence

While Nussbaum’s anthropocentric, immanent framework accounts brilliantly for the texture of Books I–IX, it collides directly with the dense, seemingly discordant arguments of Book X, chapters 7 and 8. Here, Aristotle appears to execute a dramatic pivot away from the pluralistic, politically engaged, and vulnerable life of the practical virtues toward a singular, self-sufficient, and divine ideal.

### 1. The Contemplative Ideal (*Bios Theoretikos*) vs. Practical Virtue
In Book X, Chapter 7, Aristotle explicitly identifies the highest form of *eudaimonia* with contemplation (*theoria*). This activity is superior to the exercise of the practical moral virtues for several reasons:
* It is the activity of the best part of us—the intellect (*nous*).
* Its objects are the highest, most unchanging things in the universe.
* It is the most continuous and self-sufficient activity, requiring the fewest external instruments or conditions (though it requires basic provisions).
* It is loved for its own sake, containing its own intrinsic pleasure, unmixed with the pain and struggle that characterize the political and moral spheres.

Most provocatively, in Chapter 7, Aristotle asserts that such a life is "too high for a human being":
> "For not insofar as he is a human being will he live in this way, but insofar as something divine is present in him... If then the intellect is divine in comparison with man, the life according to it is also divine in comparison with human life. No, one should not follow those who advise us, being men, to think of human things, and, being mortal, of mortal things, but one should, so far as in one lies, make oneself immortal, and strain every nerve to live in accordance with the best thing in him." (NE X.7, 1177b26–33)

### 2. The Critiques of Nussbaum's Harmonization
This passage presents an acute embarrassment for an exclusively anthropocentric, immanent reading of Aristotle. How can Nussbaum reconcile her insistence that *eudaimonia* is a species-relative, vulnerable, and pluralistic human good with Aristotle’s explicit command to "make oneself immortal" (*athanatizein*) and live according to the divine element within us, which seems to transcend our humanity altogether?

Critics like John Cooper, Richard Kraut, and Gabriel Richardson Lear have argued that Nussbaum downplays or misinterprets the structural hierarchy of Book X. They point out that Aristotle explicitly designates contemplation as the *primary* or *highest* realization of *eudaimonia*, while relegating the practical virtues of Books I–IX to a "secondary" kind of happiness (NE X.8, 1178a9). 

Nussbaum attempts to defuse this tension through what is known as the "inclusive" or "comprehensive" interpretation, combined with an insistence that the *theoria* of Book X is itself an activity of a *human* being—an exercise of human intellect that, while directed at divine objects, remains anchored in the biological and social conditions of human life. In *The Fragility of Goodness*, Nussbaum argues that *athanatizein* (making oneself immortal) does not mean trying to cease being a human being, but rather cultivating the highest human capacity. She reads the contemplative life not as an ascetic, otherworldly flight from the city and the body, but as the crowning achievement of a well-ordered human life that has secured its practical foundations through the virtues of Books I–IX.

Yet, critics counter that this harmonization strains the text. Aristotle explicitly states that the life of practical virtue is happy only "in a secondary way" (*deuteros*) and that the contemplative life is superior precisely because it escapes the messiness, vulnerability, and dependence that Nussbaum makes central to the human condition. If the highest happiness consists in transcending human vulnerability, then Nussbaum’s focus on fragility as the defining characteristic of the Aristotelian good life appears fundamentally misaligned with Aristotle’s ultimate telos.

## Part III: The Metaphysical Paradox – Human Vulnerability vs. The Divine Life

To adjudicate between Nussbaum’s immanent reading and the transcendent reading of Book X, we must look deeper into the metaphysical structure of Aristotle’s psychology and biology. The tension in the *Nicomachean Ethics* is not merely an exegetical puzzle; it is the inevitable result of Aristotle’s conception of man as a *boundary creature* (*metaxu*).

### 1. Man as the Boundary Creature
Aristotle defines human beings in the *De Anima* and the *Parts of Animals* as composite entities (*synolon*) of body and soul, uniquely situated at the ontological boundary between the mortal, changeable realm of sublunary nature and the immortal, unchangeable realm of the celestial spheres and the Unmoved Mover. 

Because man is a composite (*suntheton*), his flourishing must reflect his ontological makeup. The practical virtues (Books I–IX) govern the composite creature in its social, political, and corporeal reality—managing passions, navigating interpersonal conflict, and sustaining the polis. These virtues are thoroughly human, marked by vulnerability because the composite body is fragile, subject to disease, aging, and death.

However, the human soul also houses *nous* (intellect), which Aristotle describes in *De Anima* III.5 as separable, impassible, and divine. In the act of contemplation, the human intellect becomes identified with its object—the eternal, necessary structures of the cosmos. In this precise moment of intellectual activity, the human agent touches the divine. 

### 2. Resolving the Contradiction: Immanent Transcendence
Does this mean Book X introduces a transcendent hierarchy that anticipates New Testament doctrines of grace and otherworldly salvation? To answer this, we must examine the precise nature of the continuity and discontinuity between Aristotle’s *theoria* and Christian eschatology.

The critics of Nussbaum are correct that Book X posits a genuine hierarchy: contemplation is superior to action, and the divine element in us is higher than our composite political nature. However, Nussbaum is correct that this hierarchy does not abolish the immanent, species-relative framework of the rest of the treatise. 

The resolution of the paradox lies in understanding Aristotle’s concept of *energeia* (actuality). Contemplation is not a state achieved by leaving the body behind or by acquiring a supernatural grace that abrogates human nature; it is the supreme *actualization* of the highest human capacity. For Aristotle, there is no Platonic dualism where the body is an evil prison from which the soul must escape. Even when engaged in *theoria*, the contemplative agent remains a human being living in time, requiring bodily health, leisure (*skhole*), and external goods. 

Thus, Book X does not represent a rupture with Books I–IX, but the *telos* toward which the entire internal architecture of the human being tends. The practical virtues clear the ground, pacify the irrational passions, and establish the social stability necessary for the mind to achieve moments of contemplative clarity. The life of the practical virtues is the necessary condition for the life of contemplation; and contemplation, in turn, provides the normative vision that completes the human form of life. It is an *immanent transcendence*—a transcendence achieved from within the capacities of human nature, not by divine rescue from without.

## Part IV: The Bridge to Christian Doctrine – Aristotelian Ethics as a Rational Foundation

This brings us to the final, critical question: Can this Aristotelian framework—whether read through Nussbaum’s strictly immanent lens or through a more hierarchically nuanced reading that accommodates Book X—provide a rational foundation for Christian doctrine, or does Book X anticipate New Testament theology in a way that reveals a fundamental incompatibility with Christian metaphysics?

### 1. The Convergence of Aristotelian *Theoria* and New Testament Eschatology
At first glance, the parallels between Aristotle’s description of *theoria* and Christian mysticism or beatitude are striking, fueling the historical synthesis achieved by medieval thinkers like Thomas Aquinas. 
* **The Divine Likeness:** Aristotle’s injunction to "make oneself immortal" (*athanatizein*) echoes the Pauline and Johannine themes of partaking in the divine nature (2 Peter 1:4) and being transformed into the image of God.
* **Contemplation as Ultimate Beatitude:** The Christian *visio Dei* (the beatific vision), wherein the soul’s ultimate end is the eternal contemplation of God, bears a profound structural resemblance to Aristotle’s contemplative intellect grasping the divine Unmoved Mover.
* **Intellect as the Imago Dei:** The identification of *nous* as the divine spark within humanity aligns remarkably with the Christian doctrine of the *imago Dei*, locating the image of God specifically in human rationality and intellect.

### 2. The Radical Divergence: Grace, Creation *Ex Nihilo*, and the Resurrection of the Body
Despite these convergences, inserting Aristotelian ethics directly into Christian theology exposes profound metaphysical fractures—fractures that highlight why Nussbaum’s anthropocentric, vulnerable framework is, paradoxically, closer to certain modern theological trajectories, while classical Aristotelianism required radical transformation to serve Christian doctrine.

1. **Ontological Status of the Cosmos:** Aristotle’s universe is eternal, uncreated, and hierarchically stratified by nature. The divine intellects of the spheres and the Unmoved Mover do not create the world *ex nihilo*, nor do they exercise providential care, love (*agape*), or redemptive grace toward individual human beings. For Aristotle, the Unmoved Mover does not even know the world, as self-contemplation (*noesis noeseos*) is its sole activity. Christianity, by contrast, posits a personal Creator who intervenes in history, enters human flesh (Incarnation), and redeems the broken, vulnerable human composite.
2. **The Status of Vulnerability and the Body:** Nussbaum’s Aristotle places human vulnerability at the center of *eudaimonia*. Human life is tragic, fragile, and bound to time. In classical Christianity, while bodily vulnerability and suffering are real, they are ultimately overcome not merely through the intellectual contemplation of eternal truths, but through the bodily *Resurrection of the Dead*. Christianity radically valorizes the physical body—not as a transient vehicle for intellectual *theoria*, but as a permanent temple of the Holy Spirit destined for glorification. 
3. **Grace vs. Self-Achieved Virtue:** For Aristotle, *eudaimonia* (including the contemplative life) is an achievement—the result of habituation (*ethismos*), moral virtue, and the cultivation of intellectual capacities. It is something human beings attain "through learning or training or some other form of cultivation" (NE I.9, 1099b20). In orthodox Christian doctrine, salvation and beatitude cannot be self-achieved through human striving or intellectual excellence; they are gifts of unmerited divine *grace*.

### 3. Resolving the Apparent Contradiction: How Aristotelian Ethics Grounds Christian Doctrine
How, then, can Aristotelian ethics provide a rational foundation for Christian doctrine? The answer lies in the distinction between a *foundational rational grammar* and a *comprehensive metaphysical theology*. 

Thomas Aquinas demonstrated in the *Summa Theologiae* that Aristotelian ethics provides an indispensable philosophical scaffolding for Christian morality precisely because of its anthropocentric and teleological realism—elements that Nussbaum so powerfully defends. 

* **The Reality of Human Nature:** Christianity requires an account of human nature that is neither purely reducible to biological mechanism nor arbitrarily constructed by divine fiat. Aristotle’s species-relative framework—his insistence that human beings have a determinate nature with characteristic flourishing conditions—provides the objective moral realism that Christian ethics requires to avoid voluntarism and divine command theory.
* **The Logic of Virtue and Habituation:** While Christian theology introduces the concept of sanctifying grace, it does not bypass human psychology. The acquisition of moral virtues (temperance, fortitude, justice) through habituation and practical wisdom (*phronesis*) remains the indispensable psychological mechanism by which the passions are brought into harmony with reason. Grace does not destroy nature (*gratia non tollit naturam, sed perficit*); it perfects, elevates, and heals it. Aristotelian practical ethics provides the exact empirical and psychological description of how human agents operate within the messy, vulnerable conditions of the world.
* **The Teleological Structure of Desire:** Aristotle’s foundational premise in NE I.1—that "every art and every inquiry, and similarly every action and pursuit, is thought to aim at some good"—establishes the universal teleological structure of human agency. Christianity adopts this fundamental insight, identifying that ultimate good not as an abstract intellectual principle or the contemplation of celestial spheres, but as the personal God.

---

## Conclusion

The debate over Martha Nussbaum’s interpretation of Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* forces us to confront the deepest ambiguities of ancient moral philosophy. Nussbaum’s anthropocentric, immanent, and pluralistic framework captures the vital pulse of Books I–IX, brilliantly illuminating how human beings navigate the tragic fragility of life through species-relative goods, the incommensurability of values, and the flexible perception of practical wisdom. Yet, her attempt to completely domesticate Book X within this exclusively immanent horizon overlooks the genuine metaphysical hierarchy Aristotle establishes between the vulnerable life of the composite citizen and the divine, immortalizing activity of intellectual contemplation.

However, recognizing the reality of this transcendent dimension in Book X does not mean that Aristotle anticipates New Testament theology in a way that collapses the integrity of his ethics. The *theoria* of Book X is an immanent transcendence—an actualization of the highest human capacity from within the natural order, not a descent of supernatural grace. 

When this Aristotelian framework encounters Christian doctrine, it does not do so as a seamless precursor, but as a rigorous rational grammar. By grounding human flourishing in species-specific capacities, moral virtue, and practical reasoning, Aristotle provides the essential philosophical architecture of human agency. Christianity takes this architecture—with all its acute awareness of human vulnerability and teleological striving—and transfigures it through the doctrines of creation *ex nihilo*, divine grace, and the resurrection of the flesh. 

Thus, Nussbaum’s defense of Aristotelian fragility and the critics' insistence on the contemplative ideal are not mutually exclusive battle lines. Together, they map the exact tension that defines the human condition: a creature bound to the dust, vulnerable to the tragedies of time, yet bearing within its intellect a spark of the divine, straining upward toward an eternity it can neither fully possess nor entirely forget.
