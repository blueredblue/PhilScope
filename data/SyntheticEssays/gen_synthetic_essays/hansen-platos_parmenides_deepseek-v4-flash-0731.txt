**Title:** The Third Man and the Two Faces of Being: A Critical Evaluation of Vlastos’s Formalization of Plato’s Regress

**Introduction**

The Third-Man Argument (TMA) in Plato’s *Parmenides* (132a–b) stands as one of the most formidable and philosophically fertile puzzles in the history of metaphysics. It is a self-inflicted wound, a dialectical strike against the very Theory of Forms that Plato had spent his middle period elaborating. For centuries, commentators have debated whether this argument represents a genuine, fatal flaw in Plato’s ontology or merely a dialectical exercise designed to spur further refinement. The most influential modern treatment of this problem is undoubtedly Gregory Vlastos’s 1954 paper, “The Third Man Argument in the Parmenides.” Vlastos, with his characteristic analytic rigor, formalized the argument into a set of three precise premises—One-Over-Many (OM), Self-Predication (SP), and Non-Identity (NI)—and demonstrated that their conjunction generates an infinite, vicious regress. He concluded that Plato, in his middle-period ontology, was *inadvertently* committed to mutually inconsistent premises, and that the TMA is a genuine *reductio* of the Theory of Forms as presented in the *Phaedo* and *Republic*.

The central tension Vlastos identifies lies in the collision between Self-Predication and Non-Identity. If the Form of Largeness is itself large (SP), then it shares a property with the particulars that participate in it. But if we posit a Form to explain a common property shared by a set of things, and that set now includes the Form itself, then by Non-Identity—the principle that the Form cannot be identical to any of its participants—we are forced to posit a second Form of Largeness, and so on *ad infinitum*. This essay will argue that while Vlastos’s formalization is a masterpiece of logical clarity and successfully exposes a genuine *prima facie* tension, his conclusion that Plato’s ontology is irredeemably inconsistent is historically and philosophically misguided. The regress can be resolved by carefully parsing the semantics of predication and identity—specifically, by distinguishing between the Form *being* F-ness (identity) and a particular *having* F-ness (participation). However, this solution is not merely a modern anachronism imposed on the text; rather, it is a distinction that Plato himself gestures toward in his later dialogues, particularly the *Sophist*, and which is implicitly required by the very logic of the middle-period theory. By evaluating Vlastos’s interpretation against alternative solutions—such as the “identity/predication” distinction and the “Pauline predication” reading—I will contend that the TMA does not force us to abandon Plato’s ontology. Instead, it forces us to recognize that the regress arises from a naive, univocal reading of the verb “to be,” a reading that Plato’s mature philosophy systematically undermines.

**Section 1: Vlastos’s Formalization and the Structure of the Regress**

To evaluate Vlastos’s interpretation, we must first understand the precise logical scaffolding he constructs. The text of the *Parmenides* is deceptively simple. Parmenides asks Socrates: “I suppose you think each Form is one, and that whenever you see a number of things that are large, there seems to be a single Form over all of them, namely Largeness, and you think that is one.” Socrates agrees. Parmenides then presses: “What about Largeness itself? If you look at all the large things in the same way, will there not be one Largeness over them all, by which they are large? ... So another Form of Largeness will make its appearance, in addition to Largeness itself and the things that share in it; and again, over all these, another, which will make all of them large. And so each of your Forms will no longer be one, but infinitely many.”

Vlastos’s genius was to isolate the implicit premises that make this argument work. He identifies three:

1.  **One-Over-Many (OM):** For any set of things that share a common character (F-ness), there exists a single Form (F-ness) that is over them all and is the cause of their being F. This is the foundational postulate of the Theory of Forms.
2.  **Self-Predication (SP):** The Form of F-ness is itself F. The Form of Largeness is large; the Form of Beauty is beautiful; the Form of Justice is just. This premise is not merely a logical curiosity; it is deeply embedded in Plato’s middle-period metaphysics. The Form is the *paradigm* or *exemplar* of the property. It must possess the property to the highest degree to be the cause of that property in particulars. As Vlastos notes, SP is required for the Form to serve its explanatory function—if the Form of Largeness were not large, how could it impart largeness to other things?
3.  **Non-Identity (NI):** For any Form, F-ness, and any particular, x, that participates in F-ness, F-ness is not identical to x. This premise is also crucial. If the Form were identical to one of its particulars, then the Form would be a particular, undermining its status as a separate, transcendent entity. Moreover, if the Form were identical to a particular, then the Form would not be *over* the set; it would be *in* the set.

The regress unfolds as follows. Let \(L_1\) be the Form of Largeness. Let \(a, b, c\) be particular large things. By OM, there is a Form \(L_1\) over the set \(\{a, b, c\}\). By SP, \(L_1\) is large. Therefore, we can construct a new set \(\{a, b, c, L_1\}\). By OM, there must be a Form \(L_2\) over this new set. By NI, \(L_2\) cannot be identical to any member of this set, including \(L_1\). Therefore, \(L_2 \neq L_1\). But \(L_2\) is also large (by SP), so we can construct the set \(\{a, b, c, L_1, L_2\}\), which requires a new Form \(L_3\), and so on *ad infinitum*.

Vlastos argues that this regress is *vicious* because it is infinite and explanatory. Each new Form is posited to explain the commonality of the previous set, but the introduction of that Form only creates a new set requiring a new Form. The explanatory project never terminates. Crucially, Vlastos claims this is an *inadvertent* commitment. Plato did not consciously intend to hold all three premises simultaneously; he was unaware of their mutual inconsistency. The TMA, in Vlastos’s reading, is a *reductio* that Plato discovered through Parmenides’ questioning, revealing a deep flaw in his own theory.

**Section 2: The Philosophical Tension Between SP and NI**

The heart of Vlastos’s interpretation lies in the perceived incompatibility of SP and NI. To see why this tension is so acute, we must delve into the philosophical motivations behind each premise.

Self-Predication is not a careless slip on Plato’s part. It is a necessary consequence of the Form’s role as a paradigm. In the *Phaedo*, Socrates argues that particulars are called F because they “partake” in the Form of F-ness. But how can a Form impart a property it does not itself possess? If the Form of Beauty were not beautiful, it would be a strange cause indeed—a non-beautiful thing making other things beautiful. Furthermore, the Form is the *most real* entity; it is the perfect instance of the property. The Form of Justice is justice itself, and therefore it is perfectly just. This is the “Pauline” intuition—that the standard of a property must itself exemplify that property in the highest degree.

Non-Identity, on the other hand, is the principle that safeguards the Form’s transcendence. If the Form of Largeness were identical to a particular large thing, say a tall mountain, then the Form would be a physical object, subject to generation and decay. It would no longer be the eternal, unchanging, intelligible entity that grounds all knowledge. Moreover, if the Form were identical to one particular, it would be over that particular, but not over others in the same way. It would be a member of the set it is supposed to govern, which is logically incoherent—a set cannot be a member of itself in this explanatory sense.

The tension arises because SP forces the Form into the set of F-things, while NI forces it out. If the Form is F, then it shares a property with the particulars. The set of F-things now includes both the Form and the particulars. But if the Form is *over* this set (OM), it cannot be a member of it (NI). Yet SP says it *is* a member. The only way to resolve this is to either deny that the Form is a member of the set (which requires reinterpreting SP) or to deny that the Form is over the set (which would destroy the explanatory function of the Form). Vlastos correctly identifies this as a genuine logical contradiction if we take the verb “is” in SP and NI in the same univocal sense—namely, as the “is” of predication or existence.

**Section 3: Alternative Solutions—Parsing Predication and Identity**

Vlastos’s formalization is logically airtight given his premises. However, the history of philosophy is replete with attempts to dissolve the regress by challenging one or more of these premises, or by refining the semantics of the language involved. The most promising of these alternatives focus on distinguishing between different senses of “is,” thereby parsing predication and identity in a way that prevents the infinite regress.

**3.1 The Identity/Predication Distinction**

The most direct attack on the regress comes from philosophers like Wilfrid Sellars and, in a later revision, Vlastos himself. The core insight is that when we say “The Form of Largeness is large,” we are not making a predicative statement in the same sense as “Socrates is large.” Instead, we are making an identity statement. “The Form of Largeness is large” is elliptical for “The Form of Largeness *is* Largeness itself” or “The Form of Largeness is identical to the property of Largeness.”

If we adopt this reading, the regress collapses. Let \(L_1\) be the Form of Largeness. By SP (interpreted as identity), \(L_1\) *is* Largeness. The particulars \(a, b, c\) are large in the sense that they *participate in* or *have* Largeness. The set of things that *have* Largeness is \(\{a, b, c\}\). The Form \(L_1\) is not a member of this set because it does not *have* Largeness; it *is* Largeness. Therefore, when we apply OM to the set of things that *have* Largeness, we get \(L_1\). But \(L_1\) is not a member of that set, so we cannot form the new set \(\{a, b, c, L_1\}\) that would trigger the regress. The regress requires that the Form be a member of the set over which it presides, but the identity reading ensures that the Form stands outside the set of participants.

This solution is elegant and philosophically robust. It preserves the explanatory function of the Form (it is the cause of largeness in particulars because it *is* largeness), and it preserves the transcendence of the Form (it is not a particular large thing). However, the critical question is whether this distinction is historically plausible. Does Plato himself make this distinction? The text of the *Parmenides* does not explicitly articulate it. In fact, Parmenides’ argument seems to presuppose that “The Form of Largeness is large” is a predication on par with “Socrates is large.” If Plato had intended the identity reading, he could have easily said so. This is precisely Vlastos’s point: Plato *didn’t* make the distinction, and hence he fell into the regress.

**3.2 Pauline Predication and Focal Meaning**

A related but distinct solution is the “Pauline predication” reading, championed by Colin Strang and later considered by Vlastos. This approach suggests that when we say “The Form of F-ness is F,” we are not predicating F-ness of the Form in the same way we predicate it of particulars. Instead, the Form is F in a *normative*, *paradigmatic*, or *focal* sense. For example, “The Form of Justice is just” means “The Form of Justice is the standard of justice” or “The Form of Justice is justice *par excellence*.” In contrast, “Socrates is just” means “Socrates participates in justice in a derivative, imperfect way.”

If we adopt this reading, we can argue that the Form of Largeness is not “large” in the same sense that a mountain is large. The mountain is large because it has a certain size; the Form is large because it *defines* what it is to be large. These are different senses of “large.” Consequently, the set of things that are large *in the derivative sense* does not include the Form. The Form is large in a different sense, so it does not belong to the set of large particulars. Again, the regress is blocked because OM applies only to sets of things that share a common character *in the same sense*.

This solution is appealing because it aligns with Plato’s language in the *Republic*, where the Form of the Good is described as being “beyond being” and is the ultimate cause of all goodness. It also resonates with the *Euthyphro*, where piety is defined as what is loved by the gods, and the Form is the standard by which all pious acts are judged. However, Vlastos himself remained skeptical. He argued that while Pauline predication might be a viable philosophical move, it is not historically plausible because Plato does not explicitly signal a shift in the meaning of “is” between the Form and the particulars. The text of the *Parmenides* treats “large” univocally.

**3.3 The Dialectical Reading: The TMA as a Reductio of a Naive Theory**

A third major alternative is to deny that the TMA is a fatal objection to Plato’s mature ontology. Instead, it is a *dialectical* exercise designed to show that the *naive* version of the Theory of Forms—the version held by the young Socrates in the dialogue—is inadequate. This reading, championed by scholars like Sandra Peterson and, in a different way, by the Neoplatonists, argues that Parmenides is not simply refuting Socrates; he is training him. The TMA reveals that the Theory of Forms, as initially formulated, relies on a confused understanding of participation and predication. The solution is not to abandon the Forms but to refine the theory.

This reading gains support from the structure of the *Parmenides*. After the TMA, Parmenides goes on to discuss the problems of participation, the limits of knowledge, and the nature of the One. The dialogue ends in aporia, but it is a *productive* aporia. It points toward the need for a more sophisticated ontology, one that distinguishes between the different ways in which things can be said to “be.” This is precisely what Plato delivers in the *Sophist*, where he explicitly addresses the problem of Not-Being and introduces the concept of the “communion of kinds” (koinonia ton genon). In the *Sophist*, Plato distinguishes between the “is” of identity (e.g., “The Form of Being is Being”) and the “is” of predication (e.g., “The Form of Being participates in Sameness”). This distinction provides the resources to resolve the TMA.

**Section 4: Assessing Historical Plausibility**

The central question, then, is whether these alternative solutions are historically plausible. Vlastos’s critique rests on the claim that Plato was *inadvertently* committed to inconsistent premises. If we can show that Plato either explicitly or implicitly held a distinction that resolves the inconsistency, Vlastos’s interpretation loses its force.

Let us examine the text more closely. In the *Parmenides*, Socrates’ initial formulation of the Forms is indeed naive. He says that there is a Form of Largeness “over” the many large things. He does not specify the exact nature of the relationship between the Form and its participants. When Parmenides asks whether the Form of Largeness is itself large, Socrates does not object; he seems to accept it as a matter of course. This suggests that, at the level of the *Parmenides*’ dramatic date, Socrates has not yet made the identity/predication distinction.

However, we must ask whether this is Plato’s final word. The *Parmenides* is generally considered a transitional dialogue, written after the *Republic* but before the *Sophist* and *Philebus*. It is plausible to read the *Parmenides* as a deliberate critique of the *unrevised* Theory of Forms, a critique that Plato himself took seriously and sought to answer in his later works.

In the *Sophist* (255c–d), the Eleatic Stranger explicitly distinguishes between the Form of Being, the Form of Sameness, and the Form of Difference. He argues that “being” is not univocal; it can mean either “exists” or “is the same as.” This distinction is precisely what is needed to resolve the TMA. When we say “The Form of Largeness is large,” we can interpret this as “The Form of Largeness is the same as Largeness” (an identity statement). When we say “Socrates is large,” we interpret this as “Socrates participates in Largeness” (a predicative statement). The two uses of “is” are fundamentally different. Therefore, the Form is not a member of the set of things that *participate* in Largeness, and the regress is blocked.

Is it anachronistic to read the *Sophist* back into the *Parmenides*? Not necessarily. Plato’s dialogues are not a systematic treatise; they are a series of investigations. The *Parmenides* presents a problem, and the *Sophist* presents a solution. It is entirely plausible that Plato wrote the *Parmenides* knowing full well that the TMA could be resolved by a more careful analysis of the verb “to be,” and that he intended the reader to see the *Parmenides* as a *protreptic* to the *Sophist*. This reading preserves the historical plausibility of the identity/predication solution.

Furthermore, we can find hints of this distinction even in the middle-period dialogues. In the *Republic* (509b), Socrates says that the Form of the Good is “beyond being” (epekeina tes ousias), suggesting that the Form does not “exist” in the same way that particulars exist. In the *Phaedo* (100c), Socrates says that particulars are beautiful “by” the Form of Beauty, implying that the Form itself is not a beautiful *thing* but the *cause* of beauty. While these passages are not explicit, they suggest that Plato was aware of a distinction between the Form’s mode of being and the particulars’ mode of being.

Vlastos’s response would be that these hints are insufficient. He might argue that the *Phaedo* and *Republic* still treat the Form as a “thing” that has properties. But this is a contestable interpretation. Plato’s language is often metaphorical, and the “beyond being” passage in the *Republic* is a clear indication that the Form is not a “thing” in the ordinary sense.
