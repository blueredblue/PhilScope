The nature of person-to-person communication has been a subject of interest for philosophers across various disciplines, with Jean-Paul Sartre's concept of mutual objectification being a pivotal point of discussion. According to Sartre, human relationships are inherently conflicted, as individuals inevitably objectify one another, reducing the other person to a mere object of their own consciousness. This ontological conflict arises from the fundamental human desire for freedom and autonomy, which leads individuals to assert their dominance over others. However, this essay will argue that genuine person-to-person communication requires moving beyond Sartre's paradigm of mutual objectification toward a model of mutual transcendence, where individuals recognize and respect the subjectivity of the other person.

To achieve this, it is essential to incorporate Paul Tillich's concept of indeterminacy within the subject-subject relationship. Tillich's philosophy emphasizes the inherent uncertainty and ambiguity that characterizes human interactions. According to Tillich, the self is never fully determinate, and its identity is constantly evolving through its relationships with others. This indeterminacy is both a source of freedom and a challenge, as it requires individuals to navigate the complexities of human connection without the comfort of fixed or essentialized identities. In the context of person-to-person communication, Tillich's concept of indeterminacy highlights the need for a dynamic and adaptive approach, one that acknowledges the provisional nature of our understanding of the other person.

Luce Irigaray's notion of "the two" further complicates the picture, introducing the idea that genuine communication can only occur when both parties recognize and respect the subjectivity of the other. Irigaray argues that traditional Western philosophy has consistently neglected the feminine perspective, reducing women to the status of objects or mere reflections of masculine subjectivity. In contrast, Irigaray's concept of "the two" emphasizes the importance of acknowledging and valuing the differences between individuals, rather than attempting to assimilate or dominate the other. This approach requires a fundamental shift in our understanding of communication, from a model based on domination and control to one that prioritizes mutual recognition and respect.

The self must balance the necessary objectification of the Other-as-object with the recognition of the Other-as-subject. On one hand, objectification is an inevitable aspect of human perception, as we can never fully know the other person in all their complexity and depth. Our understanding of others is always provisional and subject to revision, and we must acknowledge the limitations of our knowledge. On the other hand, recognizing the Other-as-subject requires a willingness to transcend our own egoistic tendencies and acknowledge the autonomy and agency of the other person. This involves a delicate balance between empathy and respect, as we strive to understand the other person's experiences and perspectives without reducing them to our own preconceptions or biases.

One of the primary challenges in achieving this balance lies in the tension between preparing for interaction through static approximations and relinquishing those approximations during actual dialogue. As we prepare to engage with others, we often rely on preconceived notions or stereotypes to guide our expectations and behavior. However, these static approximations can become obstacles to genuine communication, as they prevent us from fully embracing the complexity and unpredictability of the other person. During actual dialogue, we must be willing to relinquish these approximations and engage with the other person in a more dynamic and adaptive way. This requires a willingness to listen, to learn, and to revise our understanding of the other person, rather than simply imposing our own preconceptions or expectations.

The question remains, however, whether genuine conversation can successfully replace the drive toward domination with a dynamic anticipation of mutual change. According to Sartre, human relationships are inherently conflicted, and the desire for domination is an fundamental aspect of human nature. However, this essay argues that genuine person-to-person communication can, in fact, transcend this drive toward domination, replacing it with a more reciprocal and adaptive approach. When both parties engage in a mutual process of recognition and respect, the focus shifts from domination to collaboration, and the relationship becomes a dynamic and evolving process of mutual growth and development.

This shift from domination to collaboration is not without its challenges, however. It requires a fundamental transformation in our understanding of communication, from a model based on control and manipulation to one that prioritizes mutual understanding and respect. It also requires a willingness to embrace the uncertainty and ambiguity that characterizes human interaction, rather than seeking to impose our own fixed or essentialized identities on the other person. Nevertheless, the rewards of this approach are well worth the effort, as it enables us to engage in more meaningful and authentic relationships, ones that are characterized by a deep sense of mutual respect, empathy, and understanding.

In conclusion, person-to-person communication requires moving beyond Jean-Paul Sartre's ontological conflict of mutual objectification toward a model of mutual transcendence. By incorporating Paul Tillich's concept of indeterminacy and Luce Irigaray's notion of "the two," we can develop a more nuanced understanding of human relationships, one that acknowledges the complexities and uncertainties of human connection. The self must balance the necessary objectification of the Other-as-object with the recognition of the Other-as-subject, and be willing to relinquish static approximations in favor of a more dynamic and adaptive approach to communication. While the drive toward domination is an inherent aspect of human nature, genuine conversation can, in fact, replace this drive with a dynamic anticipation of mutual change, leading to more meaningful and authentic relationships. Ultimately, this approach requires a fundamental transformation in our understanding of communication, one that prioritizes mutual respect, empathy, and understanding, and acknowledges the inherent value and dignity of the other person.

Furthermore, this approach has significant implications for our understanding of ethics and morality. By recognizing the inherent subjectivity and autonomy of the other person, we are compelled to reevaluate our moral obligations and responsibilities. We can no longer treat others as mere objects or means to an end, but rather as ends in themselves, deserving of respect, dignity, and compassion. This shift in perspective has far-reaching consequences, from the way we conduct our personal relationships to the way we structure our social and political institutions.

Moreover, this approach highlights the importance of empathy and compassion in human relationships.
