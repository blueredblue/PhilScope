## The Ghost of the Frontier: Locke, Mohr, and the Predicament of the Modern Individualist

John Locke’s *Second Treatise of Government* has long served as the foundational text for classical liberalism and, by extension, modern libertarianism and individualist anarchism. His robust defense of natural rights—life, liberty, and estate—coupled with his contractual theory of political obligation, appears to provide a principled basis for limiting state power and justifying resistance to tyranny. Yet, as the contemporary philosopher Tyson Mohr argues, Locke’s political theory is fatally anchored to a historical contingency that no longer obtains: the existence of vast, unoccupied land (*in vacuis locis*). Mohr contends that in the crowded, fully-appropriated world of the twenty-first century, Locke’s framework collapses into an incoherent mess. The right of exit—the ultimate check on state power—is neutralized when there is nowhere to go, and tacit consent becomes a coercive trap rather than a voluntary act. Consequently, Mohr concludes, Locke’s theory cannot salvage the modern individualist’s project of resisting the leviathan state; they are left with the stark and unappealing choice between submitting to majority rule or engaging in a revolution that Locke’s own criteria do not sanction.

This essay will evaluate Mohr’s argument in depth. I will first reconstruct the Lockean framework, paying particular attention to the role of *in vacuis locis* in his theory of property and consent. I will then present Mohr’s critique, showing how the disappearance of unoccupied land and the sheer scale of modern states undermine the voluntariness of consent and the viability of exit. While I will concede significant ground to Mohr—particularly regarding the inadequacy of tacit consent and the narrowness of Locke’s criteria for rebellion—I will ultimately reject his pessimistic conclusion that modern individualists are left with no legitimate recourse other than submission or revolution. My independent objection will argue that Mohr’s critique, if taken to its logical conclusion, actually *liberates* the individualist from the social contract entirely. If tacit consent is invalid, then the individualist is returned to a state of nature vis-à-vis the state. In that state, the state is merely another actor, and its taxation and coercion constitute aggression. This grants the individualist a legitimate right of *resistance*—distinct from both submission and full-scale revolution—rooted in the natural right of self-defense. This third path, while radical, is the only consistent Lockean response to the conditions Mohr describes.

### I. The Lockean Architecture: Property, Consent, and the Safety Valve

To understand Mohr’s critique, one must first grasp the intricate architecture of Locke’s political philosophy. Locke begins with a state of nature, a pre-political condition governed by the law of nature, which dictates that no one ought to harm another in their life, health, liberty, or possessions. In this state, individuals possess natural rights, but these rights are insecure due to the partiality and bias of human nature. The solution is the social contract: individuals voluntarily agree to form a commonwealth, surrendering their executive power to a central authority in exchange for the protection of their property.

Crucially, for Locke, property is not a gift of the state but a natural right acquired through labor. In the famous chapter on property, Locke argues that every man has a property in his own person, and the labor of his body and the work of his hands are properly his. When a man mixes his labor with unowned nature, he makes it his own. This is the doctrine of original acquisition. Locke’s justification for this appropriation is twofold: first, the laborer has a natural right to the fruits of their labor; second, there is enough and as good left in common for others. This latter proviso is where *in vacuis locis* becomes central. Locke explicitly notes that in the beginning, “the world was America,” a vast frontier of unoccupied land. This abundance means that appropriation does not harm others, because there is plenty of land left for them to appropriate in turn. The existence of unoccupied land is not merely an empirical fact; it is a *normative condition* for the legitimacy of original appropriation and, as we shall see, for the legitimacy of political society itself.

The social contract, for Locke, is grounded in consent. There are two types of consent: express and tacit. Express consent is given by an individual who explicitly joins a commonwealth, binding them permanently to its laws. Tacit consent, however, is far more subtle. Locke writes that any man who “hath any possession, or enjoyment, of any part of the dominions of any government, doth thereby give his tacit consent, and is as far forth obliged to obedience to the laws of that government.” This includes not only owning property but also traveling on the highways, lodging in the country, or simply enjoying the protection of the laws. The key feature of tacit consent is that it is revocable: an individual can withdraw it by leaving the territory and renouncing their enjoyment of the state’s benefits.

This revocability is the linchpin of Locke’s theory of political obligation. It provides a voluntary basis for obedience, ensuring that no one is subjected to a government they have not chosen. More importantly, the right of exit serves as a powerful check on state power. If a government becomes tyrannical, the individual can simply leave, effectively withdrawing their consent and returning to the state of nature. The state, knowing that its subjects can exit, has an incentive to respect their rights. In this sense, the right of exit is the ultimate safety valve of the Lockean system. It is what distinguishes a voluntary association from a coercive institution.

Finally, Locke’s theory includes a right of rebellion. When the government violates the trust placed in it—by acting arbitrarily, by altering the legislature, or by attempting to subjugate the people—the contract is dissolved, and the people have the right to resist. However, Locke is careful to specify that rebellion is only justified against a government that has *dissolved itself* by breaking the fundamental trust. It is not a license for perpetual insurrection. The government retains its legitimacy as long as it acts within the bounds of the law and protects the property of its citizens.

### II. Mohr’s Critique: The Vanishing Frontier and the Coercive Trap

Tyson Mohr’s argument is a powerful *reductio ad absurdum* of Lockean theory under modern conditions. He begins by noting that Locke’s entire edifice rests on a demographic and geographic premise that has been empirically falsified. The world is no longer America. There is no more *in vacuis locis*. Every habitable piece of land is under the jurisdiction of some state. This simple fact has devastating consequences for Locke’s theory of property, consent, and political obligation.

First, consider the right of exit. For Locke, exit is the mechanism by which tacit consent is withdrawn. But what does exit mean in a world with no unoccupied land? It means relocating to another sovereign state. However, this is not a genuine exit from political society; it is merely a transfer from one jurisdiction to another. The individualist is still subject to the coercion of a state, still subject to taxation, still subject to laws they did not explicitly consent to. They are merely choosing which leviathan will devour them. The right of exit, which was supposed to be a meaningful check on state power, is reduced to a right of *migration* between different flavors of servitude.

Mohr pushes this further. He argues that the lack of unoccupied land neutralizes the right of exit *as a moral concept*. The legitimacy of the social contract depends on the voluntariness of consent. But if the only alternative to consenting to a particular state is to consent to another equally coercive state, or to live as a stateless refugee (which is practically impossible and morally degrading), then the choice to remain is not truly voluntary. It is forced. An individual who stays in a state because they have no viable alternative is not consenting; they are capitulating. The right of exit, therefore, becomes a cruel joke—a formal right that is materially impossible to exercise.

Second, this has a direct impact on tacit consent. Locke’s doctrine of tacit consent is already notoriously weak. David Hume famously criticized it by pointing out that most people never explicitly consent to their government; they are simply born into it. Locke’s response is that their continued residence and enjoyment of benefits constitutes tacit consent. But Mohr argues that this response is only plausible if the individual has a genuine alternative. In Locke’s time, a dissatisfied Englishman could board a ship and sail to the Americas, where he could establish a new life in the state of nature. The choice to remain in England was, at least in principle, a free choice between two viable options. Today, however, there is no such choice. The individual cannot establish a new commonwealth on unoccupied land because there is no unoccupied land. They cannot escape the jurisdiction of *some* state. Therefore, their continued residence is not a sign of consent; it is a sign of their lack of alternatives. Tacit consent, in a fully populated world, is not consent at all—it is a coerced acquiescence.

This leads Mohr to his third point: the invalidation of tacit consent undermines the legitimacy of the state itself. If the citizens have not genuinely consented, then the state lacks moral authority over them. However, Mohr notes that Locke’s theory offers no escape from this predicament. The individual is trapped. They cannot exit to the state of nature, and they cannot claim that their tacit consent is void because Locke provides no mechanism for voiding it other than physical exit. The social contract, once entered, is binding unless the government dissolves itself. And here, Mohr turns to the question of rebellion.

### III. Taxation, Majority Rule, and the Narrow Door of Rebellion

Mohr’s assessment of contemporary taxation and government policy is particularly damning for the individualist. Locke’s criteria for rebellion are strict. The government must have committed a fundamental breach of trust—for example, by attempting to seize absolute power, by altering the legislature, or by subjecting the people to a foreign power. High taxation, however oppressive, does not inherently meet this threshold. Locke explicitly states that the legislature cannot take property without the consent of the majority. But in a modern democracy, the majority *does* consent to taxation. The government is not acting arbitrarily; it is acting in accordance with the will of the majority, as expressed through elections and legislation.
