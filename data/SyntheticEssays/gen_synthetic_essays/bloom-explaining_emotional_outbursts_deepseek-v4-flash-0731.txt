**Title: The Passionate Gap: Hursthouse, Davidson, and the Architecture of Expressive Intentionality**

**Introduction**

The philosophy of action has long been dominated by a rationalist orthodoxy, most powerfully articulated by Donald Davidson in his seminal paper “Actions, Reasons, and Causes.” Davidson’s model posits that an action is intentional if and only if it is caused by a primary reason, which consists of a pro-attitude (a desire or want) and a corresponding belief. This belief-desire pair provides the rationalizing explanation for the action, situating the agent within a web of means-end reasoning. To act is to be guided by a goal, and to have a goal is to hold a specific belief about how to achieve it. This framework has proven remarkably resilient, offering a clean, causal-mechanical account of human agency that integrates seamlessly with a physicalist worldview.

However, this orthodoxy faces a formidable challenge from a class of behaviors that seem to defy rational explanation entirely. In her influential paper “Arational Actions,” Rosalind Hursthouse identifies a category of actions performed *because of* an emotion, but not *for* a reason. These are actions like tearing one’s hair out in grief, biting a spoon in anger, or screaming and flailing in fear. These actions are not means to an end; the agent is not trying to achieve a state of affairs. They are, in a sense, pure expressions of occurrent emotional states. Hursthouse argues that these actions are genuinely intentional, yet they lack the belief-desire pair that Davidson deems essential. This paper will evaluate whether Hursthouse’s theory successfully undermines Davidson’s model. I will argue that it does—not by disproving Davidson’s causal account for standard instrumental actions, but by exposing its explanatory incompleteness for a significant domain of human behavior. By examining the distinction between *pre-named* and *post-named* epistemic states during emotional outbursts, we can see how pre-named actions challenge standard causal accounts. Furthermore, the normative dilemma surrounding moral culpability for these actions reveals a critical gap in purely causal theories. I will ultimately resolve the tension by proposing that actions driven purely by occurrent emotion do not collapse into unintentional reflexes; rather, they demand a revised framework of intentionality that distinguishes between *instrumental intentionality* and *expressive intentionality*. This pluralistic account, rooted in Hursthouse’s insights, preserves moral accountability through indirect responsibility while accurately reflecting the phenomenology of passionate agency.

**Section 1: The Davidsonian Orthodoxy and the Primacy of the Primary Reason**

To understand the force of Hursthouse’s challenge, one must first appreciate the architecture of Davidson’s theory. In “Actions, Reasons, and Causes,” Davidson sets out to rescue the concept of action from the behaviorist reductionism of his contemporaries. He argues that the distinction between an action (something an agent *does*) and a mere event (something that *happens* to the agent) lies in the causal role of mental states. An event is an action if it is intentional, and it is intentional if it is caused by a primary reason. A primary reason is a composite of two elements: a pro-attitude (a desire, want, or inclination) and a belief. For example, if I turn on the light, my primary reason is my desire to see (pro-attitude) and my belief that flipping the switch will illuminate the room. The action is rationalized because the belief and desire make sense of the action as a sensible means to a desired end.

Crucially, Davidson insists that the primary reason is not merely a justificatory explanation but the *efficient cause* of the action. The belief-desire pair must cause the action in the “right way”—a condition that Davidson himself struggled to specify, leading to the infamous problem of “deviant causal chains.” For instance, a climber might desire to be free of his partner and believe that letting go of the rope will achieve this; this desire and belief might cause him to become so anxious that he loses his grip and falls, dragging his partner down. The belief and desire caused the event, but the event was not intentional because the causal chain was not of the right kind. This internal struggle highlights a fundamental assumption: the causal chain must be mediated by the agent’s rational deliberation, however implicit.

Davidson’s model is thus deeply teleological. It presupposes that all intentional actions are goal-directed, aiming at some state of affairs the agent desires. Even actions that seem pointless, like tapping one’s foot, are explained by postulating a desire for the sensation or a belief that it relieves boredom. The model is closed: if an action is intentional, there *must* be a belief-desire pair that rationalizes it. This is not an empirical discovery but a conceptual requirement. If we cannot find a plausible belief-desire pair, Davidson argues, we have either misidentified the action or we are dealing with a non-intentional event, such as a reflex, a spasm, or a habit.

This framework provides a powerful tool for moral philosophy. If an action is caused by a belief and desire, the agent is accountable because they acted for a reason. They could have considered alternative beliefs or weighed different desires. The agent is a rational subject, and rationality is the ground of responsibility. Davidson’s theory, therefore, does not merely describe behavior; it underwrites our practices of praise and blame.

**Section 2: Hursthouse’s Arational Actions and the Pre-Named/Post-Named Distinction**

Hursthouse directly challenges the universality of this closed model. She introduces the category of “arational actions,” which she defines as actions that are done *because of* an emotion but not *for* a reason. The key distinction is between a *cause* and a *reason*. A reason is a propositional attitude that justifies the action; a cause can be any event that brings it about. In arational actions, the emotion is the cause, but there is no propositional content that constitutes a justifying reason.

Consider Hursthouse’s famous examples: a woman who, upon hearing of a loved one’s death, tears her hair out; a man who, in a fit of rage, bites a spoon so hard it bends; a person who, in intense grief, throws themselves onto a bed and sobs. In each case, the agent is not acting *in order to* express grief or anger. They are not trying to achieve a goal. If asked why they tore their hair out, they might say “because I was grieving” or “because I was so angry.” But this explanation does not cite a belief-desire pair. It does not say “I desired to relieve my grief and believed tearing my hair would do so.” The explanation is a direct causal attribution: the emotion *itself* drove the action.

To sharpen this analysis, we must introduce the distinction between *pre-named* and *post-named* epistemic states. This distinction, implicit in Hursthouse’s work, is crucial for understanding the causal structure of arational actions. A *pre-named* state refers to the occurrent emotional experience *during* the outburst, before the agent has conceptually articulated or “named” the emotion. In this state, the agent is overwhelmed by a raw, visceral feeling. The emotion is not yet an object of reflection; it is the very medium of the agent’s experience. The action performed in this state—the tearing, the biting, the screaming—is not mediated by a propositional attitude. The agent does not think, “I am angry, and I will bite this spoon.” The biting *is* the anger, an immediate somatic manifestation of the affective state.

A *post-named* state, conversely, occurs after the outburst has subsided, when the agent reflects upon what happened and retrospectively identifies the emotion. The agent says, “I was angry,” or “I was grieving.” This post-named attribution is a propositional attitude—a belief about one’s past emotional state. However, Hursthouse’s crucial insight is that this post-named belief is not the *cause* of the pre-named action. The belief comes after the action; it is a rational reconstruction, not an efficient cause. Davidson, facing an arational action, might be tempted to retroject a belief-desire pair into the pre-named state. He might argue that the agent had a desire to “express anger” and a belief that biting the spoon would do so. But Hursthouse rightly rejects this as a misdescription of the phenomenology. The agent did not have a separate desire *to express*; the action was not a means to an end but a constitutive expression of the emotion itself.

The pre-named state is thus characterized by a lack of propositional content. The emotion is occurrent and non-conceptual. It does not have a “content” that can be parsed into a belief and a desire. The desire to express anger is a second-order desire that would require the agent to have already named the emotion. In the heat of the moment, the agent is not naming anything; they are simply acting. This lack of a named emotion and a corresponding belief-desire pair is precisely what makes pre-named actions so challenging for standard causal accounts.

**Section 3: The Challenge to the Causal Account – Undermining the Universal Scope**

Does this challenge successfully *undermine* Davidson’s theory? To undermine a theory is not necessarily to prove it false, but to show that it fails to account for the full range of phenomena it claims to explain. Davidson’s theory claims to be a universal theory of intentional action. Hursthouse’s arational actions present a counterexample to this universality.

Let us examine how a Davidsonian might respond. The most common response is to insist that arational actions are not intentional actions at all. They are merely emotional reflexes, akin to a knee-jerk response or a startle reaction. The agent does not *do* anything; the emotion *happens* to them. This response, however, is deeply unsatisfying and fails to capture the phenomenology. We do not experience tearing our hair out in grief as a mere passive event. We experience it as something *we* are doing. We can stop ourselves mid-action, we can feel guilt or shame about it afterward, and we can be held morally responsible for it. Reflexes are not subject to normative evaluation—we do not blame someone for a knee-jerk. But we *do* blame people for lashing out in anger, even if the lashing out was unpremeditated. This suggests that arational actions are indeed intentional in some meaningful sense.

If Davidson concedes that they are intentional, he must find a belief-desire pair. He might posit a desire to reduce emotional tension and a belief that biting the spoon achieves this. Hursthouse’s objection is that this is an ad hoc rationalization. It imposes a means-end structure where none exists. The agent is not trying to reduce tension; the action is not a strategy. The emotion is not a mental state that causes a separate action; the emotion is partially constituted by the action. The biting is not a consequence of the anger; it is the anger made manifest.

Thus, Hursthouse’s theory undermines Davidson’s model by demonstrating that the belief-desire pair is not a necessary condition for intentional action. It exposes a fundamental gap in the causal account. Davidson’s model works beautifully for instrumental actions—actions where we have a goal and select means to achieve it. But it fails for expressive actions—actions where the goal is not separable from the action itself. By showing that there is a class of intentional actions that lack a primary reason, Hursthouse forces us to either abandon the universal claim of Davidson’s theory or to radically expand our understanding of what constitutes a belief and desire.

Can Davidson expand his theory to accommodate this? He might argue that the emotion itself contains cognitive content. For example, anger might involve the belief that one has been wronged. However, the belief that one has been wronged does not rationalize the action of biting a spoon. It rationalizes a desire for revenge or retribution, not a random act of physical aggression. The specific action of biting a spoon is not connected to the content of the anger in a rational way. It is a brute, somatic response. Therefore, the cognitive content of the emotion cannot serve as the primary reason for the specific action performed. The causal link between the emotion and the action is not mediated by a rational assessment of means and ends. It is a direct, non-rational causal link. This non-rational causal link is precisely what Davidson’s model excludes.

**Section 4: The Normative Dilemma and the Bridge of Preventative Moderation**

The failure of Davidson’s model to explain arational actions has profound normative implications. If an action lacks a belief-desire pair, can the agent be held morally responsible? This is the normative dilemma. On one hand, if the action is a mere reflex, the agent is not culpable—they were overwhelmed by an emotion they could not control. On the other hand, if the action is intentional, the agent is culpable—they chose to act. Hursthouse’s account forces us to confront this dilemma head-on. If pre-named actions are intentional but lack a reason, what is the ground of moral accountability?

We cannot simply excuse the agent on the grounds that they were “in the grip of an emotion.” To do so would be to treat them as a passive victim of their passions, undermining the very concept of moral agency. We expect adults to exercise some control over their emotional expressions. We do not excuse a person who, in a fit of rage, punches a wall, simply because they were angry. We expect them to have developed the capacity to moderate their anger. This expectation points toward a solution: the agent is not directly responsible for the specific arational action, but they are *indirectly* responsible for failing to prevent it.

This is where the capacity for *intentional preventative emotion moderation* becomes crucial. While the agent cannot directly control the occurrent emotional outburst in the pre-named state, they *can* control the conditions that lead to it. They can cultivate habits of emotional regulation, avoid triggering situations, practice mindfulness, or develop coping mechanisms. The agent has a general capacity to shape their emotional dispositions. When an agent fails to exercise this capacity, they can be held accountable for the resulting outburst.

This is analogous to Aristotle’s account of negligence. If a driver fails to check their blind spot and causes an accident, we hold them responsible for the accident, even though they did not intend to cause it. They are responsible for the failure to perform a preventative action. Similarly, the agent who lashes out in anger is responsible for the outburst because they failed to moderate their anger beforehand. The capacity for preventative moderation bridges the gap between the unintentional, pre-named action and moral culpability. It grounds the agent’s responsibility in their rational agency, not in the specific act itself.

This indirect accountability does not require a belief-desire pair for the specific action. The agent did not have a desire to bite the spoon, but they did have a general desire to be a virtuous person and a belief that controlling their anger is part of virtue. This higher-order belief-desire pair is what grounds their culpability. Thus, the normative dilemma is resolved by shifting the locus of responsibility from the occurrent action to the agent’s broader rational character. Hursthouse’s theory does not lead to moral nihilism; it refines our understanding of responsibility by introducing the concept of indirect accountability for emotional failures.

**Section 5: Resolving the Tension – A Revised Framework or a Collapse into Reflex?**

The central question remains: do actions driven purely by occurrent emotion require a revised framework of intentionality, or do they collapse into unintentional reflexes? I have already argued that they do not collapse into reflexes because they are subject to normative evaluation and can be stopped mid-action. A reflex is not something we can stop; it happens automatically. An arational action, however, involves a degree of agentive control. The agent can choose to bite the spoon or not, even if the choice is not mediated by a deliberative reason. This suggests that arational actions are indeed intentional, but they require a different kind of intentionality than that posited by Davidson.

I propose a revised framework that distinguishes between two forms of intentionality: *instrumental intentionality* and *expressive intentionality*. Instrumental intentionality is the standard Davidsonian model. It involves a belief-desire pair and is goal-directed. The agent acts *in order to* achieve a state of affairs. Expressive intentionality, on the other hand, is not goal-directed. The action is not a means to an end; it is a constitutive expression of the agent’s emotional state. The agent acts *because of* the emotion, and the action is the emotion’s outward manifestation.

Expressive intentionality is still a form of agency because it is the agent’s own action. It is not a passive event that happens to them. The agent is the author of the action, even though they are not guided by a rational plan. This is evident in the fact that the agent can reflect on the action post-hoc, name the emotion, and feel remorse or pride. This post-named reflection integrates the action into the agent’s rational self-understanding. The action becomes part of the agent’s life narrative, subject to evaluation and revision.

This revised framework resolves the tension between the occurrent emotion and the demand for rational explanation. It acknowledges that human agency is not monolithic. We are not always rational calculators, weighing beliefs and desires. We are also passionate beings, whose emotions can directly manifest in our behavior. Hursthouse’s theory forces us to expand our conception of intentional action to include these expressive dimensions. It does not undermine the concept of intentionality; it pluralizes it.

Does this mean Davidson’s theory is completely wrong? No. It remains correct for a vast domain of human action—the domain of instrumental reasoning. But it is incomplete. It fails to account for the expressive dimension of human life. By exposing this incompleteness, Hursthouse does not merely add a footnote to Davidson’s theory; she fundamentally challenges its claim to provide a complete account of intentional action. She demonstrates that the belief-desire model is not the foundation of all agency but merely one species of it. The genus of intentional action is broader, encompassing both instrumental and expressive modes.

**Conclusion**

Rosalind Hursthouse’s theory of arational actions successfully undermines Donald Davidson’s belief-desire theory of intentional action, albeit not by destroying it, but by exposing its explanatory limits. The distinction between pre-named and post-named epistemic states reveals that during emotional outbursts, agents act in a state devoid of propositional content. The pre-named action lacks both a named emotion and a corresponding belief-desire pair, directly challenging the causal account that demands such a pair for intentionality. This challenge forces us to confront the normative dilemma of moral culpability. We cannot excuse these actions as mere reflexes, yet we cannot hold them to the standard of rational deliberation. The solution lies in the capacity for intentional preventative emotion moderation. Agents are indirectly accountable for their outbursts because they failed to exercise their rational capacity to moderate their emotional dispositions. This bridges the gap between the unintentional, occurrent action and moral responsibility.

Ultimately, actions driven purely by occurrent emotion do not collapse into unintentional reflexes. They demand a revised framework of intentionality. By distinguishing between instrumental and expressive intentionality, we can acknowledge that arational actions are genuine expressions of agency, even though they lack a belief-desire pair. Hursthouse’s insight is not that emotions make us irrational, but that they make us *arational*—acting outside the scope of reason while remaining within the scope of agency. This forces us to abandon the monistic view that all intentional action is rational action. The philosophy of action must accommodate the passions, not just the intellect. It must recognize that we are creatures who do not only act for reasons, but also act *from* emotion, and that both modes of action are integral to our moral and psychological lives. In doing so, we achieve a richer, more accurate understanding of what it means to be an agent.
