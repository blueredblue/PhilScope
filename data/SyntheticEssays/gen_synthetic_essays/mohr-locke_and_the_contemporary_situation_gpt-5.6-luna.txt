# Locke, Exit, and the Predicament of the Modern Individualist

Tyson Mohr’s argument that John Locke’s political theory fails to support contemporary individualists and libertarians presents a serious challenge to the modern use of Lockean language. Locke is commonly regarded as a philosophical ancestor of limited government, private property, individual rights, and resistance to tyranny. Yet the political circumstances presupposed by Locke’s theory differ sharply from those of contemporary political life. Locke writes as though individuals may enter political society by consent, retain ultimate authority over their own lives and estates, and withdraw from a government that violates its trust. Modern individuals, however, are born into large, densely populated, territorially comprehensive states. They generally cannot find unoccupied land outside political jurisdiction, cannot practically withhold themselves from taxation, and cannot exit one political order without entering another. If tacit consent depends upon a meaningful opportunity to leave, then modern political obligations may lack the Lockean foundation frequently attributed to them.

Mohr’s argument is strongest when it exposes the tension between Locke’s theory of consent and the realities of modern territorial states. The lack of *in vacuis locis*—unoccupied places where a person might establish an independent political existence—undermines the claim that continued residence constitutes voluntary consent. The scale of modern states also makes individual exit less meaningful than Locke’s examples suggest. A person may technically emigrate, but emigration normally means submission to another state rather than recovery of a genuinely nonpolitical condition. In this respect, modern individualists may indeed be deprived of the practical alternative that makes tacit consent plausible.

Nevertheless, Mohr’s conclusion that modern individualists are left with no legitimate recourse other than submission to majority rule or revolution is too strong. Locke’s theory contains resources for challenging government that do not depend entirely upon access to unoccupied land. Moreover, the failure of tacit consent does not automatically imply either complete political illegitimacy or the immediate right of violent revolution. The more defensible conclusion is that modern circumstances expose a serious gap between Lockean legitimacy and actual political obligation. They do not, by themselves, establish that every contemporary government is tyrannical or that every individual has only two options: obedience or revolution.

The central issue is therefore not simply whether Locke can justify libertarianism. It is whether his theory can explain how individuals may remain free when they are born into political institutions they did not choose, cannot realistically leave, and are compelled to support through taxation and legal compliance.

## I. Locke’s Political Theory and the Meaning of Consent

Locke begins from the natural freedom and equality of persons. In the state of nature, no individual is naturally subject to the arbitrary will of another. Each person possesses rights to life, liberty, and estate, and each has a duty not to harm another in these fundamental interests. Political society is not therefore the original condition of human beings but an institutional arrangement created to remedy inconveniences in the state of nature. Individuals consent to establish a common authority because the state of nature lacks a settled law, an impartial judge, and an effective power of enforcement.

The purpose of political society is accordingly limited. Government exists for the preservation of property, where “property” includes not merely material possessions but also life and liberty. The legislative power is the supreme power within the commonwealth, but it is not arbitrary. It remains bound by the law of nature, must govern through established laws, and may not take property without consent. Government is a fiduciary power: rulers exercise authority on behalf of the people and for the ends for which political society was established.

This structure produces two different kinds of consent. First, Locke speaks of express consent. An individual may explicitly agree to join a commonwealth and thereby become a full member of it. Second, Locke recognizes tacit consent. A person who enjoys land or other benefits within a government’s dominion is said to give tacit consent to its laws. In the *Second Treatise*, Locke writes that anyone who possesses or enjoys part of the dominions of a government thereby gives tacit consent and is obligated to obey its laws “during such enjoyment.” This idea is meant to explain how political obligations can arise even when individuals have not signed a formal contract.

Tacit consent is attractive because it avoids the implausible requirement that every individual must expressly approve the political system before any law can bind him. It also reflects a plausible moral intuition: those who voluntarily use an institution or accept its benefits may incur obligations to support it. But Locke’s argument depends upon the voluntariness of the relevant enjoyment. If a person has no reasonable alternative to remaining within the state, then residence may resemble compelled submission rather than consent.

Locke’s discussion of departure is essential here. His theory does not imply that a person is permanently bound to a government merely because he was born within its territory. Children are under the authority of their parents until they reach maturity, but adulthood restores their natural freedom. A person who comes of age does not automatically become a perpetual subject simply because he was born in a particular country. Locke suggests that an individual may leave, relinquish his property, and thereby withdraw from the political obligations associated with its enjoyment. The theory therefore presupposes a meaningful right of exit.

This right of exit performs two functions. It supports the voluntariness of tacit consent, and it limits the authority of the state. If individuals may leave, then remaining can plausibly be interpreted as acceptance of the political order. If they cannot leave, then continued residence cannot easily bear the moral weight Locke assigns to it.

## II. *In Vacuis Locis* and the Problem of Exit

The phrase *in vacuis locis*, meaning “in empty places,” captures an important background assumption in early modern theories of political society. Locke believed that individuals could, at least in principle, move into areas not yet occupied or politically organized by established commonwealths. Although Locke did not imagine a world entirely without conflict or indigenous populations, his political argument relies on the possibility that political membership is not unavoidable. There must be some space in which a person can live without being subject to the authority of a particular government.

For the contemporary individualist, this assumption is largely absent. Nearly all habitable land is subject to the jurisdiction of a state. Even remote regions are governed by national law, international law, or some combination of legal regimes. A person who rejects the authority of his state cannot ordinarily move to territory outside political jurisdiction. He may migrate from one country to another, but this is not an escape from government as such. It is a transfer from one government to another, often contingent upon permission, documentation, taxation, and legal compliance.

This fact weakens the Lockean argument from tacit consent in several ways.

First, the absence of unoccupied land removes the possibility of refusing political membership altogether. A person may reject the laws of one state, but unless another state accepts him, he has nowhere legally recognized to go. The choice is not between political subjection and independence; it is generally between different forms of political subjection.

Second, modern states regulate movement far more extensively than the relatively decentralized political communities Locke had in mind. Exit is not merely a matter of physically leaving. A person may need a passport, visa, residence permit, employment authorization, or access to property in the destination country. The state of origin may tax citizens who leave, restrict the export of assets, or impose legal obligations that continue after departure. The destination state may refuse entry altogether. Thus, formal mobility does not necessarily amount to a genuine moral opportunity to withdraw consent.

Third, the scale of modern states changes the character of residence. Locke’s examples often concern individuals who possess land within a political community. Their use of land and enjoyment of the government’s protection are relatively direct and visible. By contrast, a contemporary person is born into a vast administrative system that regulates transportation, education, labor, health, communication, finance, and property. The individual does not ordinarily choose the relevant institutions one by one. He receives some services, but he is also compelled to finance and comply with institutions he may reject. The connection between benefit and consent becomes correspondingly weaker.

Mohr is therefore right to argue that the modern world destabilizes the Lockean basis of tacit consent. If tacit consent requires a voluntary choice to remain, and if departure is practically impossible except by submitting to another state, then mere residence cannot be treated as decisive evidence of consent. The phrase “you are free to leave” loses much of its force when there is nowhere outside the relevant system to go.

Still, two qualifications are necessary. The first concerns what “exit” must mean. Locke may not require the existence of completely ungoverned territory. His point could be that a person is free to leave one commonwealth and join another. If so, contemporary international migration preserves some degree of exit. A person dissatisfied with one legal order may move to another jurisdiction with lower taxes, greater civil liberties, or more limited government. Competition among states may impose constraints on political power even where no state-free frontier exists.

This interpretation, however, only partially rescues Locke. If every destination is itself a compulsory political order, migration offers choice among governments rather than freedom from government. It might support a weaker theory of tacit consent—consent to a particular state rather than to political authority in general—but it cannot establish that individuals consent to the basic institution of coercive government. Nor does it help those who lack the resources, legal status, family circumstances, or skills necessary to migrate.

The second qualification concerns the nature of moral consent. Consent is not always invalidated by the absence of ideal alternatives. In ordinary life, an individual may accept an imperfect contract when all available options are unattractive. A person who chooses one employer over another, one university over another, or one landlord over another may be consenting even though no option is perfect. Political authority could similarly be justified by relative choice rather than absolute independence.

Yet this analogy has limits. Ordinary contracts are usually entered into by identifiable parties, concern specified obligations, and can be terminated under known conditions. State authority is comprehensive, territorial, and backed by force. A person cannot ordinarily negotiate the terms of citizenship or refuse only those laws to which he objects. The fact that one may choose between several coercive systems does not necessarily mean that one has consented to coercion as such.

The absence of *in vacuis locis* therefore does not prove that all political authority is illegitimate, but it does show that Locke’s argument from tacit consent is insufficient in contemporary conditions. Residence may generate prudential reasons for compliance and perhaps limited reciprocal obligations, but it cannot by itself establish full moral consent.

## III. Majority Rule and the Problem of Individual Dissent

Locke’s theory does not stop with individual consent. Once individuals form a political community, he argues, the community must act as a body. Because unanimity would make collective action impossible, the majority must have authority to decide for the whole. This is not necessarily inconsistent with individual freedom, according to Locke, because individuals consent to the formation of a political body whose decisions are determined by the majority.

Here lies another difficulty for contemporary individualism. If an individual never expressly consented to join the commonwealth, and if tacit consent is invalid because exit is unavailable, then majority rule seems to rest on a missing premise. Why should a person be bound by decisions he did not make and could not reasonably refuse? The problem is especially acute when the majority enacts policies that directly violate the individual’s understanding of property, conscience, or personal liberty.

The Lockean answer is that membership in a community involves submission to the judgment of the majority. But this answer is persuasive only if the initial act of membership was voluntary. A majority may govern those who authorize it; it is less clear why it may govern those who merely happen to be born within its territory. The principle that “the majority must decide” is a rule for organizing a legitimate political body, not an independent proof that every person has consented to belong to that body.

Contemporary individualists therefore face a dilemma. If they accept majority rule, they appear to abandon the idea that individual rights impose strong limits on collective decisions. If they reject majority rule, they need some alternative mechanism for resolving disputes and providing public goods. Locke offers no fully developed third model. His theory is neither anarchist nor radically pluralist. It permits political society to impose laws on individuals once the society has been legitimately constituted.

Mohr’s claim that modern individualists are left with submission to majority rule or revolution draws its force from this dilemma. Without a genuine right of exit, an individual cannot simply decline membership. Without majority rule, the state cannot make collective decisions. And if the government violates individual rights, Locke’s ultimate remedy is resistance or revolution. The individual thus appears trapped between obedience to collective decisions and the extraordinary act of overthrowing the government.

The dilemma is real, but it is not exhaustive. Between passive submission and revolution lie constitutional litigation, electoral organization, decentralization, civil disobedience, conscientious objection, state competition, local self-government, and peaceful secession. These mechanisms may not be sufficient in every case, but their existence shows that Locke’s framework need not be reduced to a binary choice. Indeed, Locke’s own distinction between the dissolution of a government and the dissolution of society suggests that resistance can be directed at particular rulers, institutions, or laws without requiring the destruction of political association as such.

## IV. Taxation, Property, and Consent

The question of taxation is central to the dispute. Locke clearly treats property as a fundamental interest, and he insists that the legislative power cannot take any part of a person’s property without his consent. This principle has often been read as a foundation for libertarian opposition to taxation. If taxation transfers resources without the individual’s express agreement, then it appears to violate the Lockean prohibition on taking property without consent.

Locke’s actual position is more complex. He does not say that every individual must personally approve every tax. In a representative political system, consent may be given through representatives chosen by the people. Moreover, once individuals enter political society, they authorize the majority to act for the whole. Taxation enacted by a legitimate legislature may therefore count as consensual in a political rather than personal sense.

For a contemporary libertarian, this answer is unsatisfying. A person may have no realistic ability to select a representative who opposes all taxation, and even a representative elected by a majority may impose taxes on those who voted against him. The person’s property is taken regardless of his individual dissent. If the relevant standard is actual individual consent, democratic authorization is not enough.

But Locke’s theory does not straightforwardly embrace the individualist standard. He distinguishes arbitrary power from lawful power, not coercion from noncoercion. A tax may be coercively collected yet legitimate if imposed by a law enacted through the authorized political process and directed toward the preservation of the community. The key Lockean questions are therefore whether the legislature has authority to tax, whether taxation is imposed by established law, whether it is proportionate and general rather than arbitrary, and whether it serves the public purposes for which government exists.

This does not mean that all contemporary taxation is Lockean. Taxation may violate Locke’s principles when it is imposed without meaningful representation, selectively targets political opponents, confiscates property for private benefit, or funds policies unrelated to the preservation of life, liberty, and estate. A government that taxes people in order to enrich officials or favored groups acts outside its trust. A tax system that treats citizens as sources of revenue rather than as rights-bearing persons may also approach arbitrary power.

The more difficult issue is ordinary taxation for public services and social programs. Does funding education, health care, income support, environmental regulation, or infrastructure exceed the Lockean purpose of government? A strict libertarian interpretation would often say yes, particularly where taxation is used for redistribution rather than protection against force and fraud. A more historically sensitive interpretation would note that Locke’s conception of preservation is broader than the protection of bodily security. Government may need to sustain the legal, economic, and institutional conditions under which property and liberty can exist. Courts, roads, public administration, defense, and emergency measures all require collective finance.

Locke also recognizes duties of charity and the moral claims of others. A person may not enjoy an unlimited right to retain property while others face imminent starvation. His theory therefore does not establish an absolute principle against all compulsory transfer. It establishes a presumption against arbitrary taking and in favor of consent, legality, and preservation.

Mohr’s claim that contemporary taxation does not constitute a justifiable ground for rebellion under Locke’s criteria must be assessed against this background. If the claim means that Locke’s criteria make taxation almost impossible to challenge, it is overstated. Locke expressly states that taxation without consent is a violation of property. A systematic tax regime that lacks genuine representation or exceeds the purposes of government could be evidence of legislative betrayal.

If, however, the claim means that ordinary taxation under a representative government does not automatically justify rebellion, it is correct. Locke does not authorize rebellion whenever a person believes that a tax is excessive, inefficient, or morally objectionable. Government is not dissolved by every error or instance of mismanagement. Rebellion becomes justified when rulers attempt to destroy the rights they were instituted to preserve, place themselves in a state of war against the people, or systematically exercise arbitrary power.

This distinction is important. A libertarian may have a strong argument that a particular tax is unjust without yet having a Lockean argument for revolution. Political injustice and revolutionary justification are not identical. The former may demand reform, litigation, electoral resistance, or conscientious noncompliance. The latter requires a sufficiently grave and systematic breach of trust.

## V. Non-Minimal Government and the Threshold of Rebellion

Modern governments are not minimal in the classical libertarian sense. They regulate economic activity, redistribute income, provide social insurance, license occupations, supervise education, restrict land use, and maintain extensive administrative agencies. The question is whether these practices are merely objectionable or whether they dissolve the government’s legitimacy under Locke.

Locke’s criteria are demanding. The government must preserve property, rule through known laws, avoid arbitrary power, and act for the public good. A state that performs these functions imperfectly does not necessarily forfeit all authority. Locke warns against rebellion for “every little mismanagement” and recognizes that people may tolerate a series of abuses so long as they do not perceive a settled design to destroy their liberties. The threshold for justified resistance is therefore higher than the threshold for criticizing policy.

This threshold makes sense from Locke’s perspective. Government exists within conditions of uncertainty and social interdependence. Legislators must make general judgments about public safety, economic stability, defense, and welfare. Not every policy that reduces an individual’s options is an assault on liberty. Traffic laws, building regulations, taxation for defense, and public-health measures may constrain conduct while still serving the preservation of the community.

Yet Locke’s caution creates a problem for contemporary libertarians. If almost every non-minimal policy can be defended as contributing indirectly to preservation, then the limits on government become practically weak. The state may claim that extensive welfare, regulation, surveillance, and economic management are necessary for public order. The individual is then told that disagreement with these policies is merely a difference of opinion, not a violation of rights.

The proper Lockean response is to apply a cumulative test. A government need not be openly tyrannical in order to breach its trust. Repeated taxation without meaningful consent, restrictions on peaceful association, arbitrary administrative discretion, suppression of political opposition, and disregard for property may together establish a pattern of domination. The question is not whether each individual policy can be given a plausible public justification. It is whether the political system, taken as a whole, continues to treat individuals as owners of their lives and estates or has converted them into subjects of discretionary management.

On this issue, Locke’s theory remains more useful than Mohr’s binary formulation suggests. It supplies standards for judging government before the point of revolution. One can argue that a state has exceeded its proper powers, demand repeal, organize opposition, refuse cooperation with particular measures, or pursue constitutional remedies. These actions are not equivalent to passive submission, nor do they require an immediate attempt to overthrow the regime.

Still, there is a deep structural limitation. Locke gives the people the right to judge whether rulers have violated their trust, but he does not provide a precise institutional method for making that judgment. The same majority that authorizes government may support policies that violate the rights of a minority. Courts may defer to the legislature. Elections may offer only broad and infrequent choices. Administrative systems may make responsibility difficult to identify. The individual’s legal remedies may therefore be inadequate even when the moral case against government is strong.

This is where contemporary individualism exposes an unresolved weakness in Locke. The theory recognizes a right of resistance, but resistance is most effective when exercised collectively. An individual alone cannot realistically resist a modern state. He must persuade others, join organizations, form political movements, or rely on institutions. The Lockean individual is philosophically prior to the state, but in practice the individual’s ability to defend his rights depends upon collective action.

## VI. Is Revolution the Only Alternative to Submission?

Mohr’s strongest conclusion is that, once exit is unavailable and majority rule is established, the modern individualist is left with only submission or revolution. This conclusion deserves a careful distinction between logical and practical alternatives.

As a matter of logic, the conclusion does not follow. The failure of tacit consent does not imply that no other form of consent or legitimacy is possible. A political order might be justified by hypothetical consent, fair terms of cooperation, constitutional rights, public justification, or the necessity of maintaining a legal framework for peaceful coexistence. One may reject Locke’s consent theory without accepting either majority domination or revolution.

As a matter of political practice, however, the situation is more troubling. Most individuals cannot realistically establish independent communities, refuse all taxation, or unilaterally withdraw from the legal order. They must either comply or accept serious penalties. Their options are constrained by political facts rather than by philosophical argument. In this practical sense, Mohr is right that modern individualists lack the clean exit option that makes Lockean political obligation attractive.

Even so, “revolution” should not be understood as the only form of resistance. Locke’s right of resistance is broader than a violent overthrow of the state. It includes opposition to rulers who place themselves in a state of war against the people. Resistance may be defensive, selective, and proportionate. It may involve refusing to obey a particular unlawful command, protecting a persecuted person, removing an abusive official, or reforming the constitutional structure. Revolution in Locke’s sense is sometimes the dissolution of a government, not necessarily a social collapse or civil war.

There are also intermediate forms of exit. Individuals may withdraw from particular institutions, choose alternative schools, form private associations, move between jurisdictions, use civil society to provide services, and participate in local or regional self-government. These measures do not create complete independence, but they reduce dependence upon centralized authority. They can also generate evidence about whether state functions are genuinely necessary or merely protected by political privilege.

Civil disobedience presents another intermediate possibility. A person who believes that a law violates fundamental rights may publicly refuse compliance while accepting legal consequences and appealing to the conscience of fellow citizens. This is not full submission, since the individual denies the moral authority of the law, but neither is it revolution. Its legitimacy depends on the seriousness of the injustice, the nonviolent character of the resistance, and the willingness to recognize the equal rights of others.

The possibility of such intermediate responses does not eliminate the central problem. If the state can imprison, fine, tax, or otherwise coerce a person for refusing policies he has never consented to, then the person remains politically unfree in an important sense. Liberal institutions may soften this condition without abolishing it. The individual may have legal rights, but those rights are interpreted and enforced by institutions that possess coercive power over him. The fact that he can vote, litigate, or protest does not transform imposed membership into voluntary membership.

The best defense of Locke, then, should not insist that modern individuals tacitly consent simply by remaining. That claim is too implausible. Instead, Locke can be defended by separating three propositions:

1. Residence does not necessarily establish consent.
2. Political authority may nevertheless be legitimate under certain nonconsensual standards.
3. Even where authority is imperfectly legitimate, rebellion requires a grave and systematic violation of fundamental rights.

This defense concedes much of Mohr’s criticism while rejecting the conclusion that only submission or revolution remains.

## VII. An Independent Position: The Right of Exit Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

My own position is that the modern absence of meaningful exit seriously undermines tacit consent, but it does not invalidate all political obligation or reduce political choice to two alternatives. The right of exit is necessary for Locke’s particular argument from tacit consent, but it is not sufficient for political legitimacy, and its absence does not itself establish a general right of revolution.

The right of exit is necessary because consent must be voluntary. If the state claims that a person has consented merely because he was born within its boundaries and continues to live there, it must explain why remaining is a choice rather than a condition imposed by circumstance. The availability of realistic exit is one way to answer that challenge. Without it, the state cannot plausibly infer comprehensive consent from residence alone.

But exit is not sufficient because departure may be costly, coercively restricted, or morally problematic. A government should not be able to evade all limitations simply by saying that dissatisfied citizens may leave. A person who has spent his life contributing to a society may have claims against it even if he departs. Moreover, governments can violate rights in ways that affect nonresidents, and a world in which every state commits similar abuses would not be redeemed by the existence of nominal migration options.

Political legitimacy therefore requires more than consent. It requires publicly defensible institutions that respect basic rights, distribute coercive burdens fairly, and permit meaningful participation and contestation. Consent remains important because it expresses self-government, but it cannot bear the entire burden of justification. A person may be bound by a just legal order not because he has personally agreed to every law, but because the order protects equal freedom under conditions in which no person can avoid some common rules.

This argument does not vindicate every modern state. A government that imposes extensive taxation, regulates peaceful conduct without adequate justification, and treats majority approval as a license to violate minorities fails to meet Lockean standards even if it holds elections. Democratic procedures are not a blank check. Majority rule is legitimate only within a framework of rights that the majority itself cannot abolish.

At the same time, the state’s failure to satisfy a strict libertarian conception of property does not automatically authorize rebellion. A right of revolution must be tied to the underlying purpose of political society. If the government systematically places individuals under arbitrary power, confiscates property for factional purposes, suppresses opposition, and denies peaceful means of reform, resistance may be justified. If the complaint concerns ordinary taxation enacted through representative procedures for publicly defensible purposes, the Lockean case for rebellion is much weaker.

This position implies that contemporary individualists have legitimate recourse beyond submission and revolution, but those recourses are imperfect. They include political persuasion, legal challenge, institutional reform, decentralization, civil disobedience, conscientious refusal, migration where feasible, and organized resistance to specific abuses. Their imperfection is not evidence that they are meaningless. Political freedom is often a matter of degrees and institutional safeguards rather than a simple possession or absence of consent.

## Conclusion

Mohr’s criticism identifies a genuine crisis in the Lockean account of political obligation. Locke’s theory gains much of its force from the idea that individuals are naturally free, that political authority arises by consent, and that persons may withdraw from a government that violates its trust. In a world of unoccupied land and relatively limited political jurisdictions, tacit consent through residence could appear plausible. In a world of densely populated states, universal territorial jurisdiction, administrative government, and restricted migration, the argument becomes far less convincing.

The disappearance of *in vacuis locis* weakens the right of exit and thereby undermines the inference from residence to consent. Modern individuals generally cannot escape political authority altogether; they can only move among states that make similar claims to jurisdiction. For this reason, contemporary individualists are justified in rejecting the idea that mere residence constitutes full and binding consent to every law or tax.

Yet this does not entail that all modern political authority is illegitimate. Nor does it show that the only alternatives are majority submission or revolution. Locke’s distinction between ordinary misgovernment and a systematic breach of trust allows for a graduated theory of resistance. Taxation and non-minimal government may be unjust when arbitrary, confiscatory, unrepresentative, or unrelated to the preservation of rights. But they do not automatically satisfy Locke’s demanding criteria for rebellion. The proper judgment depends upon the character, purpose, and cumulative effect of governmental action.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore intermediate. Modern individualists are not fully vindicated by Locke, because the practical conditions that made tacit consent plausible have largely disappeared. But they are not abandoned to political helplessness. Locke still supplies principles of limited government, nonarbitrary law, property protection, and resistance to systematic domination. What modern circumstances reveal is that these principles require supplementation by a more developed account of constitutional rights, meaningful mobility, minority protection, institutional pluralism, and peaceful noncompliance.

Locke’s theory fails if it is treated as proving that contemporary citizens consent merely by staying where they were born. It remains valuable, however, if understood as a framework for judging whether political power continues to serve the free and equal persons from whom it claims authority. The absence of an easy exit does not make rebellion inevitable, but it does make the state’s burden of justification much heavier. A government that cannot be left must be especially careful not to rule as though its citizens had freely chosen everything it does.
