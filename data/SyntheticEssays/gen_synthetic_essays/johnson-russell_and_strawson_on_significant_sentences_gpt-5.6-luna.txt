# Denoting Phrases, Referring Uses, and the Limits of a Unified Theory

The philosophical problem posed by empty referring terms is deceptively simple. What is the status of a sentence such as “The present king of France is bald” when there is no present king of France? Does the sentence express a proposition that is false? Does it fail to express a proposition at all? Is it meaningful but neither true nor false? Or does its status depend upon what a speaker is doing in uttering it, rather than upon the sentence considered in abstraction from its use?

Bertrand Russell and P. F. Strawson offer two of the most influential answers to these questions. Russell’s theory of descriptions, presented most famously in “On Denoting,” treats definite descriptions not as names of mysterious objects but as quantificational constructions whose logical form can be made explicit. In this way, Russell aims to preserve classical logic while avoiding commitment to nonexistent entities. Strawson, by contrast, emphasizes the distinction between the meaning of a sentence and what a speaker does in using it. For him, the failure of a referring expression to pick out an object does not automatically make the sentence false; it may instead result in a failure of reference, or in a failure to make a true-or-false assertion.

The disagreement is not merely about the sentence “The present king of France is bald.” It concerns the relation between language and reality, between semantic structure and pragmatic practice, and between truth as a property of sentences or propositions and truth as a norm governing particular acts of assertion. Russell’s theory promises a systematic logical treatment of denoting phrases, while Strawson’s account captures the flexibility and contextual sensitivity of ordinary reference. Each exposes a weakness in the other. Russell can seem to disregard the difference between a sentence’s logical form and its role in conversation; Strawson can seem to leave truth conditions insufficiently determinate and to blur the line between semantic failure and pragmatic infelicity.

A unified framework combining Russell’s logical analysis with Strawson’s theory of reference can resolve much of the apparent conflict, but only if it is understood as a layered theory rather than as a simple fusion of the two views. Russell supplies a rigorous account of the truth conditions and logical commitments associated with descriptions. Strawson supplies an account of how speakers use expressions to refer and of how failed reference affects communicative acts. Together they can explain why an empty description may occur in a meaningful sentence without referring to anything, why the sentence may have a determinate truth condition even when an utterance fails to make a successful assertion, and why the conversational status of an utterance need not coincide with the truth value of the sentence type. Nevertheless, the unified framework does not entirely eliminate the problem. It must still determine when reference is semantically required, when it is merely pragmatically intended, and whether truth-value gaps should be admitted at the level of propositions or only at the level of assertion and use.

## Russell’s Logical Treatment of Denoting Phrases

Russell’s central insight is that grammatical form can conceal logical form. A phrase such as “the author of Waverley” appears grammatically to function like a singular term. It occupies the position that a proper name might occupy, and ordinary speakers may treat it as standing for some particular individual, namely Walter Scott. Yet Russell argues that the phrase does not itself name an object. Its logical contribution is instead analyzed through quantification.

“The author of Waverley is a poet” is rendered approximately as follows:

1. There is at least one author of *Waverley*.
2. There is at most one author of *Waverley*.
3. Whoever is the author of *Waverley* is a poet.

In symbolic form:

\[
\exists x[(A(x) \land \forall y(A(y)\rightarrow y=x)) \land P(x)]
\]

where \(A(x)\) means “\(x\) is the author of *Waverley*” and \(P(x)\) means “\(x\) is a poet.”

The apparent singular term has therefore disappeared. The sentence does not contain a reference to an object called “the author of Waverley.” It asserts that there exists exactly one object satisfying a certain condition and that this object is a poet. Russell’s analysis is thus not simply a theory of the meaning of one particular kind of phrase. It is also a strategy for eliminating ontological commitments that ordinary grammar seems to impose.

This strategy becomes especially important in cases involving empty descriptions. Consider:

> The present king of France is bald.

Russell analyzes this as asserting:

1. There exists at least one present king of France.
2. There is at most one present king of France.
3. Whoever is the present king of France is bald.

Since there is no present king of France, the existential component is false. The whole conjunction is therefore false. The sentence is not meaningless, and it does not require reference to a nonexistent king. It has a determinate logical form and a determinate truth value: it is false.

Russell’s solution has several advantages. First, it avoids the metaphysical puzzle of how a sentence can be about an object that does not exist. If “the present king of France” is treated as a name, then the sentence appears to require a denoted object. If no such object exists, the semantic machinery seems to break down. Russell avoids this by denying that the phrase is a name in the relevant logical sense.

Second, the analysis preserves the law of excluded middle. If “The present king of France is bald” is false, then its negation, “The present king of France is not bald,” is true, provided that the negation is understood as having scope over the entire description. This result matters because it distinguishes two forms of negation that ordinary language can obscure:

> The present king of France is not bald.

This may mean:

> It is not the case that there is exactly one present king of France who is bald.

Under this reading, the sentence is true because there is no present king of France. But another sentence,

> The present king of France is non-bald,

would be analyzed as asserting that there exists exactly one present king of France and that this individual is non-bald. That sentence is false. Russell’s theory therefore gives a principled account of scope and avoids treating the absence of a referent as a semantic catastrophe.

Third, Russell’s analysis explains why descriptions can occur in intensional and epistemic contexts without straightforwardly referring to objects. “George IV wondered whether Scott was the author of Waverley” differs from “George IV wondered whether Scott was Scott,” even though Scott was in fact the author of *Waverley*. The descriptive phrase contributes a condition under which an object is uniquely identified, rather than merely functioning as a synonym for a name. Russell’s theory can therefore account for differences in cognitive significance that a simple referential theory might miss.

Yet Russell’s position also creates difficulties. It seems to deny too much to ordinary reference. Speakers commonly use descriptions to pick out individuals. Someone may say “The man in the corner is drinking coffee” while looking toward a particular man. The description may not be intended as a quantificational assertion about uniqueness. It may function as a way of directing an audience’s attention to a person already salient in the situation. Russell’s formal analysis can represent the truth conditions of one reading of the sentence, but it may fail to describe what the speaker is doing in uttering it.

Moreover, Russell’s claim that every meaningful sentence containing a definite description has a truth value may conflict with ordinary intuitions about presupposition. “The present king of France is bald” seems to take for granted that there is a present king of France. When that assumption is false, many speakers do not respond by saying simply, “That is false.” They respond, “There is no present king of France.” The conversational reaction suggests that something has gone wrong before the question of baldness can be evaluated. It is precisely this dimension that Strawson brings to the foreground.

## Strawson on Sentences, Uses, and Reference

In “On Referring,” Strawson objects to the idea that the logical analysis of a sentence automatically determines what a speaker has said by uttering it. He distinguishes at least three things: the expression or sentence itself, the use of that expression on a particular occasion, and the act of referring performed by a speaker in that use.

A sentence type has a meaning in virtue of the conventions governing its words and structure. But the use of a sentence involves a context, a speaker, an audience, a time, and an intended act. The same sentence may be used on different occasions to refer to different individuals, or it may fail to refer altogether. Consequently, the truth or falsity of what is said cannot always be determined by considering the sentence in isolation.

Strawson’s famous example is again:

> The present king of France is wise.

According to Russell, this is false because it entails the existence of a present king of France and that existential claim is false. According to Strawson, the sentence is meaningful, but a speaker who utters it as a present-tense referring expression fails to make a true-or-false assertion if there is no present king of France. The speaker has attempted to refer, but the referring attempt has failed. The utterance is therefore defective or infelicitous rather than straightforwardly false.

Strawson’s position is often associated with the idea of presupposition. The use of “the present king of France” presupposes, in the relevant context, that there is a present king of France. If that presupposition is not satisfied, the assertion does not receive an ordinary truth value. This differs from Russell’s treatment, in which the existence and uniqueness condition is part of what the sentence asserts and therefore makes the sentence false when unsatisfied.

The distinction between sentence and use allows Strawson to explain an important fact about language: a sentence can be meaningful even when it is not successfully used to refer. Suppose a child learns the expression “the man in the moon” from stories and then utters, “The man in the moon is asleep.” The child’s sentence has a recognizable meaning. But whether the child successfully refers depends upon the conversational situation and upon whether the expression is being used fictionally, mythologically, demonstratively, or as an ordinary description. Meaning does not guarantee successful reference.

Strawson also emphasizes that reference is often not an intrinsic property of an expression. A proper name, for example, can be used to refer to different people in different contexts, and a description can be used to refer to an object that does not uniquely satisfy its descriptive content. If a host says, “The man drinking martini is my brother,” while the man is actually drinking water from a martini glass, the description may still successfully refer to him. The speaker’s demonstrative intentions and the contextual circumstances can override strict descriptive accuracy.

This feature of Strawson’s theory captures the communicative life of language more effectively than Russell’s purely logical analysis. It explains why reference is not reducible to the satisfaction of a description and why the same sentence can be used successfully in one context and unsuccessfully in another. It also makes sense of the difference between a speaker’s attempt to say something and the truth of what is said.

However, Strawson’s account raises its own problems. If an utterance involving an empty description is neither true nor false, how should its logical relations be understood? Can it be negated? Does it entail anything? How does it interact with conditionals, quantifiers, and indirect speech? A theory centered on use and presupposition may describe conversational reactions without supplying a sufficiently precise semantics.

There is also a risk that Strawson’s appeal to failed reference treats all apparent failures alike. The phrase “the present king of France” may fail because the relevant individual does not exist. But “the man in the corner” may fail because the audience cannot identify anyone satisfying the speaker’s intention, even though there are several plausible candidates. A fictional name such as “Sherlock Holmes” can be used meaningfully despite lacking an ordinary-world referent. A metaphorical or deferred use of a description may succeed without satisfying its literal descriptive content. Strawson’s emphasis on use makes these distinctions visible, but it does not by itself provide a unified account of them.

## The Dialectical Tension

The conflict between Russell and Strawson can be framed as a tension between two conceptions of linguistic analysis.

Russell is primarily concerned with the logical form of propositions and with the ontological commitments carried by apparently referring expressions. His question is: what must be true for the sentence to be true, and how can its logical structure be represented without commitment to nonexistent objects? His answer is extensional and compositional. A definite description contributes an existence-and-uniqueness condition, and the sentence has a truth value determined by whether that condition and the predication are satisfied.

Strawson is primarily concerned with the conditions under which a speaker succeeds in referring and asserting. His question is: what does a speaker do in using this sentence in a context, and what happens when the intended reference fails? His answer is pragmatic and contextual. A description can be used to refer, and its use may presuppose the existence of an object. If the presupposition fails, the utterance may be defective without being false.

The disagreement therefore concerns not only the truth value of one sentence but also the level at which the truth-value question should be asked. Russell asks about the sentence or proposition considered as an abstract logical structure. Strawson asks about an utterance as a communicative act. It is possible for Russell’s sentence to be false while Strawson’s utterance fails to make a true-or-false assertion. The two claims are not strictly contradictory if they concern different objects of evaluation.

This observation provides the beginning of a reconciliation. Yet the reconciliation must not simply say that Russell is talking about sentences and Strawson about uses, since both philosophers make broader claims. Russell sometimes presents his analysis as revealing what ordinary speakers mean, while Strawson treats presupposition as a feature of the sentence’s use and perhaps of its conventional meaning. The question is whether the distinction between levels can be made sharp enough to support a unified framework.

A central issue is whether existence is asserted or presupposed by a definite description. Russell says that in “The F is G,” existence, uniqueness, and predication are all asserted. Strawson says that the use of “the F” normally presupposes that there is a unique F. The difference affects the truth value of negated sentences.

Consider:

> The present king of France is not bald.

On Russell’s analysis, if the sentence is read with wide-scope negation, it is true because the assertion that there is exactly one present king of France who is bald is false. On a presuppositional account, however, the existence of a present king of France remains presupposed under negation. Since the presupposition fails, the sentence is neither true nor false in the ordinary use. This is one reason the dispute cannot be settled simply by appealing to intuitions about the positive sentence. The behavior of negation, conditionals, questions, and modal contexts tests the underlying theory.

The Russellian approach has the virtue of logical uniformity. The Strawsonian approach has the virtue of explaining why negative sentences do not always behave like ordinary truth-functional negations in conversation. If a person says, “The present king of France is not bald,” a hearer may reasonably object, “There is no present king of France,” rather than accept the statement as true. The presuppositional account explains this conversational pattern more naturally.

On the other hand, a strict presuppositional theory can threaten classical logical relations. If “The F is G” and “The F is not G” are both neither true nor false when there is no F, then the law of excluded middle no longer applies straightforwardly to ordinary language. That may be acceptable, but it requires a theory of partiality or a nonclassical logic. Strawson himself did not develop a fully formal system that could govern all such cases. Russell’s theory remains attractive precisely because it preserves a determinate semantics without requiring truth-value gaps.

## What a Unified Framework Would Require

A successful synthesis should distinguish at least four levels:

1. **Syntactic form:** the grammatical sentence and its possible logical analyses.
2. **Semantic content:** the truth conditions associated with a particular interpretation.
3. **Pragmatic use:** the speaker’s act of referring, asserting, questioning, or presupposing in a context.
4. **Conversational evaluation:** whether the utterance is felicitous, informative, misleading, or defective.

Russell’s theory operates primarily at the second level, though it also informs the first. Strawson’s theory operates primarily at the third and fourth levels, though it has implications for semantics. The synthesis is possible if neither level is reduced to the other.

Under such a framework, “The present king of France is bald” can receive a Russellian semantic analysis:

\[
\exists x[(K(x) \land \forall y(K(y)\rightarrow y=x)) \land B(x)]
\]

This formula is false in the actual world because the uniqueness-and-existence condition fails. The sentence therefore has a determinate truth condition and, at the level of its Russellian proposition, is false.

At the same time, a speaker’s use of the sentence may conventionally or pragmatically presuppose that there is a present king of France. If the speaker utters it in an ordinary assertoric context, the failed presupposition may prevent the utterance from constituting a successful assertion. The utterance is not simply a false assertion about a particular nonexistent king; it is an attempted act whose referential background has not been satisfied.

This distinction allows the framework to preserve both logical analysis and ordinary linguistic intuition. It says:

- the sentence can be semantically meaningful;
- its Russellian truth conditions can be determinate;
- the description need not refer to an object;
- the utterance can nevertheless fail pragmatically because the intended reference is unavailable.

The truth value belongs to the proposition or semantic interpretation; felicity and successful reference belong to the utterance. There is no contradiction in saying that the proposition is false while the utterance fails to make a standard assertion.

The framework can also explain why an empty description need not always produce the same result. Suppose a speaker says:

> The present king of France appears in the novel I am writing.

Here the phrase may be used within a fictional or hypothetical context. The speaker is not necessarily attempting to refer to an actual French monarch. The description can be embedded in a story or supposition, and the question of actual reference may be suspended. Similarly, “The first human colony on Mars will be self-governing” involves a description whose referent does not yet exist but whose use can be perfectly meaningful in a future-directed context. A purely Strawsonian account focused on actual reference may treat these cases as failures, while a purely Russellian account may miss the role of fictional and modal discourse. A layered theory can distinguish actual-world reference from reference within a model, story, or hypothetical context.

The unified framework should also recognize that not all descriptions are used in the same way. Some are **attributive**, as in “The person who committed the crime should be punished,” where the speaker may not know who the person is and intends to speak of whoever satisfies the description. Others are **referential**, as in “The man drinking martini is my brother,” where the speaker intends to single out a particular person, even if the description is not literally satisfied. Russell’s analysis is especially suited to the attributive reading. Strawson’s account captures the referential reading. A complete theory should allow a single surface sentence to support both interpretations, with context determining which is operative.

This pluralism complicates the hope for a single logical form. If descriptions can function attributively or referentially, then no one analysis may exhaust their linguistic role. Nevertheless, Russell’s logical form can remain one legitimate semantic representation, while Strawson’s theory explains how speakers exploit or depart from that representation in actual communication.

## Does the Synthesis Resolve the Problem of Truth Values?

The answer depends upon what counts as “the problem.” If the problem is how to avoid commitment to nonexistent objects while preserving meaningfulness and logical structure, Russell’s theory already provides a powerful solution. The unified framework strengthens it by explaining why ordinary speakers may experience empty descriptions as defective rather than simply false.

If the problem is how to account for the intuition that “The present king of France is bald” is neither true nor false, the synthesis offers a distinction rather than a complete elimination of the intuition. It says that the sentence, under a Russellian semantic analysis, is false, while an ordinary utterance of it may fail to have the status of a successful assertion. This is theoretically illuminating, but it does not establish that there is one uniquely correct level at which truth values belong.

The framework therefore resolves the problem by disambiguating it. “Truth value” may refer to at least three things:

- the truth value of a formalized proposition;
- the truth value of a sentence under an interpretation;
- the truth of what a speaker successfully asserts on an occasion.

These need not coincide. A sentence can have a truth condition even when an utterance fails to express a successful assertion. Conversely, a speaker can successfully refer to an object with a description that is literally inaccurate, so that the truth of the utterance depends on the intended referent rather than on strict satisfaction of the descriptive content.

Still, there are limits. The distinction between semantic falsehood and pragmatic infelicity may become unstable in cases where presupposition is conventionally encoded. If the existence of a unique F is part of the conventional meaning of “the F,” then it is not obvious that the existence condition belongs only to pragmatic use. Nor is it clear that all hearers will agree about whether a failed presupposition suspends truth evaluation or merely makes the utterance inappropriate. The theory must specify which presuppositions are semantic, which are pragmatic, and how they project through complex sentences.

The issue becomes particularly difficult with conditionals:

> If the present king of France is bald, then the Louvre is crowded.

Under Russell’s analysis, the antecedent is false, so the conditional may be true under the material conditional. But ordinary speakers may regard the sentence as infelicitous or misleading because the antecedent presupposes a nonexistent king. A presuppositional theory predicts this reaction, but must then explain how presuppositions behave in hypothetical contexts. Does the conditional inherit the presupposition? Is the presupposition suspended? Is it accommodated? Different cases appear to require different answers.

Likewise, in questions:

> Is the present king of France bald?

The question seems defective rather than false. Yet it remains intelligible, and it can prompt the answer, “France has no king.” A unified framework needs an account of interrogative content that is not reducible to the truth value of an underlying proposition. Russell’s analysis can identify the proposition at issue, while Strawson’s account explains the conversational repair. But a full theory must explain how questions inherit, suspend, or expose presuppositions.

These difficulties show that the synthesis is not a final theory of language. It is better understood as a division of explanatory labor. Russell solves the problem of logical form and ontological commitment. Strawson solves, or at least illuminates, the problem of communicative practice and failed reference. Their combination prevents each from being mistaken for a complete account of all linguistic phenomena.

## Significance Without Reference

One of the most important consequences of the debate is that significance cannot be identified with successful reference. A sentence may be significant even when one of its apparent referring expressions is empty. This point is shared, in different forms, by both philosophers.

For Russell, significance derives from the sentence’s quantificational structure. “The present king of France is bald” is meaningful because its components and logical relations determine a condition that could be satisfied or not satisfied. No nonexistent object is required for the sentence to be understood. The sentence is significant because we know what would make it true and what makes it false.

For Strawson, significance is connected with linguistic competence and use. Speakers understand the conventions governing the phrase “the present king of France,” even if they cannot successfully use it to refer in the actual context. The failure of reference is therefore not a failure of meaning. It is a failure in the performance of a particular linguistic act.

This distinction is crucial. If meaning required reference, then sentences about fictional characters, future entities, mistaken identifications, and impossible objects would all become semantically problematic. But language can be used to reason about what does not exist, to construct fictional worlds, to formulate hypotheses, and to describe errors. A robust theory must therefore explain empty or nonactual reference without treating it as meaningless.

Russell’s theory explains this by replacing reference with quantification. Strawson explains it by separating the meaning of an expression from the success of its use. A unified account can say that significance consists in structured conditions of application, while reference is a context-sensitive achievement that may or may not occur. This is a more general and more flexible conception than either meaning-as-reference or meaning-as-truth-condition alone.

Yet the account must be careful not to make significance too cheap. Merely assigning a formal structure to a string of words does not guarantee that the string has a meaningful use. A sentence may be syntactically well formed but semantically anomalous, or meaningful in one context and unintelligible in another. Strawson’s emphasis on use rightly reminds us that linguistic significance is connected to human practices. Russell’s logic, however, reminds us that these practices require structural constraints if communication and inference are to be systematic.

## Final Assessment

The dialectical tension between Russell and Strawson cannot be resolved by choosing one philosopher and dismissing the other. Russell’s theory of denoting phrases is indispensable because it shows how sentences containing apparent reference to nonexistent objects can remain meaningful and logically analyzable. By treating definite descriptions as quantificational rather than as names, Russell avoids ontological extravagance and preserves determinate truth conditions. His account explains why “The present king of France is bald” need not refer to a nonexistent king and why, under a classical analysis, the sentence is false.

Strawson’s distinction between the use of a sentence and the sentence itself is equally indispensable. It shows that linguistic communication is not exhausted by abstract truth conditions. Speakers use descriptions in contexts, with intentions, and against shared backgrounds. A description may be used to refer, and that use may fail even when the sentence remains meaningful. The resulting utterance may be infelicitous or fail to make a true-or-false assertion, rather than simply expressing a false proposition about an object.

A unified framework combining the two theories is therefore successful to the extent that it distinguishes semantic evaluation from pragmatic performance. It can assign Russellian truth conditions to the sentence while allowing Strawsonian failure of reference at the level of use. It can explain why an empty description is neither meaningless nor necessarily a successful referring expression. It can also accommodate the difference between attributive and referential uses, and between actual reference and fictional, hypothetical, or future-directed discourse.

But the framework does not completely dissolve the controversy. It relocates the central questions. We must still decide whether existence and uniqueness are asserted or presupposed, how presuppositions interact with negation and conditionals, and whether truth-value gaps belong to sentences, propositions, or only acts of assertion. No simple combination of Russellian formalization and Strawsonian pragmatics can answer all of these questions without further semantic and logical machinery.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore pluralist but not relativist. There is no single level at which all facts about empty referring terms must be represented. At the level of logical form, Russell is largely correct: a definite description can be analyzed without a referent, and the resulting proposition can have a determinate truth value. At the level of ordinary communication, Strawson is largely correct: the use of a description involves a contextual act of reference whose success cannot be read directly from the sentence’s grammar. The apparent conflict arises when the truth value of an abstract proposition is confused with the success of a concrete utterance.

The problem of empty referring terms is thus not solved by deciding whether such sentences are simply false or neither true nor false. It is solved by recognizing that both verdicts may be appropriate under different descriptions of the linguistic event. Russell tells us what the sentence says in a logical reconstruction; Strawson tells us what a speaker may be trying, and failing, to do with it. Their theories remain in tension because language is simultaneously a system of truth-apt structures and a practice of situated reference. A satisfactory philosophy of language must preserve both dimensions.
