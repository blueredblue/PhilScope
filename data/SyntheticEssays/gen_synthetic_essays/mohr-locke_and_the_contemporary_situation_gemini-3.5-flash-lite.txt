# The Lockean Impossibility: Spatial Exhaustion, Tacit Consent, and the Contemporary Libertarian Dilemma

### Introduction

In the lexicon of modern Anglo-American political philosophy, John Locke occupies a foundational, almost mythic, status. For contemporary libertarians and right-liberals, the Lockean framework—anchored in the natural right to self-ownership, private property acquired through labor, and a government whose legitimacy is strictly derivative of individual consent—serves as the primary intellectual bulwark against the encroachment of the administrative state. To the libertarian mind, taxation is tantamount to forced labor, regulatory bureaucracy is an infringement upon pre-political liberties, and the modern leviathan operates far beyond the fiduciary limits established in the *Second Treatise of Government*. 

Yet, as Tyson Mohr and other critical interpreters of early modern political thought contend, this appropriation of Locke is fundamentally anachronistic. Mohr’s thesis challenges the contemporary libertarian reliance on Lockean architecture by demonstrating that the material, demographic, and institutional presuppositions of Locke’s world have been entirely eradicated by modernity. Lockean theory, Mohr suggests, does not supply an arsenal for the contemporary tax-resister or anti-statist; rather, when properly contextualized within its seventeenth-century constraints, it exposes the incoherence of modern libertarianism while foreclosing any intermediate grounds of political resistance short of full-scale revolution or uncritical submission.

This essay will evaluate Mohr’s critique across three critical dimensions. First, it will examine how the physical exhaustion of *in vacuis locis* (vacant places) and the sheer territorial and demographic scale of the modern nation-state neutralize Locke’s mechanisms of the right of exit and tacit consent, reducing the latter to a legalistic fiction incompatible with individual autonomy. Second, it will assess whether modern taxation and expansive, non-minimal governance cross the threshold of tyranny sufficient to justify rebellion under Locke’s strict criteria of manifest, long-standing abuse. Finally, moving beyond Mohr's critique, this essay will formulate an independent objection to the fatalistic thesis that modern individuals are left with only two untenable options: absolute submission to majoritarian rule or violent revolution. By re-engaging with Locke’s foundational premises regarding the preservation of property and the residual sovereignty of the people, I will argue that a third path—grounded in constitutional pluralism, civil disobedience, and jurisdictional fragmentation—can be salvaged, thereby rescuing Lockean theory from its apparent entrapment in contemporary state hegemony.

### Part I: The Spatial and Demographic Collapse of Lockean Consent

To understand why Mohr argues that Locke fails to support contemporary libertarians, one must first reconstruct the delicate scaffolding upon which Lockean political obligation rests. For Locke, legitimate political authority cannot be naturally ascribed; it must be constructed *ex nihilo* from the moral baseline of individual liberty. Every person is born into a state of perfect freedom, subject only to the Law of Nature, which commands that no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions. Because humans are rational agents capable of discerning this law, political society can only emerge through voluntary agreement—the social contract.

However, the operationalization of this contract relies heavily on two spatial and demographic escape valves: express consent and the right of exit via the existence of unappropriated land (*in vacuis locis*). Locke is acutely aware that very few people ever explicitly sign a social contract. To account for this, he introduces the doctrine of tacit consent in Section 119 of the *Second Treatise*:

> "And this is commonly given, that every man, that hath any possessions, or enjoyment, of any part of the dominions of any government, doth thereby give his tacit consent, and is as far forth obliged to obedience to the laws of that government, during such enjoyment, as anyone under it."

Crucially, Locke conditions this tacit obligation on a profound spatial premise: the availability of open, unowned land elsewhere. For Locke, the globe was initially given to mankind in common, and individuals could legitimately carve out private property through the admixture of labor, provided there was "enough, and as good, left in common for others." This Lockean Proviso is not merely an economic rule for land acquisition; it is the structural condition that renders tacit consent morally binding. If an individual can pack up their belongings, walk to the adjacent wilderness, and establish a new community or live in a state of nature, then their continued residence within an established political jurisdiction can be genuinely interpreted as a voluntary choice to accept its laws. 

Herein lies the core of Mohr’s critique: **the closure of the global frontier has nullified the material preconditions of Lockean consent.** In the twenty-first century, *in vacuis locis* do not exist. Every square inch of habitable land on the planet is claimed, mapped, subdivided, and regulated by sovereign nation-states. There is no physical space outside the jurisdiction of a government where an individual can retreat to exercise their natural liberty without state interference. 

This spatial enclosure creates a fatal paradox for contemporary libertarians attempting to wield Locke against the modern state. If the moral force of tacit consent depends upon the real, physical possibility of exit—the ability to reject the social contract and opt out—then the absence of unowned land turns "tacit consent" into a coercive fiction. As David Hume famously observed in his critique of Locke, to say that a poor peasant consents to the laws of his country by remaining there is like saying a man on a heavily guarded ship freely consents to his captivity because he does not jump overboard into the middle of the ocean. 

Locke himself anticipated a version of this problem, noting that a man is only bound by tacit consent while he actually "enjoys" the territory, and that he may rid himself of that subjection by abandoning the property and uniting with another commonwealth, or by living in the vacuis locis. But in a fully partitioned global order, the "right of exit" has been transformed from an exit *from* state governance *into* a mere choice of *which* state master to serve. One is not exiting the paradigm of political subjection; one is merely migrating from one enclosure to another. 

Furthermore, the sheer scale of the modern nation-state exacerbates this crisis of consent. Locke’s model of the commonwealth was deeply rooted in a municipal, almost republican imagination, where the legislature was responsive, the citizenry was relatively homogenous in its immediate interests, and the geographic scale allowed for tangible oversight by property-owning freeholders. The modern state, by contrast, is a vast, bureaucratic apparatus governing hundreds of millions of people across continental landmasses. 

In a polity of 350 million souls, the direct connection between individual consent and governmental action is utterly severed. When Locke wrote that "that which begins and actually constitutes any political society is nothing but the consent of any number of freemen capable of a majority to unite and incorporate into such a society," he envisioned an act of active, participatory authorship. In the modern administrative state, an individual is born *in medias res*—conscripted into a pre-existing jurisdictional framework before the age of reason, assigned a social security number, and subjected to a sprawling corpus of statutory law and administrative regulation that they never debated, let alone authorized.

To apply Locke to this scenario is to wrench his concepts out of the very material reality that gave them moral coherence. Without unappropriated land, and within the suffocating scale of the modern leviathan, Locke’s theory of tacit consent collapses from a dynamic moral agreement into a doctrine of inescapable allegiance. Mohr’s critique thus exposes a deep vulnerability in libertarian theory: libertarians wish to use Locke to dismantle the state, yet the logic of Locke's own theory dictates that legitimate government requires conditions of spatial mobility and localized consent that modern capitalism and state-building have permanently destroyed.

### Part II: Taxation, Non-Minimal Government, and the Lockean Threshold of Rebellion

If the collapse of *in vacuis locis* undermines the validity of tacit consent, the second prong of Mohr’s argument targets the libertarian utilization of Locke regarding taxation and the scope of state power. Modern libertarians, following Robert Nozick’s famous dictum that "taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor," argue that any state exceeding the functions of the "night-watchman state"—police, courts, and national defense—violates the fundamental rights of property secured by the Law of Nature. Under this view, the modern welfare-regulatory state is an institutionalized form of theft.

Mohr contends, however, that Locke’s political economy and his criteria for justifiable rebellion do not support this expansive reading. To evaluate this claim, we must examine what Locke actually said about property, taxation, and the ends of government.

First, it is crucial to recognize that for Locke, property is not an absolute, pre-social right that exists wholly insulated from communal interest. While Locke famously asserts in Chapter V of the *Second Treatise* that "every man has a 'property' in his own 'person'," and that the "labour of his body, and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his," this natural right is bounded by internal caveats. The acquisition of property is limited by the Lockean Proviso (leaving enough and as good for others) and the spoilage limitation (not hoarding more than one can use). More importantly, once men enter into civil society, property becomes subject to the regulation and protection of the positive laws of the commonwealth.

Crucially, Locke explicitly affirms the state's power to tax property for the maintenance of government. In Section 140, Locke writes:

> "It is true, governments cannot be supported without great charge, and it is fit everyone who enjoys his share of the protection should pay out of his estate his proportion for the maintenance of it. But still it must be with his own consent, i.e., the consent of the majority, giving it either by themselves, or their representatives chosen by them."

Here, Locke ties taxation directly to representation and the common good. But what does Locke consider the legitimate "ends of political society and government"? In Chapter IX, he answers unequivocally: "The great and chief end, therefore, of men's uniting into commonwealths, and putting themselves under government, is the preservation of their property." For Locke, "property" (*proprietas*) is a broad term encompassing *lives, liberties, and estates*. 

Libertarians frequently read "preservation of property" in a strictly negative sense—meaning the state must merely refrain from interfering. But for Locke, property requires positive institutional structures to be secure. The state must establish a "known and established law," an "indifferent and upright judge," and "power to back and support the sentence when right." Creating and maintaining these public goods requires revenue. 

More to the point, Mohr’s critique emphasizes that Locke’s criteria for what constitutes a tyrannical overreach of power—thereby triggering the right of revolution—are extraordinarily high. Libertarians often speak of taxation and regulation as if any deviation from a minimal state constitutes a breach of the social contract. Locke, however, was keenly aware of the dangers of political instability and explicitly guarded against frivolous uprisings. In Section 225, he notes that people are not easily stirred from their old forms of government and that "revolutions happen not upon every little mismanagement in public affairs."

For Locke, a government only crosses the threshold into tyranny when there is a "long train of abuses, prevarications and artifices, all tending the same way," making the design of the rulers visible to the people. Specifically, resistance is justified only when the legislative or executive power acts contrary to their trust by endeavoring to take away and destroy the property of the people, or to reduce them to slavery under arbitrary power.

Does the modern regulatory-welfare state cross this Lockean threshold? From a libertarian perspective, yes: the redistribution of wealth through progressive taxation, mandatory social insurance, and comprehensive economic regulation constitutes the systematic destruction of property rights and the imposition of arbitrary power. 

However, Mohr argues that this perspective misreads Locke’s institutional framework. For Locke, the ultimate arbiter of whether the government is acting within its trust is not the individual taxpayer, nor is it the fringe political philosopher; it is **the majority**. Locke writes in Section 149 that "the community perpetually retains a supreme power of saving themselves from the attempts of anybody, even of their legislators, whenever they shall be so foolish, or so wicked, as to lay and design against the liberties and properties of the subject." But how is this supreme power exercised? Through the mechanism of majoritarian legislative representation.

In the modern context, the expansion of the state—the welfare programs, the regulatory agencies, the public infrastructure—has been constructed, authorized, and continually renewed through the mechanisms of representative democracy (imperfect though they may be). Majorities, operating through constitutional processes, have repeatedly chosen to empower states to provide public education, healthcare, labor standards, and economic safety nets. 

If Locke’s standard for legitimate governance is majoritarian consent expressed through representative institutions, then the modern non-minimal state cannot be easily dismissed as an illegitimate usurpation. To declare the welfare state a tyranny under Lockean criteria requires one to elevate the libertarian minority’s conception of property rights above the explicit majoritarian authorization inscribed in the legislative process. Yet Locke was no libertarian absolutist; he was a constitutionalist whose framework explicitly empowered majorities to regulate commerce and determine the scope of taxation for the public good. Thus, Mohr correctly observes that contemporary taxation and non-minimal governance do not trigger Locke’s criteria for rebellion, because they operate within the broad, majoritarian mandate of the legislative trust.

### Part III: The Modern Dilemma: Submission, Revolution, or a Third Way?

If Mohr’s critique holds—if the exhaustion of *in vacuis locis* invalidates tacit consent, if the scale of the modern state dissolves meaningful participation, and if taxation and expansive governance fall well short of Lockean tyranny—then contemporary individualists and libertarians are seemingly trapped in a devastating philosophical dilemma. As the title of this intellectual inquiry suggests, the individualist is left with no legitimate recourse other than **unconditional submission to majoritarian rule** or **suicidal, unjustified revolution**.

This formulation presents a stark binary: either accept the moral hegemony of the administrative state and abandon libertarian principles, or plunge society into violent chaos with no philosophical warrant. 

In this section, I will formulate an independent objection to this fatalistic thesis. While Mohr and other critics successfully expose the obsolescence of seventeenth-century Lockean mechanics in the twenty-first century, they too quickly abandon the normative core of Lockean theory. I contend that the modern individualist is *not* reduced exclusively to submission or revolution. By reconstructing Locke’s theory of fiduciary trust and combining it with modern insights in institutional pluralism, jurisdictional federalism, and civil disobedience, we can discover a legitimate middle ground of resistance and autonomy.

#### 1. The Fiduciary Limit and the Persistence of Natural Rights
First, we must challenge the notion that majoritarianism is unlimited within Lockean theory. Mohr implies that because the modern state is majoritarian, its actions are automatically shielded by Locke’s concept of the legislative trust. But Locke was deeply suspicious of absolute majoritarianism. In the *Second Treatise*, the legislature is bounded by the very Law of Nature that preceded its creation. The legislature holds power only as a *fiduciary*—a trustee acting on behalf of the principals (the people). 

A trustee does not own the estate; they manage it according to specified terms. If a trustee exceeds their mandate, their acts are null and void. In the modern context, even if a majority votes for expansive state programs, this majoritarian mandate is not absolute; it is constrained by constitutional frameworks designed precisely to protect minority rights from the tyranny of the majority. When the administrative state operates via delegated executive decrees and unaccountable regulatory agencies—largely bypassing the legislative branch altogether—it severs the chain of majoritarian authorization. This creates a crisis of the fiduciary trust that Locke’s framework can explicitly diagnose and critique.

#### 2. Civil Disobedience and Jurisdictional Exit
To escape the binary of submission or violent revolution, we must look beyond Locke’s explicit text to the broader trajectory of liberal resistance theory, integrating what political economists and legal theorists call **institutional pluralism and jurisdictional exit**. 

While physical exit to *in vacuis locis* is impossible, the digital and globalized economy has created functional equivalents of exit that do not require unowned land. Modern individuals can engage in economic secession, crypto-asset adoption, offshore corporate restructuring, and transnational arbitrage. While these methods do not allow one to escape state taxation entirely, they radically diminish the state’s monopoly power over the individual, introducing a competitive market of governance that forces states to respect jurisdictional boundaries.

Moreover, within the domestic sphere, the individual is not reduced to passive submission. Locke’s defense of the right of resistance need not be read as an all-or-nothing choice between total violent revolution and meek obedience. Between the ballot and the barricade lies the rich tradition of **principled civil disobedience and conscientious objection**. 

Locke recognized that when laws violate the fundamental trust reposed in government, individuals retain a natural right to judge their own conscience. While unlawful resistance against legitimate authority is a sin against God and man, resisting power that has exceeded its constitutional bounds is not rebellion—it is the restoration of order. Civil disobedience operates as a modern, non-violent manifestation of this Lockean appeal to heaven. By refusing to comply with unjust edicts, accepting the legal penalties, and appealing to the moral conscience of the broader community, the individual enacts a form of resistance that preserves moral autonomy without descending into societal destruction.

#### 3. Federalism and Polycentric Governance
Finally, the modern individualist can operationalize Locke through the doctrine of subsidiarity and decentralized federalism. If the continental nation-state is too vast for meaningful consent, the Lockean framework can be scaled downward. Through radical federalism, municipal localism, and the devolution of power to the smallest possible political units, the spatial and demographic distance between the citizen and the state is compressed. 

In a polycentric governance model—where individuals can freely move between municipalities with different tax rates, regulatory burdens, and public service offerings—the spirit of the Lockean right of exit is revived. One may not be able to leave the planet, but one can vote with one's feet within a federal system that respects local autonomy. This approach reconciles libertarian commitments with the realities of a populated world, transforming Locke from an obsolete relic of the frontier into a blueprint for institutional decentralization.

---

### Conclusion

Tyson Mohr’s critique performs a vital service for the history of political thought: it shatters the lazy anachronisms of contemporary libertarians who treat John Locke as a contemporary American culture-warrior. By demonstrating that the material conditions of Locke’s philosophy—namely, *in vacuis locis* and localized, direct consent—have been erased by global enclosure and the gargantuan scale of the modern nation-state, Mohr exposes the severe limits of applying seventeenth-century property theory to the twenty-first-century administrative apparatus. Furthermore, Mohr correctly notes that the expansion of the modern welfare-regulatory state, authorized by representative majorities, does not easily trip the high threshold of tyranny required to justify Lockean revolution.

However, Mohr’s conclusion—that the modern individualist is cornered into an intolerable choice between silent submission and violent revolution—underestimates the elasticity of liberal theory. Locke’s philosophy was never merely about empty land; it was about the moral sovereignty of the individual conscience and the fiduciary limits of power. By reinterpreting Locke through the lenses of institutional pluralism, constitutional federalism, principled civil disobedience, and functional economic exit, modern individualists can transcend the fatalistic binary. 

The Lockean framework, when adapted to the realities of a crowded planet, does not offer a magic wand to abolish the modern state overnight. But it does provide an enduring moral vocabulary to critique state overreach, defend minority rights against majoritarian despotism, and continually demand that those who wield political power justify their stewardship before the tribunal of free, rational human beings.
