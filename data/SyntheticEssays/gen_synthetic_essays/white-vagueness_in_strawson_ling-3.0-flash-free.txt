**The Void of Direction: Strawson’s Expressional Meaning and the Vagueness of Nonexistent Referents**

The problem of denoting non-existent objects has long stood as a fulcrum upon which the philosophy of language turns. When a speaker utters the phrase "the present king of France is bald," what exactly is happening? Bertrand Russell famously argued that the sentence is not about a non-existent king, but rather a complex logical construct that is simply false. In stark contrast, P.F. Strawson argued that the sentence is neither true nor false, but rather meaningful in a different sense—it expresses a thought that presupposes the existence of the king. To resolve this tension, Strawson developed a theory of expressional meaning, distinguishing sharply between an expression and its use, and defining the meaning of an expression as a set of general directions for its correct use. However, when applied to expressions that lack fixed referents, such as "the queen of America," Strawson’s framework reveals a profound vulnerability to vagueness. This essay evaluates Strawson’s theory by testing whether his definition of meaning collapses when confronted with expressions that lack referents. It will argue that while implicit directions in language are not inherently unreliable, speakers do not fundamentally misunderstand proper application; rather, the very notion of meaning as a set of directions for correct use becomes vacuous when the object of those directions does not exist. Ultimately, the tension between Russell’s logical paraphrases and Strawson’s theory of denoting is not resolved by choosing one over the other, but by recognizing that the directions for use are not semantic facts but pragmatic scaffolding that becomes suspended in the void of non-existence.

To understand the stakes of this evaluation, one must first appreciate the historical dialectic that birthed Strawson’s theory. Russell’s theory of definite descriptions, articulated in his seminal 1905 paper "On Denoting," proposed that sentences containing non-referring expressions are not referential at all, but logical fictions. For Russell, "The present king of France is bald" is logically equivalent to: there is an x such that x is a king of France, and for any y, if y is a king of France, then y equals x, and x is bald. By unpacking the definite description into a quantified logical structure, Russell eliminated the paradox of non-existence: the sentence is simply false because the existential quantifier fails. The strength of Russell’s logical paraphrases lies in their formal rigor; they provide a clear, unambiguous truth-condition for every sentence, thereby solving the problem of non-existence by dissolving it. Yet, Russell’s solution leaves a philosophical residue. When a normal speaker asserts "The present king of France is bald," they do not feel as though they are asserting a complex conjunction that happens to be false. They feel as though they are referring to a specific individual, even if that individual does not exist. Russell’s paraphrase captures the logical behavior of the sentence but fails to capture the psychological and pragmatic reality of its use.

It is precisely this gap that P.F. Strawson sought to bridge. In his 1950 paper "On Referring" and his later work *Individuals* (1959), Strawson introduced the crucial distinction between an expression and its use. For Strawson, the meaning of an expression is not its logical paraphrase, nor is it the referent it happens to pick out in a given context. Instead, the meaning of an expression is what the speaker *means* when they use it—a set of general directions that guide the listener in understanding the speaker's communicative intent. This is what Strawson terms "expressional meaning." When a speaker uses "the present king of France," they are not merely asserting a quantified proposition; they are employing an expression whose meaning is constituted by the general directions for its correct use. These directions include the presupposition that there is exactly one king of France, and that the predicate "bald" applies to him. If these presuppositions fail, the speaker does not make a false assertion; rather, the act of assertion is rendered defective. The sentence is "neither true nor false," but rather *meaningless* in the assertoric sense, though perfectly meaningful as an expression of thought.

Strawson’s theory of denoting, therefore, hinges on his account of expressional meaning. The meaning of "the present king of France" is the general direction: "identify the unique individual who satisfies the condition of being the king of France, and attribute the following property to him." The use is the actual deployment of this direction in a specific speech act. By separating the meaning (the direction) from the use (the act), Strawson elegantly sidesteps Russell’s logical paradox. The sentence is not false because the direction itself is not a truth-claim; the direction is simply a template for an act of reference. However, this very elegance exposes a deep vulnerability: the vagueness of the directions themselves when applied to nonexistent objects.

To test the collapse of Strawson’s framework, we must examine the phrase "the queen of America." This expression lacks a fixed referent, not because the referent is temporarily absent, but because the category "America" does not, in fact, have a queen. If Strawson’s theory defines the meaning of an expression as a set of general directions for correct use, what are the directions for "the queen of America"? They are: identify the unique individual who is the queen of America, and attribute the relevant predicate to her. But because no such individual exists, the directions cannot be fulfilled. The question then arises: do these directions collapse into meaninglessness, or do they persist as a kind of semantic ghost?

Here, the dialectical objective requires us to assess whether implicit directions in language are inherently unreliable, or if speakers fundamentally misunderstand proper application. On the first horn, one might argue that the directions are inherently unreliable. Because they are "general," they lack the specificity to function as reliable guides when the world fails to conform to their presuppositions. The direction "identify the unique queen of America" is vacuous; it points to a null set, offering no actual guidance for behavior or reference. If meaning is the set of directions, and the directions point to nothing, then the meaning of "the queen of America" is a void. In this view, Strawson’s theory collapses because it cannot account for the *content* of the expression; it only accounts for the *structure* of the attempt to refer. The directions are unreliable precisely because they are severed from a secure referential anchor.

On the second horn, one might argue that speakers do not fundamentally misunderstand proper application. When a poet writes of "the queen of America," the speaker and audience perfectly understand the directions for correct use: they know they are not referring to a real, extant monarch. The use is not a failed attempt at reference, but a successful act of poetic or rhetorical expression. The directions are followed perfectly; the speaker is using the expression to evoke a particular cultural or symbolic archetype. In this sense, the directions are entirely reliable, and the speaker understands exactly how to apply them. The apparent vagueness is not a flaw in the directions, but a feature of the context. The directions are general enough to accommodate non-literal, metaphorical, or fictional uses.

However, both of these assessments miss the deeper philosophical point that Strawson’s framework is designed to illuminate. The dialectic between the unreliable directions and the misunderstood application reveals that the concept of "correct use" is insufficient to ground meaning when referents are absent. Strawson’s directions for use are implicitly tied to a world of existing objects. When we say "the queen of America," we are applying a direction that *presupposes* existence. The vagueness arises not because the directions are bad, but because the directions are *presuppositional*. They function as a hinge upon which the assertion turns. If the hinge is missing—the object does not exist—the door of reference does not open. The directions do not collapse into nonsense, nor do speakers misunderstand them; rather, the directions are *suspended*. They remain operative as a structural template, but they fail to generate a referent. 

This suspension highlights the fundamental tension between Russell’s logical paraphrases and Strawson’s theory of denoting. Russell’s paraphrase treats the sentence as a complete logical proposition whose truth-value is determined by the existence of the referent. If the referent does not exist, the proposition is false. Strawson’s theory, conversely, treats the sentence as an expression whose meaning is a set of directions, and whose use is an act of reference that fails if the referent is absent. The tension lies in the ontological status of the directions themselves. Russell dissolves the non-existent object into logical quantifiers, thereby preserving the truth-functional integrity of language. Strawson preserves the intuitive sense of reference, but at the cost of leaving the meaning of the expression dangling—defined only by its *potential* for correct use.

The resolution of this tension requires a nuanced synthesis that acknowledges the limits of both approaches. Russell’s logical paraphrases are indispensable for clarifying the truth-conditions of assertions, but they are inadequate for capturing the expressional meaning of language. We do not merely assert propositions; we deploy expressions whose meanings are constituted by the general directions for their use. However, Strawson’s theory is vulnerable to vagueness because it treats these directions as if they are self-contained semantic entities, when in fact they are fundamentally dependent on the existence of their referents to be fully realized. The directions for "the queen of America" are not meaningless, nor are they false; they are *unfulfilled*. The meaning of the expression is the set of directions, but the *content* of the expression—the specific thought communicated—is a function of the speaker's intention to direct the listener toward an object that does not exist. 

Thus, the vagueness of Strawson’s framework when confronted with nonexistent objects is not a failure of the theory, but a revelation of its scope. The directions for correct use are not inherently unreliable; they are reliable within the domain of successful reference. When the referent is absent, the directions do not break down; they simply point into the void. The speaker does not misunderstand proper application; they apply the directions correctly, but the application yields no referential result. This is the core insight of Strawson’s theory of denoting: meaning is not reference. The meaning of "the queen of America" is perfectly clear as a set of directions—it tells us to seek a unique female monarch of the American continent. The vagueness arises only if we mistakenly expect the directions to resolve into a determinate object. 

In conclusion, evaluating Strawson’s theory of expressional meaning requires navigating the treacherous waters between Russell’s logical rigor and the pragmatic reality of human speech. Strawson’s distinction between an expression and its use, and his definition of meaning as general directions for correct use, provides a powerful framework for understanding how language functions even when reference fails. The vulnerability to vagueness when accounting for phrases like "the queen of America" demonstrates that these directions are contingent upon the existence of their intended referents. The implicit directions are not inherently unreliable, nor do speakers fundamentally misunderstand proper application; rather, the theory reveals that meaning as a set of directions is inherently prospective. It looks forward to a referent that may or may not exist. When the referent does not exist, the directions remain as a structural echo of intended reference, suspended in a semantic void. The tension between Russell’s logical paraphrases and Strawson’s theory of denoting is resolved not by one superseding the other, but by recognizing that language operates on two levels: the logical level of truth-conditions, which Russell captures, and the expressional level of communicative directions, which Strawson captures. When we speak of nonexistent objects, we are navigating the gap between these two levels—issuing directions that the world, in its silence, refuses to fulfill.
