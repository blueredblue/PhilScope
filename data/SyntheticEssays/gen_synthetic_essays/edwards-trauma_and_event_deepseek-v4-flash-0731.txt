## The Retroactive Flesh: Mythical Time, Trauma, and the Agency of the Wild Being

**Introduction**

The event, in its most radical philosophical sense, does not happen once. It erupts, certainly, but its eruption is never a punctual occurrence that can be neatly located on a chronological timeline. Instead, the event is a site of deferred meaning, a rupture whose significance is retroactively constituted by the future it makes possible. This essay evaluates the claim that Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s concept of mythical time entails precisely such a retroactive structure, one that is deeply comparable to Sigmund Freud’s theory of trauma. If this claim holds, it has profound consequences for contemporary debates concerning the nature of political and historical subjectivity. Specifically, it forces us to reassess Alain Badiou’s model of subject-formation—which posits the subject as a radical novelty constituted by fidelity to a ruptural event—against the phenomenological critique advanced by Raphaël Gély, who argues that Badiou’s subject illegitimately presupposes a pre-existing capacity for receptivity. To adjudicate this debate, I will turn to Mauro Carbone’s innovative reading of Merleau-Ponty’s late ontology, particularly his elaboration of “wild Being” and the touching-touched chiasm. Carbone’s work, I argue, offers a resolution to the apparent tension between the invisible sediment of historical events and the continuous right of rectification—the capacity to reinterpret and re-signify the past. Ultimately, I will demonstrate that a non-serial ontology of time, grounded in the chiasmic flesh, does not merely sustain a coherent account of historical agency but is, in fact, the only adequate framework for understanding how human beings can be both constituted by their history and capable of transforming it.

My argument will proceed in four stages. First, I will establish the structural isomorphism between Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time and Freud’s *Nachträglichkeit* (deferred action or retroactivity). Second, I will examine how this retroactive structure complicates Badiou’s account of the event and the subject, showing that while Badiou correctly insists on the subject’s non-substantiality, Gély’s critique rightly identifies a necessary phenomenological ground that Badiou overlooks. Third, I will introduce Carbone’s chiasmic ontology as the mediating term, demonstrating how the touching-touched structure resolves the dialectical tension between sediment and rectification. Finally, I will argue that this non-serial temporal ontology provides a robust model of historical agency, one that avoids both the deterministic trap of absolute sedimentation and the voluntarist illusion of sovereign rupture.

**I. The Retroactive Structure of Mythical Time**

To understand Merleau-Ponty’s conception of time, one must first abandon the commonsense notion of time as a linear succession of discrete “nows.” In *Phenomenology of Perception*, Merleau-Ponty argues that objective time—the time of clocks and calendars—is a derivative abstraction from the lived experience of temporality. Lived time is not a series of punctual instants but a field of presence, a “thickness” that encompasses the past and the future as horizons of the present. The past is not a dead repository of completed facts; it is a sedimented layer of meaning that continues to inform the present, not as a causal force but as a background texture against which current perceptions and actions take shape. Similarly, the future is not an empty space awaiting actualization but a project that gives the present its direction and significance.

Within this framework, Merleau-Ponty introduces the notion of “mythical time.” This is not simply the time of ancient myths, but a structural feature of all historical consciousness. Mythical time is the time of the collective unconscious, the time in which events are not ordered by mechanical causality but by symbolic resonance. In mythical time, an event does not merely occur and then pass away; it persists as a latent potential, a “primal scene” that awaits a future moment to be re-activated and given its definitive meaning. This is why Merleau-Ponty can speak of the past as a “horizon” that is never fully closed. The past is not a set of objective facts but a field of possible significations that are continuously re-opened by the projects of the present.

This structure bears a striking resemblance to Freud’s theory of trauma, specifically his concept of *Nachträglichkeit*. In his early writings, particularly the case of the “Wolf Man,” Freud observed that a traumatic event does not necessarily produce its traumatic effect at the moment of its occurrence. Instead, the first event—often a sexual seduction or a scene of primal observation—is registered by the psyche but remains meaningless or unintegrated because the subject lacks the cognitive and affective schemas to process it. It is only later, when a second event occurs that resonates with the first, that the original scene is retroactively constituted as traumatic. The first event is not traumatic *in itself*; it becomes traumatic through the deferred action of the second event, which retroactively assigns it a causal and meaningful role in the subject’s psychic history.

The parallel with Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time is striking. In both cases, the past is not a fixed substance but a dynamic structure that is continuously re-signified by the present. The event in mythical time, like the primal scene in Freudian psychoanalysis, is a “virtual” occurrence that gains its actuality only through a future retroactive constitution. This is not to say that the past is unreal or that events can be arbitrarily invented. Rather, it means that the *meaning* of an event—its significance for the subject or the collective—is not given at the moment of its occurrence but is a function of the temporal field in which it is subsequently situated. The past is a sediment, but sediment is not rock; it is a soft, permeable layer that can be reshaped by new currents.

This retroactive structure has profound implications for our understanding of historical events. Consider, for example, the French Revolution. For those who lived through it, the events of 1789 were a chaotic and ambiguous series of upheavals. It was only later, through the retrospective work of historians, politicians, and artists, that the Revolution was constituted as a singular, epochal event—the birth of modernity, the triumph of the people, or the beginning of terror. The Revolution, in its mythical dimension, is not a fixed historical fact but a site of ongoing contestation. Its meaning is retroactively re-negotiated with each new political crisis, each new social movement, each new attempt to claim its legacy. This is precisely the structure of Freudian trauma: the original event is a virtual site that is actualized differently depending on the future that encounters it.

**II. Badiou’s Event, Gély’s Critique, and the Retroactive Subject**

If the event is retroactively constituted, what does this mean for the subject who is supposed to be constituted *by* the event? Alain Badiou’s philosophy, as elaborated in *Being and Event*, presents the most rigorous contemporary attempt to theorize the event as a radical rupture. For Badiou, the event is a supplement to a situation—a paradoxical occurrence that cannot be accounted for by the situation’s own rules of representation. The event names a “void” in the situation, a point of inconsistency that exposes the situation’s incompleteness. The subject, in Badiou’s model, is not a pre-existing entity that encounters the event. Rather, the subject is a *procedure*—a fidelity to the event’s consequences. The subject is constituted by the event in the sense that it is the active bearer of the event’s truth. Before the event, there is no subject, only an individual embedded in the situation. After the event, the subject emerges as the one who decides to remain faithful to the event’s disruptive potential.

Badiou’s model is explicitly anti-phenomenological. He rejects any notion of a pre-reflective consciousness or a lived body that would condition the subject’s receptivity to the event. The event, for Badiou, is a pure rupture that cannot be anticipated or prepared for. It strikes from outside the situation, and the subject is simply the name for the process of staying true to this strike.

It is precisely this radical novelty that Raphaël Gély challenges. Drawing on the phenomenological tradition, Gély argues that Badiou’s event is philosophically incoherent because it presupposes a subject who is capable of recognizing the event *as* an event. For an occurrence to be experienced as a rupture, the subject must already possess a certain relation to the world—a background of meaning, a set of pre-reflective intentionalities, a “flesh” that allows the event to register as significant. Gély’s critique is that Badiou’s subject is a *de facto* presupposition: the subject must already be a subject *in some sense* in order to be constituted by the event. If the subject were truly a blank slate, the event would be indistinguishable from any other random occurrence. The event can only be an event *for* a subject who is already oriented towards the world, already embedded in a field of meaning.

This is where Merleau-Ponty’s retroactive structure becomes crucial. Gély’s critique seems to suggest that Badiou’s model fails because it cannot account for the subject’s pre-eventual constitution. However, if we take seriously the retroactive structure of mythical time, we can see that Badiou’s model is not necessarily invalidated but rather requires a crucial modification. The subject of the event is not constituted *ex nihilo*; rather, the subject is retroactively constituted as the one who was *always already* oriented towards the event. In other words, the event does not create a subject from nothing; it retroactively reveals that the subject was already, in some latent sense, a subject-in-waiting.

Consider the example of a political awakening. A person may live their life embedded in a particular social situation, unaware of its contradictions. Then, an event occurs—a strike, a revolution, a moment of collective refusal—that exposes these contradictions. In Badiou’s terms, this event is a rupture that names the void of the situation. But for this person to be constituted as a political subject by this event, they must already have a certain capacity for receptivity. They must be able to see the event as significant, to feel its urgency. This capacity is not created by the event; it is a pre-existing potential that the event retroactively activates. The subject is not a blank slate but a field of latent possibilities that the event brings to fruition.

This is precisely the structure of Freudian trauma. The first event—the latent potential, the pre-reflective orientation—is not meaningful in itself. It only becomes meaningful retroactively, when the second event—the traumatic rupture—activates it. The subject of the trauma is not the passive recipient of a causal chain but the active interpreter of a retroactive signification. Similarly, the subject of the political event is not a passive recipient of a ruptural force but an active participant in the retroactive constitution of the event’s meaning.

Thus, Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time actually *supports* a modified version of Badiou’s model against Gély’s critique. Gély is right that the subject cannot be a pure *tabula rasa*. But he is wrong to conclude that this invalidates Badiou’s insistence on the subject’s non-substantiality. The subject is not a pre-existing substance; it is a retroactive effect. The event does not find a ready-made subject; it *retroactively constitutes* the subject as the one who was always already capable of responding to it. This is not a return to a pre-existing subject but a radical re-conceptualization of subjectivity as a temporal process of retroactive self-constitution.

**III. Carbone’s Wild Being and the Chiasmic Resolution**

To fully articulate this retroactive subjectivity, we must turn to Mauro Carbone’s reading of Merleau-Ponty’s late ontology, particularly as developed in *The Visible and the Invisible*. Carbone focuses on Merleau-Ponty’s concept of “wild Being” (*être brut* or *être sauvage*)—the pre-objective, pre-categorical ground of all experience. Wild Being is not a thing but an element, a medium in which all beings are immersed. It is the “flesh” of the world, the shared tissue that connects the perceiver and the perceived, the subject and the object.

Central to Carbone’s interpretation is the concept of the chiasm, the intertwining of the touching and the touched. When I touch my right hand with my left hand, I experience a reversible relation: the touching hand is also touched, and the touched hand is also touching. This chiasmic structure is not limited to the body but extends to the entire field of perception. The world touches me as I touch it; I am part of the world as the world is part of me. This mutual implication dissolves the classical subject-object dichotomy and reveals a fundamental ontological reciprocity.

Carbone applies this chiasmic structure to the problem of time and history. For Merleau-Ponty, the past is not a separate realm but the “invisible” lining of the present. Just as the visible world has an invisible depth—the unseen sides of objects, the background against which figures emerge—so too does the present have an invisible historical dimension. The past is not a dead weight that determines the present from outside; it is the very texture of the present, the sedimented layer of meaning that gives the present its thickness and depth.

This chiasmic understanding of history resolves the tension between the invisible sediment of historical events and the continuous right of rectification. On one hand, the past is indeed sedimented. It is inscribed in our bodies, our institutions, our language. We cannot simply erase it or start from scratch. On the other hand, we have the right—indeed, the responsibility—to rectify the past, to reinterpret it, to re-signify it in light of new projects and new struggles. How can these two demands be reconciled?

The chiasm provides the answer. Because the past is the invisible lining of the present, it is not a fixed object but a dynamic field. The past is not a thing that we observe from a distance; it is the very medium through which we perceive and act. When we rectify the past, we are not negating it or escaping it; we are *re-folding* it, re-articulating its latent possibilities. The right of rectification is not a sovereign act of a detached subject imposing a new meaning on a passive past. Rather, it is a chiasmic act of mutual constitution: the past shapes the present, but the present also re-shapes the past.

Consider the example of historical memory. A nation’s past is not a neutral record of events but a contested field of interpretation. The way we remember a war, a revolution, or a period of oppression is not fixed. It is continuously re-negotiated through monuments, textbooks, films, and political debates. This is not a denial of the past but a deepening of it. By re-interpreting the past, we bring to light aspects that were previously invisible, we re-touch the sediment and reveal its hidden layers. The past is not a prison but a source of possibility, and the right of rectification is the right to explore this source.

Carbone’s chiasmic ontology thus resolves the apparent contradiction between sediment and rectification. The sediment is not an obstacle to rectification but its very condition. We can only rectify the past because we are already immersed in it, because the past is the flesh of our present. Rectification is not a rupture from the past but a re-articulation of the past’s own virtual potential.
