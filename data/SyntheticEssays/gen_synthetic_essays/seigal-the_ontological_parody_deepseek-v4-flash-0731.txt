### The Modal Asymmetry of Perfection: Hartshorne, Parody, and the Limits of Logical Theology

Anselm of Canterbury’s *Proslogion* argument for the existence of God stands as one of the most audacious and persistently captivating pieces of philosophical theology ever conceived. Its central intuition—that God, as “that than which nothing greater can be conceived,” must exist in reality, lest a greater being be conceived—has spawned centuries of commentary, refutation, and reformulation. The most famous and immediately damaging criticism came from Anselm’s own contemporary, Gaunilo of Marmoutiers, who deployed the “perfect island” parody. If existence is a perfection, Gaunilo argued, then a perfect island must exist, leading to absurdity and unwanted ontological commitments. For centuries, this parody was considered a decisive *reductio* of Anselm’s logical move. However, in the twentieth century, Charles Hartshorne sought to rehabilitate the ontological argument by transposing it into the key of modal logic. By distinguishing sharply between contingent and necessary existence, and by employing powerful modal axioms—specifically, Becker’s postulate—Hartshorne attempted to construct an argument that is immune to the structural parity that undermines Anselm’s original.

The central question this essay addresses is whether Hartshorne’s modal version genuinely insulates the argument from the generation of mutually exclusive entities or unwanted ontological commitments. Does moving from the claim that existence is a perfection to the claim that *necessary* existence is a perfection successfully defuse Gaunilo’s island and its more sinister modern variants, such as Michael Martin’s “Absolute Evil One”? I will argue that while Hartshorne’s modal framework successfully deflects the *contingent* parody of Gaunilo’s island by revealing its inherent modal incoherence, it does not fully escape structural parity. The modal logic itself is neutral; it applies symmetrically to any predicate that can be defined as implying necessary existence. Hartshorne’s defense against the Absolute Evil One ultimately relies not on modal logic, but on a substantive and highly contested metaphysical thesis—the privation theory of evil—to establish the impossibility of the parodic counterpart. Consequently, while Hartshorne brilliantly relocates the battlefield from logic to metaphysics, he does not definitively resolve the parody objection; he merely demonstrates that the ontological argument’s fate hinges on extra-modal claims about the nature of perfection and evil.

#### I. Anselm’s Original Argument and the Structural Flaw of the Parody

To understand Hartshorne’s innovation, one must first grasp the precise nature of Anselm’s vulnerability. Anselm’s argument in *Proslogium* II proceeds from the definition of God as *id quo nihil maius cogitari potest* (that than which nothing greater can be conceived). The fool, who denies God’s existence, nevertheless understands this definition; thus, God exists in the understanding. Anselm then asserts that existence in reality is greater than existence in the understanding alone. Therefore, if God existed only in the understanding, a greater being—one existing in reality—could be conceived. This contradicts the definition of God as that than which nothing greater can be conceived. Hence, God must exist in reality.

Gaunilo’s parody is devastating because it exposes the argument’s logical form as a *content-neutral* template. Gaunilo invites us to conceive of an island “more excellent than all other countries.” If we apply Anselm’s logic, this perfect island must exist in reality, for if it did not, a greater island—one that exists—could be conceived. The argument, if valid, would generate a myriad of perfect contingent objects—perfect islands, perfect pencils, perfect pizzas. This is an absurd *reductio*. The structural flaw in Anselm’s argument is that it treats existence as a univocal predicate that adds to the “greatness” of any subject whatsoever. It fails to distinguish between different *modes* of existence or different *kinds* of perfection. A perfect island is a perfectly *contingent* thing; its perfection is relative to its kind, which is inherently finite and dependent. The argument’s failure lies in its inability to account for the modal status of its subject. It assumes that “greatness” is a simple property that can be added to any concept, regardless of whether that concept is compatible with necessary existence.

#### II. Hartshorne’s Modal Logic of Perfection

Hartshorne’s genius was to recognize that the ontological argument’s fatal weakness was its modal blindness. In his works *Man’s Vision of God* and *The Logic of Perfection*, he reformulated the argument to make modal status explicit. The core of Hartshorne’s insight is that God, as a perfect being, cannot be conceived as merely existing; God must exist *necessarily*. Contingent existence is a limitation, a mark of imperfection. A being whose existence depends on external causes or circumstances is surpassable. Therefore, if God exists at all, God exists necessarily. This yields the crucial premise: *If God exists, then God exists necessarily* (formally, \(P \to \Box P\), where \(P\) stands for “God exists” or “Perfection is instantiated”).

This premise alone, however, is not enough to prove existence. It only establishes a conditional. To move from possibility to actuality, Hartshorne employs a powerful modal principle: Becker’s postulate. Becker’s postulate states that what is possibly necessary is necessary: \(\Diamond \Box p \to \Box p\). In standard modal systems (specifically, S5), this postulate is equivalent to the characteristic axiom \(\Diamond \Box p \to \Box p\). In S5, the modal status of a proposition is rigid: if a proposition is possibly necessary, then it is necessary; if it is possibly impossible, then it is impossible. The modal hierarchy collapses into a binary distinction between the necessary and the impossible.

Hartshorne’s argument proceeds as follows:
1.  \(P \to \Box P\) (Anselm’s principle: Perfection implies necessary perfection).
2.  \(\Diamond P\) (God is possible—the concept of a perfect being is not self-contradictory).
3.  From (1) and (2), by modal reasoning: \(\Diamond \Box P\) (If \(P\) is possible, and \(P\) entails \(\Box P\), then \(\Box P\) is possible).
4.  By Becker’s postulate: \(\Diamond \Box P \to \Box P\).
5.  Therefore, \(\Box P\) (God exists necessarily).

This argument is a masterstroke of formal elegance. It shifts the burden of proof from demonstrating actual existence to demonstrating *possibility*. If one grants that the concept of a perfect being is coherent (i.e., not logically impossible), and if one grants that perfection entails necessary existence, then the S5 modal system forces the conclusion that God exists. The argument is valid. The question is whether its premises are sound, and specifically, whether the parodies can be formulated to undermine the premises.

#### III. The Deflection of Gaunilo’s Island

How does this modal framework handle Gaunilo’s island? The island parody fails immediately because the concept of a “perfect island” cannot satisfy the modal requirement of premise (1). To parody Hartshorne’s argument, one would need to assert: *If a perfect island exists, it exists necessarily* (\(I \to \Box I\)). But this premise is false, or at least incoherent, for the simple reason that “island” is an essentially contingent concept. An island is, by definition, a physical, spatio-temporal object. Its existence is dependent on geological processes, weather patterns, and the continued existence of the sea. Its essence involves materiality, finitude, and location.

To say that a perfect island exists *necessarily* is to say that it exists in all possible worlds. But a necessary island would be a being that is not subject to the laws of physics, that is not causally dependent, and that exists *a se*. Such a being would no longer be an island; it would be a necessary substance or a deity. The predicate “necessary existence” is incompatible with the essence of “island.” Therefore, the premise \(I \to \Box I\) is not merely false; it is a category error. The concept of a “necessarily existing perfect island” is as incoherent as a “square circle” or a “married bachelor.” It is not a possible object of thought.

This is precisely where Hartshorne’s modal shift proves its worth. Anselm’s original argument was vulnerable because it treated existence as a simple predicate that could be attached to any concept. Hartshorne’s argument, by contrast, requires that the subject of the argument be a being whose *essence* entails necessary existence. The island’s essence entails contingent existence. Thus, the modal version successfully insulates the argument from the generation of *contingent* parodies. It does not commit us to necessary islands, necessary pencils, or necessary pizzas, because these concepts are modal contradictions. The argument is structurally restricted to entities that are logically capable of necessary existence—entities whose non-existence is impossible. This is a significant philosophical victory. Hartshorne has shown that the ontological argument, when properly formulated, does not generate a zoo of perfect contingent objects. The move from contingent to necessary existence effectively blocks the naive parody.

#### IV. Michael Martin’s Absolute Evil One and the Limits of Modal Neutrality

However, the victory is incomplete. The modal logic itself is content-neutral. It does not care whether the predicate in question is “perfection” or something else. If we can define a coherent concept \(E\) such that \(E \to \Box E\) and \(\Diamond E\), then Becker’s postulate will force us to conclude \(\Box E\). This is precisely the strategy of Michael Martin in his essay “The Absolute Evil One.” Martin constructs a parody that is structurally isomorphic to Hartshorne’s argument but replaces “perfection” with “absolute evil.” Let us define “Absolute Evil One” (AEO) as a being than which no greater evil can be conceived. It is omnipotent, omniscient, and supremely evil. If we apply Hartshorne’s logic, we can assert:
1.  \(E \to \Box E\) (If the AEO exists, it exists necessarily—for a being that is maximally evil would not tolerate the limitation of contingent existence; its evil would be eternal and necessary).
2.  \(\Diamond E\) (The AEO is possible—it is not a self-contradictory concept).
3.  Therefore, \(\Box E\) (The AEO exists necessarily).

If Hartshorne’s argument is valid, then Martin’s is equally valid. The logic is symmetrical. If we accept the modal axiom of S5, we must accept the existence of both a necessary perfect being and a necessary evil being, which is a contradiction, or we must reject one of the premises. Since the logic is identical, the only way to break the symmetry is to show that one of the premises in Martin’s argument is false, specifically premise (2), the possibility of the AEO, or premise (1), the claim that evil entails necessary existence.

How does Hartshorne respond? He must argue that the AEO is not a coherent possibility. His defense rests on a specific metaphysical theory of evil, namely, the privation theory. This theory, rooted in Augustinian and Thomistic thought, holds that evil is not a positive reality but a *privation* or *lack* of good. Evil is parasitic upon the good; it is the absence of due perfection. For example, blindness is not a positive entity; it is the absence of sight. Decay is not a positive force; it is the corruption of a form. If evil is a privation, then a *maximally* evil being would be a being that lacks all good.

But here is the fatal problem for the AEO. Existence itself is considered a good—a perfection. To exist is to have some degree of being, and being is good. Therefore, a being that lacks all good would lack existence itself. A maximally evil being, by this logic, would be a being that does not exist. But Martin’s AEO is defined as existing necessarily. Thus, the concept of a necessarily existing maximally evil being is incoherent. It is a contradiction in terms, because necessary existence is a great good, and a maximally evil being cannot possess any good. Therefore, \(\Diamond E\) is false. The AEO is not a possible being.

Furthermore, Hartshorne could challenge premise (1) directly. Even if we grant that evil is a positive force, a maximally evil being would not want to exist necessarily. A necessary being cannot destroy itself, cannot change its nature, and is bound by its own eternal existence. This eternal existence would be a limitation on its evilness—it cannot annihilate itself to cause the ultimate destruction, nor can it become more evil. A truly maximally evil being would prefer contingent existence, so that it could cease to exist or alter its malevolent nature. Thus, necessary existence would be a *defect* from the perspective of absolute evil. Therefore, \(E \to \Box E\) is false.

#### V. The Problem of Structural Parity and Metaphysical Burden

At first glance, Hartshorne’s response appears robust. By invoking the privation theory, he can deny the possibility of the AEO. However, this defense reveals the deep structural vulnerability of the modal argument. The modal logic itself does not provide the asymmetry; the asymmetry is imported from a substantive, and highly controversial, metaphysical doctrine.

Consider the structure of the debate. Hartshorne’s argument rests on three pillars: the definition of God as perfect, the modal axiom (S5/Becker), and the metaphysical claim that perfection entails necessary existence. Martin’s parody rests on the same modal axiom, but replaces the metaphysical claim with one about evil. To defeat Martin, Hartshorne must show that the concept of “maximal evil” is logically impossible. But his argument for this impossibility relies on the privation theory, which is not a theorem of modal logic. It is a specific theory of value and ontology.
