**The Chiasmic Archive: Mythical Time, Retroactive Trauma, and the Ontology of Historical Agency**

The relationship between time, event, and subjectivity has long constituted the central axis of philosophical inquiry, yet the precise nature of this relationship remains fiercely contested. The claim that Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s concept of mythical time entails a retroactive structure of the event comparable to Sigmund Freud’s theory of trauma offers a profound intervention into this discourse. By proposing a non-serial ontology of time—one in which the past is not merely a preceding sequence but an active, sedimentary presence that retroactively constitutes the meaning of the present—Merleau-Ponty provides a radical framework for understanding historical experience. This essay evaluates the plausibility of this claim, assessing whether such a temporal framework can support Alain Badiou’s model of subject-formation against Raphaël Gély’s critique of the pre-existing subject. Furthermore, it examines how Mauro Carbone’s reading of wild Being and the touching-touched chiasm resolves the tension between the invisible sediment of historical events and the continuous right of rectification, ultimately demonstrating that a non-serial ontology of time can indeed sustain a coherent account of historical agency.

To evaluate the claim that Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time is comparable to Freudian trauma, one must first grasp the radical departure both thinkers make from linear, serial temporality. In Freud’s theory of trauma, particularly as articulated in his work on repetition compulsion and *Nachträglichkeit* (afterwardness), the traumatic event is not simply a point on a chronological timeline. Trauma is characterized by a retroactive structure wherein the event only achieves its full, devastating meaning retrospectively. The traumatic occurrence is not fully grasped at the moment of its happening; rather, its meaning is constituted later, through the belated recognition of its consequences. The past does not merely precede the present; it retroactively invades the present, restructuring the significance of the initial occurrence. The event is not fixed until it is sedimented into the unconscious through this retroactive process.

Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time, as developed in his late writings, particularly *The Visible and the Invisible* and the *Notes from the Underground*, operates on a strikingly similar ontological logic. For Merleau-Ponty, time is not a homogeneous, measurable continuum—a straight line where the present simply replaces the past. Instead, mythical time is chiasmic and sedimentary. It is a time where the past and present intertwine, where the invisible residue of the past actively touches the present, and where the present re-touches the past. The "myth" is not a primitive narrative but a structural principle of temporal experience: the event is not a isolated point but a structural unfolding that reverberates through the "flesh" of the world. The past is not erased by the present; it is sedimented, buried, and yet persistently tactile. The invisible sediment of historical events weighs upon the present not as a causal chain but as a retroactive structural weight, much like the Freudian traumatic return.

The parallel between the two is robust: both Freud and Merleau-Ponty dismantle the serial model of time in favor of a retroactive, chiasmic temporality. In Freud, the event is retroactively constituted as trauma; in Merleau-Ponty, the event is retroactively constituted as a sediment of the flesh. Both reject the idea that the past is merely a dead archive of causes that produce present effects. Instead, both posit a dynamic where the past actively shapes the present from within, often unbeknownst to the subject. The retroactive structure is not a mere psychological quirk but an ontological feature of how events inhabit time. Thus, the claim that Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time entails a retroactive structure comparable to Freud’s trauma is not merely an analogy; it is an articulation of a shared non-serial ontology of time where the event is constituted through its own sedimentation.

This non-serial, retroactive ontology has profound implications for political and existential subjectivity, particularly when evaluated against the backdrop of Alain Badiou’s model of subject-formation and Raphaël Gély’s critique of the pre-existing subject. Badiou, in *Being and Event*, argues that the subject is constituted through fidelity to an event. An event is a rupture in the established order—a situation that breaks the rules of the existing multiplicity—and the subject is the one who declares the truth of this rupture, remaining faithful to it despite the prevailing conditions. For Badiou, the subject is not a pre-existing essence but an effect of the event; the subject is born through the act of fidelity. 

Does Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time support Badiou’s model? The answer is nuanced. On one hand, the retroactive structure of mythical time aligns beautifully with Badiou’s insistence that the subject is constituted retroactively through the event. In a non-serial ontology, the event does not simply occur at time *t* and wait for a subject to react at time *t+1*. Rather, the event retroactively structures the temporal horizon, and the subject emerges as the one who retroactively recognizes and declares the truth of this event. The subject’s fidelity is not a sequential reaction but a chiasmic intertwining of the event and the declaration, much like the touching-touched dynamic of Merleau-Ponty’s flesh. The event touches the subject, and the subject, in touching the event, retroactively constitutes the event as a truth.

However, Raphaël Gély offers a potent critique of this model, particularly concerning the "pre-existing subject." Gély, drawing on a rigorous reading of Badiou and Deleuze, argues that Badiou’s model implicitly relies on a transcendental or pre-existing subject—the "one" who declares truth. Even if Badiou claims the subject is an effect of the event, the very structure of declaration requires a subject-position that is already capable of receiving, interpreting, and declaring. Gély critiques this as a lingering Kantian or transcendental residue: a pre-existing subjectivity that stands outside the event to judge it. For Gély, this smuggles a pre-given agent back into the ontology of the event, undermining the radical immanence that Badiou claims.

It is precisely here that Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time provides the necessary philosophical scaffolding to defend Badiou against Gély’s critique while resolving the problem of the pre-existing subject. In a serial, linear ontology, the subject must indeed be a pre-existing agent who steps into the breach of the event. But in a non-serial, chiasmic ontology, the subject is not a pre-existing agent but an emergent node within the retroactive sedimentation of the event. The subject does not stand outside the event to declare it; the subject is constituted *within* the chiasm of the event’s retroactive touch. Merleau-Ponty’s concept of the chiasm—the intertwining of the touching and the touched—dissolves the subject-object duality that Gély critiques. The subject is the touching-touched itself, the point where the event retroactively sedimented into the flesh of the world becomes a lived, active fidelity. Thus, the non-serial framework supports Badiou’s subject-formation not by positing a pre-existing subject, but by demonstrating that subjectivity is an ontological effect of the retroactive sedimentation of the event within the chiasmic fabric of time.

Yet, even with this chiasmic foundation, a significant tension remains in the application of this ontology to history. On one hand, the invisible sediment of historical events—the weight of the past that retroactively structures the present—seems to render agency illusory. If the past is a sediment that touches the present from within, how can the present act upon the past? On the other hand, the continuous right of rectification—the human capacity to reinterpret, revise, and actively reshape the meaning of historical events—seems to require a linear, progressive model of time where the present can overwrite the past. How can a non-serial ontology sustain a coherent account of historical agency that accounts for both the sedimentary weight of history and the active right of rectification?

It is at this juncture that Mauro Carbone’s reading of Merleau-Ponty’s late ontology, particularly the concepts of wild Being and the touching-touched chiasm, provides the decisive resolution. Carbone, in works such as *The Thinking of the Sensible* and *A Philosophical History of Contemporary Art*, emphasizes that Merleau-Ponty’s late thought moves beyond the chiasm of the flesh toward a conception of "wild Being." Wild Being is that which escapes the chiasm—the ungraspable, excessive dimension of existence that resists being fully woven into the tactile fabric of the sensible world. However, wild Being is not a void or a negation of the chiasm; rather, it is the condition of the chiasm’s possibility. Because Being is wild, the chiasm is never a closed, static loop; it is an open, dynamic process. The touching and the touched are never perfectly aligned; there is always an excess, a remainder, a wild dimension that escapes the grasp of the sediment.

Carbone’s articulation of wild Being directly resolves the tension between the invisible sediment and the continuous right of rectification. The invisible sediment of historical events is precisely what is sedimented within the chiasm—the tactile, woven residue of the past that constitutes the present. Yet, because wild Being exceeds the chiasm, the sediment is never a sealed archive. The wild excess of Being ensures that the past is never completely captured or fixed by its sedimentation. The continuous right of rectification, then, is not an arbitrary human imposition upon a passive past, nor is it a linear progression that overwrites history. Instead, it is an ontological necessity of the chiasm itself. Because the touching-touched dynamic is dynamic and open to wild Being, the present is always capable of re-touching the past, of re-inscribing the sediment, and thereby rectifying its meaning. The right of rectification is built into the fabric of the chiasm.

This resolution demonstrates the profound coherence of a non-serial ontology of time for historical agency. In a serial ontology, historical agency is a linear chain of causation: the past causes the present, and the present, through progressive rationality, corrects the past. This model struggles to account for the weight of the past (the trauma, the sediment) and often reduces rectification to a mere negation of the past. In contrast, the non-serial ontology of mythical time, as developed by Merleau-Ponty and extended by Carbone, allows for a historical agency that is both deeply respectful of the sedimentary weight of the past and radically transformative. The subject does not overwrite the past; the subject participates in the chiasm. The subject is the locus where the invisible sediment of historical events is actively re-touched, where the retroactive weight of the past is met with the wild excess of the present. Historical agency, in this framework, is the fidelity to the event understood as a chiasmic process of retroactive sedimentation and continuous rectification.

To demonstrate the sustainability of this account, consider the act of historical revisionism or the recovery of marginalized voices. In a serial time, the past is fixed, and the present merely adds to it. In the non-serial ontology of mythical time, the past is retroactively constituted by the present’s fidelity to its event. The invisible sediment of historical oppression is not erased by the right of rectification; rather, it is re-touched, re-inscribed, and transformed through the chiasm. The subject who engages in this act of historical agency does not stand outside the sediment; they are the point of the chiasm where the wild Being of the present touches the sedimentary past, rectifying its meaning without negating its weight. The retroactive structure ensures that the past is always already touching the present, and the continuous right of rectification ensures that the present is always capable of re-touching the past.

Therefore, the claim that Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time entails a retroactive structure comparable to Freud’s trauma is not only valid but philosophically generative. It provides the necessary ontological foundation to move beyond the limitations of both Badiou’s potential transcendental residue and Gély’s critique of the pre-existing subject. By grounding subject-formation in the chiasmic, retroactive sedimentation of the event, the non-serial ontology dissolves the duality of the pre-existing agent and the passive event. Furthermore, through Mauro Carbone’s articulation of wild Being and the touching-touched chiasm, the tension between the invisible sediment of historical events and the continuous right of rectification is elegantly resolved. The wild excess of Being ensures that the sediment is never dead, while the chiasm ensures that rectification is an ontological feature of temporal experience rather than an external imposition. Ultimately, a non-serial ontology of time does sustain a coherent account of historical agency. It replaces the fragile, linear model of the agent pushing history forward with the robust, chiasmic model of the subject participating in the retroactive weaving of the touching and the touched, thereby giving historical agency the weight of the past and the wild freedom of the future.
