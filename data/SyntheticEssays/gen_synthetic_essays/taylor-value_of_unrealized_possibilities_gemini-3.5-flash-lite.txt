# The Architecture of Futurity: A Philosophical Evaluation of Don Marquis’s Future-Like-Ours Account

## Introduction

The moral evaluation of abortion hinges upon the definition of the ontological subject against which the act is directed. For decades, the ethical landscape was polarized between two seemingly intractable positions. On one side stood the traditional conservative view, asserting that human personhood—and consequently, the full moral right to life—begins at conception, often anchored in theological or strict biological essentialism. On the other side stood the liberal defense, championed by philosophers like Mary Anne Warren and Michael Tooley, which argued that fetuses lack the necessary psychological capacities, self-awareness, or agency to be considered "persons" in the moral sense, thereby rendering abortion morally permissible. 

Into this gridlocked debate stepped Don Marquis in his seminal 1989 essay, "Why Abortion Is Immoral." Marquis bypassed the quagmire of personhood debates by proposing an alternative foundational standard: the "Future-Like-Ours" (FLO) account. Marquis argued that the primary wrongness of killing an adult human being does not consist in the violation of autonomy, the infliction of pain, or the disruption of social contracts, but rather in the fact that killing deprives the victim of their future—specifically, a future of value, characterized by experiences, projects, activities, and enjoyments that are identical to the futures of adult humans. Because a fetus possesses a biological and developmental trajectory that, if uninterrupted, will result in a future identical in value to that of an adult, Marquis concluded that abortion is prima facie seriously morally wrong.

While the FLO account has earned widespread attention for its dialectical ingenuity, it has also provoked rigorous philosophical counter-attacks. Critics have challenged Marquis’s framework through the lenses of personal identity, the necessity of continuous consciousness, and the ontology of moral rights. This essay will critically evaluate Don Marquis’s Future-Like-Ours account by analyzing the substantial objections raised by Peter McInerney, Robert Lovering, and Walter Sinnott-Armstrong. 

Furthermore, this analysis will engage directly with pivotal thought experiments—specifically the isolated hermit and the paraplegic victim—to test the coherence of Marquis’s theory. By examining whether the normative weight of killing rests upon the privation of actualized psychological continuity or the mere deprivation of unrealized biological possibilities, this essay will resolve the dialectical tension between actual future ownership and potential future possibilities. Ultimately, this inquiry will assess whether abortion truly constitutes an immoral loss under a refined ontological and normative framework.

## 1. The Architecture of the Future-Like-Ours Account

To understand the objections lodged against Marquis, one must first map the internal architecture of the FLO account. Marquis begins by asking a deceptively simple question: Why is killing wrong? Common answers—such as that killing causes premature death, that it violates a person's autonomy, or that it is generally prohibited by social utility—either fail to capture the profound gravity of the act or presuppose that the victim already possesses certain advanced psychological capacities. 

Marquis contends that the best explanation for the wrongness of killing is that it *deprives the victim of the value of their future*. When a normal adult human is killed, they lose everything they currently value and everything they would have come to value. Their future is cut short, and this deprivation constitutes the greatest misfortune that can be inflicted upon them. Indeed, death is regarded as the ultimate evil precisely because it eliminates the bearer of value: the future-oriented life.

From this foundational premise, Marquis derives his anti-abortion argument via a straightforward syllogism:
1. Depriving a being of a future of value is (prima facie) seriously morally wrong.
2. Human fetuses have futures of value (futures like ours).
3. Therefore, abortion is (prima facie) seriously morally wrong.

Marquis is careful to defend his account against several anticipated objections. To the objection that fetuses lack the psychological characteristics—such as desires, consciousness, or self-awareness—necessary to be victims, Marquis responds that what makes killing wrong is not the *current* possession of desires to live, but the *future* possession of desires and experiences that will be thwarted. A temporarily unconscious person, or a suicidal person experiencing a fleeting depression, still possesses a future of value, even if their current conscious desires do not align with the preservation of their life. Thus, current psychological states are neither necessary nor sufficient for having a FLO.

Moreover, Marquis defends his account against the "potentiality" critique, which often conflates his view with traditional conservative accounts. He explicitly denies that fetuses are wrong to kill merely because they are *potential* persons in a metaphysical sense. Instead, he insists that a fetus has a valuable future not as a matter of abstract potentiality, but because it is *already* an entity with a biological and psychological continuity that will unfold into a human life of value, provided external interruptions do not occur. The biological organism *is* the bearer of that future.

## 2. Personal Identity and the Hermit: Peter McInerney’s Critique

One of the most persistent challenges to the FLO account questions the exact nature of the relationship between the current fetus and the future adult who will experience the value. This territory is mapped by Peter McInerney, who raises deep metaphysical concerns rooted in the philosophy of personal identity. 

McInerney argues that Marquis’s account glosses over the conditions required for numerical and psychological identity over time. For an individual at time $T_2$ to be identical to an entity at time $T_1$, there must be a robust continuity—either bodily, psychological, or relational. In the case of the human fetus, the psychological discontinuity between a zygote or early embryo and the resulting adult is so radical that it strains the traditional understanding of personal identity.

To expose the vulnerabilities in Marquis’s view concerning identity and value, philosophers frequently employ thought experiments. Consider Peter McInerney’s engagement with the problem of psychological discontinuity through the lens of the **Isolated Hermit** thought experiment (and related identity-shifting scenarios). Imagine a hermit who has lived an utterly isolated, ascetic life in the deep wilderness for decades, entirely severed from human society, culture, language, and relational networks. Suppose this hermit experiences no higher cognitive reflections, has no projects of value, and lives a life of bare, instinctual survival devoid of intellectual or emotional flourishing. 

If such a hermit is killed painlessly in his sleep, has he been deprived of a "future like ours"? The hermit’s future lacks the complex social interactions, career aspirations, artistic enjoyments, and intellectual pursuits that characterize the lives of the academic philosophers and citizens for whom "futures like ours" are modeled. If the FLO account is correct, the wrongness of killing the hermit must be proportionate to the value of the future he is denied. Yet, if his future is markedly impoverished compared to a flourishing modern human's future, does the FLO account imply that killing him is somehow *less* wrong than killing an ordinary person?

More acutely, McInerney applies this line of reasoning to the fetus by questioning *whose* future the fetus possesses. At the early stages of embryonic development, the biological entity undergoes such radical transformations—morphological, neurological, and psychological—that the persistence conditions of personal identity become deeply problematic. Can a microscopic cluster of cells truly be said to possess *my* future, or does the future belong to a distinct psychological entity that comes into existence only *after* the development of a functional cerebral cortex?

If psychological continuity (such as Locke’s memory criterion or a Parfitian chain of connected mental states) is a prerequisite for being the *same person* who enjoys the future, then an early fetus does not yet possess a psychological subject capable of owning that future. The early fetus is merely the biological precursor to a person, much like an acorn is the biological precursor to an oak tree. We do not mourn the "death" of an oak tree when an acorn is crushed, because the acorn is not yet an oak tree, nor does it possess the psychological or structural identity of one. 

Marquis attempts to sidestep this by claiming that the biological organism *is* the entity that persists and has the future. However, McInerney counters that this reduction collapses personal identity into mere biological continuity, which leads to absurd moral implications. If biological continuity alone suffices to ground a future of value, then destroying a human somatic cell or preventing a sperm and egg from uniting (in delayed conception) would also constitute the deprivation of a FLO—a reductio ad absurdum that Marquis tries to avoid by drawing a bright line at conception, where a unique genetic code is formed. But drawing the line at genetic uniqueness is an arbitrary biological marker that fails to solve the deep metaphysical puzzle of how a non-conscious collection of cells can be identical to a conscious adult moral agent.

## 3. Continuous Consciousness and the Paraplegic Victim: Robert Lovering’s Critique

Moving from the abstract metaphysical puzzles of personal identity to the phenomenological conditions of moral agency, Robert Lovering launches a sustained critique of the FLO account, focusing on the necessity of continuous consciousness and the moral status of subjects with compromised capacities. 

Lovering argues that Marquis’s dismissal of current psychological states is unjustified. By asserting that a being does not need to *currently* value its life or possess conscious desires in order to be wronged by death, Marquis detaches the wrongness of killing from any experiential harm felt by the victim. Lovering contends that for a being to be wronged in a morally significant way, there must be a sentient subject capable of experiencing the deprivation, or at the very least, possessing an *actualized* psychological architecture that grounds interests.

To test the boundaries of Marquis's theory regarding disabled or temporarily incapacitated individuals, we must examine the **Paraplegic Victim** (or severely cognitively disabled individual) thought experiment. Suppose an individual suffers a catastrophic accident that leaves them permanently paralyzed from the neck down, stripped of the ability to engage in most physical activities, professional pursuits, or sensory enjoyments that previously defined their life of value. Alternatively, consider a person who enters a permanent vegetative state (PVS) with no capacity for consciousness, yet whose biological organism continues to function.

According to the FLO account, does the permanently comatose patient possess a future of value? Marquis must answer affirmatively if he is to maintain that killing a comatose patient is wrong (which he does, arguing that they still have a biological future that could theoretically be valuable if they recovered, or simply that their biological life contains a future). However, Lovering points out that this leads to severe counter-intuitive results. 

If the wrongness of killing is based on the loss of a future of value, what happens when a person’s future *lacks* value, or when their capacity to experience value is permanently eradicated? Consider a terminally ill patient in excruciating, unmanageable pain, whose future consists entirely of agony leading to inevitable death. In such a case, the future is *not* a "future of value"—it is a future of disvalue. Under Marquis’s logic, killing this person would either not be wrong or would actually be morally required as an act of mercy to prevent disvalue. Yet Marquis insists that even in cases of terminal illness, killing remains prima facie wrong because it still deprives the person of whatever small value remains, or because it violates the FLO framework.

More challenging still is the case of the paraplegic or severely disabled individual whose future is radically altered. Lovering argues that if the FLO account is pushed to its logical conclusion, it devalues the lives of individuals with disabilities by measuring the wrongness of their killing against an able-bodied norm of a "future like ours." If a person’s disability prevents them from engaging in the standard array of human activities that constitute a FLO, does killing them constitute a lesser moral wrong because they lose a "lesser" future? Marquis denies this, asserting that *any* human future contains enough value to make killing wrong. But Lovering counters that this maneuver renders the "value" clause of the FLO account vacuous. If virtually any biological future counts as a "future of value," then the theory collapses into a crude biological vitalism—the very position Marquis claimed to transcend.

Furthermore, Lovering highlights the absurdity of Marquis’s treatment of temporary unconsciousness. Marquis argues that a sleeping or temporarily comatose person has a FLO because they *currently* possess the conceptual and psychological machinery, temporarily suspended, to form desires and experience value. But a fetus does not merely have *suspended* psychological machinery; it possesses *unformed* biological machinery. Equating the sleep of an adult with the embryonic state of a fetus ignores the profound qualitative difference between a mind with an active psychological history and an organism with merely programmatic potential.

## 4. Moral Rights and the Isolation of the Hermit: Walter Sinnott-Armstrong’s Critique

While McInerney targets personal identity and Lovering targets consciousness, Walter Sinnott-Armstrong launches a formidable attack on the normative mechanics of Marquis’s theory, particularly concerning the derivation of *moral rights* and the application of the FLO account to edge cases.

Sinnott-Armstrong questions the transition from the premise that *killing deprives someone of a valuable future* to the conclusion that *killing violates a moral right to life*. In normative ethics, a wrong is not merely a misfortune; a wrongful act typically involves the violation of a right held by an agent. For a being to hold a right, that being must be capable of having interests, and having interests generally presupposes some level of sentience, intentionality, or psychological agency.

To expose the normative gaps in Marquis’s framework, Sinnott-Armstrong deploys and expands upon scenarios involving isolated beings and marginal cases. Let us return to the **Isolated Hermit**, but re-examine him through the lens of normative rights and moral standing. Suppose the hermit has no social relations, no community, and no conception of rights or duties. When someone kills him, the killer deprives him of his future. But does the killer violate a *right* belonging to the hermit? 

Sinnott-Armstrong argues that rights are relational and protective of agency. To say that X has a right against Y not to be killed implies that X can claim that right, be wronged in a way that generates grievance, or possess an interest that can be cognitively frustrated. A human fetus has no conception of itself, no interests it can articulate, and no capacity to value its own future. To attribute a *moral right* to life to a zygote or early fetus based solely on the objective value of its biological future is to commit a category mistake: it confuses the *value of an outcome* with the *bearer of a moral right*.

Moreover, Sinnott-Armstrong challenges Marquis’s asymmetry argument. Marquis famously argues that contraception is *not* morally wrong because there is no determinate subject of harm when a sperm and egg fail to unite. In contraception, there is no non-empty set of candidates for a FLO; millions of sperm cells could potentially unite with an ovum, so picking one as the "victim" of contraception is arbitrary. 

Sinnott-Armstrong turns this logic back against Marquis. If the wrongness of an action is determined purely by the *deprivation of a future of value*, why isn't contraception wrong? After all, contraception deprives *some* potential future person of a FLO. Marquis replies that in contraception, there is no identifiable subject who is deprived. But Sinnott-Armstrong counters that the early fetus is equally indeterminate. At the blastocyst stage, the cell mass can still split to form identical twins (or merge to form a tetragametic chimera). Which of the future twins was the original subject of the FLO? If the early embryo can divide, then prior to twinning, there was no single determinate individual whose future was being cut short; there were merely multiple branching possibilities. 

By grounding moral status in a biological trajectory that is inherently plastic and indeterminate, Marquis’s theory collapses under its own normative weight. If the FLO account cannot cleanly distinguish between contraception (permitted by Marquis) and abortion (forbidden by Marquis) without resorting to arbitrary biological milestones like fertilization, then the theory fails to provide the principled moral guidance it promises.

## 5. Actualized Futures vs. Unrealized Possibilities: The Ontological Clash

At the heart of the debate between Marquis and his critics lies a profound ontological conflict: Does the normative weight of killing rest upon the **privation of actualized futures** or the **deprivation of unrealized possibilities**?

To resolve this dialectical tension, we must examine what it means for a future to be "owned" versus merely "possible." 

### The Privation of Actualized Futures
The defender of psychological continuity and actualized agency (drawing on McInerney and Lovering) argues that a future can only be considered a misfortune to lose if it belongs to an *actualized subject*—an entity whose psychological life is already underway, possessing a thread of consciousness, memory, or intentionality that links its present state to its future aspirations. On this view, harm is fundamentally experiential or agential. Even if death terminates future experiences without pain, it is a deprivation because it seizes away projects, relationships, and self-authored narratives that the subject is *actively living out*. 

The actualized future is anchored by a present self that cares about that future, fears its loss, or is at least actively engaged in the world. A fetus possesses no such actualized future. It has no narrative, no self-authored projects, and no psychological investment in tomorrow. Therefore, destroying a fetus does not privatively rupture an actualized psychological life; at worst, it prevents a psychological life from ever starting. Prevention is morally distinct from destruction. Just as failing to rescue a starving person in a distant country is a moral failure, but of a different order than murdering someone in front of you, failing to bring a potential person into existence (or stopping their biological development before personhood emerges) is not the moral equivalent of killing an actual person.

### The Deprivation of Unrealized Possibilities
Marquis, conversely, champions the ontology of unrealized possibilities, provided those possibilities are grounded in a continuous biological organism. For Marquis, the tragedy of death is objective. It does not require the victim to be currently mourning their loss or consciously valuing their life. If a person is rendered temporarily unconscious by a blow to the head and is then killed, they are wronged because their rich, valuable future—which they would have actualized—was stolen from them. 

Marquis extends this logic downward to the fetus. The fetus may not *currently* actualize psychological states, but it is an active, developing biological system whose internal teleology is directed toward the actualization of those very states. The future is not merely an abstract, floating possibility (like the future of an unfertilized ovum); it is a concrete biological trajectory. To cut off that trajectory is to perpetrate the ultimate theft: stealing a life of value.

### Resolving the Dialectical Tension
How do we resolve this tension? The fatal flaw in Marquis’s defense of unrealized biological possibilities lies in his conflation of **biological teleology** with **moral subjecthood**. 

An acorn has a biological teleology directed toward becoming an oak tree. A cancer cell has a biological teleology directed toward rapid proliferation. Biological development is governed by genetic and environmental laws, but teleology in nature is descriptive, not normative. It does not automatically generate moral rights or interests. For a being to have an interest in its future, it must possess the capacity for subjective experience—sentience. Without sentience, there is no "what-it-is-like" to be the entity, no subjective welfare to be promoted or frustrated, and no experiential capacity for loss.

Marquis attempts to circumvent the sentience requirement by arguing that future sentience and future value are sufficient to ground the wrongness of killing *now*. But this smuggles in the moral status of the future adult and illegitimately retrojects it onto the non-sentient embryo. A non-sentient fetus is no more harmed by abortion than a sperm cell is harmed by abstinence, because in neither case is there a conscious subject capable of experiencing the deprivation as a loss. 

The normative weight of killing *must* rest upon the privation of actualized (or at least actualizable, sentient) psychological continuity. When we kill an adult, a child, or even a late-term fetus capable of sentience, we extinguish a conscious subject whose experiential life is abruptly halted. We violate an ongoing narrative. In the case of a pre-sentient, early-stage human embryo, there is no conscious subject, no psychological narrative, and no actualized experiential capacity. There is only a biological blueprint. While that blueprint holds the *potential* to become a person, potentiality is not actuality. To treat the destruction of potentiality as morally equivalent to the destruction of actuality is to commit a category mistake that paralyzes moral reasoning.

---

## Conclusion

Don Marquis’s Future-Like-Ours account offered a brilliant rhetorical pivot in the abortion debate, moving away from the intractable battlegrounds of personhood and attempting to ground the wrongness of killing in the objective deprivation of a valuable future. However, as the rigorous critiques of Peter McInerney, Robert Lovering, and Walter Sinnott-Armstrong demonstrate, the FLO account ultimately collapses under the weight of its own metaphysical and normative inconsistencies.

By ignoring the profound metaphysical puzzles of personal identity and psychological discontinuity highlighted by McInerney, Marquis treats the early fetus as a stable owner of a future that it cannot yet conceptually or psychologically grasp. By dismissing current psychological states and sentience, Marquis’s theory struggles with edge cases like the paraplegic victim, the comatose patient, and the terminally ill, threatening to slide into an uncritical biological vitalism. And by eliding the crucial distinction between the objective value of an outcome and the possession of a moral right—as Sinnott-Armstrong exposes—the FLO account fails to explain how a non-sentient biological entity can hold a right to life or be the determinate subject of a stolen future.

When we resolve the dialectical tension between actual future ownership and unrealized biological possibilities, we must conclude that the normative weight of killing rests upon the privation of actualized psychological continuity and sentience. A fetus at the early stages of development possesses an unrealized possibility, a biological trajectory, but it does not possess an actualized future of value owned by a conscious subject. Therefore, while abortion involves the cessation of biological life, it does not constitute the moral equivalent of killing an adult human being, nor does it represent an immoral loss of a person's future. The architecture of futurity requires a conscious mind to inhabit it; without that foundation, the FLO account builds its ethical edifice upon sand.
