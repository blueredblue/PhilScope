**The Shifting Sands of Knowledge: A Critical Evaluation of Contextualism’s Response to Skepticism**

The Cartesian skeptic presents a paradox that lies at the heart of modern epistemology. We are confident that we know we have hands, that we know we are sitting in a chair, and that we know the world has existed for more than five minutes. Yet, we cannot rule out the possibility that we are disembodied brains in a vat (BIVs), being fed a perfectly coherent stream of sensory experiences by a malevolent neuroscientist. If we cannot rule out this skeptical hypothesis, and if knowledge requires the elimination of all relevant alternatives, then it seems we do not know any of the ordinary empirical propositions we take for granted. The skeptic’s argument is deceptively simple: we do not know that we are not BIVs; if we do not know that, we do not know we have hands; therefore, we do not know we have hands.

Contextualism about knowledge ascriptions emerges as one of the most sophisticated and influential responses to this paradox. Championed by philosophers such as Stewart Cohen, Keith DeRose, and David Lewis, contextualism offers a linguistic and semantic solution. It argues that the truth-conditions of sentences of the form “S knows that p” are relative to the context in which they are uttered. More specifically, the epistemic standards that a subject must meet to satisfy the predicate “knows” are determined by the conversational context of the attributor. In ordinary, low-stakes contexts, the standards are low, and we can truly say we know we have hands. In philosophical, high-stakes contexts where the skeptical hypothesis is explicitly raised, the standards are raised to a nearly absolute level, and the same claim becomes false.

This solution is elegant. It promises to reconcile our robust intuitive commitment to ordinary empirical knowledge with the undeniable logical force of the skeptical argument. However, despite its initial appeal, contextualism ultimately fails as a satisfactory philosophical resolution to the skeptical problem. While it provides a nuanced descriptive account of how we use the word “knows,” it is incapable of providing a normative, epistemic grounding for that usage. The contextualist solution collapses under the weight of three devastating objections: first, it renders ordinary knowledge hostage to the mere conversational mention of skepticism, thereby dissolving the stability of that knowledge; second, it fails to provide a principled account of how contexts determine epistemic standards or how we recognize shifts in those standards, leading to a vicious meta-contextual regress; and third, it begs the question against the skeptic by assuming the legitimacy of ordinary epistemic standards while dismissing the skeptic’s demand for absolute certainty as merely one arbitrary standard among many.

**I. The Contextualist Machinery: A Semantic Escape from a Logical Trap**

To evaluate contextualism, one must first understand its precise mechanics. The core thesis is that “knows” is an indexical or context-sensitive term, akin to “tall” or “local.” Just as a person can be tall in a context of jockeys but short in a context of basketball players, a subject can “know” a proposition in a low-standards context but fail to “know” it in a high-standards context. The standards are typically cashed out in terms of relevant alternatives. According to a relevant alternatives theory of knowledge, to know that p, a subject must be able to rule out all *relevant* alternatives to p. In an ordinary context, the relevant alternatives are mundane ones—that the bank is closed on Saturdays, that the car is not in the driveway. The skeptical hypothesis (e.g., that we are BIVs) is not a relevant alternative because it is not a live possibility in our everyday practical engagement with the world.

Contextualism adds a crucial meta-linguistic layer to this theory. The *salience* of an alternative determines its relevance. When a skeptic raises the BIV hypothesis in a philosophical conversation, the alternative becomes salient. Once salient, it cannot be ignored; it becomes a relevant alternative that the subject must rule out to warrant a true knowledge ascription. Because no finite empirical evidence can rule out the BIV hypothesis, the subject fails to meet the elevated standard. Consequently, the sentence “I know I have hands” uttered in the skeptical context is false. Yet, this does not contradict the truth of the same sentence uttered in the ordinary context, because the semantic content of “knows” has shifted. The contextualist thus dissolves the paradox: the skeptic is right in the skeptic’s context, and the ordinary person is right in the ordinary context. There is no single, absolute property of knowledge that is being contested; rather, there are multiple, context-relative properties.

This solution is initially compelling because it respects the data of our linguistic intuitions. We do, in fact, retract knowledge claims when we learn of new error possibilities. If I say, “I know the plane stops in Chicago,” and you reply, “But what if there is a mechanical failure that forces a diversion?” I might say, “Well, I guess I don’t *really* know that.” Contextualism explains this phenomenon elegantly. However, the elegance of the semantic machinery masks a profound philosophical weakness. The solution works only by relativizing the very concept of knowledge, and in doing so, it sacrifices the normative and stable character of knowledge that makes it valuable in the first place.

**II. The Inflationary Threat: The Fragility of Ordinary Knowledge**

The first major objection to contextualism is that it makes ordinary knowledge trivially fragile and hostage to conversational whim. The contextualist claims that in an ordinary context, we genuinely know that we have hands. But the skeptic can destroy this knowledge simply by *speaking*. By merely uttering the words, “But what if you are a brain in a vat?” the skeptic raises the standard, rendering the ordinary knowledge claim false. This is not a refutation of skepticism; it is a concession that the skeptic has the power to annihilate knowledge at will, without providing any new evidence or arguments.

The contextualist will respond that this is precisely the point: the skeptic *does* change the context, and in the new context, the knowledge claim is false. But this response fails to appreciate the depth of the objection. The problem is not that the knowledge claim becomes false in the skeptical context; the problem is that this demonstrates that the truth of the original ordinary knowledge claim was contingent on the *ignorance* of a possibility that is always logically present. If the mere consideration of a hypothesis—regardless of its plausibility—can defeat knowledge, then the knowledge we had in the ordinary context was never robust. It was not knowledge grounded in a reliable connection to the truth; it was merely a function of conversational oversight.

To illustrate, consider the “abominable conjunction” that contextualists themselves identify. In an ordinary context, I can truly assert, “I know I have hands, but I don’t know I’m not a BIV.” The contextualist claims this is not an abominable conjunction because the second clause is evaluated in a different context than the first. But this is a sleight of hand. The ordinary person, upon uttering this sentence, is not switching contexts mid-sentence. They are making a single, unified epistemic claim. The contextualist must either claim that the ordinary person is confused about their own context, or they must admit that the conjunction is true in a single context, which would entail that knowledge does not require ruling out the BIV hypothesis even when it is mentioned. If the latter, then the skeptic’s raising of the hypothesis does not actually change the epistemic status of the subject; it merely changes the truth-value of the *attribution*.

This leads to a deeper problem: the contextualist cannot explain why the skeptic has the *authority* to change the standards. In the bank case, a shift in standards is justified by a change in practical stakes—if my life savings depend on the bank being open, I need stronger evidence. But the skeptic’s raising of the BIV hypothesis does not change my practical stakes. It does not make the possibility of being a BIV more likely. It merely makes it *salient*. Why should mere salience have the power to alter the truth-conditions of a knowledge claim? If knowledge is a normative status—a matter of being sufficiently justified relative to the truth—then it should not fluctuate based on what possibilities are mentioned in a conversation. The contextualist is effectively allowing the skeptic to dictate the terms of the debate, and by doing so, they concede that the skeptic’s standard is a legitimate one. If the skeptic’s standard is legitimate, then the ordinary standard is arbitrary. If the ordinary standard is arbitrary, then the ordinary person’s claim to knowledge is not grounded in any objective feature of their epistemic situation, but merely in a conversational convention that the skeptic can overturn at will.

This fragility undermines the very reconciliation contextualism promises. The goal was to show that we can have ordinary empirical knowledge despite the skeptical challenge. But if that knowledge evaporates at the mere utterance of a skeptical sentence, then it is not knowledge in any meaningful sense. It is a provisional, deflationary placeholder that exists only so long as no one asks the hard questions. The contextualist might respond that this is just how language works—that knowledge is a pragmatic notion. But this response conflates the *pragmatics* of assertion with the *epistemology* of belief. The fact that we *say* we know things in low-stakes contexts does not mean we *do* know them in a philosophically robust sense. The skeptic is not asking about our conversational practices; they are asking about the justificatory structure of our beliefs. By allowing the skeptic to set the standard, contextualism concedes that the skeptic’s challenge is a legitimate one, and then it merely changes the subject—from whether we have knowledge to whether we are using the word “knows” correctly.

**III. The Meta-Contextual Regress: The Unprincipled Determination of Standards**

The second, and perhaps more fatal, objection concerns the contextualist’s inability to provide a principled account of how contexts determine epistemic standards, or how we recognize shifts in those standards. This is the meta-contextual problem. For contextualism to be a viable theory, it must specify what features of a context are relevant to setting the standard. Is it the speaker’s intentions? The audience’s expectations? The practical stakes involved? The mere salience of certain possibilities?

Consider the standard contextualist answer: the standard is set by the *salience* of error possibilities. But what makes a possibility salient? In the bank case, the possibility of the bank being closed is salient because the stakes are high. But in the skeptical case, the BIV hypothesis is salient merely because it was mentioned. This suggests that salience is a purely psychological or conversational phenomenon. If so, then the determination of the standard is arbitrary. There is no principled reason why mentioning a BIV should raise the standard to require certainty, while mentioning a mundane error (e.g., the bank changed its hours) only raises it slightly. The contextualist owes us a theory of *how* salience translates into a specific epistemic standard, but they provide none. They simply stipulate that in high-stakes or skeptical contexts, the standard is higher.

This leads to a vicious regress. To determine whether a subject knows a proposition, we must first determine what standard is in play in the current context. To determine the standard, we must identify the relevant features of the context (e.g., whether the BIV hypothesis is salient). But to identify the relevant features, we must already have some epistemic access to the context—we must know that we are in a high-stakes or low-stakes situation. However, knowing that we are in a high-stakes situation is itself a piece of empirical knowledge. Do we *know* that we are in a high-stakes context? If we do, we must meet the standard for that knowledge claim, which itself depends on the context. We are caught in a loop: we cannot evaluate a knowledge claim without knowing the context, and we cannot know the context without evaluating a knowledge claim.

To see this clearly, imagine a subject, S, who is asked, “Do you know you have hands?” in a philosophy seminar. The contextualist says that because the BIV hypothesis is salient, the standard is high, and S does not know. But how does S know that the standard is high? S might think they are in an ordinary context and answer “Yes.” The contextualist would say S is mistaken about their own context. But what makes the philosophy seminar a high-standards context? Is it the presence of the word “skeptic” in the conversation? If so, then any conversation that mentions skepticism is high-standards. But what if S is a philosopher who is so used to skeptical arguments that they no longer find them salient? Does the standard remain high? If the standard is determined by the *attributor’s* psychological state, then it is wildly subjective. If it is determined by the *objective* features of the conversation (e.g., the words spoken), then it is arbitrary and disconnected from the subject’s actual epistemic position.

The contextualist might appeal to a notion of “conversational score” (as Lewis does), where the standard is set by the rules of the language game. But even this appeals to a convention that is itself subject to contextual variation. The problem is that contextualism cannot provide an independent, non-contextual criterion for determining which context we are in. This is not just a minor technical flaw; it is a fundamental epistemic failure. If we cannot know what standard applies to our own knowledge claims, then we cannot know whether we know anything at all. The contextualist solution, which was supposed to rescue ordinary knowledge, ends up introducing a new form of meta-skepticism: we do not know whether we know, because we do not know what context we are in.

Furthermore, the contextualist’s reliance on practical stakes to set standards collapses into a form of interest-relative invariantism (IRI), which is a rival theory. If the standard is set by the subject’s practical interests (e.g., whether they will lose money if they are wrong), then the truth of the knowledge ascription does not vary with the attributor’s context but with the subject’s interests. This is a coherent position, but it is not contextualism. Contextualism specifically claims that the *attributor’s* context determines the truth-value. However, if the attributor’s context is merely a reflection of the subject’s stakes, then the theory has lost its distinctive claim. If the attributor’s context is independent of the subject’s stakes, then we have the arbitrariness problem. Contextualism thus faces a dilemma: either it collapses into IRI, or it becomes an indefensible form of linguistic relativism.

**IV. Begging the Question: The Unjustified Privileging of Ordinary Standards**

The third, and most damaging, objection is that contextualism begs the question against the skeptic. The skeptic’s argument is not merely that we fail to meet a *higher* standard; it is that we fail to meet the *only* legitimate standard for knowledge. The skeptic holds that knowledge requires certainty, or at least the elimination of all logically possible alternatives. The contextualist responds by saying that this is just one standard among many, and that in ordinary contexts, a lower standard is appropriate. But this response assumes the very point at issue: that the ordinary standard is epistemically legitimate—that it is capable of yielding *knowledge* rather than mere true belief or pragmatic acceptance.

The contextualist defines “knowledge” as “meeting the contextually relevant standard.” But this is a deflationary, almost stipulative, definition. It strips the concept of knowledge of its normative force. The skeptic is not interested in whether we use the word “knows” in a loose and pragmatic way; they are interested in whether our beliefs are *justified* in a way that rules out the possibility of massive error. The contextualist assumes that fallible justification—justification that is compatible with the truth of the skeptical hypothesis—is sufficient for knowledge in ordinary contexts. But this is precisely what the skeptic denies. By asserting that the ordinary standard is the correct one for ordinary contexts, the contextualist is assuming that the skeptic’s demand for certainty is misguided or overly stringent. However, they provide no argument for this assumption. They simply appeal to our ordinary linguistic practices.

This is a classic case of begging the question. The contextualist starts with the premise that ordinary knowledge ascriptions are true (e.g., “I know I have hands” is true in ordinary contexts). They then construct a semantic theory that preserves this premise. But the skeptic’s challenge is precisely to question whether that premise is true. The contextualist cannot simply assume that the ordinary context is veridical—that we are not, in fact, BIVs. If we *are* BIVs, then even in the ordinary context, the sentence “I know I have hands” is false, because I do not have hands. The contextualist must assume that the world is as we take it to be to get the result that we have knowledge. But that is the very thing the skeptic doubts. The contextualist is thus assuming the conclusion of the anti-skeptical argument (that we have ordinary knowledge) to build their theory, which then “proves” that conclusion. This is a blatant circularity.

Moreover, the contextualist’s dismissal of the skeptic’s standard as merely “one standard among many” is a form of epistemic imperialism. It assumes that the practical purposes of ordinary life can lower the bar for truth. But knowledge is a theoretical notion that aims at truth, not a pragmatic notion that aims at action-guidance. If a belief is false (e.g., if I am a BIV and my belief that I have hands is false), then no amount of practical convenience can make it knowledge. The contextualist might respond that in the ordinary context, we are not considering the BIV hypothesis, so the belief is not false relative to that context. But truth is not context-relative. The proposition “I have hands” is either true or false simpliciter. If I am a BIV, it is false, and no conversational context can make it true. The contextualist conflates the *truth* of the proposition with the *warranted assertability* of the knowledge claim. They are saying that in the ordinary context, it is *appropriate* to say “I know I have hands,” even if it is not strictly true. But this is a pragmatic concession, not an epistemological solution. It does not answer the skeptic’s question of whether we have justification; it merely changes the subject to a discussion of conversational norms.

By assuming that the ordinary standard is legitimate, the contextualist also assumes that the skeptic’s standard is illegitimate. But the skeptic’s standard—the demand for certainty—is not arbitrary. It is motivated by the intuition that knowledge requires a guarantee against error. The contextualist cannot simply declare this intuition invalid without argument. If the skeptic’s standard is a legitimate candidate for the meaning of “knowledge,” then the contextualist’s claim that we know in ordinary contexts is false. If the skeptic’s standard is not legitimate, then the contextualist must explain why—but they cannot do so without appealing to ordinary practices, which is question-begging. The contextualist is caught in a dilemma: either they accept the skeptic’s standard as legitimate, in which case they lose ordinary knowledge, or they reject it as illegitimate, in which case they must provide a non-question-begging argument for why fallible justification is sufficient for knowledge, which is precisely the argument they fail to provide.

**V. Synthesis: The Hollow Victory of Contextualism**

In assessing contextualism, we must distinguish between its descriptive merits and its normative failures. As a description of how we talk about knowledge, contextualism is remarkably insightful. It accurately captures the way we retract knowledge claims when new error possibilities are raised, and it explains why the skeptical paradox feels so compelling. However, as a *solution* to the skeptical problem, it is a failure. The three objections outlined above demonstrate that contextualism cannot provide a stable foundation for ordinary knowledge.

The first objection shows that contextualism makes ordinary knowledge hostage to conversational whim. The skeptic’s mere utterance of a hypothesis destroys the knowledge that contextualism was supposed to protect. This reveals that the “knowledge” the contextualist grants us in ordinary contexts is not genuine knowledge but a fragile, provisional status that exists only in the absence of skeptical scrutiny.
