# Systematic Justification, Practical Coherence, and the Scope of Rationality

The question whether rational agents must make their desires systematically justifiable lies at the intersection of two influential accounts of practical reason. On one side, Michael Smith develops an ideal of the fully rational agent whose desires are not merely mutually consistent but integrated into a coherent and unified practical outlook. On the other, Bernard Williams argues that reasons are internal to an agent’s “subjective motivational set”: what an agent has reason to do depends on what could be reached through sound deliberation from the motivations the agent already possesses. Williams’s position appears to allow a plurality of independent projects and concerns, provided that they do not generate practical conflict that the agent cannot resolve. Smith’s position, by contrast, seems to imply that the rational agent must aspire to something stronger: specific desires should be intelligible as applications of more general principles or desires, and the agent’s practical outlook should constitute an integrated whole.

The central issue is therefore not simply whether rational agents should avoid inconsistency. It is whether rationality requires a more ambitious form of practical systematization. Must an agent reduce independent, underived preferences to a unified desiderative profile, in something like the way theoretical reasoning seeks to organize beliefs under general principles? Or is a weaker form of coherence—avoiding conflicts, sustaining deliberative stability, and responding appropriately to one’s existing motivations—sufficient?

This essay argues that the stronger requirement is not a necessary condition of rational deliberation. Systematic justification can be an important ideal for some agents, especially when their projects are interconnected or when moral reflection calls for a unified standpoint. But neither Smith’s ideal of the fully rational agent nor Williams’s internal-reasons account establishes that every rational agent must subsume particular desires under general ones. The analogy between theoretical and practical reasoning is illuminating but ultimately limited. Practical rationality must regulate relations among desires, beliefs, intentions, and actions, but it need not transform all desires into expressions of one foundational or unified preference. Consequently, internal reasons need not become non-relativistic merely because rational deliberation involves some degree of systematic justification. Their content may remain relative to an agent’s motivational set while still being subject to objective standards of coherence, deliberative soundness, and consistency.

## I. Two Conceptions of Practical Coherence

It is useful to distinguish two senses of coherence. The weaker sense is primarily negative and procedural. An agent is coherent when her beliefs, desires, intentions, and actions do not stand in practically defeating relations with one another. She does not simultaneously intend incompatible actions, endorse a goal while deliberately frustrating the only available means to it, or treat two considerations as decisive when they cannot both be acted upon. This form of coherence is often connected with instrumental rationality. If an agent wants an end and believes that a certain means is necessary, she has a reason to pursue the means, assuming no stronger countervailing consideration intervenes.

The stronger sense is positive and structural. It requires that the agent’s desires form an intelligible system. Particular desires should be justified by, or at least integrated with, more general desires, values, or principles. An agent who wants to help a particular friend, for example, might understand this desire as an instance of a more general concern for friendship, loyalty, or the welfare of those to whom she is attached. A desire to complete one particular painting might be connected to a more general commitment to artistic creation. The stronger conception seeks not merely the absence of conflict but unity: the agent should be able to explain why her various desires belong together and how they fit into a general account of what she is trying to do with her life.

These two forms of coherence should not be conflated. An agent can possess many desires that do not conflict without being able to derive them from a common principle. A person might care about her children, enjoy gardening, value historical research, and wish to maintain a particular friendship. These concerns may coexist peacefully, but there may be no single general desire from which all of them follow. Their unity could be biographical rather than logical: they belong to one person’s life without being instances of a single value.

Conversely, an agent might possess a highly unified outlook that produces conflict among particular commitments. Someone who is committed to maximizing a general value may face situations in which different applications of that value compete. Unity at the level of principle does not guarantee smoothness at the level of action. Thus stronger systematization and weaker conflict-avoidance are distinct achievements.

The philosophical question is which achievement rationality requires. Smith’s account of fully rational agents appears to favor the stronger conception. Williams’s account of internal reasons more naturally supports the weaker one, though Williams does not treat rationality as mere psychological consistency. His notion of a “sound deliberative route” imposes substantive constraints on what follows from an agent’s motivations. The dispute is therefore not between rationality and irrationality, but between two models of what rationality is for.

## II. Smith and the Fully Rational Agent

Michael Smith’s account begins from a broadly Humean picture of action. Desires motivate agents, while beliefs represent the world and guide the selection of means. A belief alone does not normally motivate; it must be combined with an appropriate desire. Yet Smith also seeks to explain how practical thought can be rational rather than merely causal. If an agent has a desire and a belief about how to satisfy it, there is a rational connection between the desire and the action that would fulfill it. Practical rationality concerns the organization of desires and beliefs in such a way that action is guided by the agent’s reasons.

Smith’s discussion of the fully rational agent is especially important because it treats rationality as involving more than the ability to avoid elementary instrumental mistakes. The fully rational agent is not simply someone whose beliefs are consistent and whose actions efficiently promote whatever desires happen to be strongest. Rather, the agent’s desires themselves must be in an appropriate relation to one another. The ideal agent has desires that are coherent, stable, and capable of surviving reflection. The fully rational standpoint is one from which the agent’s practical commitments are not merely a collection of psychological impulses but a rationally organized set.

This ideal can be interpreted in at least two ways. On a modest interpretation, Smith requires that an agent’s desires be jointly satisfiable and that the agent be disposed to resolve conflicts among them in a rational way. On a stronger interpretation, he requires that the desires be systematically justifiable: the agent should be able to see particular desires as supported by, or integrated into, more general motivational principles. The stronger interpretation is what raises the question of whether fully rational agency requires reducing independent preferences into a unified desiderative profile.

Smith’s analogy with theoretical reasoning encourages this stronger interpretation. In theoretical reasoning, isolated beliefs are not fully rationally satisfactory merely because they are individually plausible. We seek relations of entailment, explanation, and mutual support. We want our beliefs to form a system. An agent who believes that all humans are mortal and that Socrates is human has reason to believe that Socrates is mortal. The rational status of the conclusion is explained by its place in a larger structure of beliefs. Similarly, in practical reasoning, one might think that a desire to perform a particular action should be rationally intelligible in relation to more general desires. If the agent desires the welfare of her children, and believes that taking a particular action will promote that welfare, then her desire to take the action is not arbitrary. It is supported by a broader motivational commitment.

Smith’s model offers an attractive picture of practical unity. It explains why rational agents do not merely respond to desires one at a time. They reflect on what they want, compare their desires, identify conflicts, and determine which commitments should govern their actions. The fully rational agent’s practical outlook is thus something like a structured system rather than a heap of motivational states.

Nevertheless, there is an important ambiguity in the idea that desires must be systematically justified. To say that a desire is justified by a more general desire could mean that the general desire is merely explanatory. A person’s desire to visit a particular museum may be explained by her love of art. But explanation need not amount to rational justification. The particular desire might be perfectly rational even if the agent has no explicit general commitment to art. Alternatively, “justified” might mean that the agent would endorse the particular desire after reflecting on the more general value it serves. This is a stronger claim, but it risks requiring too much self-consciousness from ordinary rational agents.

Moreover, not all desires seem to stand in a hierarchical relation. Some desires may be original components of a life rather than applications of general principles. The desire to spend time with a particular person may arise from the history of the relationship itself. The desire to preserve a specific memory, continue a particular project, or return to a beloved place may not be derived from a general concern that could independently justify it. Such desires can still be intelligible and rationally defensible. Their justification may be historical, relational, or personal rather than systematic in the sense of being subsumed under an abstract general desire.

Smith’s fully rational agent can therefore be understood as setting an ideal of reflection rather than imposing a strict derivational requirement on every desire. Rationality may require that an agent be able to respond to conflicts and revise commitments when reflection exposes incoherence. It does not necessarily require that every desire be generated by a general principle.

## III. Williams and the Subjective Motivational Set

Bernard Williams’s account begins from a different direction. He is skeptical of the idea that reasons for action can be detached from an agent’s actual motivations. His internal-reasons thesis holds, roughly, that an agent has a reason to act only if there is some sound deliberative route from the agent’s existing motivational set to a motivation to perform the action. The “subjective motivational set,” or S, includes desires in a broad sense: concerns, commitments, dispositions of evaluation, projects, emotional attachments, and other motivational elements.

Williams’s use of “desire” is therefore not limited to momentary appetites. A person’s commitment to being a good parent, loyalty to a friend, concern for justice, love of music, or ambition to complete a project may all belong to S. This expansive conception allows Williams to explain why reasons can arise from complex personal commitments rather than only from simple preferences.

The internal-reasons thesis is not the claim that whatever an agent happens to want automatically provides a reason. Williams distinguishes between a mere desire and a sound deliberative route. An agent may be ignorant, confused, subject to false beliefs, or temporarily dominated by an irrational impulse. Deliberation can reveal that a desired end cannot be achieved by a proposed means, that two commitments conflict, or that a purported desire is unstable and unsupported by the agent’s broader motivational outlook. What matters is that the reasoning proceeds from elements of S in a way that is sufficiently sound.

At the same time, Williams does not require S to be a fully unified system. The motivational set can contain a plurality of projects and concerns. Some may be only loosely connected. Some may be inherited from personal history rather than chosen through reflection. Others may conflict, in which case the agent must deliberate about their relative importance. But the mere existence of independent motivations does not show irrationality.

This is one of the crucial differences between Williams’s account and a strong version of systematic justification. For Williams, the source of a reason is not necessarily a general principle that stands above all particular desires. A reason can emerge from a particular project or relationship. If I have undertaken to meet a friend, that commitment can give me a reason to go to the meeting even if I cannot derive it from a general theory of friendship or promise-keeping. The commitment belongs to my motivational set, and deliberation from it can generate a reason for action.

Williams’s position nevertheless includes a form of systematization. Deliberation is not merely the expression of whichever desire happens to be strongest. It involves considering how one motivation bears on another, whether a proposed action coheres with one’s projects, and whether one’s beliefs are accurate. A person who wants to finish a dissertation but repeatedly chooses actions that make completion impossible may be practically irrational, even if the desire is genuine. Similarly, someone who values both friendship and honesty may need to determine how to act when those commitments collide.

The systematization in Williams is local and relational rather than totalizing. It concerns whether a proposed action can be justified from the agent’s motivational set, not whether the entire set can be reduced to one unified principle. Internal reasons are generated through pathways of deliberation. There need not be a single foundational desire at the end of every pathway. The process can terminate in a commitment that is simply part of the agent’s practical identity.

This feature of Williams’s account makes it particularly well suited to pluralism. Human lives contain commitments that are not all commensurable or derivable from one another. A person may care about justice, family, artistic expression, intellectual truth, and personal independence. These concerns may sometimes support one another, but they may also remain distinct. Rational deliberation can still occur among them without reducing them to a common denominator.

## IV. Is Strong Unity Required by Rational Agency?

The strongest argument for systematic justification begins with the thought that rational agency requires self-government. If an agent’s desires are merely independent impulses, then the agent is governed by them rather than governing herself. To be rational is to reflect on what one wants, determine which desires deserve authority, and organize one’s conduct around a coherent practical identity. A unified desiderative profile would seem to provide the basis for this self-government.

There is force in this argument. A person who treats every desire as an isolated command may lack an intelligible conception of what she is doing. She may move from one pursuit to another without any ability to explain her choices. When desires conflict, she may simply oscillate. In such cases, the absence of systematization appears to be a defect of rational agency, not merely an absence of philosophical sophistication.

Strong unity can also support diachronic agency. A rational person is not merely responding to present motivations but shaping a life over time. Long-term projects require subordinating immediate desires to broader commitments. Someone who values becoming a doctor must sometimes resist the desire to avoid difficult study. Someone who values friendship must sometimes sacrifice convenience. These cases suggest that rational agency involves a hierarchy of motivations in which general commitments govern particular choices.

Yet these considerations do not establish that all rational desires must be subsumed under general ones. First, self-government does not require complete unity. It may require only that the agent recognize, negotiate, and manage plurality. A constitutional political order governs diverse interests without reducing them to one interest. Likewise, an individual can exercise rational agency by maintaining several commitments and deciding among them through judgment. The capacity to adjudicate conflicts is not the same as the possession of a single principle from which all commitments derive.

Second, the idea of a unified desiderative profile may be underdeterminate. What counts as the relevant level of generality? If I want to write a particular novel, this may be an instance of a desire to write, to create art, to express myself, to contribute to culture, or to live meaningfully. No single description is uniquely correct. The same desire can be subsumed under several general concerns, and those concerns may themselves be subsumed under broader ones. If systematicity requires a final level of unity, it is unclear where the process should stop.

Third, excessive unity can be a form of irrationality. An agent may force all her motivations into one abstract principle and thereby ignore the distinctive significance of particular relationships or projects. A parent who interprets concern for a child only as an application of a general principle of benevolence may misunderstand the personal character of parental love. A friend who treats loyalty as nothing more than a derivative of a universal moral rule may fail to appreciate why this particular relationship matters. Practical rationality may sometimes require preserving particularity rather than dissolving it into generality.

Fourth, the demand for unity risks confusing rational justification with autobiographical explanation. An agent may be able to tell a coherent story about how her desires fit together without thereby showing that they are rational. Conversely, a desire may be rationally defensible even if it cannot be integrated into a comprehensive story. A person’s attachment to a particular landscape or inherited tradition may be important and reasonable without being derivable from a general value that she accepts independently. Its significance may consist precisely in its particular history.

The stronger conception is therefore best treated as an ideal of reflective integration, not as a universal criterion of rationality. Some agents and some decisions call for broad systematization. But the possibility of rational agency does not depend on achieving complete unity.

## V. The Analogy Between Theoretical and Practical Reasoning

The analogy between theoretical and practical reasoning is central to the debate. In theoretical reasoning, rationality seems to demand organization. Beliefs should be consistent, responsive to evidence, and connected by explanatory or inferential relations. A person who believes that a proposition is true but also believes its negation without recognizing the conflict is irrational. Similarly, a person who accepts a conclusion while rejecting the premises from which it follows may be failing to reason properly.

Why should practical reasoning be different? If beliefs must be systematized, perhaps desires should be systematized as well. Just as an agent’s belief that the premises are true provides a basis for believing the conclusion, a general desire might provide a basis for a particular desire. The desire for a just society could support a desire to oppose a particular injustice. The desire for health could support a desire to exercise. In both cases, rationality appears to involve moving from general commitments to particular judgments.

The analogy has genuine explanatory value. Practical reasoning does exhibit structures analogous to theoretical inference. It contains means-end relations, conditional reasoning, consistency constraints, and relations of support. If an agent intends an end, believes that a means is necessary, and has no defeating reason, then she should intend the means. If she believes that she cannot perform two incompatible actions, she cannot rationally intend both without qualification. If she values a project, she should take its requirements seriously. These are not merely causal regularities; they are rational relations.

However, the analogy breaks down in several respects.

First, beliefs aim at truth, while desires do not aim at truth in the same way. Theoretical rationality is constrained by how the world is. If two beliefs contradict one another, at least one must be rejected because the world cannot simultaneously be as both beliefs represent it to be. Desires, by contrast, are not representations that must correspond to a single order of facts. It is possible to desire both artistic achievement and leisure, or both loyalty to a friend and impartial fairness, without one desire being false. The problem arises only when these desires bear on a common choice or when their joint satisfaction is impossible.

Second, practical values may be plural and non-comparable. Theoretical beliefs can sometimes be integrated through a common standard of truth, even when deciding which belief to abandon is difficult. Practical commitments may resist such integration. The value of friendship and the value of justice are not obviously rival hypotheses awaiting resolution by appeal to a single practical truth. They may be distinct sources of reasons. An agent can rationally recognize both without reducing one to the other.

Third, theoretical systematization often seeks explanatory simplicity, whereas practical life may properly involve differentiated commitments. A scientific theory that explains many phenomena under a few principles is generally preferable to a collection of unrelated hypotheses. But an individual’s practical outlook is not necessarily improved by eliminating all independent values. A rich life may consist in the coexistence of several projects that are valuable for different reasons.

Fourth, practical reasoning is action-guiding in a way theoretical reasoning is not. Theoretical reasoning can remain incomplete while one suspends judgment. Action often cannot be suspended. When two genuine values conflict, an agent may need to choose without being able to show that one is universally more fundamental. Rational choice may therefore involve judgment, prioritization, and acceptance of loss rather than deduction from a unified system.

Finally, practical reasons are partly constituted by commitments and relationships. A promise can generate a reason because one made it; a friendship can generate a reason because it is this friendship; a project can generate reasons because one has undertaken it. These facts are not necessarily instances of general desires in the agent’s psychology. Their normative significance may depend on the particular structure of the agent’s life.

The theoretical analogy thus supports a requirement of local organization but not total reduction. Practical rationality needs enough structure to guide deliberation, but the structure need not be a single hierarchy or deductive system.

## VI. Independent Preferences and the Possibility of Rational Pluralism

The strongest challenge to systematic justification comes from the possibility of independent underived preferences. Suppose an agent values both playing music and caring for an elderly parent. She does not regard either value as an application of the other, nor does she derive both from a further general principle. She simply regards both as important. Is she irrational?

It would be implausible to say so merely because her values are independent. Rationality should not require a person to provide an ultimate justification for every commitment. At some point, practical reasoning must begin from motivations that are not themselves inferred from prior motivations. If every desire required a more general desire to justify it, the process would either continue indefinitely or terminate in an arbitrary foundational desire. The demand for systematic justification cannot eliminate the need for underived commitments; it can only relocate them.

One might respond that a fully rational agent would possess a single ultimate desire, such as a desire for what is best, or a desire to do what she has most reason to do. But this proposal does not solve the problem. If the agent’s ultimate desire is maximally abstract, it may fail to explain why particular relationships and projects matter. If it is substantive, such as maximizing welfare or promoting autonomy, then it imposes a controversial ethical theory on rationality. The concept of rationality would no longer be a general standard of deliberation but a disguised substantive doctrine about what everyone should value.

A pluralist account avoids this result. It can maintain that agents must be responsive to the implications of their commitments without insisting that all commitments share a common source. The person who values both music and parental care can reason instrumentally from each value, recognize when they conflict, and deliberate about priorities in context. She may sometimes decide that the parental commitment takes precedence, but this need not be because parental care is more fundamental in her general theory. It may be because of the urgency of the situation, the history of the relationship, or the specific obligations generated by dependence.

This does not mean that any collection of desires is rational. A pluralist can criticize desires that are incoherent, unstable, based on false beliefs, or incompatible with the agent’s own settled commitments. Nor does pluralism imply that all choices are equally justified. It allows for rational criticism without requiring a single comprehensive scale.

The distinction between independence and isolation is important. An underived preference can still be integrated into a practical life. My love of a particular sport may be underived, but it may fit with my friendships, health goals, and sense of identity. The desire is not derived from those other commitments, yet it is not isolated from them. Practical unity may therefore be achieved through relations of accommodation, mutual support, and narrative continuity rather than strict derivation.

This conception also better captures the role of personal identity. A person’s reasons are often shaped by what she has done, promised, cared about, and become attached to. These elements are not selected from nowhere, but neither are they always chosen by an act of rational construction. Rationality operates within an already developing practical identity. It can revise that identity, but it cannot generate all of its contents from a single principle.

## VII. Internal Reasons and the Question of Relativism

The final issue concerns whether internal reasons must be non-relativistic once systematic justification is required. Williams’s internal reasons are relative to the agent’s subjective motivational set. If an agent has no motivational basis from which a sound deliberative route to an action can proceed, then the agent may lack an internal reason to perform it. This view is sometimes criticized as relativistic because different agents may have different reasons depending on what they care about.

The demand for systematic justification might appear to overcome this relativism. If rationality requires every agent to organize desires under general principles, perhaps the relevant principles must be universal or objective. A reason would then not depend on the contingent contents of S but on standards that apply to all rational agents. The requirement of unity would transform internal reasons into something closer to external reasons.

This inference should be resisted. Systematic justification does not by itself determine the content of the system. An agent can be required to deliberate coherently from her commitments without being required to possess the same commitments as everyone else. The standards of sound deliberation may be non-relative in form while the reasons generated by deliberation remain relative in content.

For example, suppose two agents have different projects. One is committed to caring for a child, while the other is not a parent. The first has reasons to arrange her schedule around the child’s needs that the second does not possess. This difference need not reflect a failure of universal rationality. Both agents can be subject to the same formal standards: accurate belief, consistency, attention to means, responsiveness to relevant information, and willingness to revise incoherent commitments. Yet the reasons they have may differ because their lives and motivational sets differ.

The same point applies even if we adopt a stronger conception of systematization. An agent might organize her desires around a commitment to artistic creation, another around scientific inquiry, and another around family life. Their practical outlooks can each exhibit coherence without converging on one universal desire. Systematicity is a property of the relations among an agent’s motivations, not necessarily a requirement that all agents possess identical motivations.

There are, however, limits to the relativism Williams’s view can permit. If an agent’s motivational set is radically defective—for example, if it is formed by manipulation, ignorance, or self-destructive compulsion—then deliberation from it may not yield a genuine reason. Williams’s idea of a sound deliberative route allows criticism of an agent’s motivational set without appealing to a fully external moral standpoint. Some motivations may be excluded because they are unstable, dependent on false beliefs, or incapable of surviving informed reflection. This introduces standards that are not simply whatever the agent currently happens to want.

Likewise, an agent’s practical reasoning must respect certain general constraints. She cannot simultaneously regard two incompatible actions as required in the same respect. She cannot rationally claim to value a project while denying all of its necessary conditions. She must recognize relevant facts and revise beliefs when evidence demands it. These constraints are non-relativistic in the sense that they apply to rational agents as such. But they do not entail that the final reasons generated by deliberation are independent of the agent’s motivational set.

The best conclusion is therefore a layered one. Formal standards of rational deliberation are not wholly relative. They govern all agents. Substantive reasons, however, may remain internal and agent-relative because they arise from different projects, commitments, and attachments. The requirement of systematic justification, understood moderately, does not erase this distinction.

## VIII. A Reconciliation of Smith and Williams

The apparent opposition between Smith and Williams can be reduced by distinguishing two roles played by rationality. Smith emphasizes the ideal of an agent whose practical outlook is reflectively stable and unified. Williams emphasizes the conditions under which reasons can guide an agent from within her actual motivational standpoint. Both perspectives capture something important.

Smith is right that practical rationality cannot be reduced to the causal strength of desires. Agents should be able to reflect on their motivations, identify contradictions, understand the implications of their commitments, and form stable intentions. A person whose desires continually defeat one another, or whose actions systematically undermine her own projects, falls short of rational agency. Some aspiration toward integration is essential.

Williams is right that integration need not mean reduction to a single general desire. Practical thought begins from a motivational set that contains particular concerns, personal commitments, and historically shaped attachments. Deliberation can be rational when it proceeds from these elements through sound reasoning. It need not reconstruct the agent’s entire motivational life under one ultimate principle.

A moderate synthesis would say that rationality requires systematization in three senses.

First, there must be **logical and practical consistency**. The agent should not hold incompatible beliefs or intentions without recognizing the conflict.

Second, there must be **instrumental and deliberative connectivity**. The agent should be able to see how actions bear on her ends and how particular choices express, support, or threaten her commitments.

Third, there must be **reflective stability**. When the agent becomes aware of conflicts or false assumptions, she should be capable of revising her desires, beliefs, or priorities rather than simply ignoring the problem.

None of these requirements entails that every particular desire be subsumed under a general desire. They permit a pluralistic structure in which some motivations are foundational for the agent, others are derived, and still others are related through history, identity, or mutual accommodation. The practical system can be organized without being unified in the strongest sense.

This moderate position also preserves the value of the fully rational ideal. A fully rational agent might indeed seek more comprehensive unity than ordinary rationality requires. She might examine how her projects fit together, whether her commitments express a coherent conception of a good life, and whether some desires should be abandoned because they cannot be reconciled with her deepest concerns. Such reflection can be admirable and sometimes necessary. But an ideal of maximal integration should not be confused with the minimum conditions for rational deliberation.

## Conclusion

Making desires systematically justifiable is not a necessary component of rational deliberation if systematic justification means reducing every particular desire to a set of general, ultimately unified principles. Michael Smith’s fully rational agent rightly highlights the importance of reflective coherence, stable commitments, and the rational organization of desire. But the ideal of a fully integrated practical outlook does not show that rationality requires the elimination of independent or underived preferences. Bernard Williams’s subjective motivational set offers a more plausible account of the plurality from which deliberation actually proceeds. It allows reasons to arise from projects, relationships, and commitments that are not all derivable from a single general value, while still requiring sound deliberative connections among motivations, beliefs, and actions.

The analogy between theoretical and practical reasoning supports some systematization but not complete unity. Beliefs are constrained by a common aim of truth, whereas desires can embody multiple values that are neither reducible to one another nor fully comparable. Practical rationality therefore requires coherence, but coherence can be pluralistic. It can consist in the capacity to coordinate, prioritize, revise, and act upon diverse commitments rather than in the derivation of all commitments from one foundational desire.

Finally, internal reasons need not be non-relativistic merely because rational deliberation is subject to systematic standards. The standards governing sound reasoning—consistency, accuracy, instrumental adequacy, and reflective stability—may apply generally. But the reasons generated through deliberation can remain relative to an agent’s motivational set. Rationality need not require that all agents value the same things; it requires that agents reason responsibly from the things they value, while remaining open to criticism and revision.

The rational agent, then, need not be a perfectly unified will. She may instead be a reflective pluralist: someone whose many concerns are sufficiently connected to guide action, sufficiently stable to constitute a life, and sufficiently open to revision to count as rational, without being reduced to a single comprehensive desiderative system.
