# From Pure Act to Possible Becoming: Hartshorne, Kant, and the Modal Ontological Argument

The ontological argument attempts to establish God’s existence from the concept of God alone. Unlike cosmological or teleological arguments, it does not begin with an empirical feature of the world and reason toward a transcendent cause. It begins with a definition, description, or idea of the greatest conceivable being and asks whether that concept entails its own instantiation. St. Anselm’s classical formulation claims that a being than which none greater can be conceived must exist not merely in the understanding but in reality, because existence in reality is greater than existence in the understanding alone. Immanuel Kant famously objected that existence is not a predicate or perfection that can be added to a concept. A hundred actual thalers do not contain more conceptual predicates than a hundred possible thalers; they contain more reality, but not a greater set of properties. Thus, Kant concluded, no analysis of a concept can by itself establish that anything corresponding to it exists.

Charles Hartshorne’s revision of the ontological argument seeks to preserve its central insight while avoiding the crude claim that existence is an ordinary property. Hartshorne shifts the argument from existence as a first-order predicate to existence as a modal status. The decisive question is no longer whether a perfect being possesses the property of existence in addition to other properties, but whether a perfect being would exist necessarily rather than contingently. If God is a being whose existence is either impossible or necessary, and if God’s existence is possible, then God must exist necessarily. Hartshorne therefore transforms the ontological argument into a modal argument concerning necessity, possibility, and maximal perfection.

This revision has considerable philosophical importance. It recognizes the force of Kant’s objection and attempts to answer it without abandoning the a priori character of the argument. It also permits Hartshorne to revise the traditional doctrine of God as *actus purus*, or pure actuality. The God of classical theism is immutable, timeless, absolutely simple, and without unrealized potentiality. Hartshorne, by contrast, understands God as possessing both an abstract, necessary pole and a concrete, changing pole in relation to the world. God’s abstract nature is eternal and unsurpassable, while God’s concrete experience develops as the world develops. This dipolar conception allows Hartshorne to combine divine necessity with divine becoming.

The central issue, however, is whether this combination genuinely resolves the difficulties in Anselm’s argument and in the classical concept of God. Does modal coextensiveness successfully avoid Kant’s critique? Does it dissolve the internal tensions generated by the idea of *actus purus*? And does the assumption that God’s existence is possible supply a legitimate bridge between an a priori concept of perfection and the necessary existence of a perfect being? The answer is qualified. Hartshorne’s revision is a powerful clarification of the logical structure of the ontological argument, and his rejection of pure actuality exposes real tensions in classical theism. Nevertheless, modal coextensiveness does not itself prove possibility, and potentiality, although it helps explain divine becoming, cannot by itself convert an abstract concept into a concrete existent. Hartshorne succeeds more as a critic and reformer of the classical argument than as someone who decisively establishes its conclusion.

## Anselm’s Argument and the Problem of Existence

In the *Proslogion*, Anselm defines God as “that than which nothing greater can be conceived.” Anselm’s argument depends upon distinguishing between existing merely in the understanding and existing in reality. Even a person who denies God’s existence can understand the expression “a being than which none greater can be conceived.” The concept is therefore present in the mind. Anselm then argues that a being existing in reality is greater than a similar being existing only in the understanding. If the greatest conceivable being existed only in the understanding, one could conceive of a greater being, namely, the same being existing in reality. That would contradict the definition of God. Therefore, God must exist in reality.

Anselm later presents a stronger version of the argument. A being that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than a being that can be conceived not to exist. God, as the greatest conceivable being, must therefore possess necessary existence. Anselm’s mature formulation is not simply that God happens to exist, but that God exists in such a way that nonexistence is impossible. This second form is especially important for Hartshorne, because it permits the ontological argument to be expressed in modal terms.

The classical argument, however, appears to treat existence as a perfection. Anselm’s comparison between a being existing in the understanding and the same being existing in reality suggests that reality adds greatness. Kant’s critique directly attacks this move. A concept of one hundred actual coins and a concept of one hundred possible coins contain the same determinate features: number, shape, weight, and so forth. The difference is not a further predicate within the concept. Rather, in the case of the actual coins, there is something corresponding to the concept. Existence does not enlarge the concept; it posits the object falling under it.

Kant’s criticism is not merely that existence is not a contingent property like color or size. His deeper point is that existence is not a property of an object in the same way at all. To say that something exists is to say that the concept has an instance. One cannot move from the analysis of a concept to the assertion that an instance exists without some additional premise. The concept of a perfect being may include omniscience, omnipotence, goodness, and necessary existence, but it does not follow that anything exemplifies the concept.

Kant’s argument creates a serious difficulty for Anselm. If necessary existence is simply treated as another perfection, the argument seems to repeat the mistake of treating existence as a predicate. If it is not treated as a predicate, then the defender of the ontological argument must explain why a concept of necessary existence has existential force.

## Hartshorne’s Modal Revision

Hartshorne’s main contribution is to reformulate the argument in terms of necessity and possibility. Instead of saying that existence is a perfection added to a concept, he distinguishes between contingent existence and necessary existence. A contingent being exists but could fail to exist. A necessary being exists in every possible circumstance or possible world. The difference between these is modal rather than merely descriptive.

For Hartshorne, divine perfection is not exhausted by possessing a collection of excellent properties. It includes a mode of existence that is unsurpassable. A being that exists contingently can be imagined absent, whereas a necessary being cannot. If necessary existence is a genuine mode of superiority, then a God who exists necessarily would be greater than a God who exists only contingently. Hartshorne thereby preserves the intuition that divine perfection includes a special relation to existence while avoiding the claim that existence is a simple first-order predicate.

The argument can be represented roughly as follows:

1. God is, by definition, a maximally perfect or unsurpassable being.
2. A maximally perfect being would not exist merely contingently.
3. Therefore, if God exists, God exists necessarily.
4. God’s existence is possible.
5. If it is possible that a necessary being exists, then a necessary being exists.
6. Therefore, God exists necessarily, and hence actually.

The crucial modal principle is usually associated with the logic of possible worlds. If it is possible that there exists a necessarily existing being, then there is a possible world in which such a being exists necessarily. But if the being exists necessarily in that world, it exists in every possible world, including the actual world. In modal notation, the relevant inference is:

\[
\Diamond \Box G \rightarrow \Box G
\]

In systems such as S5, this inference is valid. Here, “possibly necessarily” entails “necessarily.” The argument therefore does not infer existence merely from the concept of God. It infers necessary existence from the possibility of necessary existence.

This is a substantial improvement over the naïve form of Anselm’s argument. Kant’s objection that existence is not a predicate does not directly refute the proposition that something exists necessarily. Necessity is not being added as an ordinary attribute alongside wisdom or power. Rather, the argument concerns the modal scope of the existential claim. The question is whether the statement “God exists” is contingent or necessary.

Hartshorne’s strategy can also be described in terms of modal coextensiveness. The predicates “divinely perfect” and “necessarily existent” are said to be coextensive, at least within the relevant modal framework. A being that is genuinely divine cannot be merely contingent, because contingent existence would be a limitation incompatible with maximality. Conversely, if a necessarily existent being is possible, it cannot fail to be actual. The identity of divine perfection and necessary existence is therefore not a simple identity between two ordinary properties. It is a modal relationship between a concept of unsurpassability and a condition of existence.

Yet modal coextensiveness must be carefully distinguished from proof. To say that two concepts are coextensive is not by itself to show that either concept has an instance. “Unicorn” and “horned horse” may be coextensive in a certain conceptual context without there being any unicorns. Likewise, even if “maximally perfect being” and “necessarily existent being” coincide in meaning, this does not establish that such a being is possible. The argument’s force depends upon the additional premise that the concept is not only intelligible but metaphysically possible.

## Does Hartshorne Answer Kant?

Hartshorne answers one version of Kant’s criticism but not all of it. He successfully points out that the modal ontological argument does not need to treat existence as an ordinary predicate. It does not say that a being becomes greater by acquiring the property “exists,” as though existence were analogous to wisdom or strength. Instead, it says that if the divine concept is instantiated, its mode of instantiation must be necessary rather than contingent.

This is a genuine logical advance. The modal argument is not vulnerable to the simple objection that one cannot define something into existence by adding existence to its definition. The argument’s conclusion is not generated by the mere addition of an existential predicate. It depends on the relation between possibility and necessity.

However, Kant’s deeper objection remains relevant. The modal argument still needs to get from a concept to an instance. The shift from “existence is a perfection” to “necessary existence is a modal status” does not eliminate the need for an existential premise. It relocates the controversial premise. Instead of assuming that existence is a perfection, Hartshorne assumes that the existence of a perfect being is possible. But this assumption may contain much of the conclusion in compressed form.

Consider the difference between the following claims:

- It is possible that a being with certain excellent properties exists.
- It is possible that a necessarily existing being exists.
- It is possible that God, understood as maximally perfect, exists.

The first claim may be relatively weak. One can perhaps imagine a being with great knowledge and power. The second is much stronger. It states that a being whose existence cannot fail is itself possible. The third depends on how divine perfection is defined. If necessary existence is built into the concept of God, then saying that God is possible already means that necessary existence is possible. The argument then yields actuality by the modal principle, but only because the essential possibility premise has been granted.

This is not necessarily circular, but it is not innocuous. The defender must show that the concept of necessary existence is coherent and metaphysically possible. Merely failing to detect a contradiction in the concept may not be sufficient. There is a difference between logical consistency, conceptual intelligibility, and metaphysical possibility. A concept can be linguistically meaningful while failing to correspond to any genuine possibility. The concept of a “round square” is explicitly contradictory, but not every impossibility is so transparent. The philosophical problem is whether the divine attributes, taken together, form a coherent possibility.

Hartshorne sometimes treats the alternative as a stark disjunction: God is either impossible or necessary. If the divine concept is coherent, then God cannot be contingent, because contingency would be inconsistent with maximality. But the conclusion still depends upon the rejection of impossibility. One cannot simply infer that possibility follows from conceivability. A perfect being may be conceivable in the weak sense that a sentence can be formed and understood, while remaining metaphysically impossible because its attributes conflict.

Thus, Hartshorne’s argument successfully avoids Kant’s narrow criticism but not Kant’s broader warning. No conceptual analysis alone can establish that reality contains an instance of the concept unless possibility is independently warranted. The modal argument is valid under its premises, but its crucial premise is not a theorem of logic.

## The Internal Tensions of *Actus Purus*

The classical doctrine of God as *actus purus* originates in Aristotelian and scholastic metaphysics. Finite beings are composites of actuality and potentiality. They possess some realized features and some unrealized capacities. Change occurs when a potential becomes actual. A thing becomes warm because it has the potential to be warm and that potential is actualized. Pure actuality, by contrast, would contain no unrealized potentiality. God, as pure act, would therefore be wholly actual, immutable, timeless, and metaphysically complete.

This doctrine has a powerful rationale. If God had unactualized potentials, God would depend upon something that actualizes those potentials. A perfect being should not depend upon an external cause. Moreover, change seems to imply movement from a lesser state to a greater state, or at least the loss of one state and acquisition of another. If God can become better, God was previously less than perfect; if God can become worse, God is subject to loss. Pure actuality therefore seems to protect divine perfection from limitation, dependence, and instability.

At the same time, the doctrine generates serious internal tensions. The first concerns divine freedom and creation. If God is immutable and fully actual, how can God freely create a world? A decision to create seems to involve a transition from not creating to creating. If the divine will is eternally and immutably directed toward creation, then creation may appear necessary rather than free. If the will changes, then God is not immutable. Classical theology responds by distinguishing between God’s eternal act and the temporal effect, but the distinction can seem to preserve the difficulty rather than eliminate it.

A second tension concerns knowledge. If God knows a changing world, does God’s knowledge change as the world changes? If God acquires new information, God has potentiality and is not pure act. If God’s knowledge does not change, then it becomes difficult to explain how God knows temporal events as temporal. Classical thinkers often maintain that God knows all temporal events in one timeless act. This protects immutability but raises questions about whether timeless knowledge can genuinely be knowledge of becoming.

A third tension concerns relationality. Love, response, sympathy, and personal interaction seem to involve receptivity. To love another is not merely to possess a static perfection; it is to be affected by the other. But a being incapable of being affected may seem incapable of entering into genuine reciprocal relations. The God of pure actuality can cause the world, but the world cannot causally affect God. This asymmetry may be compatible with metaphysical independence, but it sits uneasily with the personal language of prayer, compassion, and divine response.

A fourth tension concerns the relation between perfection and activity. If all possibility has already been actualized in God, divine activity cannot introduce anything genuinely new into divine life. God may cause new things in the world, but nothing new occurs in God. This raises a question about whether the God of *actus purus* is active in a meaningful sense or is instead a static metaphysical principle.

Hartshorne’s process-oriented theology emerges partly from these difficulties. He argues that the traditional concept of absolute perfection confuses two different ideals. One ideal is independence, permanence, and immunity from loss. The other is responsiveness, growth, novelty, and the capacity to receive value. A being that is perfect in the first sense may be deficient in the second. Hartshorne therefore distinguishes between an abstract or necessary aspect of God and a concrete or contingent aspect. God’s abstract nature is unsurpassable and necessary, while God’s concrete experience changes through relationship with the world.

## Modal Coextensiveness and the Classical Contradictions

Can modal coextensiveness resolve the tensions in *actus purus*? It can clarify one issue but cannot solve all of them. If divine perfection and necessary existence are coextensive, then it follows that God cannot be contingent in the same way ordinary beings are contingent. This gives a coherent account of divine necessity. It also allows Hartshorne to preserve some version of classical transcendence: the divine nature is not dependent upon the world for its basic identity.

But modal coextensiveness concerns the relation between concepts or properties. It does not explain how a necessary being can be related to a changing world, nor does it resolve the tension between immutability and responsiveness. The fact that God necessarily exists does not entail that God is immutable. Necessity of existence and necessity of every state are distinct claims. A being may necessarily exist while undergoing contingent changes in its concrete experience. Hartshorne exploits precisely this distinction.

The distinction may be expressed as follows. God’s abstract essence is necessary: in every possible world, God possesses the fundamental structure that makes God divine. God’s concrete states, however, depend upon the history of the world and may differ from one possible world to another. God necessarily exists, but God does not necessarily have this particular experience, this particular relationship, or this particular consequent state. Divine necessity therefore applies to the identity and existence of God, while contingency applies to the concrete content of divine life.

This strategy does address some contradictions in *actus purus*. It permits God to know a changing world through genuinely changing experience. It allows divine love to include receptivity and response. It preserves freedom by denying that every concrete feature of divine action is fixed by an immutable decree. It also provides a way to understand creation as a real relationship rather than as a merely temporal effect of a timeless act.

Nevertheless, the success is conditional. Hartshorne’s God is no longer the God of absolute pure actuality. The divine concrete pole includes potentiality, change, and dependence upon the world. This may be philosophically attractive, but it is not a reconciliation of process theism with classical theism in the strict sense. It is a replacement of one account of divine perfection with another.

The question then becomes whether Hartshorne’s modified concept remains genuinely maximal. Classical theism defines perfection partly through complete actuality and independence. Hartshorne defines perfection through both permanence and capacity for enrichment. He argues that an inability to experience new value is not a perfection but a limitation. The classical God has no unrealized potential, but for Hartshorne this means that the classical God cannot receive anything and cannot genuinely participate in the world’s becoming. Hartshorne’s God is unsurpassable in abstract structure but capable of concrete growth.

This conception involves a subtle reinterpretation of “greater than.” A being is not simply greater because it is more independent or less affected. In some respects, vulnerability and receptivity are positive values. A being capable of appreciating beauty, responding to suffering, and participating in relationships may be greater than an entity sealed against all change. Hartshorne therefore rejects the assumption that maximal perfection must be identical with maximal actuality in the Aristotelian sense.

Whether this succeeds depends on the theory of value one adopts. If independence is the supreme perfection, process theism compromises divine greatness. If relational richness and capacity for experience are also intrinsic goods, pure actuality may itself appear deficient. Modal coextensiveness does not decide between these competing standards. It can show that, given a particular account of perfection, necessary existence follows. It cannot determine which account of perfection is correct.

## Abstract Being and Concrete Becoming

The distinction between abstract being and concrete becoming is central to evaluating Hartshorne’s revision. An abstract concept is a structure of meaning. It specifies conditions that something would satisfy if it existed. A concept of a perfect being may include unlimited knowledge, goodness, power, and necessary existence. In this sense, the concept belongs to the order of abstract being. It is not yet a concrete event, substance, or personal reality.

Concrete becoming belongs to a different order. It involves temporal process, actual relations, causal interactions, and the realization of possibilities. A concrete being does not merely have a definition; it has a history. It undergoes changes and enters into relations. If the ontological argument begins with an abstract concept, it must explain how the concept relates to concrete reality.

Classical rationalism tends to minimize this gap. It treats the necessary structure of the concept as already carrying existential force. If necessary existence belongs to the essence of God, then God cannot fail to exist. Hartshorne’s process metaphysics, by contrast, emphasizes that concrete reality is not exhausted by abstract description. The divine abstract nature may be necessary, but God’s concrete existence is actualized through a living relation with the world.

Hartshorne’s dipolar conception can therefore be interpreted as an attempt to bridge abstract being and concrete becoming. God’s abstract pole supplies identity, necessity, order, and eternal value. God’s concrete pole supplies actuality, experience, responsiveness, and temporal development. Neither pole is sufficient alone. A purely abstract God would be an eternal possibility without concrete life; a purely concrete God would be dependent and transient. The divine reality consists in the unity of necessary abstract structure and contingent concrete experience.

This is a philosophically productive model, but it raises a question about what exactly the ontological argument establishes. Suppose the argument proves, or at least plausibly establishes, that a necessary divine nature is possible. Does that establish the existence of a concrete personal God who experiences the world? Not automatically. The modal conclusion may establish that some necessarily existing entity exemplifies a certain abstract structure. It does not by itself establish the full concrete content of Hartshorne’s God.

For example, if “God” is defined as a necessary abstract order of value, the ontological argument may establish something close to the necessary existence of that order, assuming its possibility. But Hartshorne’s God is not merely an abstract order. God is also a concrete experiencer who receives the world into divine life. The existence of the abstract pole does not logically entail the existence of a concrete pole unless one has an additional metaphysical principle: namely, that the necessary divine nature necessarily seeks or requires concrete realization in a world of becoming.

Hartshorne can argue that abstract and concrete poles are inseparable. A God without a concrete pole would be incomplete, because value requires realization and relational experience. Yet this claim is not a consequence of modal logic. It is a substantive metaphysical thesis about the nature of actuality, value, and divine life. The ontological argument may be compatible with it, but it does not prove it by itself.

## Can Potentiality Bridge the Gap?

Potentiality appears to offer a bridge between abstract perfection and concrete existence. A possibility is not yet an actuality, but it is oriented toward realization. In ordinary metaphysics, a seed has the potential to become a tree, and the tree is the actualization of that potential. Perhaps, then, the concept of a perfect being contains a potential for existence that can be actualized through modal reasoning.

This analogy is limited. The potentiality of a seed is grounded in an existing concrete subject. There is already a seed that can become a tree. The possibility of God’s existence, by contrast, is not the potentiality of an already existing object unless one has already granted some divine reality. The concept of God is not a metaphysical seed in the same sense. A concept can represent a possibility without possessing an internal power to realize itself.

Moreover, potentiality ordinarily requires an actualizing condition. If a possibility becomes actual, something explains the transition. In a finite process, the explanation may be a cause, a set of conditions, or the operation of natural powers. But in the ontological argument, no external actualizer is available without undermining the argument’s aim of establishing God as necessary and self-sufficient. The transition from possible God to actual God cannot be understood as a temporal becoming, because God’s existence is supposed to be necessary and not an event occurring at a particular moment.

Hartshorne’s process metaphysics treats potentiality differently. Potentiality is not merely a deficiency waiting to be eliminated. It is part of the structure of becoming and creativity. Novelty emerges as possibilities are realized in concrete occasions. God provides an ordering and persuasive principle for these possibilities, but God does not unilaterally determine every event. This offers a richer account of how abstract value can enter concrete history. Divine perfection can be an ideal lure rather than a completed static state.

Still, divine potentiality does not prove divine actuality. It explains how a God who is already actual can participate in becoming; it does not explain why the abstract concept of such a God must have a concrete instance. Potentiality presupposes some field in which potentials are operative. It cannot, without further argument, generate an existent from an idea.

The same point applies to possibility in modal logic. “Possibly God exists” is not equivalent to “God is potentially actual in a process.” Logical possibility concerns what could be the case; potentiality concerns powers, tendencies, or unrealized capacities within a metaphysical system. The two notions can be related but should not be conflated. A proposition may be possible without there being a process that brings it about. Conversely, a power may be present in an existing entity even if its exercise is blocked by circumstances.

The premise that God’s existence is possible must therefore be defended at the level of metaphysical coherence. One must ask whether the divine attributes can coexist, whether necessary existence is itself a coherent modality, and whether the abstract and concrete poles can belong to one being without contradiction. Potentiality can illuminate the transition from abstract value to concrete realization once a divine reality is granted, but it does not independently establish that reality.

## The Possibility Premise and Its Burden

The claim that God’s existence is possible is often presented as modest. It may seem easier to affirm possibility than actuality. Yet in the modal ontological argument, it bears a heavy burden. If God is defined as necessarily existent, then “God is possible” means that a necessarily existent being is possible. This is not merely the possibility that some powerful or intelligent entity exists. It is the possibility of an entity whose nonexistence is impossible.

The difficulty can be seen in the distinction between positive and negative concepts. It is not enough to say that the divine concept has no obvious contradiction. The argument needs a reason to believe that the concept corresponds to a genuine metaphysical possibility. This may involve an analysis of divine attributes, a theory of essence, or a broader account of reality in which necessary beings are permitted.

Hartshorne’s process view may improve the plausibility of the concept by rejecting some of the apparent contradictions of classical theism. If God is not both absolutely immutable and fully responsive, if God does not possess exhaustive unilateral control while also allowing creaturely freedom, and if divine knowledge is not timelessly fixed in a way that excludes genuine novelty, then the concept may be more coherent. The process God may therefore be more possible than the God of strict *actus purus*.

But increased coherence is not proof. One may argue that Hartshorne’s God is possible because the divine attributes are distributed across abstract and concrete poles. The abstract pole is necessary; the concrete pole is contingent and changing. This solves certain contradictions by distinguishing different senses of divine perfection. Yet it also creates new questions. Can a being be necessary if its concrete existence depends upon a contingent world? Is the concrete pole an essential part of God or merely an accidental aspect? If it is essential, then the existence of God may depend upon the existence of a world. If it is accidental, then the God who exists necessarily may not be the relational God described by process theology.

Hartshorne can respond that God necessarily has some concrete experience because some world or process is necessary, even if no particular world is necessary. But this introduces a broader cosmological thesis: that reality as a whole, or the existence of some world, is necessary. The ontological argument alone does not establish this. Alternatively, Hartshorne may maintain that God’s concrete pole is necessary as a capacity for relationship, while the particular contents of that relation are contingent. This distinction is more plausible, but it means that only the capacity, not the realized history, is necessary.

The possibility premise thus remains the point at which the argument is most vulnerable. A critic can accept the validity of the inference from possible necessary existence to necessary existence while denying that the antecedent has been established. This is analogous to accepting the validity of an argument while rejecting its premises. The modal reformulation protects the argument from formal invalidity, but it does not compel assent to the metaphysical picture.

## Modal Coextensiveness: Resolution or Restatement?

Modal coextensiveness successfully resolves the problem of treating existence as a simple predicate only in a restricted sense. It shows that one can understand divine necessity without imagining existence as a perfection that increases the descriptive content of a concept. It also makes explicit the distinction between contingent and necessary beings. In that respect, Hartshorne’s revision is philosophically superior to the most literal version of Anselm’s reasoning.

However, modal coextensiveness does not by itself resolve the internal contradictions of *actus purus*. The contradictions concern the relation between divine necessity, immutability, knowledge, freedom, relationality, and becoming. Modal relations alone cannot decide whether a necessary being may change, whether a timeless entity can respond, or whether a perfect being should be receptive to value. These are questions in metaphysics and philosophy of religion, not merely in modal logic.

Hartshorne’s answer is not to preserve pure actuality but to revise the meaning of perfection. He holds that necessary existence belongs to the abstract divine nature, while concrete divine experience includes becoming. The result is a God who is necessary without being wholly immutable, perfect without being devoid of potentiality, and eternal without being isolated from time. This is a coherent alternative to classical theism if one accepts the distinction between abstract and concrete poles.

The cost, however, is substantial. Hartshorne’s God is not omnipotent in the classical sense, since God cannot unilaterally determine creaturely decisions. God is not immutable in the strict sense, since the divine concrete experience changes. God is not wholly independent of the world, since the world contributes to the concrete content of divine life. Whether these are defects or virtues depends upon the standard of perfection. Hartshorne argues that omnipotence understood as unilateral control is not intrinsically superior to persuasive power, and that immunity from change is not superior to the ability to receive and integrate value. His revisions are therefore principled, not merely evasive.

The deeper philosophical question is whether “perfect being” has a fixed meaning independent of theological construction. If perfection means maximal possession of every great-making attribute as traditionally conceived, then Hartshorne’s God may not qualify. If perfection means unsurpassability relative to a balanced set of values, including relationality and responsiveness, then the classical God may appear conceptually impoverished. The ontological argument cannot settle this dispute because it depends upon the prior definition of perfection.

## Conclusion

Hartshorne’s revision of the ontological argument represents a significant response to Kant. By translating the argument into modal terms, Hartshorne avoids the claim that existence is an ordinary predicate that can simply be added to a concept. The relevant distinction is between contingent and necessary existence. If a maximally perfect being is possible and if maximal perfection entails necessary existence, then the existence of such a being follows within an appropriate modal system. The argument is therefore formally stronger and conceptually more sophisticated than Anselm’s original comparison between existence in the understanding and existence in reality.

Yet the argument’s central assumption remains that God’s existence is possible. This assumption is not trivial. If “God” includes necessary existence, then the possibility premise already asserts the possibility of a necessarily existent being. The modal inference from possible necessity to necessary actuality may be valid, but the premise requires an independent defense. Conceptual intelligibility, absence of obvious contradiction, and imaginative conceivability may support the premise, but they do not conclusively establish metaphysical possibility.

Hartshorne’s distinction between abstract being and concrete becoming offers a valuable way to understand divine existence after Kant. The abstract pole of God can be necessary, eternal, and unsurpassable, while the concrete pole can be temporal, relational, and capable of development. This distinction addresses genuine tensions in the classical doctrine of *actus purus*. It explains how God might be responsive to the world, possess changing knowledge of temporal events, and participate in genuine relations without surrendering all divine necessity.

Nevertheless, modal coextensiveness does not by itself solve the contradictions of pure actuality. It provides a framework within which those contradictions can be avoided, but only by changing the classical meaning of divine perfection. Nor does potentiality adequately bridge the gap between an a priori concept and concrete existence. Potentiality explains becoming within an already existing reality; it does not possess the power to instantiate an abstract concept without further metaphysical principles.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore neither that Hartshorne proves the existence of God nor that his revision is philosophically empty. He demonstrates that Kant’s objection does not eliminate every possible form of ontological reasoning. Necessary existence is not an ordinary predicate, and modal logic can produce a valid argument from possible necessary existence to necessary existence. Hartshorne also shows that the classical ideal of *actus purus* is not the only model of perfection and may contain tensions between immutability and relationship, or between completeness and responsiveness.

But the argument remains conditional. If a coherent concept of a necessarily existent, maximally perfect being is metaphysically possible, then its existence follows under the relevant modal logic. Hartshorne’s process theology may make such a concept more coherent by distinguishing necessary abstract being from contingent concrete becoming. It does not, however, transform possibility into actuality by potentiality alone. The bridge between perfection and existence must still be supplied by a substantive theory of modality, value, and divine reality. Hartshorne’s achievement lies less in proving that the bridge has been crossed than in showing where the bridge must be built.
