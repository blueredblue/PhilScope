**The Unstable Equilibrium: Why Compromise Platonism Fails to Resolve the Intrinsic-Extrinsic Dilemma**

The philosophy of mathematics is haunted by a peculiar dual allegiance. On one hand, mathematics appears to be the paradigm of a priori knowledge, a discipline whose truths are discovered through pure reason rather than empirical investigation. On the other hand, mathematics is undeniably the language of science, an indispensable tool without which physics, economics, and engineering would be inconceivable. This tension has given rise to two dominant, yet deeply flawed, realist positions. The first, associated with Kurt Gödel, grounds mathematical truth in a quasi-perceptual faculty of mathematical intuition, thereby securing a robust ontology of abstract objects but at the cost of a mysterious and epistemically opaque access to them. The second, associated with W.V.O. Quine and Hilary Putnam, grounds mathematical truth in the holistic confirmation of our best scientific theories, thereby naturalizing mathematical knowledge but at the cost of reducing mathematical objects to mere posits of scientific convenience, vulnerable to underdetermination and lacking the unique, categorical character Platonism demands.

In response to this bifurcation, a third position has emerged—one that I shall call *compromise Platonism*. Its most sophisticated proponent is Penelope Maddy, who, across her career, has attempted to synthesize the intrinsic justifications of the Gödelian tradition with the extrinsic justifications of the Quine/Putnam tradition. Maddy’s project is noble: she seeks to preserve the realist commitment to a mind-independent mathematical reality while simultaneously grounding our knowledge of that reality in a scientifically respectable, naturalistic methodology. However, as this essay will argue, compromise Platonism is an inherently unstable philosophical equilibrium. When faced with a direct conflict between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence—a conflict starkly exemplified by the historical case of the parallel postulate and the contemporary case of the Axiom of Choice—the compromise Platonist is forced into a trilemma. She must either side with intrinsic support, thereby collapsing into the epistemic mysticism of Gödelian Platonism; side with extrinsic support, thereby collapsing into the holistic instrumentalism of Quine/Putnam Platonism; or abandon bivalent truth values for mathematical statements, thereby relinquishing the core realist commitment that motivated the compromise in the first place. In each case, the compromise Platonist succumbs to the very traditional criticisms that plague its parent theories, revealing that the conflict between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence is not a mere methodological wrinkle but a fundamental metaphysical fracture that no realist framework can successfully bridge.

**I. The Two Poles: Gödelian Intuition and Quinean Indispensability**

To understand the challenge facing compromise Platonism, one must first appreciate the distinct virtues and fatal vices of its constituent parts. Gödelian Platonism is the archetype of a robust mathematical realism. It posits a realm of abstract objects—sets, numbers, functions—that exist independently of human thought and language. Mathematical truth is simply correspondence to this realm. The central epistemological question, famously articulated by Paul Benacerraf, is how we, as causally situated physical beings, can have knowledge of these acausal, non-spatiotemporal entities. Gödel’s answer was to posit a faculty of mathematical intuition, a quasi-perceptual ability to "see" the properties of sets, analogous to sense perception but directed at a different kind of object. This intuition is not infallible, but it provides a non-inferential, immediate source of justification for axioms such as the Axiom of Choice or the Continuum Hypothesis.

The strength of this position is its clear, univocal ontology. There is a determinate fact of the matter about whether the Continuum Hypothesis is true, even if we cannot decide it. The weakness is the epistemological faculty itself. Gödelian intuition is notoriously vague. What is its mechanism? How do we distinguish it from mere psychological conviction, cultural prejudice, or wishful thinking? Benacerraf’s challenge is not just about causal contact; it is about the reliability of the faculty. If intuition is a black box, then any axiom we find "intuitively obvious" becomes a candidate for truth, including contradictory ones. The history of mathematics is replete with "obvious" claims that turned out to be false—the parallel postulate being the most famous example. Gödelian Platonism offers no principled way to adjudicate between competing intuitions, nor does it explain why our intuition should track a reality that is, by definition, causally inert. Consequently, it retreats into a form of rationalist mysticism that is scientifically unpalatable.

The Quine/Putnam position, by contrast, offers a thoroughly naturalized epistemology. It rejects the idea of a special mathematical intuition. Instead, it argues that mathematics is continuous with the empirical sciences. Our best scientific theories—physics, chemistry, biology—quantify over mathematical objects. They say things like "There exists a function f such that..." or "There exists a set of real numbers...". According to the indispensability argument, we are committed to the existence of the entities over which our best theories quantify, because we have no other way to account for their empirical success. Therefore, we are committed to the existence of mathematical objects. The justification for mathematical axioms is thus *extrinsic*: they are justified because they are indispensable to the empirical success of science.

This position neatly solves the epistemic access problem. We know about sets and numbers the same way we know about electrons and quarks—through the holistic confirmation of the scientific theory that posits them. However, it introduces a devastating problem of *mathematical content*. Quine’s holism is notoriously coarse-grained. It tells us that our theory as a whole is confirmed, but it does not tell us which specific axioms within that theory are true. For example, both Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with the Axiom of Choice (ZFC) and ZF with the negation of the Axiom of Choice (ZF+¬AC) can be used to develop the mathematics required for physics. They both imply the same empirical consequences, but they differ on the existence of certain sets. Since they are empirically equivalent, Quinean holism cannot decide between them. The Quinean Platonist is left with a radical underdetermination of mathematical ontology by scientific evidence. Furthermore, as Putnam himself later recognized, the indispensability argument does not necessarily yield Platonism. It could just as easily yield a form of modal structuralism, where we are committed not to a unique realm of objects but to the possibility of various structures. The Quinean position, therefore, fails to secure the categorical, unique truth-values that Platonism requires; it collapses into a pragmatic instrumentalism where mathematical truth is merely a function of empirical utility.

**II. Maddy’s Compromise: The Marriage of Intuition and Naturalism**

Penelope Maddy’s project, particularly in her later work *Naturalism in Mathematics*, is a sophisticated attempt to salvage the best of both worlds. She rejects the grand, cosmic Platonism of Gödel—the idea that we have a mysterious access to a transcendent realm—while retaining a commitment to the objectivity of mathematics. She also rejects the coarse-grained holism of Quine, which fails to respect the internal autonomy of mathematical practice. Maddy’s solution is a *methodological naturalism* that takes mathematical practice as the ultimate arbiter of mathematical truth, but which recognizes two distinct sources of justification.

The first is *intrinsic justification*. This is justification that arises from within mathematics itself. It includes the intuitive obviousness of certain axioms, but Maddy grounds this not in a mystical Gödelian faculty but in the ordinary cognitive processes of mathematicians. It involves considerations of simplicity, elegance, fruitfulness, and the ability to organize and unify mathematical domains. Intrinsic justification is the kind of evidence that leads mathematicians to accept the Axiom of Choice because it yields elegant theorems, or to reject it because it leads to counterintuitive consequences like the Banach-Tarski paradox. It is a naturalistic account of mathematical intuition: intuition is not a direct perception of abstract objects but a complex, theory-laden judgment based on the internal coherence and pragmatic success of mathematical theories.

The second is *extrinsic justification*. This is justification that comes from outside mathematics, specifically from the role mathematics plays in the empirical sciences. This is where Maddy incorporates the Quine/Putnam insight, but she does so in a more nuanced way. She argues that when mathematics is used in physics, and that physics is empirically successful, this success provides evidence for the truth of the mathematical axioms involved. However, Maddy insists that this extrinsic evidence is not the *only* evidence. It is one source of justification among several. The compromise Platonist holds that both intrinsic and extrinsic evidence are indicators of truth, and that a fully justified mathematical theory should ideally be supported by both.

The problem, of course, is that intrinsic and extrinsic evidence can conflict. What happens when the internal elegance of a mathematical theory pulls in one direction, while the demands of empirical science pull in another? It is here that the compromise Platonist faces her gravest challenge. To see this clearly, we must examine the two historical and contemporary case studies that most vividly illustrate this conflict: the parallel postulate and the Axiom of Choice.

**III. The Dilemmas: The Parallel Postulate and the Axiom of Choice**

The parallel postulate is the classic example of intrinsic evidence leading mathematicians astray. For over two thousand years, Euclid’s fifth postulate—that through a point not on a given line, exactly one parallel line can be drawn—was considered intrinsically obvious. It was a truth of reason, self-evident to any competent geometer. Generations of mathematicians tried to prove it from the other postulates, and their failure was attributed to a lack of ingenuity, not to the falsity of the postulate. This is a perfect instance of Gödelian intrinsic justification: a claim accepted on the basis of its intuitive obviousness and its role in organizing the rest of geometry.

However, the development of non-Euclidean geometries in the nineteenth century—by Gauss, Bolyai, and Lobachevsky—demonstrated that the negation of the parallel postulate is perfectly consistent. Replacing the postulate with a hyperbolic or elliptic alternative yields rich, coherent, and internally beautiful mathematical structures. Intrinsically, these geometries are just as justified as Euclidean geometry. But the decisive blow came from physics. In the early twentieth century, Einstein’s general theory of relativity used non-Euclidean geometry to describe the curvature of spacetime. The extrinsic evidence, the empirical success of relativity, forced a radical reinterpretation. The parallel postulate was not a universal truth of space; it was merely a truth of a particular, flat geometry. The intrinsic evidence for the postulate was revealed to be a psychological artifact, a product of our limited experience with large-scale spatial structures.

Now, how does a compromise Platonist handle this? If she is a realist, she must believe that there is a fact of the matter about the geometry of physical space. General relativity tells us that physical space is non-Euclidean. But this does not mean that Euclidean geometry is false *simpliciter*; it means that Euclidean geometry is true of an abstract structure, but not of physical space. The Platonist can simply say that Euclidean geometry and hyperbolic geometry are both true, but they are true of different abstract structures. This is a form of mathematical pluralism, which is perfectly compatible with realism about abstract objects. The conflict here is not between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence *within* mathematics, but between the intrinsic evidence for a particular axiom and the extrinsic evidence for which abstract structure is instantiated in physical reality. The Platonist can accommodate this by separating the mathematical realm (where both geometries are true) from the physical realm (where only one applies).

But this resolution is too easy. It does not address the deeper problem: what about the *internal* conflict between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence for a single mathematical axiom? The Axiom of Choice (AC) provides a much more challenging case.

The Axiom of Choice states that for any collection of non-empty sets, there exists a choice function that picks an element from each set. For finite collections, this is trivially true. For infinite collections, it is a profound and controversial assertion. Intrinsically, AC is a mixed bag. On one hand, it seems intuitively obvious: if you have a bunch of boxes with things in them, you can pick one thing from each box. On the other hand, it leads to the Banach-Tarski paradox, which states that a solid ball can be decomposed into a finite number of pieces and reassembled into two identical copies of the original ball. This is intuitively absurd. It violates our basic physical intuitions about volume and matter. Intrinsic evidence, therefore, is deeply divided. Some mathematicians (like Hilbert) found AC indispensable for proving fundamental theorems in analysis and algebra. Others (like Lebesgue and Borel) found it repugnant because it posits the existence of non-measurable sets, which seem to be pure artifacts of the axiom.

Extrinsic evidence, however, is overwhelmingly in favor of AC. It is essential for a vast array of theorems in functional analysis (Hahn-Banach theorem), topology (Tychonoff's theorem), algebra (every vector space has a basis), and set theory itself (the Well-Ordering Theorem, the cardinal comparability of sets). Without AC, huge swaths of modern mathematics collapse. Moreover, AC is used implicitly in many arguments in physics, particularly in the foundations of quantum mechanics and in the analysis of infinite-dimensional spaces. From a Quine/Putnam perspective, AC is justified because it is indispensable to the empirical success of science. The extrinsic evidence is so strong that it seems to overwhelm the intrinsic objections.

Here we have a genuine conflict. Intrinsic evidence is ambiguous, but the negative intrinsic evidence (the paradoxes, the non-constructive nature) is significant. Extrinsic evidence is unambiguously positive. How does the compromise Platonist decide? She cannot simply say "both AC and ¬AC are true of different structures" because they are not different structures; they are contradictory statements about the same universe of sets. If she is a realist about sets, then either there exists a choice function for every collection, or there does not. The conflict is not between two equally valid abstract structures; it is a conflict between two sources of evidence for a single ontological fact.

**IV. The Three Resolutions and Their Fatal Consequences**

Faced with this dilemma, the compromise Platonist has three options. Each one, as I will demonstrate, forces her to abandon the compromise and regress to one of the pure, flawed positions.

**Resolution A: Side with Intrinsic Evidence (The Gödelian Slide).** The compromise Platonist might argue that intrinsic evidence, properly understood, is the primary arbiter of mathematical truth, and that extrinsic evidence is merely a pragmatic bonus. In the case of AC, she would side with the constructivists and reject AC because it leads to paradoxes and non-constructive existence claims that violate our mathematical intuitions. But this immediately collapses into pure Gödelian Platonism. Why should we trust the intrinsic intuition that non-measurable sets are repugnant? Why should we trust the intuition that AC is obvious? The history of the parallel postulate shows that intrinsic intuition can be profoundly wrong. If the compromise Platonist prioritizes intrinsic evidence, she must provide a criterion for distinguishing reliable from unreliable intuition. But any such criterion will have to be either circular (using intuition to validate intuition) or will have to appeal to something outside mathematics, such as psychology or cognitive science, which brings us back to the naturalistic problem. Moreover, by prioritizing intrinsic evidence, she alienates mathematics from its empirical applications. If AC is rejected, much of physics becomes mathematically unsupported. The compromise Platonist cannot have it both ways: she cannot claim to be a naturalist who respects the role of mathematics in science while simultaneously allowing a mysterious, non-naturalistic intuition to overrule the empirical success of science. She succumbs to Benacerraf’s challenge—how can this intuition track a mind-independent reality?—without offering any new answer.

**Resolution B: Side with Extrinsic Evidence (The Quinean Slide).** Alternatively, the compromise Platonist might argue that extrinsic evidence is the ultimate arbiter, and that intrinsic evidence is merely a psychological heuristic. In the case of AC, she would accept it because it is indispensable to science, despite its paradoxical consequences. But this collapses into pure Quinean Platonism, with all its attendant vices. The first problem is underdetermination. As noted, ZFC and ZF+¬AC are empirically equivalent. Both can be used to develop the mathematics needed for physics. The extrinsic evidence does not distinguish between them. If the compromise Platonist sides with extrinsic evidence, she must admit that she has no principled reason to prefer AC over ¬AC, since both are compatible with scientific success. She could, of course, adopt a pragmatic stance and choose the axiom that makes mathematics easier or more elegant, but that is no longer a Platonist stance; it is a formalist or instrumentalist stance. The second problem is the loss of mathematical autonomy. If extrinsic evidence is the ultimate arbiter, then mathematics becomes a mere servant of physics. But mathematics has its own internal dynamic, its own questions and problems that are pursued for their own sake, often centuries before they find empirical application. The Quinean position cannot account for the fact that mathematicians accept axioms based on intrinsic grounds—like the Axiom of Infinity, which has no direct empirical application—without reducing them to mere fictions. By siding with extrinsic evidence, the compromise Platonist surrenders the objectivity of mathematics to the contingency of scientific fashion. She succumbs to the criticism that Quinean Platonism is not actually Platonism at all, but a form of holistic empiricism that fails to secure the necessary uniqueness and autonomy of mathematical truth.

**Resolution C: Abandon Bivalent Truth Values (The Anti-Realist Slide).** The final option is to abandon the demand for a unique, bivalent truth value for statements like AC. The compromise Platonist might argue that AC is not simply true or false, but rather true in some models of set theory and false in others. This is the position of mathematical pluralism or structuralism, which is currently popular in the philosophy of mathematics. But this is a direct abandonment of Platonism. Platonism, by definition, posits a single, mind-independent universe of sets. If AC is true in one model and false in another, then there is no fact of the matter about whether choice functions exist *simpliciter*. There is only a fact about whether they exist in this or that structure. This is a form of anti-realism, or at best a structuralism that denies the existence of a privileged universe. Maddy herself, in her later work, has moved towards a version of set-theoretic pluralism, arguing that we should accept a multiverse of set-theoretic models rather than a single universe. But this is a tacit admission that the compromise Platonism has failed. By abandoning bivalence, the compromise Platonist gives up on the very thing that made Platonism attractive in the first place: the idea that mathematical statements have determinate truth values that are independent of our theories and practices. She is left with a position that is indistinguishable from a sophisticated form of nominalism or formalism, where mathematical truth is relative to a chosen framework. She succumbs to the criticism that Platonism is a "theology of mathematics" that cannot be sustained in the face of mathematical practice.

**V. The Inescapable Fracture: Why the Compromise Cannot Work**

The fundamental reason the compromise Platonist fails is that she conflates *evidence* with *truth*. Intrinsic and extrinsic justifications are both epistemic notions—they are reasons we have for believing a mathematical statement to be true. But they are not truth-makers. Truth-makers are the abstract objects themselves. If intrinsic and extrinsic evidence conflict, this indicates that at least one of them is not tracking truth. The compromise Platonist needs a meta-principle to decide which source of evidence is more reliable. But she cannot provide one without begging the question.

If she says "intrinsic evidence is more reliable," she must explain why our mathematical intuitions are reliable trackers of abstract reality. This is the Gödelian problem, and Maddy’s naturalistic gloss—grounding intuition in mathematical practice—does not solve it. Mathematical practice is a human activity, subject to historical contingency and psychological bias. If intuition is just "what mathematicians happen to find obvious," then it is not a reliable guide to a mind-independent reality. It is merely a description of our cognitive dispositions.

If she says "extrinsic evidence is more reliable," she must explain why empirical success is a reliable guide to abstract truth. This is the Quinean problem. Empirical success tells us that a theory is useful, but it does not tell us that the entities posited by the theory exist, let alone that the specific axioms of the theory are true. The success of general relativity does not tell us that the Axiom of Choice is true; it tells us that the mathematics used in relativity is useful. The connection between utility and truth is precisely what the Quinean argument is supposed to establish, but it only establishes that *some* mathematics is true, not *which* mathematics.

The compromise Platonist is caught in a dilemma. She wants to be a naturalist, respecting the methods of science and mathematics. But naturalism, when taken seriously, leads to a deflationary account of mathematical truth. It leads to the view that mathematical truth is just what our best theories say it is, which is a form of anti-realism. She also wants to be a Platonist, respecting the objectivity and uniqueness of mathematical truth. But Platonism, when taken seriously, requires a non-naturalistic epistemology—a mysterious intuition—which is scientifically unpalatable. The compromise attempts to hold these two incompatible commitments together, but the conflict between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence reveals that they cannot be held together.

The case of the parallel postulate is instructive. The intrinsic evidence for the postulate was overwhelmingly strong for two thousand years. The extrinsic evidence, when it finally arrived, was so powerful that it forced mathematicians to abandon the intrinsic claim. This shows that extrinsic evidence can override intrinsic evidence. But it also shows that intrinsic evidence can be profoundly wrong. If intuition can be so wrong about something as "obvious" as the parallel postulate, why should we trust it about anything? The compromise Platonist cannot answer this question. She can only say that intuition is fallible, but that does not tell us when to trust it and when to distrust it. The Axiom of Choice is even worse. Here, intrinsic evidence is split, and extrinsic evidence is strong but not decisive (because of underdetermination). The compromise Platonist has no principled way to resolve the split.

In conclusion, compromise Platonism is a failed project. It does not succeed in reconciling the intrinsic and extrinsic justifications. When faced with a direct conflict, it is forced to choose one or the other, and in doing so, it inherits the fatal flaws of the chosen parent. If it chooses intrinsic, it succumbs to the epistemic mysticism of Gödelian Platonism, unable to explain the reliability of intuition. If it chooses extrinsic, it succumbs to the holistic instrumentalism of Quine/Putnam Platonism, unable to secure the uniqueness and autonomy of mathematical truth. If it abandons bivalence, it abandons Platonism altogether, collapsing into a pluralist or structuralist anti-realism. The dilemma of the Axiom of Choice and the parallel postulate is not a minor technical problem; it is a symptom of a deep metaphysical fracture. The compromise Platonist wants to have her cake and eat it too—to be a naturalist and a Platonist—but the conflict between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence shows that these two commitments are fundamentally incompatible. The only honest conclusion is that the realist framework, whether pure or compromised, cannot resolve the tension between our mathematical intuitions and our scientific needs.
