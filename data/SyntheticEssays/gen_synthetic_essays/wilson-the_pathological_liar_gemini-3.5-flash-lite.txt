# Pathologies of Meaning: An Evaluation of Kirkham’s Criteria and the Exclusionary Defense of the Liar’s Paradox

## Introduction

The Liar’s paradox—exemplified by the sentence “This sentence is false”—has long served as the perennial stress test for semantic theories of truth. For millennia, philosophers and logicians have attempted to formulate an account of truth that can accommodate, dissolve, or domesticate this linguistic anomaly without fracturing the architecture of classical logic. In his seminal work *Theories of Truth: A Critical Introduction*, Richard Kirkham sets forth a rigorous methodological framework, establishing five adequacy criteria that any successful solution to the paradox must satisfy. Kirkham’s criteria demand that a solution be specific, avoid ad hoc postulations, preserve bivalent intuitions, avoid overkill, and achieve completeness. 

However, evaluating these criteria requires looking upstream at the nature of the paradox itself. Is the Liar sentence a genuine truth-bearing proposition whose semantic properties have eluded us, or is it a *pathological case* within natural language semantics—an aberration analogous to division by zero in arithmetic or the Weierstrass function in real analysis? 

This essay will argue that the Liar’s paradox is indeed a semantic pathology. When viewed in this light, two of Kirkham’s central criteria—the demand for *specificity* and the *avoidance of ad hoc postulations*—are exposed as fundamentally misapplied, resting on a category mistake regarding the nature of natural language. By treating the Liar sentence as a well-formed semantic entity requiring a substantive, non-arbitrary diagnostic profile, Kirkham’s criteria demand an explanatory mechanism for what is, in truth, a structural breakdown of semantic compositionality. 

Building upon this diagnostic, I will defend an *exclusionary approach* that denies truth values altogether to self-referential contradictions. I will demonstrate that this exclusionary stance—far from being an indefensible ad hoc maneuver—satisfies Kirkham’s criteria of completeness, successfully avoids the "overkill" of trivializing non-paradoxical discourse, and preserves our core bivalent intuitions regarding truth, all without requiring the construction of a totalizing, error-free formal system for natural language.

## Part I: The Liar’s Paradox and Natural Language Pathologies

To understand why the Liar’s paradox resists standard semantic analysis, we must first articulate the mechanics of the paradox and its place within natural language. Consider the sentence (L):

> (L) Sentence (L) is false.

If we assume classical bivalence—the principle that every meaningful proposition is either true or false, but not both—we are immediately driven to contradiction. If (L) is true, then what it asserts must be the case; hence, (L) is false. Conversely, if (L) is false, then since (L) asserts its own falsity, it must be true. Classical logic thus collapses into triviality via the Tarski biconditional ($\text{True}(\ulcorner P \urcorner) \equiv P$), proving that our naïve semantic intuitions regarding truth are inconsistent when applied to self-referential idioms.

Historically, responses to this collapse have bifurcated into two broad camps: revisionary and exclusionary. Revisionary approaches (such as revision theory of truth, paracomplete logics with truth-value gaps, and paraconsistent logics with truth-value gluts) seek to expand or modify the underlying logic or the semantic conception of truth to accommodate (L) as a legitimate, truth-evaluable object within the system. Exclusionary approaches, conversely, argue that (L) fails to express a genuine proposition, occupying a status akin to syntactic nonsense or semantic failure; hence, it is neither true nor false because it fails to secure a truth-value bearer in the first place.

To properly evaluate these approaches, we must analyze the ontological and semantic status of the Liar sentence. I propose that (L) is best understood as a **pathological case** within natural language semantics. In mathematics, a pathology is a property, construction, or phenomenon that is considered abnormal or counterintuitive because it violates the smooth, expected behavior modeled by general theories. 

Consider two classic mathematical analogies:
1. **Division by Zero:** In standard arithmetic, the operation $a / 0$ is undefined. It is not assigned a numerical value (neither zero, nor infinity, nor any real number) because admitting it into the field axioms destroys the algebraic structure, leading to catastrophic proofs like $1 = 2$. Division by zero is not a "hidden number" waiting to be discovered by a more expansive arithmetic; it is a structural limit of the arithmetic framework itself.
2. **The Weierstrass Function:** In real analysis, functions are expected to be differentiable almost everywhere. Karl Weierstrass, however, constructed a real-valued function that is continuous everywhere but differentiable *nowhere*. This function is a mathematical pathology—it stretches the limits of topological definitions, forcing mathematicians to refine what it means to be continuous and differentiable without abandoning differential calculus.

The Liar sentence is the exact semantic analogue of these mathematical pathologies. Natural language is a compositional system designed to convey information about states of affairs in the world. Its semantic machinery relies on a delicate balance of reference, predication, and hierarchical stratification. When self-reference is introduced without restriction, it loops back upon the semantic machinery of truth-attribution, short-circuiting the compositional hierarchy. 

Crucially, the Liar sentence *looks* grammatically and syntactically well-formed. It mimics the surface structure of meaningful declarative sentences. However, its semantic payload is nil. Just as division by zero uses the symbols of arithmetic ($a$, $/$, $0$) in a configuration that violates the presuppositions of the division operator, the Liar sentence uses the vocabulary of natural language (nouns, verbs, truth-predicates) in a configuration that violates the presuppositions of semantic evaluation. It is a well-formed syntactic string that fails to secure a truth-value because its semantic rules are self-defeating.

## Part II: Richard Kirkham’s Five Criteria Evaluated

In *Theories of Truth*, Richard Kirkham establishes five methodological criteria that any adequate solution to the Liar’s paradox must meet. These criteria are designed to filter out ad hoc maneuvers and ensure that a proposed solution does not solve the paradox at the expense of our broader understanding of truth. Let us examine each criterion:

1. **Specificity:** The solution must provide a specific diagnosis of why the Liar sentence fails, rather than merely stipulating that it does not have a truth value. It must identify the precise mechanism or rule within semantics that generates the failure.
2. **Avoidance of Ad Hoc Postulations:** The solution must not introduce rules or restrictions solely for the purpose of defusing the paradox. The constraints must be independently motivated by general principles of language and logic.
3. **Preservation of Bivalent Intuitions:** The solution should respect our pre-theoretic intuition that true and false are mutually exclusive and exhaustive for declarative sentences, minimizing unnecessary deviations from classical bivalence.
4. **Avoidance of Overkill:** The solution must not be so broad or restrictive that it eliminates non-paradoxical, useful self-referential sentences (such as "This sentence is written in English").
5. **Completeness:** The solution must be generalizable, solving not just the simple Liar, but strengthening variations (such as the Curry paradox and the Extended Liar) without collapsing.

When we evaluate these criteria against the backdrop of the *pathological view* of natural language semantics, a profound tension emerges. Specifically, **Kirkham’s criteria of specificity and the avoidance of ad hoc postulations are systematically undermined by the very nature of semantic pathologies.**

### The Breakdown of Specificity

Kirkham demands *specificity*: a principled account of *why* the Liar sentence goes wrong. In a non-pathological linguistic failure—such as a category mistake like "The number 7 is green"—specificity is easily achieved. We can point to the sortal mismatch between the numerical subject and the chromatic predicate. 

However, with a semantic pathology like the Liar, demanding a "specific mechanism" of failure often assumes that the Liar sentence possesses a coherent semantic structure that is merely blocked by a specific rule violation. If the Liar is a pathology akin to division by zero, there is no discrete "mechanism" of failure other than the self-referential circularity itself operating upon a global deflationary or correspondence property of truth. Asking for a specific diagnosis of the Liar beyond "it involves a self-referential vicious circle that undermines truth-attribution" is like asking for a specific psychological diagnosis of why a person cannot lift themselves by their own bootstraps. The impossibility is structural, not contingent upon a hidden mechanical failure. 

### The Trap of "Ad Hoc" Accusations

Similarly, the criterion demanding the *avoidance of ad hoc postulations* becomes an epistemological trap when applied to pathologies. In mathematics, when we declare that division by zero is undefined, is this an *ad hoc* rule? From the perspective of naive arithmetic, one might complain: "Why single out zero? Why can't we divide by it?" The response is that the exclusion of division by zero is not an arbitrary, groundless stipulation; it is a structural necessity to preserve the consistency of the field.

Yet, critics of exclusionary solutions often label them "ad hoc" because they rule out self-referential sentences without deriving this exclusion from a grand, overarching theory of all natural language meaning. But as we shall argue in Part III, natural language is not a formal axiomatic system; it is an organic, historically contingent, and inherently open-ended medium. Expecting a pristine, non-ad-hoc formal principle to neatly carve out all semantic pathologies without remainder is to misunderstand the messy, non-compositional corners of human language.

## Part III: The Exclusionary Approach Defended

Despite the pressures of Kirkham’s criteria, an exclusionary approach can be robustly defended. The exclusionary approach posits that self-referential contradictions like (L) are simply not truth-bearers. They are semantically defective strings that fail to express propositions. Consequently, they are neither true nor false.

Let us evaluate how this exclusionary stance satisfies Kirkham’s five criteria, provided we reinterpret *specificity* and *ad hocness* through the lens of semantic pathology.

### 1. Satisfying Completeness and Strengthening Variations
The exclusionary approach handles strengthening variations of the Liar with ease. Consider the Strengthened Liar:

> (S) Sentence (S) is not true.

If (S) is true, then it is not true—a contradiction. If (S) is not true, then since (S) asserts its own non-truth, it *is* true—another contradiction. 

Revisionary approaches (such as paracomplete accounts like Kripke’s) struggle here because they assign (L) a designated "neither true nor false" third value (or truth-value gap), but then the Strengthened Liar (S)—which asserts "I am not true"—can be evaluated within the metalanguage as *truly* not true, regenerating the paradox at a higher level (the Revenge Liar). 

The exclusionary approach avoids this infinite regress cleanly. Because (S), like (L), involves a vicious self-referential circle that undermines truth-attribution, it fails to express a proposition. It is not assigned a "third truth value" (which would require a non-classical logic and invite revenge paradoxes); rather, it is excluded from the domain of semantic evaluation altogether. It is propositionally vacuous. Thus, completeness is achieved not by expanding the ontology of truth values, but by policing the boundaries of propositional content.

### 2. Avoiding Overkill
A major criticism of exclusionary and gap-based theories is *overkill*: if we ban self-reference or truth-value attribution to solve the Liar, do we accidentally invalidate benign, useful self-referential sentences? 

Consider:
* ($M_1$) "This sentence is written in English."
* ($M_2$) "I am currently speaking."

These sentences are self-referential, yet they are entirely unproblematic. They do not generate paradoxes. Does the exclusionary approach commit overkill by declaring *all* self-referential sentences defective?

No. The exclusionary approach does not ban *self-reference as such*; it bans **semantic circularity that hooks into truth-attribution in a paradoxical manner**. ($M_1$ and $M_2$) refer to their own syntactic medium or the pragmatic act of utterance, not to their own semantic truth-value. They do not assert "I am true" or "I am false." Therefore, they do not short-circuit the truth predicate. The exclusionary approach draws the line precisely at the intersection of self-reference and truth-theoretic self-application, thereby successfully avoiding overkill.

### 3. Preserving Bivalent Intuitions
Critics often argue that denying truth values to the Liar abandons bivalence. However, the exclusionary approach preserves bivalence where bivalence belongs: *within the domain of propositions*. 

Classical bivalence states that for any meaningful proposition $P$, $P$ is either true or false. The exclusionary approach does not deny this principle; it restricts its scope. It argues that (L) is not a proposition. Bivalence is a norm governing *propositions*, not a blanket rule covering every grammatically well-formed string of words we can dream up. Just as the law of excluded middle ($P \lor \neg P$) does not apply to "Green ideas sleep furiously" (because it is semantically nonsensical), it does not apply to the Liar (because it is semantically pathological). By relegating the Liar to the realm of non-propositions, bivalence is preserved for all legitimate truth-bearers.

### 4. Re-evaluating Specificity and Ad Hocness
Earlier, we noted that Kirkham demands specificity and the avoidance of ad hocness. How does the exclusionary defense satisfy these under our pathological model?

* **Specificity:** The specific diagnosis provided by the exclusionary approach is **semantic grounding failure via circular truth-attribution**. A sentence that predicates truth or falsity of itself in a way that generates an infinite, non-converging truth-dependency chain lacks the semantic foundation required to express a determinate proposition. This is as specific a diagnosis as declaring that a physical structure with infinite weight and zero surface area cannot support itself.
* **Avoidance of Ad Hocness:** The restriction is not ad hoc because it is derived from a general, independently motivated constraint on semantic compositionality: **the Principle of Non-Circular Grounding**. Meaningful language requires that the semantic content of a complex expression be grounded in non-circular reference or compositional derivation. When a sentence defines its truth-value entirely in terms of its own negation, it violates the grounding requirement. This is no more ad hoc than requiring that a computer program not contain an infinite, non-terminating recursive loop without a base case.

## Part IV: Why Natural Language Does Not Require a Totalizing Formal System

A foundational presupposition underlying Kirkham’s criteria—and much of analytical philosophy of language—is the assumption that natural language is, or ought to be treatable as, a **totalizing formal system**. This assumption is inherited from the Fregean and Tarskian tradition, which views natural language as a potentially rigorous calculus whose semantics can be exhaustively formalized without remainder.

Tarski famously argued that a natural language cannot contain its own truth predicate without inconsistency (Tarski's Undefinability Theorem). Tarski’s solution was to stratify language into an infinite hierarchy of object languages and metalanguages ($L_0, L_1, L_2 \dots$). While Tarskian stratification avoids paradox, it fails descriptively: real natural languages do contain their own truth predicates (we routinely say, "What John said is true").

Subsequent philosophers, dissatisfied with Tarski’s artificial hierarchy, sought theories that allow natural language to be **semantically closed**—that is, capable of expressing its own truth predicate unrestrictedly. It is precisely in the pursuit of a semantic closure that Kirkham’s criteria become weaponized against exclusionary accounts. Theorists demand a neat, systematic, non-ad-hoc formal mechanism that explains semantic closure without paradox.

Herein lies the core error: **Natural language is not a formal system, nor is it semantically closed in the clean, systematic way logicians imagine.** 

Natural language is an evolved, highly flexible, pragmatic, and inherently open-textured medium. It tolerates ambiguities, vagueness, indexicality, and—crucially—pathologies. Just as human anatomy includes vestigial organs and physiological quirks (such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve or the blind spot in the retina) that do not conform to an optimal engineering blueprint, human language includes structural anomalies that defy neat formalization.

To demand that an adequate solution to the Liar’s paradox provide a totalizing, elegant, non-ad-hoc formal mechanism that preserves semantic closure while accommodating the Liar is to demand that natural language possess a consistency and systematicity it simply does not have. The Liar’s paradox is precisely the friction generated when we attempt to force an organic, pragmatically anchored linguistic system into the rigid Procrustean bed of classical formal semantics.

The exclusionary approach succeeds precisely because it *abandons* the quest for a totalizing formal semantics of natural language. It recognizes that natural language contains structural limits—fault lines where semantics breaks down. By acknowledging that certain self-referential loops result in semantic collapse, the exclusionary approach refuses to invent an artificial metaphysics (such as infinite truth-value gaps or dialetheic truth-gluts) just to rescue a pathological sentence from its own logical suicide.

---

## Conclusion

Richard Kirkham’s criteria for solutions to the Liar’s paradox provide a valuable methodological checklist for formal epistemology. However, when applied to natural language semantics without accounting for the pathological nature of the Liar sentence, these criteria impose unrealistic demands. By treating the Liar as a well-formed semantic entity requiring a substantive, non-ad-hoc diagnostic profile, Kirkham’s criteria—particularly specificity and the avoidance of ad hocness—misunderstand the character of semantic anomalies.

When we classify the Liar’s paradox as a pathological case within natural language semantics—analogous to division by zero or the Weierstrass function—the entire landscape shifts. We see that the failure of the Liar is structural and unyielding. 

The exclusionary approach defended in this essay respects this reality. By denying truth values to self-referential contradictions, the exclusionary view:
* **Achieves completeness** by effortlessly neutralizing strengthening variations (like the Revenge Liar) without collapsing into infinite value hierarchies.
* **Avoids overkill** by carefully distinguishing between benign self-reference (which tracks syntax or utterance) and vicious truth-theoretic circularity.
* **Preserves bivalent intuitions** by restricting bivalence to its proper domain: genuine propositions.
* **Redefines specificity and ad hocness** by grounding the exclusion in independent principles of semantic grounding and non-circular compositionality, mirroring the structural exclusions found in mathematics.

Ultimately, the exclusionary approach frees us from the chimera of constructing a totalizing formal system for a natural language that is inherently open, flexible, and occasionally self-defeating. The Liar’s paradox is not a puzzle to be solved by inventing new logic; it is a warning sign posted at the edge of semantic coherence. We do not need to build a house upon the fault line; we simply need to recognize where the ground gives way.
