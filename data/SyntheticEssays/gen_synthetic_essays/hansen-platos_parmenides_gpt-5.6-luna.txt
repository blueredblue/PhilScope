# Vlastos, Self-Predication, and the Third-Man Argument

Gregory Vlastos’s interpretation of the Third-Man Argument remains one of the most influential and controversial accounts of Plato’s critique of Forms in the *Parmenides*. The argument appears to show that the theory of Forms, if combined with apparently innocent assumptions about predication and participation, generates an infinite regress of Forms. If several beautiful things are beautiful because they participate in the Form of Beauty, and if the Form of Beauty is itself beautiful, then the original beautiful things and the Form of Beauty seem to require a further Form to explain their common beauty. The same reasoning can then be repeated indefinitely.

Vlastos’s importance lies not merely in having emphasized the regress, but in having isolated the premises that make it work. In his reconstruction, Plato’s theory is committed to a combination of the One-over-Many Principle, Self-Predication, and Non-Identity. The One-over-Many Principle says that whenever several things are F, there is a single Form in virtue of which they are F. Self-Predication says that the Form of F is itself F. Non-Identity says that the Form of F is distinct from the particular things that participate in it, or more generally that whatever is F in virtue of something else is not identical with that thing. Together these claims produce the Third-Man regress.

The philosophical question is whether this result exposes a genuine inconsistency in Plato’s ontology or whether it arises from an overly literal and insufficiently differentiated understanding of Platonic predication. The issue is especially difficult because the *Parmenides* itself presents the argument as a challenge rather than as a final verdict. Plato may be dramatizing the weaknesses of a young Socrates’ theory, or he may be forcing the reader to distinguish several senses of “is,” “participates in,” and “is one.” Alternative interpretations seek to dissolve the regress by rejecting Self-Predication, revising Non-Identity, distinguishing different kinds of predication, or denying that a Form belongs to the same plurality as its participants. Each alternative has philosophical merit, but each also faces a problem of historical plausibility: how far can one revise the apparent commitments of the theory without ceasing to interpret Plato?

The most defensible conclusion is that Vlastos correctly identifies a structural tension in the theory of Forms as it is often presented, but that he does not establish that Plato is straightforwardly committed to an outright contradiction. The argument demonstrates that the theory requires a more sophisticated account of predication and explanation than the simple formula “particulars are F by participating in the Form of F.” Plato’s ontology need not collapse into an infinite regress if Self-Predication and Non-Identity are carefully distinguished. Yet the fact that such distinctions are necessary confirms the force of the Third-Man Argument: Plato’s theory cannot remain at the level of an unqualified one-over-many metaphysics.

## I. The Third-Man Argument and Vlastos’s Reconstruction

The Third-Man Argument occurs in the first major section of the *Parmenides*, where Parmenides examines Socrates’ commitment to separate Forms. Socrates maintains that there are Forms of likeness and unlikeness, plurality and unity, justice, beauty, and goodness. He also appears willing to accept Forms corresponding to ordinary objects such as man, fire, and water, though he hesitates about such things as hair, mud, and dirt. Parmenides then presses him on the relation between particulars and Forms.

The Third-Man passage begins from the thought that a plurality of things having a common character requires a Form. If a number of things are large, for example, they are large by virtue of the Form of Largeness. But if the Form itself is large—if Largeness is large—then there is a plurality consisting of the original large things and the Form of Largeness. This plurality must, according to the one-over-many principle, be explained by another Form of Largeness. Yet if the second Form is itself large, the same reasoning applies again. Hence there is not one Form but an indefinitely increasing series of Forms.

Vlastos reconstructs the argument through three principles:

1. **The One-over-Many Principle (OM):** If several things are F, there is a single Form in virtue of which they are all F.

2. **The Self-Predication Principle (SP):** The Form of F is itself F. Beauty is beautiful; Justice is just; Largeness is large.

3. **The Non-Identity Principle (NI):** If something is F by virtue of participating in or being related to a Form, it is not identical with that Form.

These principles are not obviously absurd individually. Indeed, each can seem integral to the theory of Forms. The theory requires a Form to explain why many particulars are alike. The Form must somehow be relevantly related to the property it explains; otherwise it would be mysterious why the Form of Beauty explains beauty rather than, say, justice. And the Form must be distinct from the beautiful or just particulars, since Plato’s theory is intended to distinguish intelligible realities from sensible instances.

The regress arises as follows. Let \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) be beautiful particulars. By OM, there is a Form \(B_1\), the Form of Beauty, in virtue of which \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are beautiful. By SP, \(B_1\) is beautiful. Therefore, \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), and \(B_1\) are all beautiful. By OM, there must be another Form \(B_2\) in virtue of which all four are beautiful. By SP, \(B_2\) is beautiful. Thus \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), \(B_1\), and \(B_2\) require a further Form \(B_3\), and so on without end.

NI is needed to ensure that the explanatory Form cannot simply be identified with one of the things already included in the plurality. If \(B_1\) could be identical with one of the beautiful things, or if \(B_2\) could be identical with \(B_1\), the regress might be blocked. But if the Form must be distinct from the things it explains, each new Form is genuinely new. The argument therefore depends not merely upon the presence of self-predication but upon a conflict between self-predication and the explanatory role of the Form.

Vlastos’s central insight is that this conflict is internal to the theory’s own apparent logic. Plato cannot simply say that the Form of Beauty explains the beauty of particulars while denying that the Form itself is beautiful, since the Form is supposed to be Beauty itself and is often described in predicates that seem to apply to it. Nor can he simply identify the Form with its instances, since the distinction between Forms and particulars is foundational. The regress seems to arise because the Form must both be an instance of the property and be something fundamentally different from the instances it explains.

Vlastos characterizes the problem as a “self-predication” difficulty rather than merely as a problem about participation. If “the Beautiful is beautiful” has the same logical form as “Helen is beautiful,” then the Form belongs to the class of beautiful things. Once it belongs to that class, it falls under the one-over-many principle along with the particulars. The theory cannot both use “beautiful” univocally in these two statements and prevent the Form from entering the regress.

## II. Is the Argument a Formal Contradiction?

The strongest interpretation of Vlastos’s analysis is that Plato’s ontology contains mutually inconsistent premises. On this reading, Plato is committed to all of the following:

- Forms are separate from particulars.
- Forms explain the common character of particulars.
- The Form of F is itself F.
- All F things, including Forms, are F in virtue of an F Form.
- No thing can explain its own possession of a property.

If these claims are taken without qualification, the regress is unavoidable. The Form of Beauty is beautiful, but it cannot be beautiful in virtue of itself, because NI excludes self-explanation. It must therefore be beautiful in virtue of another Form. But that second Form, being the Form of Beauty, is also beautiful and cannot explain itself. The argument creates an infinite sequence not because of a contingent defect but because the original explanatory schema reproduces itself at every stage.

However, it is important to distinguish an infinite regress from a formal contradiction. An infinite regress is not automatically inconsistent. Some theories tolerate infinite structures. A theory of Forms could, in principle, accept infinitely many Forms of Beauty. The deeper problem is explanatory: if each Form requires a further Form to explain its possession of the relevant property, no Form ever succeeds in explaining beauty. The proposed explanation is indefinitely deferred.

Moreover, the three principles are not formally inconsistent in isolation. OM, SP, and NI can be jointly satisfied if one allows an infinite hierarchy of Forms. What makes the result philosophically objectionable is the failure of the Forms to perform the explanatory function assigned to them. The regress is therefore best described as a self-undermining explanatory structure rather than a simple contradiction in the narrow logical sense.

There is nevertheless a genuine tension between SP and NI. If the Form of F is F, then it appears to be one of the F things. If it is one of the F things, then the Form is subject to the same explanatory requirement as the particulars. But if it is not one of the F things, then “the Form of F is F” must have a different meaning from ordinary predication. Alternatively, if the Form is F in exactly the same way as particulars are F, then it cannot occupy an entirely different ontological category. The theory wants the Form to be both an F and not an F in the relevant sense: it is F because it is the essence or standard of F, but it is not F as a participant in the Form of F.

This is not necessarily a contradiction, because “is F” may be ambiguous. Yet Plato’s texts often do not explicitly mark the ambiguity. The Third-Man Argument exploits precisely this lack of differentiation. It takes the surface grammar of Platonic claims and asks whether the theory can sustain the ontological distinctions it requires.

## III. The Historical Problem of Self-Predication

The doctrine of Self-Predication is central to Vlastos’s interpretation, but its historical status is contested. Plato frequently says things that appear to support it. The Form of Beauty is “the Beautiful itself”; the Form of Justice is “Justice itself”; the Form of Largeness is “the Large itself.” In dialogues such as the *Symposium*, the *Phaedo*, and the *Republic*, Forms are described as beautiful, just, equal, or good in an especially pure and complete manner. Such passages make it difficult simply to deny that Plato thinks Forms possess the properties corresponding to their names.

Yet there are several possible meanings of Self-Predication.

First, the claim may be **ordinary self-predication**: the Form of Beauty is beautiful in the same sense that a beautiful body is beautiful. This reading generates the Third-Man problem most powerfully, but it may be too crude. Plato’s insistence that Forms are separate, unchanging, and intelligible suggests that they are not merely additional members of the same class as sensible particulars.

Second, the claim may be **essential or definitional predication**. The Form of Beauty is beautiful because beauty is what it is. “Beauty is beautiful” could mean that the Form is the reality or essence of beauty, not that it instantiates a property whose existence is independent of it. In this case, the Form is not an ordinary beautiful object alongside beautiful things. It is the standard or nature in virtue of which those things are beautiful.

Third, the claim may be **paradigmatic predication**. The Form is beautiful in the sense that it is the paradigm of beauty. To call it beautiful is to say that it exemplifies beauty in the maximal or authoritative way, not that it possesses an additional property numerically distinct from itself. The Form’s relation to beauty is therefore not the same as a particular’s relation to beauty.
