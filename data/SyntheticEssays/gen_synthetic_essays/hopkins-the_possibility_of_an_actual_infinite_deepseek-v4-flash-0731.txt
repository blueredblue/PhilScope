**The Illusion of Absurdity: William Lane Craig, the Actual Infinite, and the Coherence of LDS Eternalism**

The question of whether the universe has a beginning has haunted philosophy and theology for millennia. Within contemporary analytic philosophy, no one has defended the finitude of the past with more vigor and rhetorical force than William Lane Craig. His *kalam* cosmological argument, which posits that the universe began to exist, rests heavily on a series of philosophical arguments designed to demonstrate the impossibility of an actual infinite. Craig argues that if an actual infinite—a collection of things that is truly infinite in number—could exist in reality, it would lead to absurdities. He employs thought experiments such as Hilbert’s Hotel, an infinite library, and the Principle of Correspondence to argue that the actual infinite is not merely counter-intuitive but logically impossible. If successful, these arguments would devastate any theological framework that posits a beginningless reality. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS) is precisely such a framework, holding that intelligence, matter, and time are eternal and uncreated. This essay will argue that Craig’s philosophical arguments against the actual infinite fail to demonstrate genuine logical impossibility. Instead, they conflate the mathematical properties of transfinite sets with the physical behavior of finite objects, and they elevate the psychological limitations of finite human intuition to the status of metaphysical law. Consequently, LDS eternalism, which embraces a beginningless past and an eternal God, remains a philosophically coherent position that can consistently accommodate the actual infinite, provided one correctly distinguishes between set-theoretic mathematics and the ontology of time.

To evaluate Craig’s project, we must first understand the precise nature of his claims. Craig distinguishes between a *potential* infinite—a collection that is always finite but growing without limit—and an *actual* infinite—a completed totality that has an infinite number of members. He concedes that the potential infinite is coherent and exists in mathematics, but he insists that the actual infinite is “mathematically” consistent yet “metaphysically” impossible. That is, while Cantorian set theory can manipulate infinite sets without formal contradiction, the real world cannot contain such a collection. To prove this metaphysical impossibility, Craig employs a series of *reductio ad absurdum* arguments. The most famous is Hilbert’s Hotel, a hypothetical hotel with an infinite number of rooms. If the hotel is completely full, a new guest arrives. The manager simply moves the guest in Room 1 to Room 2, the guest in Room 2 to Room 3, and so on, freeing up Room 1 for the new arrival. The hotel remains full, despite accommodating an additional guest. Craig finds this absurd. He argues that a hotel that is full and yet can still accommodate more guests is a contradiction. Similarly, he evokes the Principle of Correspondence, which states that two sets have the same cardinality if their members can be paired one-to-one. In an infinite set, a proper subset (like the set of even numbers) can be put into a one-to-one correspondence with the whole set (the natural numbers). Craig argues that this violates the fundamental principle that the whole is greater than its parts, making the existence of such a set in reality impossible. Finally, he offers the example of an infinite library. If you remove all the books numbered greater than 3, you have 3 books left. If you remove all the odd-numbered books, you have an infinite number left. Craig argues that subtracting an infinite number of books from an infinite library can yield either a finite number or an infinite number, which he deems a logical contradiction.

These arguments are rhetorically powerful because they exploit our deep-seated, evolutionarily honed intuitions about finite quantities. However, a careful philosophical analysis reveals that Craig’s reductios do not demonstrate logical impossibility; rather, they demonstrate the failure of finite arithmetic and finite intuition when applied to transfinite mathematics. The central flaw lies in Craig’s implicit assumption that the operations of addition, subtraction, and comparison, which are defined for finite sets, must behave identically for infinite sets. This is a category error. In Cantorian set theory, the "absurdities" Craig identifies are not contradictions; they are the *defining properties* of infinite sets. A set is said to be Dedekind-infinite precisely if it can be put into a one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself. The fact that the even numbers can be paired with the natural numbers is not a logical contradiction; it is the very criterion by which we identify an infinite set. When Craig says that a full hotel can accommodate a new guest, he is not revealing a contradiction but merely describing the behavior of a countably infinite set. The set of natural numbers, when a new element is added, remains the same cardinality (aleph-null). This is not a violation of logic; it is a consequence of the definition of infinity. To insist that this is absurd is to insist that reality must conform to the axioms of finite arithmetic, which is a metaphysical assumption, not a logical necessity.

Craig’s use of the infinite library suffers from the same equivocation. The "contradiction" he identifies—that subtracting an infinite set can yield different results—is not a contradiction in set theory. The operation of set subtraction is well-defined: A \ B is the set of elements in A not in B. If A is the set of all natural numbers and B is the set of all odd numbers, then A \ B is the set of even numbers, which is infinite. If B is the set of all numbers greater than 3, then A \ B is {1, 2, 3}, which is finite. There is no contradiction here; there are simply two different subsets being subtracted. The "absurdity" only arises if one insists that subtracting an "equal" number of books (infinite) should yield the same result. But "equal" in cardinality does not mean "identical" in composition. The set of odd numbers and the set of numbers greater than 3 have the same cardinality (both are countably infinite), but they are different sets. The result of subtraction depends on which specific elements are removed, not merely on the cardinality of the removed set. Craig’s argument treats infinite collections as if they were finite bags of marbles, where subtracting half the marbles leaves half the marbles. But infinite sets do not behave that way, and there is no logical law that says they must. The "absurdity" is a product of our finite cognitive architecture, not of any formal inconsistency.

This brings us to the crux of the matter: the tension between finite human intuition and the properties of the actual infinite. Craig’s arguments are fundamentally appeals to intuition. He asks us to imagine a hotel and to feel the absurdity of a full hotel accepting more guests. But why should our ability to *imagine* a scenario dictate its *metaphysical possibility*? The history of science is replete with examples where reality defied our intuitions. Quantum mechanics tells us that a particle can be in two places at once, which is deeply counter-intuitive, yet it is empirically verified. Special relativity tells us that time dilates at high velocities, which violates our common-sense notion of absolute time. If we rejected every theory that failed to align with our finite intuitions, we would still be living in a Newtonian, Euclidean world. Craig might respond that the infinite is not merely counter-intuitive but logically contradictory, whereas quantum mechanics is merely strange. However, as we have seen, the "contradictions" he identifies are only contradictions if one presupposes the validity of finite arithmetic. The Principle of Correspondence is not a logical law; it is a definition within set theory. The fact that a part can equal the whole in cardinality is not a contradiction in the same way that "2+2=5" is a contradiction. It is a surprising property of infinite collections, but it is a coherent and rigorously defined property.

The deeper issue is that Craig conflates the *mathematical* infinite with the *ontological* infinite. A mathematical set is an abstract object, defined by its membership and relations. Hilbert’s Hotel is a thought experiment about a mathematical structure, not a description of a physical building. When Craig says that such a hotel cannot exist in reality, he is making a claim about the physical instantiation of infinite cardinalities. But why should physical reality be restricted to the properties of finite sets? Craig’s argument assumes that if an infinite collection existed, it would have to obey the same rules of arithmetic as finite collections. This is precisely what is at issue. The burden of proof is on Craig to show that physical reality cannot contain a collection that is Dedekind-infinite. He has not done so. He has merely shown that such a collection would behave differently than a finite collection, which is trivially true. The "absurdity" is not a logical contradiction but a violation of our psychological expectations.

The strongest of Craig’s arguments, and the one most relevant to LDS eternalism, is the argument from successive addition. Craig argues that an actual infinite cannot be formed by adding one member after another. If the past were beginningless, then the present moment would have been reached only after traversing an infinite number of past events. But one cannot traverse an infinite series, because there is no starting point. You can never arrive at the present if you have to count through an infinite number of prior moments. This argument is compelling because it appeals to our experience of time as a sequence of discrete, successive moments. However, this argument relies on a specific theory of time—the A-theory (or presentism), which holds that only the present moment is real and that time flows. If one adopts the B-theory of time (or eternalism), which holds that all moments in time are equally real and that time is a dimension like space, then the "traversal" problem dissolves. In the B-theory, the past is not something that was traversed; it is simply a coordinate in a four-dimensional spacetime manifold. The present moment is not a moving point but merely a subjective indexical. If the universe is a four-dimensional block, then the past being infinite is no more problematic than the spatial universe being infinite. We do not ask how we "traversed" an infinite spatial distance to get to our current location; we simply exist at a point in space. Similarly, in a beginningless B-theoretic universe, we simply exist at a point in time, and the infinite past is just a feature of the manifold.

Does LDS theology require a specific theory of time? LDS doctrine is notoriously flexible on this point, but it strongly leans towards an eternalist or at least a non-presentist view. LDS scripture teaches that "intelligence, or the light of truth, was not created or made, neither indeed can be" (D&C 93:29). This suggests that there is an eternal, uncreated substrate of reality, including time itself. LDS theology often speaks of God as being "eternal" and "without beginning of days or end of years" (Moses 1:3). This implies that God has always existed and that time is co-eternal with God. If time is beginningless, then the past is an actual infinite. Craig’s successive addition argument would imply that God could not have reached the present moment. However, if we adopt a B-theory of time, this objection evaporates. God does not "traverse" time; God exists in all times simultaneously. LDS theology has a strong tradition of this view, often referred to as "enlargement" or "expansion" of the soul, where God sees the end from the beginning. Joseph Smith taught that God "knows all things" because he "comprehendeth all things" in an eternal now. This is perfectly compatible with a B-theoretic eternalism.

Even if one insists on an A-theory of time, Craig’s successive addition argument is not fatal. The argument assumes that the past is composed of a discrete, countable series of events. But if time is continuous, then between any two moments there are infinitely many moments. In a single second, there are an infinite number of instants. If the universe has existed for a finite duration, say 13.8 billion years, then it has already traversed an infinite number of instants (since the continuum of time has an infinite number of points). Therefore, the "traversal" of an infinite series is not impossible; it happens every second. The problem is that Craig conflates a *countably* infinite series (like the natural numbers) with an *uncountably* infinite continuum (like the real numbers). The successive addition argument applies to counting discrete events, but time, as a physical quantity, is not necessarily discrete. If time is continuous, then the past being infinite is no more problematic than the past being finite; both contain an infinite number of instants. The "traversal" objection is a red herring based on a discrete model of time.

This brings us directly to the question of whether LDS doctrine can consistently accommodate an actual infinite despite Craig’s objections regarding arithmetic operations. Craig’s arithmetic objections—that 1 + infinity = infinity, or that infinity - infinity is indeterminate—are only problematic if one insists that these operations must yield intuitively satisfying results. But in transfinite mathematics, these results are perfectly well-defined. In LDS theology, the idea that God adds to his knowledge or that new souls are born into an eternal universe does not create a contradiction. If the number of past events is aleph-null, then adding one more event still yields aleph-null. This does not mean that the new event does not exist; it simply means that the cardinality of the set of past events remains the same. This is a mathematical fact, not an absurdity. Craig might argue that this is absurd because it violates the principle of "the whole is greater than the part." But this principle is an axiom of finite arithmetic, not a universal logical law. In set theory, the principle of correspondence replaces it. LDS theology can embrace this. God’s omniscience does not require that the "number" of facts he knows be finite; it can be infinite. The fact that adding a new fact doesn't change the cardinality of the set of facts God knows is not a limitation on God; it is simply a property of infinity.

Furthermore, Craig’s argument that the actual infinite cannot exist in the real world because it would lead to "absurdities" in the physical manipulation of objects (e.g., moving guests in Hilbert’s Hotel) is irrelevant to LDS theology. LDS theology does not posit a physical hotel with an infinite number of rooms. It posits an eternal God and an eternal universe. The "absurdities" Craig identifies arise from trying to *operate* on an infinite collection in a finite way—e.g., moving a guest from room 1 to room 2. But this operation requires a *finite* amount of time to complete for each move, and Craig assumes that the infinite number of moves would require an infinite amount of time. However, in an eternal universe, there is an infinite amount of time. The manager of Hilbert’s Hotel could move the guests if he had an infinite amount of time, which he does. The "absurdity" of the hotel is not a logical contradiction but a practical impossibility for a finite being. For an infinite being like God, such operations are trivial. Craig’s thought experiments are anthropomorphic; they project the limitations of finite agents onto an infinite reality.

Perhaps the most profound flaw in Craig’s argument is his implicit commitment to a particular metaphysical framework that LDS theology does not share. Craig is a classical theist who holds to divine simplicity, timelessness (in a qualified sense), and a strong doctrine of creation *ex nihilo*. For Craig, the universe must have a beginning because God created it out of nothing. LDS theology rejects creation *ex nihilo*, holding instead that God organized pre-existing, eternal matter and intelligence. This metaphysical difference is crucial. Craig’s arguments against the actual infinite are designed to support the *kalam* argument’s second premise: the universe began to exist. If the universe began to exist, then there must be a transcendent cause. LDS theology agrees that the *organized* universe (the cosmos as we know it) had a beginning, but it insists that the underlying reality—matter, intelligence, and time—is eternal. Craig’s arguments, if successful, would rule out this eternal substrate. But as we have seen, they are not successful. The actual infinite is not logically impossible; it is merely counter-intuitive. Therefore, LDS theology can maintain its doctrine of eternal matter and time without falling into contradiction.

In conclusion, William Lane Craig’s philosophical arguments against the actual infinite—Hilbert’s Hotel, the Principle of Correspondence, the infinite library, and the successive addition argument—fail to demonstrate the impossibility of a beginningless past. These arguments are powerful rhetorical devices that exploit the natural limitations of finite human intuition, but they do not establish logical contradictions. The "absurdities" Craig identifies are simply the properties of transfinite mathematics, which are rigorously defined and internally consistent. The Principle of Correspondence is not a contradiction but a definition of infinity. The infinite library yields different results for subtraction because different subsets are being subtracted, not because of a logical error. The successive addition argument fails because it assumes a discrete, A-theoretic view of time that LDS theology is not obligated to accept. LDS eternalism, with its emphasis on uncreated intelligence, eternal matter, and a God who exists in an eternal now, provides a coherent metaphysical framework that can accommodate the actual infinite. The tension between finite intuition and infinite reality is not a sign of impossibility but a reminder of the profound mystery of eternity. Craig’s arguments may persuade those who already accept a finite past, but they do not constitute a decisive refutation of beginningless time. The infinite remains a logical possibility, and LDS theology stands as a robust alternative to the finite, temporal finitism that Craig champions. The human mind, bound by the finite categories of space and time, may struggle to grasp the infinite, but that struggle is a reflection of our limitations, not a proof of the infinite’s impossibility.
