# Emotion, Arational Action, and the Limits of the Belief–Desire Model

## Introduction

Donald Davidson’s theory of intentional action is one of the most influential versions of the belief–desire model. According to Davidson, an agent acts intentionally when the action is caused in the appropriate way by a reason that rationalizes it. A reason, in its standard form, consists of a pro-attitude toward some end together with a belief about how the action will promote that end. If I switch on a light because I want the room illuminated and believe that pressing the switch will illuminate it, my action is intentional. The desire supplies the motivational element, while the belief supplies the action’s intelligible connection with the world. Davidson’s theory is causal, but it is not merely mechanical: the cause must also constitute a reason for which the action was performed.

Rosalind Hursthouse’s discussion of “arational actions” challenges the assumption that all intentional action must be explained by a belief–desire pair. Her examples concern actions performed under the pressure of emotion: an angry person strikes someone, a frightened person runs away, or a jealous person destroys an object. Such conduct may be intelligible in light of the emotion, but it need not be guided by a belief about what the action will achieve, nor by a desire for that achievement. The agent may act in the grip of anger or fear without deliberating, without representing an end, and without conceiving the action as a means to anything. The action is not necessarily irrational, because the agent may not be reasoning badly at all. Yet it is not wholly unintelligible or merely accidental. Hursthouse therefore proposes a category of action that is “arational”: action that is motivated by an emotion but is neither rationally selected as a means nor irrationally selected through defective reasoning.

The significance of this proposal depends partly on how we understand emotional episodes. A useful distinction is between **pre-named** and **post-named** epistemic states. A pre-named emotional state is one in which an affective disturbance is already shaping conduct before the agent identifies, classifies, or names it as anger, fear, shame, jealousy, or resentment. A post-named state is one in which the agent can retrospectively or contemporaneously recognize the emotion and organize an explanation around it. This distinction is not a distinction between two different emotions. It is a distinction between two levels of the agent’s epistemic relation to an emotion: the emotion’s immediate motivational operation and the later conceptual grasp of that operation.

The distinction helps illuminate the force of Hursthouse’s challenge. In a pre-named outburst, the agent may perform an action that is caused by an occurrent emotional state before possessing any corresponding belief–desire pair. There may be no desire to injure, no belief that injury will accomplish a goal, and perhaps not even a fully formed belief about the situation. The action therefore appears to resist Davidson’s standard causal account. Yet the challenge is not decisive in its simplest form. Davidson can respond that a desire need not be linguistically formulated or consciously entertained, and that an emotion may itself incorporate both evaluative cognition and motivation. If every emotionally driven action can be redescribed as caused by a suitably broad pro-attitude and belief, Hursthouse’s counterexamples lose their force. But such a response may make the belief–desire theory vacuous: if every motivational state counts as a desire and every affective registration of a situation counts as a belief, the theory no longer explains why actions are intentional; it merely redescribes their causes.

The central issue is therefore not whether emotions cause action. Clearly they do. The issue is whether all intentional action must be explained through the structure of a reason as Davidson understands it. This essay argues that Hursthouse successfully undermines a strong version of the belief–desire theory, but not Davidson’s entire account of intentional action. Pre-named emotional outbursts reveal that some actions are purposive, intelligible, and attributable to an agent without being selected through a belief–desire pair. They require a more pluralistic framework of intentionality, one that recognizes affective motivation and action-readiness as distinct from practical reasoning. At the same time, not every emotionally caused movement is an arational action. Some episodes are genuinely reflexive or involuntary. The appropriate conclusion is therefore neither that all emotional outbursts remain intentional nor that they all collapse into reflexes. Rather, intentionality has gradations and forms: emotional actions can be minimally intentional without being deliberatively intentional.

This conclusion also clarifies the moral question. If an agent performs a pre-named action unintentionally, direct culpability for the action itself may be inappropriate. Nevertheless, the agent may be indirectly accountable when they possessed the capacity and opportunity to moderate, interrupt, or avoid the emotional disposition that generated the outburst. This form of responsibility depends not on the action’s having been intentionally chosen at the moment, but on the agent’s earlier failure to regulate a foreseeable tendency. Yet the appeal to preventative control must be carefully limited. Mere abstract ability to control emotion is insufficient for culpability; what matters is reasonable foreseeability, practical opportunity, and the agent’s actual capacity under the circumstances.

## Davidson’s belief–desire theory

Davidson’s account begins with the idea that intentional action is action performed for a reason. To give a reason for an action is to identify a pro-attitude toward some kind of action or outcome and a belief that the action is relevant to that outcome. Suppose a person opens an umbrella because she wants to remain dry and believes that opening the umbrella will protect her from the rain. The desire explains why remaining dry matters to her, while the belief explains why opening the umbrella appears appropriate. Together they constitute a primary reason.

Davidson’s distinctive contribution is to insist that a reason must also be a cause. A person may have a desire to remain dry and believe that an umbrella would help, but if she opens the umbrella accidentally while reaching for something else, that reason does not explain the action in the relevant sense. An intentional action is not merely an action for which a reason can be supplied after the fact. The motivating reason must cause the action.

This causal requirement is intended to avoid an overly intellectualist account. The agent need not consciously reason through premises and a conclusion. A desire and belief can operate spontaneously, habitually, or unconsciously. Nevertheless, the action is explained by a rationalizing structure: the agent is oriented toward an end and sees the action as connected to that end. The theory thus combines three ideas:

1. intentional actions are actions for reasons;
2. reasons involve a motivational pro-attitude and a world-directed belief;
3. the reason must cause the action.

This framework has considerable explanatory power. It distinguishes intentional action from bodily movements caused by spasms, seizures, reflexes, or external force. It also explains why intentional actions are intelligible from the first-person perspective. To understand why someone raised a hand, one wants to know what they were trying to do or accomplish and what they believed about the circumstances.

The difficulty arises when conduct is not organized around an explicitly represented end. An angry person may slap another person before thinking, “I want to hurt this person,” or even before thinking, “This person has wronged me.” A frightened person may flee without believing that fleeing will produce safety. A person overwhelmed by humiliation may shout or destroy something without wanting any identifiable result. The action can be understood as an expression or eruption of an emotion, but not necessarily as a means chosen in light of a belief.

One response is to expand the theory. The desire need not be a desire for a specific outcome; it might be a desire to strike, escape, silence, or retaliate. The belief need not be articulated; it might be an implicit perception that the other is threatening, insulting, or dangerous. This strategy preserves the formal structure of the theory. But it raises a question about theoretical content. If an angry action is explained by an angry motivation, and the angry motivation is simply classified as a desire, then the theory may have changed the vocabulary without explaining the emotional phenomenon. The distinctive point about the action is precisely that it is not mediated by an ordinary end-directed desire and instrumental belief.

## Hursthouse and the category of arational action

Hursthouse’s notion of arational action is designed to occupy the space between rational and irrational conduct. Rational action is guided by reasons in a way that makes the action an appropriate means to an end, at least from the agent’s perspective. Irrational action is also reason-guided in this sense, but the reasoning is defective: the agent’s beliefs may be false, the means may be inappropriate, or the desire may conflict with better reasons. Arational action is different. It is not the result of practical reasoning that can be assessed as rational or irrational. It is motivated by something—especially emotion—but not by a reason in the ordinary belief–desire structure.

Consider an angry person who strikes someone in an outburst. If we say that she struck the person because she wanted to hurt him and believed that striking would hurt him, then the action appears intentional and perhaps irrational. But this may misdescribe the episode. The person may not have wanted pain as an end. She may not have represented the strike as a means. She may not have had enough cognitive distance from the emotion to form a judgment about what she was doing. The action was not selected after a practical assessment; it was emitted by an emotional state.

Yet it would be misleading to call the strike a mere reflex. A reflex is normally a movement triggered by a stimulus through a relatively fixed physiological mechanism, such as the withdrawal of a hand from a hot surface. Emotional outbursts often have a different character. They are responsive to the agent’s social and evaluative situation. They express anger, fear, contempt, or shame. They may be flexible, context-sensitive, and directed toward a person or object. The agent may later recognize the conduct as “something I did,” even while denying that it was something deliberately chosen. The action therefore has a form of intelligibility and agency that a reflex lacks.

The term “arational” marks precisely this middle position. The action is not governed by a reason, but it is not random. It is intelligible through the affective organization of the agent. Emotion is not merely an internal push that produces bodily movement. It establishes an action tendency: anger prepares the agent to confront, attack, or retaliate; fear prepares the agent to flee, freeze, or defend; shame prepares the agent to hide, withdraw, or repair a social relationship. These tendencies can guide conduct without becoming beliefs and desires in the Davidsonian sense.

Hursthouse’s point is strongest against a theory that identifies intentionality with the presence of a rationalizing reason. If an agent can act in a way that is expressive, directed, and attributable to an emotional state without acting for a belief–desire reason, then the belief–desire theory is incomplete. It may still explain a large and important class of actions, especially deliberative actions, but it cannot claim universal scope.

## Pre-named and post-named epistemic states

The distinction between pre-named and post-named emotional states sharpens this issue. The distinction concerns the timing and availability of conceptual recognition.

In a **pre-named state**, the emotion is operative before the agent has identified it. The agent may experience physiological arousal, attentional narrowing, bodily tension, and an impulse toward a characteristic action. The person may not yet know whether what they feel is anger, fear, shame, or resentment. Indeed, the person may lack any explicit judgment about what is happening internally. The emotional state nevertheless alters perception and conduct. A minor gesture is seen as threatening; an ambiguous remark is heard as insulting; escape or retaliation becomes immediately salient.

In a **post-named state**, the agent has acquired some conceptual grasp of the emotion. The naming may occur during the episode—“I am furious”—or afterward—“I was acting out of jealousy.” Once the emotion is named, the agent can often form beliefs and desires about it. The angry person may think, “He insulted me,” “I want him to feel what I felt,” or “If I confront him, he will stop.” The original outburst may then be incorporated into an ordinary practical explanation.

Naming does not create the emotion. Nor does failure to name it make the episode unintelligible. Rather, naming makes available a reflective level at which the agent can classify, evaluate, and potentially regulate the emotional state. The difference between pre-named and post-named states is therefore epistemic and practical. Before naming, the emotion may be experienced as an undifferentiated pressure or impulse. After naming, it becomes an object of thought and possible control.

The pre-named case poses the sharper problem for Davidson. If the agent has not yet formed a belief about the relevant situation and has no articulated desire for an outcome, then the conventional primary reason is absent. Suppose a person hears a sudden insult, feels a surge of anger, and throws a glass before understanding what they are doing. The glass-throwing is not naturally explained by the desire to break the glass or by a belief that breaking it will achieve some purpose. The action is an immediate enactment of an emotional state.

A post-named version may be different. If the agent recognizes the anger and thinks, “I want to frighten him, and throwing the glass will frighten him,” then the action may be intentional in Davidson’s sense, even if the emotion supplies the motivational energy. The emotion has been integrated into a belief–desire structure. This does not mean that naming always transforms an action into an intentional one. A named emotion can still overwhelm the agent. But naming creates the possibility of reflective mediation.

The distinction also reveals why retrospective explanation must be treated cautiously. After an outburst, an agent may say, “I hit him because I was angry.” This explanation is genuine, but it does not by itself show that the agent had an antecedent desire and belief. The post-named description may be a conceptual reconstruction of a pre-named episode. It tells us what emotion caused the conduct, not necessarily what reason the agent acted upon at the time. We must distinguish the truth of an emotional explanation from the presence of a Davidsonian primary reason.

## How pre-named actions challenge causal accounts

Pre-named actions challenge standard causal accounts in two related ways. First, they challenge the causal **content** of the explanation. Second, they challenge the assumption that causal explanation and rationalization coincide.

The first challenge concerns the absence of a named emotion. If an agent cannot yet identify the state as anger or fear, then the action is not caused by a consciously represented emotion. This matters because a simple “emotion caused the action” explanation may seem too coarse. The emotion is not functioning as an explicit reason. It is more like an occurrent condition that organizes attention, perception, and bodily readiness.

The second challenge concerns the absence of a belief–desire pair. The agent does not necessarily desire the action’s consequences or believe that the action will bring them about. The action may be a direct expression of affect rather than an instrument for an end. The angry person does not strike in order to achieve a represented objective; striking is simply what the anger makes immediately available.

A Davidsonian can reply that the theory never required the belief and desire to be named. Human beings act on implicit beliefs and unarticulated desires all the time. An animal can flee because it desires safety and believes, in a primitive or nonconceptual sense, that escape is useful. Similarly, an angry person may be said to possess an implicit desire to retaliate and an implicit belief that the target is an appropriate object of retaliation. On this view, pre-naming is irrelevant. The states need not be linguistically or reflectively accessible.

This reply is plausible up to a point. Davidson’s theory need not be intellectualist. If “belief” and “desire” are understood broadly enough to include nonconscious, nonlinguistic, and affect-laden representations, many emotional actions may fit the model. But the broader the categories become, the less the theory distinguishes itself from a general theory of motivational causation. There is a significant difference between an agent’s being directed toward an end and an agent’s being swept into a pattern of action by an emotion. If every action tendency is called a desire, and every affective appraisal is called a belief, the theory loses the ability to explain this difference.

Furthermore, emotions are not obviously reducible to either component. An emotion can include an appraisal, bodily feeling, attention, expressive posture, and action tendency. Its motivational power may not derive from a separate desire added to a belief. Anger is not necessarily a neutral perception of insult plus an independent desire to retaliate. The evaluative perception and the impulse may be parts of one integrated affective state. The emotion’s causal role is therefore not always modular in the way the belief–desire model suggests.

Hursthouse does not need to show that no emotional action can be redescribed in belief–desire terms. She needs only to show that such redescription is not always the best explanation and that the theory’s categories are not exhaustive. On that weaker claim, the argument succeeds. Pre-named actions demonstrate that an agent can be affected by an emotion in a way that is action-guiding but not reason-guided. They are cases in which the causal history of action includes an occurrent affective orientation without a prior practical judgment.

Still, the challenge should not be overstated. Davidson’s causal theory is not refuted merely because a person lacks conscious awareness of a belief or desire. Nor does the absence of a named emotion entail the absence of all cognition. A pre-named emotional state may involve implicit evaluations: the person may perceive another as threatening or insulting even without conceptualizing the perception. Thus, Hursthouse’s examples undermine a narrow and explicitly propositional reading of Davidson, but they do not disprove every functional reconstruction of the belief–desire model.

The most defensible conclusion is that Davidson’s theory is a theory of one important form of intentional action—reason-guided action—not a complete theory of agency. Hursthouse shows that action can be intelligible and agent-related without being organized by practical reasoning.

## The normative dilemma: culpability without intention

The problem becomes especially difficult when the pre-named action is morally objectionable. If an agent lashes out in anger before recognizing the anger, is the agent morally culpable? At first glance, there is a dilemma.

On one side, if the action was not intentional, then it may seem unfair to hold the agent responsible for it. Moral culpability commonly presupposes some form of control, awareness, or voluntariness. A person who involuntarily sneezes into another’s face is not culpable in the same way as a person who intentionally spits. If an emotional outburst is genuinely pre-named and arational, perhaps it should be treated like an involuntary movement.

On the other side, emotional outbursts are not always morally exempt. People are often blamed for uncontrolled anger, drunken aggression, cruelty under stress, or repeated failures to restrain themselves. If an agent knows that they become violent when provoked and repeatedly places themselves in situations where violence is likely, it seems inadequate to say that the final blow was unintentional. The agent may not have chosen the blow at the moment of action, but they may have been responsible for allowing the relevant disposition to develop or remain unchecked.

This produces the distinction between **direct** and **indirect** accountability. Direct accountability concerns the outburst as performed. If the action was not guided by an intention or conscious choice, direct culpability may be reduced or absent. Indirect accountability concerns prior conduct and character formation: failing to develop emotional regulation, refusing reasonable preventative measures, entering a predictable confrontation, or neglecting to withdraw when signs of escalation appeared.

The capacity for intentional preventative emotion moderation can bridge the gap between unintentional action and moral accountability. An agent may be unable to interrupt the outburst once the emotional state has reached a certain intensity, yet still be able to intervene earlier. The person may notice bodily signs of anger, avoid particular provocations, leave the room, seek assistance, develop strategies of self-control, or refrain from using intoxicants that predictably weaken inhibition. If the agent has a reasonable opportunity to take such measures and culpably refuses, then the eventual outburst may be indirectly attributable to that earlier failure.

This argument resembles familiar responsibility practices. A person who loses control because of a foreseeable and preventable condition may be blamed not simply for the moment of loss of control but for creating or failing to prevent the condition. The relevant intention may therefore occur before the outburst. The agent need not have intended to strike at the moment of striking; it may be enough that they intentionally cultivated, entered, or failed to interrupt the circumstances that made striking likely.

However, the appeal to preventative capacity must not become a blanket justification for blaming people for every emotional failure. The mere fact that a person could, in some abstract sense, have controlled an emotion does not establish culpability. Several conditions matter.

First, the danger must have been reasonably foreseeable. If the outburst was unprecedented and no warning signs were available, indirect responsibility is weak. Second, the agent must have had a practical opportunity to intervene. Advice such as “just calm down” is not a meaningful option when the emotional episode has already overwhelmed the agent’s capacities. Third, the preventative measure must be proportionate and realistically available. It is unreasonable to demand perfect emotional mastery or total avoidance of ordinary social situations. Fourth, the agent’s control must be assessed in light of psychological, social, and medical conditions. Some individuals have substantially impaired regulatory capacities, and moral judgment should reflect that fact.

There is also a danger in treating every emotional disposition as self-created. People inherit temperamental tendencies and are shaped by trauma, social conditions, education, and relationships. An agent may be responsible for responding to a disposition without being its ultimate author. Indirect accountability should therefore be graded rather than binary. The agent may deserve criticism, excuse, mitigation, treatment, or protection from dangerous circumstances rather than straightforward blame.

The normative lesson is that intentionality and responsibility cannot simply be identified. An action may be unintentional at the moment while still being attributable through a prior failure of self-management. Conversely, an intentional action may be excused or mitigated by coercion, severe impairment, or extraordinary circumstances. The moral question must therefore ask both: Was the outburst intentional? And what control did the agent have over the conditions that produced it?

## Purely occurrent emotion: revised intentionality or reflex?

The deepest conceptual question is whether actions driven purely by occurrent emotion should be treated as a revised form of intentional action or as unintentional reflexes. Two tempting answers must be resisted.

The first is to preserve an overly expansive notion of intention. On this approach, every emotionally caused movement is intentional because it originates in the agent’s motivational system. But this collapses the distinction between agency and mere causation. A seizure, startle response, or involuntary kick may originate in the person’s body without being something the person did intentionally. Not everything caused by an agent is an action attributable to the agent as an intentional performance.

The second is to classify all pre-named emotional conduct as reflexive. This preserves a sharp distinction between intention and involuntary movement, but it ignores the structure of emotional behavior. Anger and fear are not simple stimulus-response mechanisms. They interpret situations, organize attention, and direct conduct toward objects. Emotional actions can be modified by context and can express the agent’s evaluative orientation even when they are not consciously chosen. Calling all of them reflexes removes the agency that makes moral and psychological explanation possible.

A better framework distinguishes at least three dimensions:

1. **Voluntary control:** whether the agent could initiate, inhibit, or redirect the movement at the relevant time.
2. **Practical intentionality:** whether the agent represented an end and acted through a belief–desire reason.
3. **Affective directedness:** whether the conduct expressed an emotion’s orientation toward a person, object, or situation.

A conventional intentional action scores highly on all three dimensions. A reflex scores low on all three. An arational emotional action may have low practical intentionality but substantial affective directedness, and perhaps some limited degree of voluntary control. This intermediate structure explains why such actions are not ordinary intentional actions while also not being mere reflexes.

The concept of **minimal intentionality** is useful here. An action may be minimally intentional when it is an organized, agent-relative response to a perceived situation, even if it is not performed under a consciously represented reason. Minimal intentionality does not require a belief–desire pair. It requires that the movement be part of the agent’s affective engagement with the world rather than a purely physiological event. A frightened person’s sudden flight may be minimally intentional because it is directed away from danger, even if the person does not formulate the thought that running will increase safety. An angry person’s gesture may be minimally intentional because it is expressive of confrontation, even if the person does not intend a particular consequence.

This revised notion should not be confused with saying that the agent fully intended the outcome. A person may minimally intend an aggressive gesture without intending injury; may intend escape without intending to abandon others; may intend expression without intending humiliation. The moral and legal significance of an action depends heavily on these distinctions.

A pluralistic theory of intentionality would therefore recognize at least two central kinds of agency. **Reason-guided agency** consists in action caused by beliefs and desires that rationalize the action. **Emotion-guided agency** consists in conduct organized by an occurrent affective state, often without deliberative mediation. The second kind can be arational rather than irrational. It can be intelligible and attributable without being selected as a means to an end.

Does this revised framework undermine Davidson? It undermines the claim that the belief–desire structure is necessary for every intentional action. It does not undermine the claim that, where an action is performed for a reason, the reason is appropriately understood as a cause. Davidson remains valuable for explaining deliberative and instrumentally structured conduct. His theory also provides an important contrast case: it tells us what is missing in pre-named emotional action. What is missing is not all agency, but the rationalizing relation between represented ends, beliefs, and action.

There is a further possibility: perhaps Davidson’s theory can be revised rather than rejected. The notion of a primary reason could be expanded to include nonpropositional affective orientations. Instead of identifying every such orientation with a desire, the theory could distinguish desires, evaluative perceptions, emotions, habits, and action-readinesses as different kinds of motivating states. The causal condition could remain, but the rationalizing condition would be pluralized. Some actions would be explained by reasons; others by emotions that make the action intelligible without rationalizing it.

Such a theory would preserve Davidson’s insight that intentional action requires an appropriate connection between an agent and the action while rejecting the assumption that this connection must always be a belief–desire relation. It would also accommodate the pre-named/post-named distinction. Post-named emotion may become integrated into practical reasoning; pre-named emotion may guide action in a more immediate and nonconceptual manner.

## Conclusion

Hursthouse’s theory of arational action successfully challenges the strong form of Donald Davidson’s belief–desire theory. Pre-named emotional outbursts expose cases in which an agent acts under the influence of an occurrent emotion before identifying that emotion and without possessing a corresponding belief–desire pair. The action may be expressive, directed, intelligible, and attributable, yet not performed for a reason in the standard Davidsonian sense. A retrospective statement such as “I did it because I was angry” identifies an emotional cause but does not prove that the agent acted from an antecedent desire and belief.

Davidson can respond that beliefs and desires may be implicit, nonconscious, or nonlinguistic. That response shows that pre-naming alone does not refute the theory. But if all emotional action tendencies are redescribed as desires and all affective appraisals as beliefs, the theory risks becoming explanatorily empty. Hursthouse’s more important achievement is to reveal that the motivational architecture of emotion need not have the same structure as practical reasoning.

The moral implications are equally important. A pre-named outburst may be unintentional at the moment of performance, and this can limit direct culpability. Yet an agent may be indirectly accountable when they could reasonably have anticipated and moderated the emotional tendency through earlier avoidance, preparation, withdrawal, or self-regulation. Such accountability must remain sensitive to foreseeability, opportunity, proportionality, and psychological capacity. Responsibility cannot be grounded in a mere abstract capacity to control emotion.

Finally, emotionally driven action should neither be assimilated entirely to intentional choice nor reduced entirely to reflex. Some emotional movements are reflexive or involuntary. Others possess a minimal intentionality grounded in affective directedness rather than belief–desire reasoning. The best account therefore requires a revised, pluralistic framework of intentionality. Davidson explains reason-guided action; Hursthouse identifies action that is arational but still agentive. Together, they suggest that human agency is not a single mechanism but a layered field in which deliberation, emotion, habit, and bodily responsiveness can each organize conduct in different ways.
