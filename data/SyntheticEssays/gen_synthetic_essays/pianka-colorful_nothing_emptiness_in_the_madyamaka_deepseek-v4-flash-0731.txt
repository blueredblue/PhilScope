### The Middle Way Between Ground and Abyss: Emptiness, Causation, and the Limits of Explanation

The history of philosophy is, in large part, a history of the quest for a ground—a search for an immutable substrate beneath the flux of experience, a fundamental principle that explains why things are as they are and not otherwise. From Plato’s Forms to Aristotle’s substance, from Descartes’ *res cogitans* to Kant’s transcendental unity of apperception, the human intellect has sought a foundational reality that anchors the world. The Madhyamaka school of Mahāyāna Buddhism, articulated with ruthless precision by Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE) and elaborated by Chandrakīrti (c. 600–650 CE), represents one of the most radical and sophisticated challenges to this foundationalist impulse. At its core lies the doctrine of *śūnyatā*—emptiness—which posits that all phenomena, including the self and the physical world, are devoid of inherent existence (*svabhāva*). Yet, this is not a simple nihilism. Madhyamaka claims to chart a "Middle Way" between the extremes of eternalism (material realism) and annihilationism (nihilism). This essay will evaluate this claim, first by examining Nāgārjuna’s refutation of causation, which dismantles the notion of inherent essence, and second by analyzing Chandrakīrti’s Sevenfold Reasoning, which applies this critique to the self and physical objects. The central thesis is that Madhyamaka’s conception of emptiness, particularly the doctrine of the "emptiness of emptiness," provides a sophisticated resolution to the paradox of dependent designation, but it does so by redefining the very nature of explanation. While this successfully avoids the absurdities of realism and nihilism, it leaves a significant tension: the collapse of ultimate ontology, while not leading to *arbitrary* natural regularities in a conventional sense, does necessitate a pragmatic and therapeutic re-framing of what it means to "explain" the world, a move that critics may rightly see as a form of explanatory non-foundationalism that borders on arbitrariness if judged by classical metaphysical standards.

#### I. The Refutation of Causation: Dissolving the Essence of Things

To understand emptiness, one must first understand what it is empty *of*. The target of Madhyamaka’s critique is *svabhāva*, often translated as "inherent existence," "own-being," or "intrinsic nature." A thing possesses *svabhāva* if it exists independently, autonomously, and essentially—if its existence is not contingent upon anything else. For a realist, a physical object like a tree has *svabhāva*; it is what it is regardless of its relations to other things. Similarly, the self has *svabhāva* if it is a permanent, unchanging, and independent entity. Nāgārjuna’s genius lies in his realization that if anything possessed *svabhāva*, it could not enter into relations—most fundamentally, it could not participate in causation. Causation is the very fabric of the empirical world; things arise, persist, and cease in dependence on other things. Therefore, if Nāgārjuna can demonstrate that causation is conceptually incoherent when applied to entities with *svabhāva*, he will have shown that *svabhāva* is a metaphysical fiction.

The opening chapter of Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK) is a masterclass in deconstructive logic. He examines the four possible ways in which an effect could arise from a cause: (1) from itself, (2) from another, (3) from both, or (4) from neither. This is the *catuṣkoṭi* (tetralemma). Nāgārjuna systematically refutes each option. If an effect arises from itself, it is already existent, making its "arising" redundant and meaningless; there is no transition from non-existence to existence. If an effect arises from another, strictly speaking, this implies that the cause is completely independent of the effect. But if the cause and effect are utterly independent, there is no connecting link between them. Anything could arise from anything else—a sprout could arise from a rock. The relationship of "causing" would be purely arbitrary and empirically ungroundable. The third option, arising from both, inherits the defects of the first two. The fourth option, arising from neither, is a logical absurdity, as it posits an effect with no causal history whatsoever.

Nāgārjuna’s point is not to prove that causation does not occur in the conventional world—fire does burn wood, and seeds do produce sprouts. Rather, he is demonstrating that *causation cannot be understood as a real, objective relation between independently existing entities*. The very concept of a cause presupposes a determinate effect, and the concept of an effect presupposes a determinate cause. They are mutually dependent designations. If we attempt to ground this relation in the *svabhāva* of the entities involved, we collapse into incoherence. The cause cannot contain the effect (otherwise it is not a cause but the effect itself), and the effect cannot be entirely absent from the cause (otherwise there is no connection). Therefore, the notion of causal power—a hidden essence that makes a cause produce its effect—is a chimera. What we call "causation" is simply the conventional observation of regular, dependent sequences. The seed is a cause of the sprout only because the sprout arises in dependence on the seed; there is no metaphysical "glue" binding them.

This refutation has profound implications. If things lack *svabhāva*, they lack an unchanging essence. They are, in Nāgārjuna’s terms, "empty." But crucially, emptiness is not nothingness. It is the very fact that things are *dependent*. In the famous verse from MMK XXIV:18, Nāgārjuna states: "Whatever is dependent arising, that is emptiness. That is dependent designation. That is the middle way." Emptiness is synonymous with dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*). Things do exist, but they exist only in relation to other things, only as a result of causes and conditions, and only as conceptual designations. The tree exists, but it exists as a nexus of relations—soil, water, sunlight, seeds, and the human mind that labels it "tree." It has no independent, self-powered existence. By refuting causation, Nāgārjuna does not destroy the world; he destroys the *ground* of the world, the idea that the world is a collection of self-standing substances.

#### II. Chandrakīrti’s Sevenfold Reasoning: The Chariot and the Self

If Nāgārjuna’s refutation of causation is the logical foundation, Chandrakīrti’s Sevenfold Reasoning is its phenomenological application. In his *Madhyamakāvatāra* (Entering the Middle Way), Chandrakīrti uses the example of a chariot—a composite object—to analyze the relationship between a whole and its parts. This analysis serves as a template for understanding all composite phenomena, including physical objects (the aggregates of form, feeling, perception, etc.) and, most importantly, the self. The question is: What is the chariot? It is not its wheels, its axle, its chassis, or its poles. It is not the sum of these parts, nor is it something separate from them. Chandrakīrti systematically examines seven possible ways in which the chariot could exist relative to its parts:

1.  **The chariot is the same as its parts.** This is untenable, because the chariot is a unity, while the parts are multiple. If the chariot were the same as its parts, then each part would be the chariot, and the whole would be a mere aggregation without a distinct identity.
2.  **The chariot is different from its parts.** This posits a chariot that exists independently of its constituent parts. But if we remove all the parts, no chariot remains. A "chariot" separate from wheels and axle is a pure abstraction, a phantom.
3.  **The chariot possesses its parts.** This presupposes the existence of the chariot as an owner prior to the parts it owns. But there is no chariot to be found apart from the parts; the owner is a conceptual projection.
4.  **The chariot depends on its parts.** This is subtly flawed. If the chariot depends on the parts, it is not identical to them, but it also cannot exist without them. However, this dependence is one-sided; the parts do not depend on the chariot. This posits a chariot that is a kind of emergent property, but emergent properties still require a substrate. Chandrakīrti argues that this "dependence" is merely nominal.
5.  **The parts depend on the chariot.** This reverses the relationship, suggesting that the parts are defined by their role in the whole. But a wheel is a wheel because of its shape and function, not because it is part of a chariot. The wheel could exist in a wagon. This dependence is also merely conceptual.
6.  **The chariot is the combination of parts.** This is the same as the "sum" theory. But a mere aggregation of parts does not constitute a functional unity. A pile of chariot parts is not a chariot; it is a pile. The arrangement matters, but even an arrangement is just a spatial relation, not a chariot.
7.  **The chariot is the shape of the parts.** This reduces the chariot to a geometrical form. But a shape is an abstraction, not a concrete entity. Furthermore, the shape of a chariot is dependent on the specific arrangement; it is not an intrinsic property.

Chandrakīrti concludes that the chariot is not found anywhere in any of these seven ways. It does not exist *ultimately* (*paramārtha-sat*). Yet, we use the word "chariot" successfully; we ride in it, we pull it, we see it. Therefore, the chariot exists *conventionally* (*saṃvṛti-sat*). It is a *prajñapti*—a designation, a label, a mental construct that is applied to a collection of parts in a certain arrangement. The chariot is real in the sense that it functions, but it is not real in the sense of having an inherent, independent essence.

This analysis is directly applied to the self. The self, or person (*pudgala*), is not the same as the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness), nor is it different from them. It does not possess them, nor do they possess it. It is not the combination of the aggregates, nor is it their shape. Just as the chariot is a designation imputed upon its parts, the self is a designation imputed upon the continuously changing flow of psychophysical aggregates. There is no unchanging "I" behind the thoughts, feelings, and perceptions. The "self" is a convenient label for a process, not a substance.

This has a dual effect. On the one hand, it demolishes the notion of a Cartesian *ego*, a soul, or a permanent self. On the other hand, it does not deny that we exist as functioning beings. We act, we suffer, we strive for liberation. The conventional self is a valid basis for moral responsibility and spiritual practice. The mistake of the "eternalist" is to reify this conventional self into an ultimate, unchanging entity. The mistake of the "nihilist" is to conclude that because there is no ultimate self, there is no self at all, and therefore morality and practice are meaningless. Madhyamaka avoids both by affirming the conventional reality of the self while denying its ultimate reality.

#### III. The Middle Way: Avoiding Material Realism and Nihilism

The core of Madhyamaka’s claim to be a "Middle Way" lies in its navigation between these two extremes. Material realism, or more broadly, essentialism, holds that physical phenomena and the self possess *svabhāva*. This view, Nāgārjuna argues, leads to the extreme of "existence" (*śāśvata*). If things have an inherent nature, they are eternal in their essence. They cannot truly arise or cease; they merely manifest or conceal their pre-existing nature. This makes change illusory and leads to a static, substantialist metaphysics. Furthermore, as shown in the refutation of causation, this view is logically incoherent, because it cannot explain how such entities interact.

Nihilism, on the other hand, holds that because things lack *svabhāva*, they do not exist at all. This is the extreme of "non-existence" (*uccheda*). If nothing exists, then the world is a void, a hallucination, and there is no basis for moral distinctions or cause and effect. Nāgārjuna is adamant that emptiness is *not* nihilism. To say that a chariot is empty is not to say there is no chariot; it is to say that the chariot lacks an independent essence. The chariot functions, it carries people, it is a chariot. Emptiness does not negate conventional existence; it negates the *mode* of existence that we falsely project onto it.

This is where the famous equation of emptiness and dependent origination is crucial. The fact that something is empty is precisely the reason it can exist and function. If a seed had a fixed, unchanging nature, it could never become a sprout. It is because the seed is empty—because it lacks a permanent essence—that it can undergo change and produce an effect. Emptiness is the condition for the possibility of change, causation, and multiplicity. A truly real, independent entity would be frozen, isolated, and incapable of interaction. The world of our experience—dynamic, interconnected, and plural—is only possible because its constituents are empty.

Thus, the absence of inherent essence in physical phenomena and the self does not lead to a collapse into nothingness. It leads to a profound re-understanding of reality as a dynamic, relational process. The physical world is not a collection of atoms with intrinsic properties; it is a web of interdependent events. The self is not a soul-substance; it is a stream of consciousness and physical processes. This view avoids the absurdity of realism (which cannot explain change or interaction) and the absurdity of nihilism (which cannot explain the conventional efficacy of the world). The world is a dependently originated, conventionally designated reality. It is "like a dream, like an illusion, like a bubble, like a shadow." But a dream is not nothing; it is a real experience with real effects on the dreamer.

#### IV. The Emptiness of Emptiness: Resolving the Paradox of Dependent Designation

Having established that all phenomena are empty, Madhyamaka faces a critical objection. If emptiness is the ultimate nature of reality, does emptiness itself have *svabhāva*? If emptiness is an inherent property of things, then it is a ground, a foundation, and the whole deconstructive project collapses into a new form of essentialism. If emptiness is not empty, then it is a self-existing absolute, which contradicts the principle that everything is empty. This is the paradox of emptiness.

Nāgārjuna’s response is the doctrine of the *emptiness of emptiness* (*śūnyatā-śūnyatā*). Emptiness is not a thing, a property, or a transcendent reality. It is a *deconstructive concept*—a therapeutic tool. It is like a medicine that cures a disease; once the disease of grasping at *svabhāva* is cured, the medicine is no longer needed. To reify emptiness into an ultimate reality would be to fall back into the very disease it is meant to cure. Emptiness is itself dependent on the objects that are empty. Just as "tall" is dependent on "short," emptiness is dependent on the phenomena it characterizes. It has no independent existence. It is a label we apply to things to indicate their lack of inherent nature.

This resolves the paradox of dependent designation. The paradox arises because we assume that for something to be real or meaningful, it must be grounded in something else that is ultimately real. If the self is a designation, what is it designated *upon*? It is designated upon the aggregates. But the aggregates themselves are empty; they are designations upon further parts, and so on. This threatens an infinite regress. If we try to find the ultimate referent of our concepts, we will never find it; we will only encounter more emptiness.

Madhyamaka’s solution is to stop looking for the ultimate referent. The regress is not a problem; it is the nature of reality. Dependent designation does not require a final, non-dependent foundation. It is a process of mutual imputation. The concept "chariot" depends on the concept "parts," and the concept "parts" depends on the concept "whole." Neither is more fundamental. They arise together in a web of conceptual interdependence. This is not a paradox; it is a description of how language and cognition work. We construct our world through concepts, and those concepts are themselves empty of inherent existence.

Does this successfully resolve the paradox? Yes, but only if we accept a radical shift in perspective. The paradox only exists if we insist on a foundationalist metaphysics—if we demand that every designation must ultimately refer to a non-designated, self-existing entity. Madhyamaka rejects this demand. It argues that the very idea of a "ground" is a conceptual projection born of the craving for certainty. The emptiness of emptiness shows that emptiness is not a ground; it is the *absence* of a ground. It is the realization that reality is fundamentally unfounded, a "groundless ground." This is not a defeat; it is a liberation. It frees us from the anxiety of seeking an absolute foundation and allows us to engage with the world as it is—interdependent, conventional, and dynamic.

#### V. The Challenge of Explanatory Arbitrariness and Natural Regularities

The most formidable challenge to this Madhyamaka framework concerns the regularity of natural phenomena. If we deny inherent causation and ultimate essence, how do we explain the fact that fire reliably burns wood, that water reliably quenches thirst, and that the laws of physics appear to be universal and invariant? If there is no inherent causal power, why is the world not a chaotic flux of random events? The critic might argue that by removing the metaphysical ground of causality, Madhyamaka renders the natural world's order entirely arbitrary. If the "cause" is merely a conventional designation, then why does the designation "fire" consistently precede the designation "burned wood"? What constrains the conventional world to operate in the stable ways that it does?

This is the crux of the accusation of explanatory arbitrariness. To answer it, we must distinguish between two levels of explanation: conventional (*saṃvṛti*) and ultimate (*paramārtha*). On the conventional level, Madhyamaka fully accepts the reality of cause and effect. Fire burns wood because that is the established order of dependent origination. The regularity is not arbitrary; it is conditioned. The seed produces a sprout because of the specific conditions of soil, water, and warmth. This is not a random process; it is a highly specific, intersubjectively verifiable sequence. Madhyamaka does not deny the empirical sciences; it denies that the objects of science have *svabhāva*. A physicist can study the laws of nature perfectly well without positing an inherent "essence" of electrons or gravity. The laws are descriptions of how phenomena dependently arise. They are conventional truths, and they are as reliable and non-arbitrary as any empirical regularity.

The problem arises when we ask the *ultimate* question: Why are these particular laws in place rather than others? Why does fire burn wood and not water? A realist might answer: because fire has the inherent nature of heat, and wood has the inherent nature of combustibility. Madhyamaka rejects this. It says that fire and wood do not have inherent natures; they are empty. So why do they interact in this way? The Madhyamaka answer is that this is simply how dependent origination unfolds. There is no deeper "why" behind it. The regularity of nature is a brute fact, but it is a *conventional* brute fact, not a metaphysical one.

This is where the charge of arbitrariness bites. If there is no ultimate explanation for why the laws of nature are what they are, then the world is ultimately ungrounded. The order we observe is, from an ultimate perspective, a kind of cosmic accident—a dependent arising that could have been otherwise. The critic will say that this is precisely explanatory arbitrariness. If we cannot provide an ultimate reason for the regularity, then we have failed to explain it. We have merely described it.

Madhyamaka’s response is to challenge the very notion of "ultimate explanation." The demand for an ultimate explanation—for a reason why the world is this way and not otherwise—presupposes that there is a ground, a *svabhāva* of the universe, that determines its order. But we have already shown that *svabhāva* is incoherent. The demand for an ultimate explanation is a category mistake. It is like asking, "What is the color of a sound?" or "What is the smell of a number?" The question is based on a false premise. The world is not a thing that has an essence; it is a process of dependent arising. To ask for an ultimate explanation of this process is to ask for something that does not exist.

Does this constitute a successful resolution? It depends on what one expects from philosophy. If one expects philosophy to provide a metaphysical ground for the natural order—a "theory of everything" that explains why the laws of physics are necessary—then Madhyamaka is a failure. It explicitly denies the possibility of such a theory. It offers a *deconstructive* philosophy, not a constructive one. It does not build a system; it dismantles all systems.

However, if one recognizes that the search for an ultimate ground is a source of suffering and conceptual confusion, then Madhyamaka’s refusal to provide one is a strength. The regularity of nature is not arbitrary in the sense of being random or chaotic. It is highly structured and predictable. But it is not *metaphysically necessary*. It is contingent. And this contingency is not a flaw; it is the very nature of emptiness. The world is a tapestry of dependent relations, and its patterns are real, but they are not etched in stone.

The charge of arbitrariness ultimately rests on a confusion between *conventional* non-arbitrariness and *ultimate* grounding. Madhyamaka fully embraces conventional non-arbitrariness. The laws of nature are not arbitrary; they are constrained by the web of dependent origination. But it rejects ultimate grounding. The world is not grounded in a transcendent reality, a creator God, or a set of necessary essences. It is grounded in nothing other than its own dependent arising. This is not "arbitrary" in the sense of being random; it is "unfounded" in the sense of lacking a transcendent anchor. And this unfoundedness, Madhyamaka argues, is not a problem to be solved but a reality to be accepted.
