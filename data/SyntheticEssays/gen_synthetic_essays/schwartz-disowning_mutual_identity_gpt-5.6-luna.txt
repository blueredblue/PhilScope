# The Skeptical and Deific Mistakes: Metaphysical Error, Spirit-Murder, and the Possibility of Social Parity

## Introduction

Societal violence against marginalized groups is often explained in terms of prejudice, economic conflict, political propaganda, or individual pathology. These explanations are indispensable, but they can remain incomplete. They tell us that perpetrators harbor racist, homophobic, misogynistic, or otherwise exclusionary attitudes, yet they may not fully explain how another human being becomes available for humiliation, torture, abandonment, or death. A deeper account must ask what sort of picture of personhood makes such violence intelligible to those who commit it and to institutions that excuse, minimize, or distribute responsibility for it.

One promising approach interprets oppression as rooted in two metaphysical errors: the skeptical mistake and the deific mistake. The skeptical mistake is the denial, withholding, or radical downgrading of the Other’s interiority. It treats the marginalized person as an uncertain mind, a body without a fully credible perspective, or a social appearance whose testimony requires special proof. The deific mistake is the elevation of the self, or of the dominant social group, into a quasi-divine standpoint: autonomous, self-authorizing, entitled to define reality and to judge which beings count as fully human. The two errors are complementary. The skeptical subject doubts the reality or authority of the Other while taking his or her own perspective as immediately valid. The resulting social world is one in which some people are presumed to possess interiority, agency, and reason, while others are treated as objects, threats, symbols, or causes of the trouble inflicted upon them.

This essay argues that these errors help explain what may be called *spirit-murdering the Other*: the destruction not only of a person’s body or legal interests but of the social recognition that makes agency, dignity, and a livable self possible. The murders of Matthew Shepard and Michael Griffith in the Howard Beach incident provide concrete examples. In both cases, violence was not merely an assault upon biological organisms. It was an attempt to discipline the meaning of a person’s presence: a gay man was made into a target whose identity could be punished, while a Black man was rendered an intruder in a supposedly white space. The law’s recurrent use of victim-responsibility defenses—provocation, self-defense, “gay panic,” contributory negligence, and related doctrines—often reproduces the same metaphysical structure by shifting attention from the wrongdoer’s action to the victim’s identity, conduct, or alleged failure to avoid danger.

Nevertheless, the skeptical and deific mistakes should not be treated as the sole causes of social violence. They are philosophical abstractions of historically specific systems: white supremacy, heteronormativity, patriarchy, nationalism, and unequal legal power. Nor can a physicalist account of shared human identity, by itself, establish social parity. It may undermine fantasies of radically separate kinds of human beings, but it does not dictate equal concern. A more adequate solution requires a plural ontological framework: embodied physical commonality, relational personhood, social recognition, and revised standards of reasonability. Such a framework can weaken solipsistic alienation, but only when embodied in institutions that treat every person as an equally authoritative participant in the common world.

## I. Two Metaphysical Errors

The skeptical mistake begins with a problem familiar from the philosophy of mind: how can one know that another being has a mind? I cannot experience your consciousness directly. I encounter your body, language, gestures, and actions, and infer that you possess an interior life. In ordinary human relations this “problem of other minds” is not usually a practical obstacle. We respond to others as persons, not as hypotheses. But social hierarchy can convert an epistemological puzzle into a political weapon. The dominant subject applies skepticism selectively. The minds of members of the dominant group are presumed; the minds of marginalized people are questioned.

The testimony of a white male property owner may be accepted as evidence, while the testimony of a Black person is treated as anger, manipulation, or unreliability. The account of a heterosexual victim may be regarded as an ordinary report, while a gay victim’s account is interpreted through stereotypes about deception, seduction, or sexual threat. Women’s accounts of coercion have historically been discounted because their dress, sexuality, or prior conduct was presumed to undermine their credibility. The issue is not merely that a stereotype makes a person look bad. It changes the ontological status of the person’s speech. The speaker is no longer encountered as someone who can disclose reality; he or she becomes an object from which information must be extracted, corrected, or distrusted.

The skeptical mistake therefore does not consist in recognizing that knowledge of another person is mediated and fallible. That recognition is intellectually sound. Its error lies in transforming the ordinary limits of interpersonal knowledge into a reason for unequal recognition. Skepticism becomes a social practice of withholding personhood. The marginalized person is treated as a body whose experiences do not automatically count as knowledge and whose self-description does not control the meaning of the encounter.

The deific mistake is the inverse movement. Here the self assumes a perspective from nowhere, a position from which it can define the world without being defined by others. The deific subject imagines itself as fully autonomous, rational, and self-transparent. It does not see its identity as dependent upon language, history, institutions, or recognition. It experiences its own norms as simply normal and its own boundaries as naturally authoritative. In a racialized society, the dominant group can experience its own spatial, cultural, and bodily presence as universal while construing others as marked, particular, or invasive. In a heteronormative society, heterosexuality appears not as one form of life among others but as the unmarked structure of reality. The deific mistake is thus not merely personal arrogance. It is the social elevation of a partial perspective into a universal standard.

Enlightenment rationality contributes ambiguously to this structure. Its ideals of universal humanity, autonomy, freedom, and reason are genuinely emancipatory. They provide resources for criticizing inherited status hierarchies and affirming equal citizenship. But Enlightenment traditions often imagined the rational subject in abstraction from embodiment, dependence, history, and social location. The “universal” subject frequently turned out to be coded as male, white, propertied, heterosexual, and culturally dominant. Those excluded from the model were not always openly declared nonhuman. More often, they were admitted conditionally: as beings who might become rational, civilized, respectable, or self-governing if they approximated the dominant norm.

This is where the skeptical and deific mistakes converge. The deific subject claims the authority to establish the standard of rationality; the skeptical subject doubts that the Other can satisfy it. The dominant group becomes both judge and implicit model of humanity. The Other must prove what the dominant subject is simply presumed to be.

## II. From Metaphysical Error to Spirit-Murder

Physical violence is often preceded, accompanied, or followed by a destruction of social intelligibility. To spirit-murder someone is to attack the conditions under which that person can appear as a full self: a speaker, agent, neighbor, citizen, or bearer of claims. The person may remain biologically alive, but the social world has been arranged so that his or her perspective is inaudible, identity is defined by outsiders, and suffering is converted into evidence of personal defect.

This notion should not be confused with a claim that symbolic injury is identical to physical murder. It is not. Rather, spirit-murder names a continuum of practices that make physical violence easier, excuse it after the fact, or impose a continuing injury upon survivors and communities. Racial insult, public humiliation, institutional disbelief, stereotypical representation, and legal victim-blaming may all participate in the same structure without having the same severity or consequences.

The skeptical mistake produces spirit-murder by denying the Other’s subjectivity. The person is seen but not encountered. A Black pedestrian becomes “a danger,” a gay man becomes “a sexual provocation,” an immigrant becomes “an illegal,” and a woman becomes “a temptation” or “a liar.” These descriptions do not merely misrepresent existing persons. They replace persons with roles in a dominant narrative. Once the person is reduced to a role, violence can be experienced by the perpetrator as a response to the role rather than as an action against an individual.

The deific mistake produces spirit-murder by treating the dominant self as entitled to impose the meaning of the encounter. If the dominant subject feels threatened, insulted, contaminated, or challenged, that feeling is treated as self-validating. The question becomes not whether the target actually threatened anyone, but whether the dominant subject experienced the target as a threat. The latter experience is then used to justify coercion. This is why racial and sexual violence often contains an element of punishment. The victim is not only attacked; he or she is disciplined for occupying a space, displaying an identity, or refusing subordination.

Spirit-murder is especially visible in the transformation of victimhood into culpability. The victim’s body, identity, location, clothing, speech, sexuality, or failure to retreat becomes the explanatory center of the event. The perpetrator’s agency recedes. The victim is made responsible for having become the kind of person against whom violence was, supposedly, understandable.

## III. Matthew Shepard and the Ontology of the Threatening Body

Matthew Shepard’s murder in Wyoming in 1998 is commonly remembered as an anti-gay hate crime. Shepard, a twenty-one-year-old gay student, was abducted, beaten, tied to a fence, and left to die. Aaron McKinney and Russell Henderson were convicted for the crime. The brutality of the attack and the symbolic setting—Shepard’s body exposed and abandoned in a rural landscape—made the murder an international emblem of homophobic violence.

The attack cannot be adequately described as an encounter between two individuals in which one happened to possess an objectionable prejudice. It expressed a social ontology in which gay identity was constructed as a threat to the moral and sexual order. The perpetrators did not merely dislike Shepard. They treated his identity as an offense that could warrant domination. His body was turned into a message: a warning about what happens to those who visibly or audibly depart from heteronormative expectations.

The skeptical mistake appeared in the denial of Shepard as a subject whose identity could be self-defined and whose vulnerability demanded recognition. A gay man was not encountered as a neighbor with a particular life but as a stereotype—predatory, deceptive, feminizing, or contaminating. His interiority disappeared behind the category assigned to him. The fact that Shepard was a person with relationships, aspirations, fears, and a perspective on the world became irrelevant to the perpetrators’ story. The person was replaced by “the homosexual,” an abstract figure against whom violence could be staged.

The deific mistake appeared in the perpetrators’ presumption that they could enforce the boundaries of legitimate masculinity. They acted as though their own gendered and sexual identity entitled them to determine which forms of intimacy, embodiment, and self-presentation could exist in public. The violence was an attempted reassertion of authority: the dominant self’s right to define the social reality in which the Other must live.

The legal history surrounding the case also reveals the relation between metaphysics and victim responsibility. McKinney’s lawyers explored a “gay panic” theory, associated with the idea that a defendant’s discovery or perception of a man’s homosexuality produced a sudden loss of self-control. Although the defense did not ultimately become the central basis of the trial in the simple popular version often reported, its very availability illustrates a troubling legal possibility: that the victim’s identity can be represented as a provocation. The victim’s sexual orientation is treated as an event capable of explaining, and potentially mitigating, the defendant’s violence.

This is a paradigmatic form of spirit-murder. Shepard is not only killed; the social meaning of his being gay is placed on trial. The defense implicitly asks whether his identity made the attack understandable. It shifts the normative question from “Why did the defendant assault him?” to “What did the victim do to produce the defendant’s reaction?” The law thereby risks treating the victim’s existence as a causal force with moral significance equal to, or greater than, the perpetrator’s decision to use violence.

A crucial distinction must be preserved here. Causal explanation is not moral justification. Shepard’s identity may have been part of the causal history of the attack in the sense that the perpetrators targeted him because they believed he was gay. But it does not follow that his identity provoked the violence in a legally or morally exculpatory sense. To say that a victim’s identity was the reason for an assault is to identify the assailant’s discriminatory motive, not to establish the victim’s responsibility.

## IV. Howard Beach and the Racial Production of Space

The Howard Beach incident in Queens, New York, in 1986 offers a parallel but distinct example. A group of Black men entered a predominantly white neighborhood, encountered white youths, and were pursued and attacked. Michael Griffith, attempting to escape, was struck and killed by a passing car on a roadway. The event generated intense public outrage, political mobilization, and criminal prosecutions. It became a prominent symbol of racial violence and of the racialized control of public space.

The significance of Howard Beach lies partly in the geography of the event. The attackers’ violence communicated that the neighborhood was not simply a place inhabited by white residents; it was imagined as a white domain whose boundaries Black people crossed at their peril. Griffith’s presence was treated as an intrusion. The skeptical mistake rendered him less as a person navigating a public environment than as a racialized object whose motives were suspect. He and his companions were not granted the presumption of ordinary belonging. Their presence required explanation.

The deific mistake appeared in the assumption that the dominant residents could define the meaning of the neighborhood and enforce its racial boundaries. The attackers behaved as though white identity conferred an authority to determine who could enter, who belonged, and who could be driven away. This authority was not necessarily articulated as a formal political doctrine. It could operate through habit, fear, insult, pursuit, and the expectation that Black people should understand the limits imposed upon them.

The case also illustrates how racial violence can be obscured by narratives of mutual conflict. If an incident is described as a fight between groups, attention may shift toward the conduct of the victims: Why were they in that neighborhood? Why did they run? Did they argue? Did they take a wrong turn? Such questions can be relevant to reconstructing facts, but they become ideological when they imply that the victims’ presence or conduct made the violence morally intelligible. The more basic fact—that racialized aggressors claimed authority over their movement—disappears.

In a racially stratified society, movement itself can be read as a provocation. Black people may be treated as threatening when they enter affluent areas, as suspicious when they remain in stores, as aggressive when they protest, and as evasive when they flee. This is a social trap in which almost any action confirms the preexisting stereotype. The Other is first constructed as a threat and then blamed for eliciting the response that the construction makes appear reasonable.

Howard Beach thus exemplifies spirit-murder through the annihilation of civic standing. Griffith was not merely deprived of physical safety. His equal right to occupy public space was denied. The attack communicated that his citizenship was conditional and that white residents could treat his presence as a challenge to their sovereignty. The death on the roadway was the physical culmination of a process in which a person had already been symbolically expelled from the common world.

## V. Victim Responsibility Defenses and the Legal Reproduction of Metaphysical Error

Legal systems must distinguish among many forms of responsibility. A victim may contribute causally to an accident without being morally culpable. A person may make a risky choice without forfeiting the right not to be assaulted. A defendant may sincerely feel threatened without possessing a reasonable basis for using deadly force. The difficulty is that law frequently moves among these categories without sufficient discipline. When it does, victim responsibility defenses can reproduce the skeptical and deific mistakes.

Self-defense provides the clearest example. In principle, self-defense protects a person against an actual or reasonably perceived imminent threat. It is not a license to punish people for their identities or to impose private judgments about social danger. Yet the standard of reasonability has historically been shaped by racial and gender stereotypes. A white defendant’s fear of a Black man may be treated as more credible than the Black man’s fear of the defendant. A man’s fear of a woman’s rejection may be narratively inflated into fear of humiliation or emasculation. A heterosexual defendant’s reaction to a gay man may be cast as an understandable threat to sexual or gender identity.

The problem is not simply that individual jurors harbor prejudice. The legal category of the “reasonable person” can itself conceal a dominant perspective. If reasonability is modeled on the unmarked subject, then the experiences of marginalized people appear excessive while the fears of dominant people appear ordinary. The law becomes skeptical about the victim’s reality and deferential toward the defendant’s self-description.

Provocation doctrines raise a similar concern. Traditional provocation law sometimes mitigates homicide when a defendant experiences a sudden loss of self-control in response to conduct deemed sufficiently provocative. Historically, the range of recognized provocations has reflected assumptions about honor, masculinity, sexuality, and property. Insults to a dominant man’s dignity may be taken seriously, while the victim’s vulnerability is ignored. The “gay panic” theory is especially revealing because it treats the discovery or perception of homosexuality as capable of provoking an exceptional emotional response. A victim’s identity is thereby redescribed as an assault on the defendant.

This structure is metaphysically deific: the defendant’s emotional response is taken as the authoritative meaning of the encounter. If he experiences another person’s identity as an attack, the identity becomes legally relevant as though it were an act of aggression. At the same time, the skeptical mistake denies the victim’s independent perspective. The victim is not considered as someone who might experience fear, coercion, or danger; his existence is mainly considered as an input into the defendant’s psychology.

Civil law contains related forms of victim responsibility, including contributory negligence, assumption of risk, and failure-to-mitigate doctrines. These principles can serve legitimate purposes. People sometimes knowingly encounter risks, and legal systems may need to allocate losses among multiple causal contributors. But their application to social violence requires caution. A person’s decision to enter a neighborhood, express an identity, attend a party, accept a ride, or fail to anticipate a hostile reaction does not ordinarily transfer responsibility for another person’s intentional assault.

The distinction between negligent injury and intentional subordination is crucial. If a person carelessly crosses a dangerous road, the person’s conduct may be relevant to an accidental collision. But racial or homophobic violence is not a natural hazard independent of human choice. It is produced by agents acting within structures of meaning. To treat the victim’s failure to avoid those agents as equivalent to the agents’ decision to attack is to obscure agency.

The victim-responsibility framework can also produce a legal form of spirit-murder after the event. Survivors must not only prove that they were harmed; they must defend their character. Courts, police, media, and public opinion may investigate the victim’s sexual history, criminal record, neighborhood, clothing, friendships, or emotional reactions. The victim is converted from a claimant into a suspect. This damages the social credibility necessary for justice and teaches communities that seeking protection may expose them to further scrutiny.

The law cannot eliminate all causal inquiry. Nor should it refuse to consider a defendant’s actual beliefs, mental state, or the circumstances surrounding an event. The challenge is to prevent subjective experience from becoming self-validating. A revised legal standard must ask whether the belief or reaction was reasonable in light of the actual facts, available alternatives, the proportionality of the response, and the equal status of the person against whom force was used.

## VI. Can Physicalism Resolve the Problem?

One possible response to the metaphysical errors is a physicalist account of shared human identity. Human beings are embodied organisms with broadly shared biological capacities and vulnerabilities. We depend on similar bodily systems, experience pain through physical processes, require social interaction for development, and possess overlapping capacities for language, emotion, planning, and self-awareness. No race or sexual orientation marks a separate biological species. A physicalist ontology can therefore undermine fantasies that marginalized groups are fundamentally different kinds of beings.

Physicalism has important political uses. It can challenge claims that some groups are naturally closer to animals, irrationality, contamination, or social danger. It emphasizes that injury is real in the body and that the suffering of an Other is not less material because it is interpreted through a different social identity. The body tied to a fence and the body struck by a car are not symbolic abstractions; they are vulnerable human bodies. This recognition can counter the skeptical tendency to treat another person’s pain as theatrics or metaphor.

Yet physicalism alone cannot establish equality. The fact that all humans are physical organisms does not logically entail that all deserve equal concern. A society could accept biological commonality while ranking people according to intelligence, productivity, nationality, beauty, or conformity. Historical racism itself has often operated through pseudo-physicalist classifications. Biology can be invoked not only to establish commonality but to naturalize hierarchy.

Nor does physicalism fully capture personhood. A person is not merely a body considered from the outside. Personhood includes memory, self-interpretation, social relations, projects, vulnerability, and the capacity to give and receive reasons. A strictly reductive description may explain pain as neural activity without capturing the injustice of dismissing someone’s pain or denying the authority of their testimony. What matters politically is not only that the victim has a nervous system but that the victim is a subject whose experience should count in the shared world.

Physicalist commonality must therefore be supplemented by relational and social ontology. Persons are embodied, but they become selves within relations of recognition, language, care, and institutional status. We are not isolated Cartesian minds, nor are we interchangeable organisms. We are vulnerable beings whose identities are partly constituted through social practices. This account avoids both errors: it rejects the skeptical fantasy that other minds are inaccessible objects while also rejecting the deific fantasy that the self is self-grounding and independent of others.

A relational ontology does not mean that society creates every aspect of identity or that individuals lack autonomy. It means that autonomy is socially enabled. The ability to speak, work, form relationships, move safely, and make claims depends upon institutions and norms that recognize one as a person. Social parity is thus not achieved merely by declaring that everyone has the same inner essence. It requires constructing relations in which no group monopolizes the authority to define reality.

## VII. Revised Reasonability and the Reconstruction of the Common World

If metaphysical errors are embedded in legal and social standards, then overcoming them requires revising reasonability. The “reasonable person” should not be understood as a neutral abstraction silently modeled on dominant experience. Reasonability should be objective, evidence-sensitive, and attentive to the equal status of all participants.

In self-defense law, this would mean asking whether a perceived threat was proportionate to the force used, whether the belief was grounded in observable conduct rather than stereotype, whether the defendant had safe alternatives, and whether the alleged threat consisted merely in the victim’s identity or presence. A person cannot reasonably regard another as an imminent deadly threat simply because the other is Black, gay, transgender, undocumented, or socially unfamiliar. Subjective fear may explain conduct, but it should not automatically authorize it.

In provocation law, the law should distinguish between conduct that threatens bodily integrity and conduct that merely challenges status, pride, or prejudice. Rejection, disclosure of sexual orientation, racial insult, refusal to submit, or the assertion of equal rights should not be treated as equivalent to violent assault. Otherwise the law protects the dominant subject’s expectation of deference.

Reasonability should also be plural rather than solipsistic. Courts and institutions should consider how the event appears from the standpoint of the person who was targeted, especially where power is unequal. This does not mean that every subjective perception is decisive. It means that the victim’s experience is evidence, not noise. A person fleeing a racial attack is not simply an unpredictable body in motion; the flight may be an intelligible response to a danger that the dominant legal perspective has minimized.

More broadly, reasonability must be anti-subordination. It should ask whether a legal rule, defense, or institutional practice reinforces a hierarchy in which some people must bear extraordinary burdens to secure ordinary safety. A formally neutral rule can be substantively unequal if it treats marginalized people as responsible for anticipating and avoiding the violence directed at them.

But revised reasonability cannot substitute for material and institutional transformation. Police protection, prosecutorial commitment, jury education, anti-discrimination enforcement, and accurate public narratives are necessary. The common world is not rebuilt by philosophical declaration alone. If a person is nominally recognized as equal while remaining exposed to violence, excluded from housing, disbelieved by authorities, or represented as inherently dangerous, the skeptical and deific structures remain intact.

## VIII. The Limits and Promise of Overcoming Solipsistic Alienation

The language of solipsistic alienation identifies a genuine danger: the dominant subject becomes enclosed within its own perspective and mistakes that perspective for reality itself. The Other appears only as a projection of fear, desire, disgust, or resentment. Overcoming this alienation requires cultivating practices of encounter in which people are accountable to perspectives not generated by themselves.

Yet one should avoid imagining that perfect mutual transparency is possible. We never fully possess another person’s consciousness. Recognition does not dissolve difference or guarantee empathy. The goal is not to eliminate the opacity of other minds but to establish ethical and political norms that respond to opacity with humility rather than domination. Because I cannot know another completely, I should not conclude that the other’s testimony is worthless. The limits of knowledge should support caution, listening, and due process—not hierarchy.

This point also reveals why alternative ontological frameworks must remain self-critical. Relational theories can become oppressive if they define people only by group membership or social role. Physicalist theories can become reductionist. Universalist theories can erase difference. Identity-centered theories can treat communities as internally uniform. No single ontology automatically generates justice.

A successful framework would combine several commitments:

1. **Embodied commonality:** all humans are materially vulnerable beings whose pain, freedom, and survival matter.
2. **Irreducible subjectivity:** persons are not exhausted by external classifications; they possess perspectives and projects that demand recognition.
3. **Relational autonomy:** individuality develops through social relations and institutions rather than existing outside them.
4. **Equal epistemic standing:** marginalized people are not required to prove their humanity before their testimony counts.
5. **Anti-subordination:** social and legal norms must be evaluated by their effects on status, safety, and participation.
6. **Fallibilist reason:** no group’s perspective is omniscient, and dominant interpretations must remain open to correction.

These commitments address the skeptical and deific mistakes together. They reject the denial of other minds by affirming the authority of lived experience, and they reject the fantasy of a self-authorizing judge by locating every knower within embodied and historical relations.

## Conclusion

The claim that societal violence against marginalized groups stems from the skeptical mistake and the deific mistake is illuminating if it is understood as a diagnosis of the structure of domination rather than as a complete causal theory. The skeptical mistake withholds full recognition from the Other. The deific mistake elevates the dominant self into a universal judge. Together they produce a social world in which some people are treated as unquestioned subjects and others as questionable objects. This world makes spirit-murder possible: the victim’s voice, identity, belonging, and moral standing are damaged before, during, and after physical violence.

Matthew Shepard’s murder demonstrates how a gay man can be transformed from a person into a supposed provocation and how homophobic violence attempts to police the boundaries of legitimate identity. The Howard Beach incident demonstrates how racial violence can deny a Black person’s equal right to inhabit public space, converting civic presence into intrusion. In both cases, victim-responsibility narratives threaten to shift moral attention from the assailant’s agency to the victim’s identity, conduct, or failure to avoid danger. Legal doctrines such as self-defense and provocation can become vehicles for this shift when “reasonability” is silently defined by dominant fears and expectations.

A physicalist account of shared human identity can challenge the fiction that marginalized groups are fundamentally alien kinds of beings, but physicalism alone cannot produce justice. Equality requires an ontology that is at once embodied, relational, subjective, and institutional. It requires standards of reasonability that refuse to treat prejudice as perception, identity as provocation, or survival behavior as culpability. It requires institutions that recognize marginalized people as authoritative participants in the common world.

True social parity will not arise from discovering a metaphysical essence shared by all humans if that essence remains detached from social practice. It will arise when common embodiment, reciprocal recognition, and equal institutional standing are joined. The task is not to become gods who possess complete knowledge of one another, nor skeptics who retreat into private experience. It is to become fallible agents capable of meeting one another as real, vulnerable, and equally entitled persons. Only then can the Other cease to be a projection of dominant fear and become what the Other has always been: a subject with whom the world is shared.
