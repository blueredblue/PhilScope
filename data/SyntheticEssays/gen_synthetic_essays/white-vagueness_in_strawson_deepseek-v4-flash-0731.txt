### The Ghost in the Rules: Strawson, Nonexistent Referents, and the Limits of Expressional Meaning

The philosophy of language in the mid-twentieth century was defined by a seismic collision between two opposing visions of how words hook onto the world. On one side stood Bertrand Russell, armed with the scalpel of formal logic, eager to dissect ordinary language and expose its misleading surface grammar. On the other stood P.F. Strawson, a defender of the messy, contextual, and irreducibly human practices of everyday speech. At the heart of their dispute lies the problem of referring expressions—those phrases like "the present king of France" or "the queen of America" that appear to point at something, yet refer to nothing at all. Russell famously argued that such phrases are not genuinely referring expressions at all, but disguised descriptions that must be logically paraphrased to reveal their true truth-conditions. Strawson countered that Russell had fundamentally misunderstood the nature of meaning, reference, and the relationship between language and its users. For Strawson, the meaning of an expression is not the object it denotes, but rather a set of general directions for its correct use. This essay will evaluate Strawson's theory of expressional meaning by subjecting it to the crucible of nonexistent referents, specifically the phrase "the queen of America." I will argue that while Strawson's theory does not fully collapse under this pressure, it reveals a profound vulnerability to vagueness that threatens its explanatory power. However, this vulnerability is not fatal. The apparent collapse can be resolved by refining Strawson's notion of "directions for use" to distinguish between the *semantic content* of an expression (which is precise and descriptive) and the *pragmatic presupposition* of existence (which is a feature of the speaker's act of referring). Ultimately, I will contend that the tension between Russell's logical paraphrases and Strawson's theory of denoting is not a zero-sum conflict but a complementary division of labor: Russell captures the truth-conditional skeleton of propositions, while Strawson illuminates the semantic and pragmatic flesh of natural language. The "queen of America" does not destroy Strawson's theory; rather, it exposes the need for a rigorous theory of presupposition that Strawson only gestures toward, and it reveals that the "directions" are not vague in their descriptive content but conditional in their existential commitment.

#### I. Strawson's Framework: Meaning, Use, and the Act of Referring

To understand the stakes of the "queen of America" problem, one must first grasp the foundational distinctions Strawson lays out in his seminal 1950 paper, "On Referring." Strawson's central target is Russell's theory of descriptions, which he accuses of committing a "systematic ambiguity" between an expression and its use. Strawson insists on a tripartite distinction that Russell allegedly conflates: the *sentence*, the *use of a sentence* (the statement), and the *utterance of a sentence*. Similarly, for expressions, we must distinguish the *expression* itself, the *use of the expression* (to refer to a particular object), and the *utterance of the expression* (the physical act of speaking or writing it). For Strawson, meaning is a property of the *expression* (and the *sentence*), not of its use or utterance. Crucially, meaning is not reference. The meaning of an expression is not the object it is used to refer to on a particular occasion, nor the class of objects it could refer to across all occasions. Rather, meaning is constituted by a set of "rules, habits, and conventions" that govern the correct use of the expression.

This is Strawson's famous doctrine of "expressional meaning." He writes: "The meaning of an expression is not the object it is used to refer to on a particular occasion, nor the set of objects it is used to refer to on all occasions, but the set of rules or conventions governing its use." These rules are *general directions* for how to employ the expression in discourse. They tell a competent speaker how to deploy the expression to pick out a particular item, given the appropriate contextual conditions. For example, the meaning of "the present king of France" is not the (nonexistent) king, but rather a set of instructions: "Use this expression to refer to the unique individual who currently satisfies the predicate 'is king of France'." The meaning is a *function* from contexts to referents—or, in cases of failure, to a breakdown in the act of referring.

Referring itself, for Strawson, is not a property of expressions but an *act* performed by speakers. A speaker refers to something by using an expression in a particular context, with the intention of identifying a specific object for an audience. The expression is the tool; the referring is the action. This is why Strawson emphasizes that "referring is not something an expression does; it is something that someone can use an expression to do." The meaning of the expression provides the *rules* for this act, but it does not guarantee its success. The success of an act of referring depends on the existence of a unique object that satisfies the descriptive conditions embedded in the expression's meaning, as well as on the audience's ability to identify that object.

This framework is elegant and intuitively appealing. It explains why we can understand sentences about fictional or nonexistent entities. We understand the sentence "The present king of France is bald" because we grasp the *meaning* of "the present king of France"—we know the rules for its use—even though there is no such person to refer to. The sentence is meaningful, though the *statement* made by uttering it in our world may be neither true nor false (or, as Strawson later refined, involves a presupposition failure). The meaning is a set of directions; the reference is a particular application of those directions.

#### II. The Challenge of Nonexistent Referents: The Case of "The Queen of America"

Now we arrive at the test case that threatens to unravel this neat picture: the expression "the queen of America." Unlike "the present king of France," which has a historical referent (Louis XIV, etc.) and a clear descriptive content, "the queen of America" is a phrase that has never had, does not currently have, and almost certainly never will have a referent. America is a republic; it has no monarchy. Yet the expression is perfectly meaningful. We understand it. We can use it in sentences like "The queen of America is wise" or "The queen of America lives in Washington." We know what it *would* be for such a sentence to be true, even if we know it is false (or vacuous).

According to Strawson, the meaning of "the queen of America" is a set of general directions for its correct use. But what are these directions? They cannot be directions to *point* to the queen, or to *identify* her uniquely, because there is no queen to point to or identify. The directions must be something like: "Use this expression to refer to the unique individual who is a female monarch of the United States of America." This is a perfectly coherent rule. It specifies a *descriptive condition*. However, the challenge arises when we ask: *What happens when the condition is not met?* Does the meaning of the expression collapse? Does the rule become vacuous or nonsensical?

Russell's answer is to deny that "the queen of America" is a referring expression at all. In "On Denoting," Russell argues that definite descriptions are not genuine proper names. They are *incomplete symbols* that have no meaning in isolation but only contribute to the meaning of the proposition in which they occur. The sentence "The queen of America is wise" is not of the subject-predicate form it appears to be. Its logical form is: "There exists an x such that x is a queen of America, and for all y, if y is a queen of America then y=x, and x is wise." This is a conjunction of three propositions: (1) existence, (2) uniqueness, and (3) predication. Since the existence clause is false (there is no queen of America), the entire conjunction is false. The sentence is not meaningless; it is simply false. Russell's paraphrase eliminates the troublesome referring expression entirely, replacing it with a quantified variable. There is no need for a nonexistent referent because there is no referent at all—only a description that fails to be satisfied.

Russell's approach is a masterful feat of logical analysis. It dissolves the problem of nonexistent referents by showing that the apparent reference is an illusion. The sentence "The queen of America is wise" is not about a nonexistent queen; it is about the (empty) class of things that satisfy the description. It asserts that this class is non-empty, which it is not. Therefore, the sentence is false. This is a clean, rigorous solution.

But Strawson rejects this solution. He argues that Russell's paraphrase distorts the nature of ordinary language. When a speaker utters "The queen of America is wise," they are not making a complex existential claim. They are, in their own mind, referring to a particular person. The speaker presupposes that there is a queen of America. If there is no such person, the speaker has not made a false statement (according to Strawson); rather, the speaker has *failed to make a statement*—or, more precisely, the question of the statement's truth or falsity does not arise because a presupposition has been violated. Strawson introduces the notion of *presupposition*: a statement presupposes the existence of its referent. If the presupposition fails, the statement is not false but *infelicitous* or *void*. The sentence "The queen of America is wise" is meaningful, but the *statement* it would be used to make is neither true nor false because there is no queen to be wise.

This is where the vulnerability to vagueness emerges. Strawson's theory of meaning as "directions for use" seems to rely on the idea that these directions are *general* and *applicable* to a range of contexts. But for an expression like "the queen of America," the directions are, in a crucial sense, *inapplicable* to our world. They direct us to perform an act of reference that is impossible. Does this mean the directions are vague? Or does it mean they are simply conditional?

#### III. Testing the Metaphor: Are the "Directions" Vague or Conditional?

The critique of Strawson's theory on the grounds of vagueness runs as follows: If the meaning of an expression is a set of directions for correct use, then for an expression to have meaning, there must be some determinate content to those directions. For "the queen of America," the directions seem to be: "Identify the unique female monarch of the USA." But since there is no such person, the directions are *unfulfillable*. They do not tell us what to do in the actual world. They only tell us what to do in a hypothetical world where such a queen exists. This suggests that the directions are not *directions for use* in any practical sense, but rather *descriptions of a possible state of affairs*. The meaning of the expression collapses into a mere propositional content, stripped of its referential function.

If this critique holds, then Strawson's theory is vulnerable to the charge that it cannot distinguish between meaningful referring expressions and meaningless ones. What is the difference between "the queen of America" and "the round square"? Both seem to describe impossible or nonexistent entities. Yet we intuitively feel that "the queen of America" is meaningful in a way that "the round square" is not (or at least, it is meaningful in a different way). Strawson might respond that "the round square" is meaningless because the predicates "round" and "square" are logically incompatible, whereas "queen of America" is not logically incompatible—it is merely empirically false that there is such a person. But this response relies on a distinction between logical and empirical impossibility that Strawson does not fully articulate.

To defend Strawson, we must refine the notion of "directions for use." The directions are not *instructions for finding a referent* in the world. They are *rules for constructing a descriptive content* and *rules for applying that content to a context*. The meaning of "the queen of America" is composed of the meanings of its constituent parts: "queen" (a female monarch), "of" (a relation of belonging or governance), "America" (the United States of America), and the definite article "the" (which signals uniqueness). The directions tell us how to combine these parts to form a complex predicate: "the unique x such that x is a queen and x is of America." This complex predicate is perfectly precise. It has a determinate extension: the set of things that satisfy it, which happens to be empty.

The apparent vagueness arises only if we demand that the "directions" include a *guarantee of reference*. But Strawson explicitly separates meaning from reference. The meaning is the rule; the reference is the application. The rule for "the queen of America" is conditional: "If there is a unique individual satisfying the description, then use this expression to refer to that individual." This is not vague; it is a precise conditional. The rule is reliable because it tells us exactly what counts as a correct application. The problem is not with the rule but with the *world*—the world does not provide an individual that satisfies the condition.

Thus, the first horn of the dialectical dilemma—that the directions are inherently unreliable—is false. The directions are not unreliable; they are *conditional*. They are reliable in the sense that they correctly specify the conditions under which the expression can be used to refer. The unreliability lies in the *speaker's* epistemic state: the speaker may falsely believe that the condition is satisfied. This brings us to the second horn of the dilemma.

#### IV. The Dialectical Turn: Unreliable Directions or Misunderstanding Speakers?

The dialectical objective posed by the prompt asks us to assess whether implicit directions in language are inherently unreliable or if speakers fundamentally misunderstand proper application. We have already argued that the directions are not unreliable—they are conditionally precise. Now we must examine whether speakers misunderstand the proper application of these directions.

Consider a speaker who utters "The queen of America is wise." According to Strawson, this speaker is *presupposing* that there is a queen of America. The speaker is using the expression "the queen of America" with the intention of referring to a particular person. But since there is no such person, the speaker's act of referring fails. Does the speaker misunderstand the *meaning* of the expression? No. The speaker knows perfectly well what "queen of America" means—they know the descriptive content. The speaker misunderstands the *world*. They have a false belief about the political structure of the United States. This is not a linguistic misunderstanding but a factual one.

However, there is a deeper issue. Strawson's theory of meaning as "directions for use" is supposed to account for *correct* use. If a speaker uses "the queen of America" to refer to, say, the President of the United States, they are misusing the expression. But what if a speaker uses it to refer to nothing at all? Is that a misuse? Strawson would say that the *act of referring* fails, but the *use of the expression* is not necessarily incorrect—it is simply unsuccessful. The expression has meaning because it has a rule-governed use; the failure to refer is a pragmatic failure, not a semantic one.

But this raises the question: What is the *proper application* of the rules? The rules tell us that "the queen of America" refers to a unique female monarch of the USA. Since there is no such monarch, the rules have no actual application in our world. The rules are, in a sense, *dormant*. They are like a key that fits no lock. The speaker who utters the sentence is not misunderstanding the rules; they are misapplying them by assuming there is a lock to fit.

This suggests that the second horn of the dilemma—that speakers fundamentally misunderstand proper application—is also too strong. Speakers do not misunderstand the *meaning*; they misunderstand the *existential presupposition*. They fail to recognize that the conditions for successful reference are not met. This is not a failure of linguistic competence but a failure of factual knowledge.

Yet, there is a residual problem. Strawson's theory of meaning as "directions for use" is supposed to explain how we *understand* expressions. If the directions for "the queen of America" are conditional and unfulfilled, how do we understand the sentence "The queen of America is wise"? We understand it because we can *simulate* the conditions. We can imagine a world in which there is a queen of America, and we can evaluate the sentence in that hypothetical world. This is a cognitive capacity that Strawson's theory does not fully explain. The "directions" are not just rules for actual use; they are rules for *possible* use. This introduces a modal dimension to meaning that Strawson's ordinary-language framework struggles to accommodate.

#### V. Resolving the Tension: Russell's Paraphrase vs. Strawson's Denoting

The dialectical tension between Russell and Strawson can now be addressed directly. Russell's logical paraphrase of "The queen of America is wise" as "There exists a unique x such that x is a queen of America and x is wise" is a brilliant *eliminative* strategy. It removes the problematic referring expression and replaces it with a quantified variable. This avoids the need for a nonexistent referent and provides a clear truth-value: the sentence is false.

Strawson's theory, on the other hand, preserves the surface grammar of ordinary language. It insists that "the queen of America" *functions* as a referring expression in the sentence, even if it fails to refer. The meaning of the expression is not its truth-conditional contribution but its rule-governed use.

How can we resolve this tension? One might argue that Russell and Strawson are answering different questions. Russell is asking: "What is the logical form of the proposition expressed by this sentence?"
