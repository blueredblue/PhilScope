David Hume's sentimentalist account of moral reasoning, as outlined in his seminal work "A Treatise of Human Nature," posits that moral judgments are derived from sentiments, rather than reason. According to Hume, our moral distinctions are based on the feelings of approval or disapproval that arise from our observations of human behavior. This sentimentalist approach to ethics is rooted in the concept of sympathy, which Hume defines as the ability to share in the feelings of others. However, this account has been subject to various criticisms, including the objection that sympathy lacks internal value judgments and fails to provide universal obligations. This essay will evaluate Hume's sentimentalist account against these objections, and assess whether incorporating Aristotle's notion of habituated character and rational control can resolve the vulnerabilities of emotion-based ethics while maintaining that feelings remain an indispensable motivational component of moral action.

One of the primary concerns with Hume's sentimentalist account is that sympathy, as a basis for moral judgment, lacks internal value judgments. Sympathy, in and of itself, does not provide a moral framework for evaluating the actions or behaviors that we observe. For instance, we may sympathize with an individual who is motivated by a desire for revenge, without necessarily endorsing the moral validity of their actions. This raises the question of how we can distinguish between morally justifiable and unjustifiable actions, based solely on sympathetic feelings. Hume's response to this objection is that our moral judgments are based on a general point of view, which takes into account the common interests and sentiments of humanity. However, this response is not entirely convincing, as it relies on an assumption that our sympathetic feelings will naturally align with the greater good.

Moreover, the sentimentalist account of moral reasoning has been criticized for failing to provide universal obligations. If moral judgments are based solely on sympathetic feelings, then it is possible that different individuals will have different moral obligations, depending on their unique emotional responses to various situations. This would seem to undermine the notion of universal moral principles, which are essential for maintaining social cohesion and promoting moral accountability. Hume's account, in this regard, appears to be vulnerable to the charge of moral relativism, where moral judgments are relative to the individual's emotional experiences, rather than being based on objective moral standards.

Another significant concern with Hume's sentimentalist account is that the absence of rational constraints within pure sentiment risks validating harmful inclinations or malicious desires. If our moral judgments are based solely on sympathetic feelings, without the influence of rational reflection, then it is possible that we may sympathize with actions that are morally reprehensible. For example, we may sympathize with an individual who is motivated by a desire for vengeance, without considering the harmful consequences of their actions. In such cases, our sympathetic feelings may lead us to condone or even encourage behaviors that are morally wrong. This highlights the need for rational constraints on our emotional responses, in order to ensure that our moral judgments are guided by a sense of moral principle, rather than mere emotional inclination.

In light of these criticisms, it is worth considering whether incorporating Aristotle's notion of habituated character and rational control can resolve the vulnerabilities of emotion-based ethics. Aristotle's concept of habituated character refers to the development of virtuous habits through repeated practice and training. According to Aristotle, the cultivation of virtuous habits enables individuals to develop a character that is disposed towards virtuous actions, even in the absence of rational reflection. This approach to ethics emphasizes the importance of emotional regulation and the development of moral virtues, such as compassion, justice, and temperance.

The incorporation of Aristotle's notion of habituated character into Hume's sentimentalist account could provide a means of resolving the vulnerabilities of emotion-based ethics. By cultivating virtuous habits and developing a character that is disposed towards virtuous actions, individuals can ensure that their sympathetic feelings are guided by a sense of moral principle, rather than mere emotional inclination. This approach would also provide a means of distinguishing between morally justifiable and unjustifiable actions, based on the development of a virtuous character, rather than relying solely on sympathetic feelings.

Moreover, the incorporation of rational control into Hume's sentimentalist account could provide an additional means of ensuring that our moral judgments are guided by a sense of moral principle, rather than mere emotional inclination. Rational control, in this context, refers to the ability to reflect on our emotional responses and to evaluate their moral implications. By exercising rational control over our sympathetic feelings, we can ensure that our moral judgments are based on a consideration of the moral principles at stake, rather than simply following our emotional inclinations.

In conclusion, Hume's sentimentalist account of moral reasoning, while providing a compelling explanation of the role of emotions in moral judgment, is vulnerable to various criticisms. The absence of internal value judgments and the lack of universal obligations are significant concerns, which highlight the need for a more nuanced approach to emotion-based ethics. The incorporation of Aristotle's notion of habituated character and rational control into Hume's account provides a means of resolving these vulnerabilities, while maintaining that feelings remain an indispensable motivational component of moral action. By cultivating virtuous habits and developing a character that is disposed towards virtuous actions, individuals can ensure that their sympathetic feelings are guided by a sense of moral principle, rather than mere emotional inclination. Furthermore, the exercise of rational control over our emotional responses provides an additional means of ensuring that our moral judgments are based on a consideration of the moral principles at stake, rather than simply following our emotional inclinations.

Ultimately, a comprehensive approach to ethics must take into account both the emotional and rational aspects of human experience. By acknowledging the importance of emotions in moral judgment, while also incorporating rational constraints and the development of virtuous habits, we can develop a more nuanced understanding of moral reasoning and its relationship to human action. This approach would recognize that moral judgments are complex and multifaceted, involving both emotional and rational components, and that a comprehensive ethics must take into account the interplay between these different aspects of human experience.

In addition, it is worth noting that the incorporation of Aristotle's notion of habituated character and rational control into Hume's sentimentalist account is not without its challenges. For instance, it may be difficult to determine how to balance the role of emotions and reason in moral judgment, and how to ensure that our sympathetic feelings are guided by a sense of moral principle, rather than mere emotional inclination. Furthermore, the development of virtuous habits and the exercise of rational control require a significant amount of moral education and training, which may not be readily available to all individuals.

Despite these challenges, the incorporation of Aristotle's notion of habituated character and rational control into Hume's sentimentalist account provides a promising approach to resolving the vulnerabilities of emotion-based ethics. By recognizing the importance of emotions in moral judgment, while also acknowledging the need for rational constraints and the development of virtuous habits, we can develop a more nuanced understanding of moral reasoning and its relationship to human action. This approach would provide a means of distinguishing between morally justifiable and unjustifiable actions, based on the development of a virtuous character, rather than relying solely on sympathetic feelings. Ultimately, a comprehensive approach to ethics must take into account both the emotional and rational aspects of human experience, and recognize that moral judgments are complex and multifaceted, involving both emotional and rational components.

Moreover, the implications of this approach extend beyond the realm of individual ethics, and have significant consequences for our understanding of moral obligation and social responsibility. By recognizing the importance of emotions in moral judgment, and acknowledging the need for rational constraints and the development of virtuous habits, we can develop a more nuanced understanding of how individuals can work together to promote moral accountability and social cohesion. This approach would emphasize the importance of moral education and training, as well as the need for individuals to cultivate virtuous habits and develop a character that is disposed towards virtuous actions.
