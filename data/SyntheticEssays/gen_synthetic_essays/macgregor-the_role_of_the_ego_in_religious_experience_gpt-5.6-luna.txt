# The Ego as Witness: Saturation, Calling, and the Word of Life

Religious experience has often been interpreted through the activity of a subject: the believer perceives a divine object, forms a representation of it, judges its reality, and responds through faith, worship, or ethical action. Even when the object is said to exceed ordinary experience, the basic structure can remain familiar. There is a subject on one side and an object on the other; the subject receives information, organizes it, and confers meaning upon it. Such a model makes human consciousness the implicit origin of religious experience. God, transcendence, or revelation may be exceptionally difficult objects, but they remain objects whose significance must ultimately be constituted by the finite ego.

Jean-Luc Marion, Paul Ricoeur, and Michel Henry each challenge this structure, although they do so in markedly different ways. Marion describes the saturated phenomenon as an event in which intuition gives more than intentional concepts can contain. Ricoeur analyzes the religious relation through the paired structure of call and response, in which the self discovers itself as addressed before it becomes an autonomous interpreter. Henry distinguishes the “word of the world,” which belongs to the sphere of exterior manifestation and objective representation, from the “word of life,” which is given in the immanent self-affection of life itself. Together, these accounts support a central thesis: religious experience is possible precisely because the finite ego does not constitute the phenomenon or master its meaning. The ego is constituted, summoned, and transformed by a higher other or by a mode of givenness that exceeds its initiative.

This thesis, however, should not be understood as a simple rejection of subjectivity. If the subject is entirely eliminated, there can be no experience, no response, and no testimony. The issue is not whether the subject exists, but what kind of subject it is. The subject of religious experience is not an originator who produces meaning from an independent position. It is a witness: one who receives, undergoes, interprets, responds to, and bears testimony concerning what gives itself. The witness is finite and situated, but this finitude is not merely a limitation. It is the condition under which excess can become experience at all.

The essential philosophical problem is therefore twofold. First, how can there be experience when what is given exceeds the capacities of finite cognition? Second, how can religious experience remain genuinely meaningful without being reduced either to subjective projection or to an incomprehensible event that cannot be identified, interpreted, or communicated? The concepts of saturation, call and response, and the word of life offer complementary answers. Marion clarifies the excess of donation; Ricoeur clarifies the dialogical and hermeneutic form of the response; Henry clarifies a mode of revelation prior to worldly objectification. Their differences also expose important tensions. A satisfactory account must preserve both the priority of what gives itself and the irreducible activity of the one who receives.

## I. Marion and the Phenomenon That Exceeds the Ego

Marion’s phenomenology begins from a reversal of the usual hierarchy between subject and object. In much of modern philosophy, the subject is treated as the condition under which objects can appear. Phenomena become intelligible insofar as they can be intended, categorized, synthesized, and known by consciousness. Even when the subject does not create objects in a literal sense, the object as experienced is structured through the subject’s intentional activity. The ego appears as the center from which experience is organized.

Marion contests this privileging of constitution by emphasizing donation. Phenomena should not first be understood as objects that a subject constitutes, but as what gives itself. The question is not simply what the subject does to phenomena but how phenomena arrive, with what force, and under what conditions of excess or limitation. A phenomenon can be poor in intuition, as in a formal abstraction or an empty logical schema, or it can be rich in intuition. The saturated phenomenon is one in which intuition overwhelms the concepts and intentions available to the subject. What is given exceeds what can be anticipated, comprehended, or adequately represented.

This excess has several dimensions. In the saturated phenomenon, the phenomenon can be overwhelming with respect to quantity, quality, relation, and modality. It may offer too much intuition to be gathered into a single perspective, possess a depth that cannot be exhausted by description, resist causal or conceptual determination, and impose itself with a force that cannot be reduced to the subject’s decision. Historical events, works of art, the face of the other, and certain forms of revelation can all exhibit saturation. They are not merely difficult objects. They are phenomena whose mode of appearance disrupts the subject-object economy itself.

A saturated phenomenon does not appear as something over which the ego exercises mastery. Rather, it appears as an event that takes place upon the subject. The subject is “struck,” “summoned,” or “overwhelmed.” This does not mean that the subject becomes passive in the sense of being inert. It means that the subject’s activity is secondary to an initiative that it did not originate. The ego can attend, interpret, remember, name, and respond, but it cannot claim to be the ultimate source of what appears.

This is the philosophical significance of amazement. Amazement is not simply an emotional reaction to something unusual. It is a structural experience of being confronted by more than one can constitute. In ordinary perception, the subject tends to regard itself as the pole that organizes the given. In amazement, this orientation is reversed. The phenomenon takes precedence, and the subject encounters itself as dependent upon what it receives. The ego does not first stand as a sovereign spectator and then decide that something is astonishing. It discovers itself as astonished because the event has exceeded its anticipatory powers.

Religious experience is a privileged case of this structure. Religious phenomena are not merely objects with supernatural properties added to them. If divine revelation is treated as an object among objects, it is already reduced to the conditions of ordinary mastery. Revelation, understood phenomenologically, is what gives itself in such a way that its source, depth, and significance cannot be exhausted by finite conceptualization. The divine is not known as one more item within the inventory of beings. It is encountered as an excess that transforms the field in which beings are understood.

This helps explain why religious experience often includes experiences of awe, wonder, fear, gratitude, conversion, and dependence. These responses are not accidental psychological effects attached to an otherwise neutral perception. They express the reversal of constitutive priority. The believer does not simply observe a religious object and then generate an attitude toward it. Rather, the believer is seized by an experience that places the self in the position of receiver. The religious subject is constituted by the event of donation.

Marion’s account is especially powerful in showing why an experience can be genuine even when it cannot be conceptually mastered. The impossibility of total comprehension does not imply the absence of givenness. On the contrary, what is given may be so rich that conceptual adequacy becomes impossible. The failure to contain the phenomenon is not necessarily a failure of experience. It can be the mark of a phenomenon whose mode of appearing surpasses ordinary objectivity.

Yet Marion’s position generates a difficulty. If the saturated phenomenon exceeds every concept, how can it be recognized as a phenomenon at all? How can one distinguish revelation from confusion, projection, hallucination, or ideological manipulation? If excess is treated as an automatic sign of transcendence, any intense experience might claim divine authority. Moreover, if the subject is described primarily as overwhelmed, the account risks neglecting the subject’s interpretive and critical responsibilities.

These problems do not invalidate the concept of saturation, but they require a more differentiated account. Saturation should not be identified with sheer intensity. An experience is not religiously significant merely because it is emotionally powerful. The excess of a saturated phenomenon concerns the relation between givenness and conceptual mastery, not simply the strength of a feeling. Further, the phenomenon must be interpreted within practices of discernment, language, memory, and community. The witness does not invent what is given, but neither does it receive the given without interpretation. Marion’s phenomenology is therefore most persuasive when it establishes the priority of donation without eliminating hermeneutics.

## II. Amazement and the Excess of Intuition

The mechanism of amazement deserves special attention because it connects Marion’s phenomenology to the problem of finite cognition. How can a finite subject experience something that exceeds its finite conditions? The answer must avoid two opposite errors. One error is to claim that the finite subject comprehends the infinite without remainder. The other is to claim that whatever cannot be comprehended cannot be experienced. The notion of excess intuition offers a way between these extremes.

Finite cognition is structured by limits. It receives from a particular perspective, through a body, within a history, and by means of concepts formed in a language and culture. It cannot take in the totality of what is given at once. Every object of experience is presented through profiles, aspects, and partial determinations. Even ordinary objects exceed any single perception, since the visible side refers to hidden sides and possible further encounters. Finite experience is therefore already marked by a relation between givenness and inexhaustibility.

The saturated phenomenon radicalizes this structure. What appears cannot be gathered into a stable synthesis adequate to its donation. The subject receives more than it can organize. But the excess is not located beyond all relation to cognition. It is experienced precisely as an inability to complete the cognitive task. The subject grasps that there is more, even though it cannot grasp the more as a total object. This is a paradox of knowledge: one can know that something exceeds one’s knowledge without possessing the excess in an exhaustive form.

Amazement is the affective and existential form of this paradox. It is the moment in which the subject becomes conscious of being surpassed. In ordinary cognition, the ego tends to move from uncertainty toward determination and from receptivity toward control. In amazement, this movement is interrupted. The phenomenon remains present, but its presence cannot be converted into a final possession. The subject is held in an interval between reception and comprehension.

This interval is not a defect that must be overcome as quickly as possible. It can become the very site of religious experience. The finite ego encounters a meaning that is not manufactured by it but that nevertheless addresses it. The excess of intuition thus does not destroy intelligibility; it changes its form. Meaning becomes something to be received and interpreted over time rather than something to be completed in a single act of conceptual mastery.

The infinite, in this context, should not be imagined as a very large object. An infinite object would still belong to the order of objects and would merely exceed the finite subject quantitatively. Religious transcendence is more radical. It concerns a source or mode of givenness that cannot be placed within the same horizon as the finite knower. The infinite gives itself without becoming fully enclosed by the categories of the finite. The paradox is that it is available in experience without being available as a possessable object.

The solution to the tension between finite cognition and infinite donation therefore lies in distinguishing reception from comprehension. The finite subject can receive more than it can comprehend. It can be affected, transformed, and called by what it cannot render exhaustively present to itself. In fact, if infinite donation were fully comprehended by the finite ego, it would cease to be infinite in any meaningful sense. The incompleteness of understanding is not an accidental obstacle but a structural consequence of the relation.

This does not mean that every interpretation is equally valid. The impossibility of final comprehension does not abolish truth. A finite interpretation can be more or less faithful, more or less responsive to the phenomenon, more or less capable of preserving its excess rather than reducing it. Religious language often functions in this way. Metaphor, symbol, narrative, liturgy, and testimony do not necessarily fail because they do not provide literal possession of the infinite. They can be modes of attending to a reality that exceeds direct conceptual capture.

Amazement also reconfigures time. The saturated phenomenon is not exhausted at the instant of its appearance. Its meaning unfolds through recollection, repetition, interpretation, and response. A religious event may be experienced as immediately decisive, but its significance becomes intelligible only through a temporal process. The subject looks back, revises its understanding, and discovers new dimensions in what initially appeared. The excess of the phenomenon is therefore not only a surplus of content; it is a surplus of future interpretation.

## III. Ricoeur: Call, Response, and the Hermeneutics of the Self

Where Marion emphasizes the excess of donation, Ricoeur emphasizes the structure through which the self becomes capable of responding to what addresses it. Ricoeur’s philosophy resists both the sovereign ego and the disappearance of subjectivity. The self is neither an absolute origin nor a mere effect. It is a mediated, interpreted, and responsible self, formed through language, narrative, action, and relation to others.

The concept of call and response articulates the religious form of this relation. A call is not simply a piece of information transmitted from one consciousness to another. It is an address that demands a response. It summons the hearer to recognize that its existence is not self-grounding. The call may be expressed through scripture, prophetic speech, the face of the other, conscience, historical suffering, or an event of grace. Its essential feature is not a particular sensory form but its addressive character. It comes as something directed toward me, requiring not merely cognition but decision.

The response, correspondingly, is not a creative act that produces the call’s authority. A response presupposes that something has already addressed the respondent. Yet the response is not passive repetition. To respond is to interpret, accept, resist, question, or transform the meaning of the call. The call becomes historically and existentially effective only through a response, but its priority is not thereby erased. The response is dependent without being mechanical.

This structure clarifies the status of the subject in religious experience. The subject is constituted as a respondent. Before the ego can posit itself as a self-sufficient source of meaning, it is already situated within language, tradition, relationships, and demands that call it into responsibility. The self becomes itself by answering. In this sense, subjectivity is fundamentally responsive rather than purely constitutive.

Ricoeur’s account is valuable because it prevents the phenomenology of excess from becoming a philosophy of mere overwhelm. A subject that is only struck by an event remains insufficiently described. Religious experience includes action, interpretation, commitment, and responsibility. The called subject must discern what is being asked, determine how to respond, and accept accountability for the response. The experience of transcendence is therefore inseparable from ethical and hermeneutic consequences.

Call and response also introduce a necessary ambiguity. A call is never encountered without interpretation. One must decide whether what appears is genuinely a call, what its content may be, and to whom or what it should be attributed. The danger of false certainty is therefore internal to religious experience. A person can mistake desire, fear, social pressure, or ideological command for divine vocation. Ricoeur’s hermeneutics is important precisely because it recognizes that self-understanding is mediated by symbols and narratives whose meanings cannot be reduced to immediate subjective certainty.

The response is thus a process of interpretation that remains open to correction. It involves suspicion as well as affirmation. A religious symbol can disclose meaning, but it can also be appropriated by systems of domination. A command can call one toward justice, but claims of divine authority have also been used to legitimate violence and exclusion. The priority of the call cannot function as a license to bypass critical reflection. If the subject is a witness, it must also be a discerning witness.

Ricoeur’s conception of the self as capable of selfhood through alterity complements Marion’s account of donation. Marion explains why the phenomenon cannot be mastered; Ricoeur explains how the subject can inhabit a relation to what it cannot master. The self does not need to convert the call into an object in order to respond. It can understand through narrative, metaphor, practical judgment, and lived commitment. The response is an act of finite interpretation that remains answerable to an infinite or transcendent source.

At the same time, Ricoeur’s model introduces an important qualification to the thesis that the subject is constituted by a “higher other.” The other should not be conceived as a simple external agent who stands above the subject and imposes an unambiguous message. The call is received through linguistic and historical mediation. The respondent is formed not only by the source of the call but also by the traditions, communities, and stories within which the call becomes recognizable. This makes religious experience less like the reception of a private supernatural signal and more like participation in a field of meaning that claims the subject.

Ricoeur therefore protects the irreducibility of the subject without returning to subjectivism. The subject is not the originator of the call, but it is responsible for its response. It is not the master of meaning, but it is an interpreter of meaning. It is not self-constituting, but neither is it a blank surface. It is a “capable” self: capable of speaking, acting, promising, imputing responsibility, and giving testimony. Religious experience becomes credible not merely through its intensity but through the transformations and forms of responsibility that follow from it.

## IV. Michel Henry and the Word of the World and the Word of Life

Michel Henry offers a different but complementary route to the problem of revelation. His distinction between the word of the world and the word of life concerns two fundamentally different modes of manifestation. The word of the world belongs to the realm of exteriority, visibility, objectification, and representation. In the world, things appear at a distance from one another. They can be seen, measured, described, and placed within relations of spatial and temporal exteriority. This is the domain of ordinary phenomenality and objective knowledge.

The word of life, by contrast, is not primarily an external message or an object that appears before consciousness. Life manifests itself through an immediate self-affection. To live is to experience oneself in the very act of being affected. Pain, joy, effort, desire, and suffering are not first encountered as external objects. They are felt from within. Life reveals itself in an immanence that precedes the distance between subject and object.

Henry’s point is not that the world is unreal or irrelevant. Scientific and ordinary worldly knowledge are genuine within their domain. The problem arises when the mode of manifestation proper to the world is taken as the only possible mode of phenomenality. If only what appears externally and objectively counts as real, then the inner revelation of life is either ignored or reduced to a secondary psychological event. Henry reverses this priority. The most originary revelation is not the appearance of an object in the world but the self-revelation of life in affectivity.

This distinction bears directly on religious experience. If religious experience is forced into the structure of worldly objectivity, God must be treated as an extraordinary entity that appears in the same basic way as other entities. Religious knowledge then becomes a problem of evidence: what observation, argument, or representation could establish the existence of the divine object? Henry’s phenomenology suggests that this is the wrong question. The divine may be given not as an object in the world but as the very power of self-affecting life, as a deeper mode of manifestation that makes worldly experience possible.

The “word of life” is not a spoken sentence transmitted through external space. It is the non-objective revelation by which life gives itself. In Henry’s Christian orientation, this life is related to divine Life, and the human living subject is not self-grounding but generated within a more originary life. The individual does not first possess life and then happen to reflect upon it. The individual is given to itself in living. Its subjectivity is an effect of a prior self-revelation that it did not produce.

This provides a distinctive interpretation of the thesis that the ego is constituted by a higher other. The other is not necessarily encountered as an external figure standing across from the subject. The higher other can be understood as the originary life in which the subject finds itself affectively given. The subject is not constituted by an object but by a mode of revelation more intimate than objectivity. The divine is not merely beyond the self; it is also the interior source of the self’s capacity to feel, suffer, rejoice, and exist.

Henry’s account intensifies the critique of the constituting ego. In worldly phenomenology, consciousness is often described as intentional: it reaches toward objects, gives them meaning, and organizes them. But life, in Henry’s sense, does not first appear through intentional distance. It is revealed in the immediate experience of being alive. The self cannot stand outside this self-affection to constitute it. It is already immersed in it. The subject is therefore not an originator of manifestation but a living manifestation of a prior power.

This position also helps resolve a problem left open by Marion. If the saturated phenomenon is too excessive for conceptual mastery, one might still imagine it as an external event that overwhelms the subject. Henry shifts attention from an external excess of intuition to an internal excess of affective self-givenness. Religious revelation need not consist only in extraordinary events, visions, or objects. It may occur in the depths of suffering, joy, compassion, and the irreducible experience of life. These experiences are not necessarily “religious” in themselves, but they provide the phenomenological ground upon which religious interpretation can arise.

Yet Henry’s distinction creates its own difficulties. If the word of life is radically immanent and non-objective, how can it be distinguished from ordinary feeling or subjective affect? A private experience of pain is certainly real as self-affection, but does its reality by itself establish a divine source? Henry’s emphasis on immanence risks making transcendence difficult to articulate. If God is encountered only as the immanent self-revelation of life, the alterity of God may appear to collapse into the subject’s own affective interiority.

This criticism is significant. Religious experience generally contains not only intimacy but also address, demand, and otherness. The divine is not simply the fact that I feel alive. It may be encountered as that which judges, calls, forgives, or commands me. Henry’s account can preserve otherness by distinguishing the individual’s life from the Life that gives it, but the distinction must be carefully maintained. Otherwise the word of life becomes indistinguishable from self-presence.

The best interpretation of Henry is therefore not that interior affectivity automatically proves divine revelation, but that it identifies a dimension of manifestation that cannot be reduced to worldly objectification. Religious experience may involve a recognition that the life I experience is received rather than self-produced. The word of life names the depth at which the subject discovers itself as dependent upon a more originary giving. Ricoeur’s call and response can then supply the dimension of address that Henry’s immanence alone may lack, while Marion’s saturation can describe how this life exceeds the subject’s attempts to contain it.

## V. The Subject as Witness Rather Than Originator

The three perspectives converge in a redefinition of subjectivity. The ego is not abolished, but decentered. It does not disappear into transcendence; it is given a new role. The subject becomes a witness.

To call the subject a witness is to place it between passivity and activity. A witness receives an event, but must also attend to it. A witness does not create what happened, but must interpret and testify concerning it. A witness is exposed to something that precedes its account, yet no event becomes historically meaningful without some form of testimony. Witnessing is therefore a mode of participation in truth that does not imply origination.

This model is especially appropriate to religious experience. The believer does not manufacture revelation, but neither does revelation exist for the believer apart from its reception. The religious subject is the site at which donation becomes acknowledged, interpreted, and embodied. The witness is constituted by what it bears witness to, but it also contributes to the articulation and transmission of what has been given.

The distinction between originator and witness can be stated phenomenologically. The originator would be a subject for whom meaning begins in an act of constitution. The witness is a subject for whom meaning arrives as an event that must be received. The originator seeks to secure the phenomenon within its own conceptual horizon. The witness recognizes that the phenomenon precedes and exceeds that horizon. The originator treats alterity as material for its own synthesis. The witness allows alterity to remain alterity while attempting a faithful response.

This does not make the witness infallible. Indeed, the witness’s finitude is evident in the partiality of every testimony. No witness sees everything, and no testimony reproduces an event without interpretation. Religious witness is likewise perspectival, embodied, and historically conditioned. The fact that a subject is constituted by a higher other does not mean that every account the subject gives is free of distortion. The witness can misunderstand, exaggerate, suppress, or instrumentalize what it claims to have received.

For this reason, testimony must be tested through practices of accountability. In religious contexts, such practices may include communal discernment, scriptural interpretation, ethical fruits, critical reflection, and comparison with the testimonies of others. A claim to have received a call cannot be validated solely by the intensity of the claimant’s experience. A genuine religious response would ordinarily manifest itself in forms of responsibility, humility, love, justice, and openness to correction. These criteria do not convert transcendence into an empirical object, but they help distinguish receptive witness from self-authorizing projection.

The witness is also constituted retrospectively. One may not recognize an experience as a call or revelation at the moment it occurs. Meaning can emerge later, through recollection and reinterpretation. Ricoeur’s narrative account of selfhood is essential here: the subject becomes capable of saying “I” by integrating events into a story, but that story remains open to revision. A religious experience may reorder the narrative identity of the subject, transforming the interpretation of its past and the possibilities of its future.

Marion’s saturated phenomenon and Ricoeur’s witness thus converge in the idea of an event that gives the subject its identity. The subject is not first a completed self who encounters a meaningful event. It becomes a different self through the event. This is especially clear in conversion, vocation, repentance, and forgiveness. The person who receives a call cannot simply add it as new information to an unchanged identity. The call can disclose that the previous self-understanding was inadequate. The response creates a new orientation.

Henry adds that this transformation is rooted in the affective fact that the subject is alive and affected before it can objectify itself. The witness is not merely a detached observer of revelation. It is a body and a living interiority. It suffers, hopes, desires, and rejoices. The truth of religious experience is therefore not exhausted by propositional correctness. It includes a transformation in how life is felt and lived. The witness bears testimony not only in words but also in the manner of existence that follows from what has been received.

## VI. Infinite Donation and Finite Conditions

The central philosophical tension can now be stated more precisely. If donation is infinite or excessive, while the subject is finite, embodied, and historically conditioned, how can the subject receive the donation without either reducing it or being destroyed by it?

One response would be to say that the infinite cannot truly be experienced by the finite. It could be approached asymptotically but never given. This protects the transcendence of the infinite at the price of making religious experience merely indirect or symbolic. Another response would be to say that the infinite becomes fully present within finite consciousness. This secures experience but risks reducing the infinite to a content possessed by the ego.

The concepts discussed here support a third option: the infinite is experienced as infinite, that is, as exceeding the conditions of its reception. The finite subject does not contain the infinite; it receives itself as addressed by what it cannot contain. The relation is genuine precisely because it remains asymmetrical. The infinite is present in a mode that preserves its excess.

This asymmetry is not a failure of relation. A relation does not require equality between its terms. Indeed, some relations are possible only because one term cannot be reduced to the other. The face of another person cannot be fully possessed by my concepts, yet I can encounter and respond to the other. Similarly, divine donation can be genuinely experienced without becoming an object completely available to finite cognition.

The finite conditions of experience are not simply barriers to donation. They are the means through which donation becomes concrete. The body provides vulnerability; language provides articulation; memory provides continuity; imagination opens possibilities; community provides criteria of interpretation; action gives the response historical form. Without these conditions, there would be no human experience of transcendence, only an abstract postulate of what lies beyond experience.

This means that finitude is ambivalent. It limits comprehension but enables reception. A completely infinite consciousness, if such a notion were coherent, would have no external excess against which to be amazed. Amazement requires a finite standpoint. The subject’s inability to master the phenomenon is therefore not merely an epistemic deficiency. It is the condition of being affected by a reality greater than itself.

The infinite donation is also mediated by finite forms. Religious experience is expressed in words, images, rites, narratives, and ethical practices. These forms necessarily limit and shape what they express. Yet limitation need not equal falsification. A finite symbol can faithfully point beyond itself precisely by refusing to exhaust what it signifies. The symbol becomes inadequate not because it says nothing but because it says something whose meaning cannot be closed.

Here Ricoeur’s hermeneutics is indispensable. Interpretation does not overcome the excess of donation; it preserves and negotiates it. A symbol gives rise to thought because it says more than can be reduced to a single proposition. The interpretive task is not to choose between literal possession and total skepticism. It is to engage in a disciplined movement in which the interpreter allows the symbol to challenge preconceptions while also examining the historical and ideological conditions under which it is received.

Henry’s word of life reminds us that the infinite is not necessarily encountered only in representations. The deepest religious experience may be the realization that one’s own life is received. The subject does not produce the affective substance of its existence. It finds itself already given to itself. This self-givenness can be interpreted religiously as participation in a higher Life. In that interpretation, the infinite is not a distant object but an intimate source.

Marion, by contrast, prevents this intimacy from becoming mere self-enclosure. The source that gives itself in life can still exceed every attempt to possess it. The infinite is both intimate and transcendent, present and unmasterable. Ricoeur then shows how this relation becomes responsible through the structure of response. The resulting model avoids both an external supernaturalism and an immanent subjectivism.

## VII. Evaluation and Limits

The central thesis is compelling insofar as it identifies a necessary condition of religious experience: the ego must be displaced from the position of absolute origin. If the religious phenomenon were entirely constituted by the subject, religion would be indistinguishable from projection. The difference between revelation and fantasy would disappear, since both would be products of the same constitutive activity. Religious experience requires that something be given which the subject does not control.

But the thesis becomes problematic if “higher other” is treated as a simple explanation that ends inquiry. To say that the ego is constituted by a higher other does not yet tell us how the other is identified, how its call is interpreted, or how claims about it are evaluated. The language of transcendence can itself become an instrument of domination. A person or institution may claim to speak for the higher other and thereby demand obedience. Any phenomenology of religious experience must therefore preserve the possibility of critique.

The subject as witness is a useful solution because witnessing includes both receptivity and responsibility. The witness does not create the event, but must answer for the way it testifies. In religious life, this means that the experience of being called cannot be separated from the obligation to examine the consequences of one’s response. If a purported revelation produces cruelty, contempt, or immunity from criticism, its claim to transcendence is weakened, even if the experience itself was subjectively overwhelming.

There is also a danger in describing the subject as constituted entirely by alterity. The subject’s own agency, creativity, and freedom might appear merely derivative. Yet response is not an inferior form of action. The capacity to respond is itself a form of freedom. A call does not determine the response in the manner of a physical cause. It opens a field of possibilities within which the subject must decide. The subject is constituted by alterity not as a puppet but as a responsible respondent.

Ricoeur’s account is especially important here because it places freedom within dependence. The self becomes capable of action because it is addressed, trusted, promised something, or held accountable. Absolute independence would not necessarily be freedom; it might be isolation. A self constituted through call and response can possess agency without pretending to be self-originating.

Henry’s contribution is strongest when religious experience is understood as a transformation of the manner in which life is lived from within. His distinction between the word of the world and the word of life demonstrates why objective evidence cannot exhaust the meaning of religion. Faith is not simply a hypothesis about an invisible object. It concerns the mode in which existence is received and felt. Yet Henry’s framework requires supplementation by Ricoeur’s account of language and Marion’s account of excess, because immanent self-affection alone does not establish the alterity or ethical demand characteristic of religion.

Marion’s contribution, meanwhile, is strongest as a critique of mastery. Saturation explains why the divine cannot be reduced to conceptual possession and why amazement is more than psychological surprise. But his model must be joined to discernment and interpretation. Otherwise, the authority of excess can become too easy to claim. Not every overwhelming experience is revelation; not every inability to explain is transcendence. The witness must be able to distinguish fidelity to the phenomenon from fascination with intensity.

Together, these thinkers suggest that religious experience has a threefold structure. First, there is donation: something gives itself beyond the subject’s constitutive control. Second, there is manifestation in either worldly event, call, or affective life: the donation becomes available in a particular mode. Third, there is response: the finite subject interprets, bears, and enacts what it has received. None of these moments can be reduced to the others. Donation without response would not become human experience; response without donation would be projection; manifestation without interpretation would remain indeterminate.

## Conclusion

Marion’s saturated phenomenon, Ricoeur’s call and response, and Henry’s distinction between the word of the world and the word of life each challenge the image of the ego as the sovereign origin of meaning. Marion shows that phenomena can give more intuition than concepts can contain. Ricoeur shows that the self becomes itself by responding to an address that precedes its autonomy. Henry shows that the most originary revelation may occur not in external objectivity but in the immanent self-affection of life, through which the subject discovers that it is given to itself.

The central thesis that religious experience is possible because the finite ego does not constitute or master the phenomenon is therefore persuasive, provided it is stated carefully. Religious experience requires an excess that comes before the subject’s control. The ego is constituted by a higher other, but this constitution is not the mechanical production of a passive object. It is the formation of a responsive, interpretive, embodied, and responsible self.

Amazement names the event in which this structure becomes felt. Through excess intuition, the subject encounters more than it can synthesize. It does not comprehend the infinite as a finite object; it experiences the infinite as that which gives itself while exceeding comprehension. The tension between finite cognition and infinite donation is resolved not by eliminating either term but by distinguishing reception from mastery. The finite subject can receive what it cannot contain, and its inability to contain the gift is part of what makes the gift recognizable as transcendent.

The resulting subject is neither an autonomous originator nor an empty vessel. It is a witness. As witness, it is dependent upon what it receives but active in attending, interpreting, responding, and testifying. Its testimony is finite and fallible, yet not therefore meaningless. The witness’s task is to preserve the excess of what has been given without converting that excess into a warrant for domination or certainty. Religious experience is thus not the possession of an infinite object by a finite mind. It is the transformation of a finite life through an encounter with what it did not originate and cannot finally master.

The philosophical significance of this redefinition extends beyond religion. It contests the assumption that freedom requires absolute self-foundation, that knowledge requires total conceptual control, and that subjectivity must precede all meaningful relation. The witness demonstrates another possibility: the self can become free by answering, can know by receiving, and can become itself through what exceeds it. In this sense, the higher other does not negate the subject. It calls the subject into existence as a being capable of amazement, responsibility, and truth.
