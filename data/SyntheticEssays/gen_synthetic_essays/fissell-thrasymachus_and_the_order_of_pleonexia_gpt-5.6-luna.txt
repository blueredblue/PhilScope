# Pleonexia, Rule, and the Coherence of Thrasymachus’s Immoralism

Thrasymachus’ intervention in the first book of Plato’s *Republic* is often treated as philosophically unstable. He appears to offer several incompatible definitions of justice: justice is “the advantage of the stronger”; justice is obedience to the laws of the ruling power; justice is the advantage of another; and, finally, complete injustice is more profitable and more powerful than justice. The apparent difficulty is intensified by the distinction between the ruler considered as a ruler and the ruler considered as an individual. If the ruler is genuinely wise and never errs, as Thrasymachus initially claims, then justice should be advantageous to the ruler, because justice consists in serving the ruler’s advantage. Yet if justice is the advantage of another, justice appears disadvantageous to the just person. Socrates exploits this tension by arguing that every genuine craft benefits its object rather than its practitioner. Thrasymachus’ account then seems to collapse into either a descriptive theory of political power—justice is simply whatever the powerful command—or a confused and inconsistent version of conventionalism or positivism.

This interpretation misses the deeper unity of Thrasymachus’ position. His claims are not best understood as a sequence of accidental shifts between incompatible theories. They are expressions of an immoralist doctrine organized around the primacy of *pleonexia*: the disposition to have more, to dominate, and to secure an unequal advantage over others. Justice is not, for Thrasymachus, fundamentally a norm of reciprocal fairness or a principle of common benefit. It is a political mechanism by which the ruling power extracts advantage from the ruled. Injustice, by contrast, is the direct expression of the natural and political order of superiority. The successful individual who possesses the power and intelligence to dominate others therefore realizes the deepest truth of the political world.

The apparent conflict between the perspective of the subject and the perspective of the ruler can be resolved by distinguishing two figures whom Thrasymachus’ argument tends to run together: the aspiring tyrant, who must commit complete injustice by usurping and subordinating existing powers, and the established ruler, whose conduct is governed by an expert knowledge of administration and whose selfishness is exercised scientifically rather than impulsively. The first represents pleonexia in its most naked and revolutionary form; the second represents pleonexia institutionalized as rule. This distinction does not make Thrasymachus a consistent political philosopher in every modern sense. His argument remains deliberately provocative, and Plato gives him no systematic account of the transition from successful seizure of power to stable government. But it does show that his doctrine need not be dismissed as a mere confusion between descriptive and normative claims.

The most ambitious defense of Thrasymachus would go further. It would treat pleonexia not simply as a psychological desire or a political strategy, but as a normative cosmic order: the principle according to which the stronger naturally ought to possess more and the inferior ought to serve. On this interpretation, Thrasymachus is not merely saying that the powerful happen to impose their will. He is saying that successful domination discloses the structure of value itself. Strength, superiority, and the capacity to command are not morally neutral facts; they are standards of what is fitting. Such a reading gives Thrasymachus a genuine immoralist doctrine and helps answer Maguire’s charges of inconsistency. Yet it also reveals the limits of the defense. The cosmic interpretation can unify Thrasymachus’ claims, but it cannot fully resolve every dialectical problem raised by Socrates. In particular, it does not establish that domination is beneficial to the dominant, nor does it prove that the tyrant’s life is happier than the just person’s. Thrasymachus can be defended against the charge of elementary incoherence, but not against every substantive objection Socrates raises.

## I. Thrasymachus’ central principle: justice as institutionalized pleonexia

Thrasymachus’ opening definition, that justice is “the advantage of the stronger,” is frequently interpreted as a crude version of legal positivism. On this view, “the stronger” means whoever controls the state, and “justice” means whatever laws that power happens to enact. Justice is therefore reducible to political convention. If the ruling party commands one thing, justice is that thing; if it commands the opposite, justice becomes the opposite. Thrasymachus would then be saying nothing more than that the law is whatever the lawgiver makes it.

There are textual reasons to resist this interpretation. Thrasymachus does indeed insist that different regimes make different laws in accordance with their own interests: democracies establish democratic laws, oligarchies oligarchic laws, and so on. But he does not stop with the claim that law is an expression of power. He also argues that it is just for subjects to obey these laws because obedience serves the advantage of the ruling power. His central concern is therefore not merely the origin of legal rules but the asymmetrical relation of command and subordination that law creates. Law is important because it stabilizes an order in which some rule and others serve.

This is where *pleonexia* becomes the key to the doctrine. The term is often translated as “having more,” but its meaning is richer than simple greed. It includes the desire to acquire more than one’s share, to possess greater power, to subordinate others, and to refuse equality when equality would limit one’s advantage. Thrasymachus’ immoralism rests on the claim that this desire is not an unfortunate deviation from political life. It is the motive that structures political life. The rulers make laws in their own interest because they seek more; the subjects are called just because they are compelled to accept less. Justice therefore names the moral vocabulary by which the powerful organize and legitimize their superiority.

This explains why Thrasymachus’ later claims about injustice are not random reversals. He says that injustice, if practiced on a sufficiently complete scale, is more profitable, powerful, and effective than justice. The small-scale wrongdoer is punished, because he lacks the power to impose his interpretation of justice. The successful tyrant, however, appropriates the resources of the city and makes others work for him. His conduct is condemned by conventional morality, but from Thrasymachus’ perspective conventional morality is precisely the instrument used by the many to restrain superior individuals. The tyrant is “completely unjust” because he does not merely violate isolated rules; he overturns the existing distribution of power and makes the whole community serve him.

The contrast between ordinary injustice and complete injustice is therefore a contrast between unsuccessful and successful pleonexia. A petty thief desires more but remains subject to the law. He is pleonectic in motive but weak in execution. The tyrant desires more and has the power to redefine the order under which others live. His injustice is complete because it is not merely a violation within a political order; it becomes the organizing principle of a new order. Thrasymachus’ admiration for such a figure is not based on the proposition that all rule-breaking is admirable. It is based on the proposition that the highest form of political excellence consists in transforming one’s private desire for more into public authority.

This point also clarifies why Thrasymachus is not simply a positivist. A positivist account would treat the validity of justice as dependent upon the enacted rule. Thrasymachus instead evaluates regimes by the extent to which they express and reward superiority. The law is not valuable merely because it is enacted. It is valuable, from the ruler’s perspective, because it secures the ruler’s advantage; and the person who can rise above the law and appropriate the state is superior to the ordinary law-abiding subject. Thrasymachus’ standard is thus not legal validity but domination. Law is one instrument of pleonexia, while successful illegality may be a higher instrument.

## II. Ruler and subject: the source of the apparent contradiction

The major difficulty arises when Thrasymachus’ definition is examined from different social perspectives. From the subject’s perspective, justice is obedience to the commands of the ruler and is therefore “the advantage of another.” The just person serves someone else. From the ruler’s perspective, however, justice is obedience from the subjects and should be advantageous to the ruler. How can the same practice be both the advantage of another and a disadvantage to oneself? And if the ruler seeks his own advantage, why does Thrasymachus later insist that the ruler, considered as a ruler, never makes mistakes?

Socrates’ craft analogy is designed to exploit exactly this tension. Every craft, he argues, benefits the object upon which it operates: medicine benefits the body, navigation benefits the passengers, and the shepherd’s art benefits the sheep. If ruling is a craft, then ruling should benefit the ruled. The ruler qua ruler should not be seeking his own advantage. Thrasymachus’ claim that justice is the advantage of the stronger would therefore be false, because the genuine political craft aims at the advantage of its subjects.

Thrasymachus’ first response is to deny that the shepherd’s concern for the sheep is genuinely directed at the sheep. The shepherd fattens and preserves the sheep for his own purposes. He may care for them, but his care is instrumental. The same applies to political rule. Subjects are maintained, disciplined, and organized because they are useful to the ruler. The ruler’s “care” is therefore compatible with exploitation. The craft analogy does not refute Thrasymachus unless it can be shown that the end of a craft must be the benefit of its object in a non-instrumental sense.

Thrasymachus’ second response is more important. He distinguishes the craft from the economic or political use to which the craft is put. The physician, considered strictly as a physician, aims at health; but the physician may practice medicine for payment. Likewise, the ruler, considered strictly as a ruler, possesses knowledge of ruling; but the ruler may exercise that knowledge for personal gain. Socrates attempts to separate the art of ruling from the acquisition of wages, whereas Thrasymachus insists that the actual ruler is a person who uses the art in a social context. The question is not simply what the craft qua craft aims at, but who benefits from the skilled exercise of power.

This distinction can be made coherent if Thrasymachus’ “ruler” is understood in two senses. In the technical sense, the ruler is an expert administrator who knows how to organize subjects, extract resources, prevent rebellion, and preserve the regime. In the political and personal sense, the ruler is the individual or class that owns, controls, or benefits from this organization. The technical knowledge is directed toward the stability and productivity of the ruled population, but its ultimate purpose is to secure the ruler’s superior position. The subjects benefit in a limited and instrumental way: they are kept alive, productive, and orderly because those conditions serve the dominant power.

This interpretation resolves the ruler-subject tension without eliminating it. For the subject, justice is genuinely disadvantageous because it requires subordination and prevents the subject from taking more. For the ruler, the same justice is advantageous because it confirms the ruler’s authority and channels the subjects’ labor toward the ruler’s benefit. Justice is thus relational. It is not one undifferentiated condition that has the same value for everyone. It is the name for an arrangement in which the advantage of one class is constituted by the disadvantage and obedience of another.

The distinction also explains why Thrasymachus can say both that the ruler does not err and that rulers make laws for their own advantage. The ruler qua expert does not err about the means required to maintain rule. The ruler may, of course, make a false empirical judgment or miscalculate a policy. But Thrasymachus’ stronger claim is that genuine expertise, insofar as it is expertise, does not prescribe what is contrary to its defining end. The ruler who makes a law that accidentally harms his own authority has acted badly as a ruler, just as a physician who worsens health has failed as a physician. Yet the end of ruling is not the welfare of the subjects; it is the preservation and extension of the ruler’s power. Thus the ruler’s expertise consists in knowing how to make subjects serve that end.

The apparent contradiction therefore depends on an ambiguity in “advantage.” The subjects’ advantage may be an instrumental advantage granted to them so that they remain useful. The ruler’s advantage is the final political advantage of domination. Thrasymachus does not need to deny that subjects receive benefits. He needs only to deny that these benefits are the ultimate standard of political rule. A well-fed, secure, and prosperous subject may still be politically disadvantaged if the subject lacks the ability to determine the terms of his life and is compelled to produce more for another.

## III. The aspiring tyrant and the established ruler

The most fruitful way to sharpen this distinction is to separate the aspiring tyrant from the established ruler. Thrasymachus’ description of complete injustice seems to praise the tyrant who seizes the city, enslaves its citizens, and appropriates its wealth. Yet his account of the ruler qua ruler emphasizes expertise, stability, and freedom from error. A tyrant who merely acts on appetite may be violent but not genuinely expert. He may possess the desire to dominate without possessing the knowledge required to preserve domination. How, then, can the tyrant be the paradigm of the ruler?

The answer is that the aspiring tyrant and the established ruler represent successive moments in the realization of pleonexia. The aspiring tyrant is the individual whose desire for more breaks through the existing order. He refuses the distribution of power and wealth that law calls just. His injustice is “complete” because he seeks not an isolated advantage but the entire political structure. He wants others to become instruments of his life. The very comprehensiveness of his ambition makes him the clearest embodiment of pleonexia.

Yet aspiration alone is not enough. If the tyrant cannot organize the city, secure obedience, manage resources, and neutralize rivals, his injustice will fail. He will remain a criminal within the old political order rather than becoming the founder of a new one. The established ruler is therefore the successful tyrant—or, more precisely, the pleonectic individual whose domination has become institutionalized. The ruler’s scientific selfishness consists in using expert knowledge to turn private superiority into durable public authority.

The word “scientific” is crucial. Thrasymachus does not praise unregulated appetite. A person who wants more but cannot calculate the means of acquiring and retaining it is not admirable on his own terms. Political domination requires knowledge. The established ruler must know when to reward and punish, how much wealth to extract, how to divide subjects against one another, and how to represent coercion as law. The ruler’s selfishness is “scientific” because it is disciplined by an understanding of the conditions under which power can be maintained.

This distinction also explains why the established ruler may appear to serve the subjects. A ruler who destroys the productivity of the city destroys the source of his own power. He must therefore preserve the city’s basic functioning. The expert shepherd does not slaughter the flock immediately; he breeds, feeds, and manages it. The ruler’s care is real as a technique but false as a moral orientation. It is care for the sake of appropriation. The ruler benefits the subjects in order to benefit from them.

From this perspective, the tyrant’s complete injustice is not simply a violation of justice but the attempted replacement of one regime of pleonexia with another. Existing laws express the advantage of an established ruling group. The aspiring tyrant’s injustice challenges that group and seeks to make the tyrant himself the stronger. If he succeeds, his private pleonexia becomes public justice: the new laws will define obedience to him as just and resistance as unjust. The distinction between justice and injustice is therefore historically relative at the level of law but not normatively empty at the level of domination. What remains constant is the structure of superiority.

This account permits Thrasymachus to make a distinction between the “true” ruler and the mere office-holder. An office-holder may be nominally powerful but lack the knowledge to preserve his authority. A true ruler is one who possesses the relevant expertise and whose commands effectively organize the political order. The claim that the ruler does not err applies to the latter. In this sense, Thrasymachus’ theory resembles the distinction between the person who occupies a role accidentally and the person who performs its function successfully. But unlike Socrates, Thrasymachus defines the function of ruling in terms of control rather than service.

The distinction does not make Thrasymachus’ doctrine morally acceptable. It makes it internally structured. The aspiring tyrant explains the origin of domination; the established ruler explains its administration. The former reveals the aggressive essence of pleonexia, while the latter reveals its rational and institutional form. The ruler’s expertise does not replace selfishness; it perfects it.

## IV. Pleonexia as a normative order of strength

The strongest defense of Thrasymachus interprets pleonexia as more than a recurring human motive. It is a normative order grounded in strength. On this reading, the stronger do not merely possess more because they happen to be able to take more. They ought to possess more because superiority itself generates a claim to rule. The weak are not wronged when they are subordinated; rather, they are assigned the position appropriate to their inferior nature. Justice, in the deeper and perhaps more paradoxical sense, consists in the proper distribution of power according to strength.

This interpretation has an ancient pedigree. The claim that nature establishes one standard while convention establishes another is familiar from the sophistic debates represented elsewhere in Plato. Thrasymachus’ distinctive contribution is to make political power the measure of natural right. His “natural justice” is not a peaceful harmony in which each performs a complementary function. It is an order of unequal possession in which the stronger appropriately take more. Political law is just insofar as it expresses the strength of the ruling power; when it obstructs a superior individual, it becomes an artificial restraint imposed by the many.

Framing pleonexia as a normative cosmic order helps answer the charge that Thrasymachus moves illegitimately from “the stronger in fact rule” to “the stronger ought to rule.” If the doctrine were merely descriptive, that inference would indeed be invalid. But Thrasymachus need not make it. He can reject the moral premise that facts of power are normatively inert. For him, strength is not an accidental property external to value. It is the visible manifestation of superiority, and superiority is itself the basis of right. The person capable of commanding, acquiring, and preserving more displays a higher mode of being. The fact of successful domination is therefore not simply evidence of power; it is evidence of worth.

This interpretation also gives meaning to Thrasymachus’ apparent admiration for injustice. Injustice is valuable not because it breaks rules as such but because it realizes the natural hierarchy of strength. The unjust person takes more, commands more, and is less constrained by the interests of inferiors. Justice, by contrast, imposes equality, reciprocity, and restraint. It forces the superior to behave as though he were no better than those beneath him. From the standpoint of cosmic pleonexia, that is not virtue but self-mutilation.

Maguire’s inconsistency charges can be understood as objections to this normative elevation of power. If Thrasymachus says that justice is the advantage of the stronger, then either “stronger” means those who actually hold power or it means those who are genuinely superior. If it means the former, the theory is descriptive and vulnerable to counterexamples: rulers can make mistakes, and subjects can sometimes benefit from laws. If it means the latter, then the theory risks becoming tautological: whoever ought to rule is defined as the stronger, and whatever the ruler does is just because the ruler is superior. The cosmic interpretation answers the first horn by acknowledging its normative character, but it does not entirely escape the second. It requires an account of what strength means beyond mere success.

Thrasymachus appears to supply such an account through expertise. The stronger are not simply those who can use brute force for a moment. They are those who can achieve and maintain command. Strength includes intelligence, strategic knowledge, courage, and the ability to organize others. The established ruler is superior because he can convert these capacities into a stable order. In this way, expertise differentiates genuine superiority from temporary victory. The successful tyrant is not merely the strongest because he has won once; he is strong because he can make his victory durable.

Still, the cosmic defense remains vulnerable. It may show that Thrasymachus has a norm—superiority should dominate—but it does not establish why that norm should be accepted.
