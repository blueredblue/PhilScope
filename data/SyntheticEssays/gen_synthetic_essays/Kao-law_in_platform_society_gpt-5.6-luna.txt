# Digital Platforms as Quasi-Public Actors: Justice, Property, Consent, Domination, and Expressive Freedom

Digital platforms occupy an unstable position in contemporary political life. They are formally private firms, organized around property rights, contractual terms, and profit-seeking exchange. Yet they also perform functions that resemble those of public institutions. They structure access to political debate, distribute news and information, mediate social relationships, organize economic opportunities, and establish rules governing speech and association for billions of people. A decision by a platform to suspend an account, demote a post, alter a recommendation system, or make certain information difficult to find can affect a person’s reputation, employment, political participation, and ability to communicate with others. The fact that such decisions are made by private corporations does not make their effects politically insignificant.

This institutional ambiguity generates a central normative question: should large digital platforms be governed as quasi-public actors subject to constitutional and public law principles? An affirmative answer need not imply that platforms are identical to states, nor that all private speech spaces must become government-controlled forums. Rather, it may mean that platforms whose power is sufficiently pervasive and socially indispensable should bear public-law duties of transparency, procedural fairness, non-arbitrariness, and accountability. The challenge is to determine what justifies such duties and how they can be imposed without destroying legitimate private association, property rights, or expressive freedom.

This essay assesses that question through the theories of John Rawls, Robert Nozick, Helen Nissenbaum, Philip Pettit, and Ronald Dworkin. Rawls’s theory of distributive justice supports treating access to digital communication as an increasingly important primary good, especially where communication platforms are necessary for exercising basic liberties and accessing social and economic opportunities. Nozick, by contrast, emphasizes the moral significance of private property and voluntary exchange, raising a powerful objection to state regulation that would convert private platforms into public utilities by coercion. Nissenbaum’s theory of contextual integrity complicates the assumption that users’ formal agreement to terms of service constitutes meaningful consent, particularly when users lack reasonable alternatives or cannot understand the platform’s informational practices. Pettit’s republican account of freedom as non-domination reveals how corporate governance may be objectionable even when it does not interfere constantly: the mere exposure to uncontrolled and unaccountable discretionary power can undermine freedom. Finally, Dworkin’s account of expressive freedom counsels against imposing substantive orthodoxy on platforms or compelling them to host all speech. Public law should therefore focus less on dictating outcomes and more on making algorithmic governance intelligible, contestable, and non-arbitrary.

The best conclusion is a qualified one. Major digital platforms should be treated as quasi-public actors when their scale, social centrality, and infrastructural importance give them effective power over citizens’ communicative and associational opportunities. But quasi-public status should not erase their private character or authorize comprehensive state control over content. Public law should impose structural and procedural obligations—transparency, reasons, appeals, consistency, independent auditing, data protections, and safeguards against discriminatory or arbitrary treatment—while preserving room for platforms to pursue distinctive expressive missions. The result would be a constitutionalization of platform governance without a wholesale nationalization of digital speech.

## I. The Case for Quasi-Public Governance

The traditional public-private distinction assumes that public institutions exercise coercive authority while private actors operate through voluntary exchange. On this model, constitutional principles constrain governments, whereas private firms remain largely free to determine whom they serve and what rules govern their property. Digital platforms challenge this distinction because they combine private ownership with public-like functions.

A social media platform may be privately owned, but it can serve as a principal venue for political campaigns, public-health communication, journalism, protest coordination, and interpersonal association. A search engine can affect which sources of knowledge are visible. A video platform can determine which cultural and political materials reach mass audiences. A professional networking site can influence access to employment. A marketplace platform can operate as a gateway to commercial participation. These platforms do not merely offer optional entertainment. They increasingly mediate the conditions under which individuals exercise speech, association, education, work, and democratic participation.

The argument for quasi-public treatment is therefore functional rather than formal. An institution may be legally private while exercising a form of power that has public significance. Public law has often responded to analogous circumstances through doctrines governing common carriers, public accommodations, utilities, fiduciaries, and entities performing public functions. The justification is not that private ownership disappears, but that certain private powers become subject to obligations when they control access to basic social goods or exercise authority over vulnerable persons.

Still, the functional argument must be carefully limited. If every firm that influences public life were treated as a public actor, the distinction between private association and government would collapse. Newspapers, publishers, universities, religious organizations, employers, and even families shape speech and social opportunity. Their private autonomy has moral value. A public-law regime that required every institution to treat all viewpoints equally could destroy pluralism rather than protect it.

The relevant question is therefore not whether platforms matter publicly, but whether particular platforms possess a combination of characteristics that justifies public obligations. Relevant factors include market concentration, network effects, the absence of realistic substitutes, the essentiality of the service for civic or economic participation, the extent to which platform rules govern public discourse, and the opacity of automated decision-making. The larger and more indispensable a platform becomes, the less plausible it is to regard its rules as merely private arrangements between equal parties.

## II. Rawls: Digital Access as a Primary Good

Rawls’s theory of justice offers the strongest foundation for treating access to digital communication as a matter of public concern. Rawls identifies certain “primary goods”—rights and liberties, opportunities, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect—as goods that rational persons would want whatever their particular conception of the good. A just society must arrange its basic institutions so that citizens have fair access to these goods.

Digital communication is not necessarily a primary good in exactly the same form as income or political liberty. A smartphone, an account on a specific platform, or access to a particular application may be contingent and replaceable. But the capacity to participate in digitally mediated communication increasingly functions as an instrument for securing several established primary goods. It supports freedom of expression and association, political participation, educational opportunity, access to employment, and recognition as a social equal. It also affects the social bases of self-respect, since exclusion from ordinary channels of interaction may communicate civic and social inferiority.

Rawls’s first principle requires equal basic liberties, including freedom of thought, conscience, speech, association, and political participation. If communication platforms are the practical venues through which these liberties are exercised, then formal legal freedom is insufficient unless citizens possess meaningful access to the relevant communicative infrastructure. A person may have a legal right to speak, but that right is substantially weakened if the dominant channels of public communication are inaccessible, prohibitively expensive, or governed by opaque rules that permit arbitrary exclusion. Digital exclusion can thus become a liberty problem rather than merely a consumer problem.

The second principle, particularly fair equality of opportunity, provides an additional basis for regulation. Access to online networks increasingly affects educational resources, employment opportunities, professional reputation, and political influence. Unequal access may reflect not only differences in income but also discriminatory moderation, biased recommendation systems, inaccessible design, language exclusions, or account suspensions that prevent users from participating in economic and civic life. A platform can therefore distribute opportunities even when it does not directly distribute money.

Rawls’s difference principle also illuminates the distributive significance of platform design. If digital systems generate enormous economic and social benefits, their institutional rules should be arranged to improve the position of those who are least advantaged. This does not mean that every platform decision must maximize equality or that platforms must provide unlimited access. It does suggest that the benefits and burdens of digital governance should not be structured in ways that predictably intensify existing disadvantage. For example, a moderation system that disproportionately removes the speech of minority-language users, a verification system that excludes low-income participants, or an algorithm that systematically disadvantages certain groups may be unjust even if the platform is privately profitable and users formally agreed to its terms.

Treating access to digital communication as a primary good does not require a right to remain on every platform. Rawls’s framework distinguishes between the importance of a good and the institutional form through which it is supplied. A just society might guarantee universal broadband, public digital infrastructure, interoperability, or access to multiple communication channels rather than requiring one private company to host every speaker. This distinction is crucial. If a particular platform is indispensable because the state has allowed it to become a near-monopoly, public regulation may be appropriate. But the ultimate Rawlsian goal is fair access to the social conditions of citizenship, not a personal entitlement to any specific corporate service.

Rawls also places limits on how public institutions may pursue justice. Basic liberties cannot simply be traded away for efficiency, and the principles of justice are addressed primarily to the basic structure of society. This supports constitutional protections for users but also counsels against ad hoc governmental control of individual speech. Public regulation should secure fair conditions for communication while preserving the equal liberties of citizens who disagree about religion, politics, and morality.

From a Rawlsian perspective, the central wrong in unregulated platform power is not merely that corporations make unpopular decisions. It is that private governance may control access to primary goods without being organized by principles that citizens could reasonably accept as fair. If digital communication is central to the exercise of liberty and opportunity, then leaving its governance entirely to corporate discretion risks placing a basic feature of citizens’ lives outside the requirements of public justification.

## III. Nozick: Property, Voluntary Exchange, and the Limits of Public Claims

Nozick’s libertarian theory presents a direct challenge to this Rawlsian position. In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, Nozick defends rights of self-ownership, legitimate acquisition, legitimate transfer, and freedom from patterned redistributive interference. If a person acquires property justly and transfers it voluntarily, the resulting distribution is just, regardless of whether it conforms to an egalitarian pattern. The state’s role is correspondingly limited: it may protect persons against force, fraud, and rights violations, but it may not use coercive redistribution to impose a preferred social pattern.

Applied to digital platforms, Nozick’s argument begins with ownership. A platform may be created through private investment, contractual exchange, and entrepreneurial labor. Its owners have a right to determine the terms on which others may use it. Users who join accept those terms, at least in principle. If a platform chooses to remove content or terminate accounts, it may be exercising a property right rather than public authority. A compulsory requirement to host speech could amount to an unjust appropriation of the owner’s labor and resources.

Nozick would also question the move from social importance to entitlement. The fact that a service becomes valuable or widely used does not necessarily mean that others acquire a right to control it. A talented individual who becomes indispensable in a market does not thereby lose ownership of the fruits of his or her labor. Likewise, a successful platform might argue that its success reflects voluntary choices by users, advertisers, and investors. Imposing public-law obligations could punish innovation and undermine the incentives that produced the service.

The libertarian objection is strongest where regulation seeks to dictate the substantive viewpoint of a platform. A platform may possess an expressive identity. It may wish to promote particular norms, curate a particular kind of community, or exclude content it regards as harmful or incompatible with its mission. Requiring it to carry all lawful speech could compel it to communicate messages it rejects. This concern anticipates Dworkin’s defense of expressive freedom and cannot be dismissed as mere corporate self-interest.

Nozick’s framework also draws attention to the coercive character of public regulation. Regulations may be described as transparency requirements or fairness obligations, but they are ultimately backed by legal penalties. If the state forces a company to reveal proprietary algorithms, retain users it wishes to exclude, or redesign its service for distributive purposes, it is interfering with property and association. The burden of justification therefore falls on those who would impose such duties.

Yet Nozick’s account faces difficulties when applied to networked platforms. First, the voluntariness of exchange depends on background conditions. A user’s agreement may be formally voluntary while being practically unavoidable. If one platform controls access to friends, employment networks, political audiences, or essential information, refusal may carry severe social costs. Second, Nozick’s theory is especially concerned with legitimate acquisition and transfer. It does not automatically validate a market structure produced by state-granted privileges, mergers, network effects, data accumulation, or the exclusion of competitors. If a platform’s dominant position is not simply the outcome of repeated voluntary exchanges among equals, then its claim to unrestricted control is less secure.

Third, property rights do not normally include a right to exercise arbitrary power over others. Ownership of a bridge, utility, or communications network may coexist with duties of nondiscrimination or reasonable access when the asset is essential to social participation. Even on a libertarian view, one person’s property rights cannot include a right to defraud, coerce, or impose unconsented risks on others. If algorithmic systems manipulate users, exploit intimate data, or discriminate in ways that users cannot detect or contest, regulation may protect rather than negate meaningful voluntary exchange.

Finally, Nozick’s theory may understate the political nature of platform rules. A platform’s content policies do not merely govern the use of property by discrete customers. They establish a normative order for a community of speakers, listeners, advertisers, moderators, and observers. When the platform becomes a principal forum for political communication, its decisions affect the effective distribution of public voice. A regime of purely private discretion can therefore create a form of governance without the accountability typically demanded of governing institutions.

Nozick does not prove that platforms should be free from all public regulation. He instead establishes three constraints. Regulation must respect legitimate ownership, avoid unnecessary coercion, and distinguish between protecting users against force or deception and imposing a substantive pattern of speech or social equality. A defensible quasi-public regime must meet those constraints by targeting the conditions of platform power rather than simply transferring control from corporations to the state.

## IV. Nissenbaum: Contextual Integrity and the Illusion of Consent

The legitimacy of private platform governance is often defended through consent. Users click “agree,” accept terms of service, and receive access in exchange for providing data and following platform rules. But Helen Nissenbaum’s framework of contextual integrity shows why this contractual model is inadequate for evaluating digital practices.

Nissenbaum argues that privacy is not simply secrecy or control over information. It is the appropriate flow of information within social contexts. Different contexts—health care, education, friendship, commerce, politics, employment—have distinctive norms governing who may send or receive information, about whom, and under what conditions. A flow of information can therefore be objectionable even if the individual technically consented, because the flow violates the norms appropriate to the context.

Digital platforms routinely collapse contexts. Information disclosed for social interaction may be used for advertising, political profiling, insurance decisions, or behavioral prediction. Data supplied to communicate with friends may be analyzed to infer psychological traits and sold or shared with actors outside the user’s expectations. A person may agree to a general privacy policy without understanding that the platform will combine location, browsing, contacts, messages, and inferred characteristics. The problem is not simply insufficient disclosure. It is that platform practices may transform the context in which information is used.

Nissenbaum’s theory also challenges the assumption that terms of service express genuine consent. Consent is meaningful only if the individual can understand the practice, assess its consequences, and refuse without unreasonable sacrifice. Long, technical agreements presented as a condition of participation do not necessarily meet these requirements. A user may know that “data will be collected” while remaining unable to understand the relevant informational flows, the inferential models built from the data, or the parties who will receive the resulting predictions.

The absence of reasonable exit options intensifies this problem. A person may technically be free to leave a platform, but exit may mean losing contact with family, professional opportunities, political audiences, or access to public services. Network effects make the value of participation depend on the participation of others. If everyone important to a user is on one platform, individual withdrawal is not a realistic alternative. Consent under these conditions resembles acceptance of a privately administered infrastructure rather than a freely negotiated exchange.

This does not mean that all user consent is illusory. Individuals can make meaningful choices among services, use privacy tools, negotiate through collective action, or participate in platforms with genuinely different business models. Nor does the absence of perfect alternatives eliminate every moral significance of agreement. But the more a platform is indispensable and the less intelligible its practices are, the weaker the consent-based justification becomes.

Nissenbaum therefore supports a shift from notice-and-consent toward contextual governance. Public law should ask whether data flows are appropriate to the social context, whether users reasonably expect them, whether they are necessary for the service, and whether affected individuals can challenge improper uses. This approach also applies to content moderation and algorithmic ranking. A user may join a platform expecting chronological communication among friends, only to discover that an opaque system ranks content according to commercial or political criteria. The relevant issue is not merely whether the user accepted a clause authorizing “personalization,” but whether the informational and communicative practices preserve the norms of the context in which participation occurred.

The lack of exit options also has institutional implications. If meaningful exit cannot discipline platform behavior through ordinary market competition, then public regulation must substitute for some of the accountability that competition would otherwise provide. Interoperability, data portability, and non-discrimination can create more realistic exit possibilities. Users might be able to move social graphs, messages, or reputational records to rival services. Such measures respect property and innovation more than simply requiring a dominant platform to become a government agency, while reducing the coercive force of dependence.

Nissenbaum’s contribution is thus twofold. She reveals that privacy and consent are relational and contextual, not merely individual and contractual. She also shows that the voluntariness of digital participation depends on institutional conditions, including the availability of reasonable alternatives. Where those conditions are absent, platform governance cannot be justified solely by pointing to a click-wrap agreement.

## V. Pettit: Corporate Power as Domination

Pettit’s republican theory defines freedom not simply as non-interference but as non-domination. A person is dominated when another possesses the capacity for arbitrary interference in that person’s choices, even if the interference is not constantly exercised. The classic example is a benevolent master: the master may refrain from interfering, but the slave remains unfree because his or her security depends on the master’s uncontrolled will.

This framework captures an important feature of platform power. Users may not experience constant interference. They may post, communicate, and browse without interruption. Yet they remain vulnerable to the platform’s unilateral capacity to suspend accounts, change rules, alter visibility, infer personal characteristics, or manipulate the informational environment. The central concern is not that every platform decision is wrongful. It is that users lack adequate control over the basis, timing, and review of those decisions.

Corporate content moderation can therefore constitute arbitrary power under certain conditions. It is arbitrary when it is not constrained by publicly knowable rules, when similarly situated users are treated inconsistently, when decision-makers have incentives unrelated to the stated policy, when affected persons cannot obtain reasons or appeal, or when the system is designed to conceal rather than justify its exercise of power. A platform may claim to apply neutral rules while its automated systems disproportionately burden particular groups. It may enforce policies selectively against politically unpopular users. It may revise standards without meaningful notice. Such practices expose users to the platform’s will.

The republican analysis does not imply that all moderation is domination. Rules can reduce domination by protecting users from harassment, threats, fraud, and coordinated abuse. Unmoderated spaces may enable powerful speakers to silence others through intimidation or manipulation. A platform that establishes clear, consistent, and contestable rules may enhance freedom by making participation more secure. The question is whether moderation is controlled by non-arbitrary standards and subject to mechanisms through which users can contest its application.

Pettit’s emphasis on contestability is particularly important. A person is less dominated when interference is open to effective challenge through procedures that do not depend on the goodwill of the interferer. In the platform context, contestability requires more than a generic help page. It may include notice of the alleged violation, an explanation of the decision, access to human or specialized review, reasonable time for appeal, protection against retaliation, and publication of aggregate enforcement data. It may also require institutional separation between commercial incentives and moderation decisions, or at least disclosure of how those incentives operate.

Algorithmic systems intensify the problem of domination because they can exercise power without a clearly identifiable decision-maker. An automated ranking model may demote a user’s speech without formally removing it. A recommendation engine may determine which political claims become visible. A risk score may trigger account restrictions based on inferred behavior. The user may not know what happened, what factors were considered, or how to correct an error. In this setting, domination arises not only from human discretion but from the inability to identify and challenge the operative rule.

Pettit’s theory also helps distinguish legitimate platform authority from arbitrary power. Authority is not necessarily domination if it is constrained by stable rules, publicly defensible purposes, and opportunities for contestation. A university, workplace, or association can exercise disciplinary authority without dominating every member if its rules are known, its procedures fair, and its decisions reviewable. Similarly, a platform can retain editorial and community standards while being required to justify their administration.

There is, however, a republican danger in excessive state control. If public officials acquire the power to direct platforms’ moderation decisions, users may exchange corporate domination for governmental domination. A state ministry that determines which political content must be promoted or removed may be more threatening than a private platform, especially where the state controls police, taxation, and criminal law. Non-domination therefore requires dispersed and contestable authority, not simply the substitution of public for private power.

The republican solution is institutional rather than purely substantive. It should reduce the capacity of any single actor to interfere arbitrarily. This can be achieved through competition and interoperability, procedural rights, independent oversight, judicial review of certain decisions, transparency about automated systems, and protection for whistleblowers and researchers. The objective is not to guarantee every user a preferred result but to ensure that power is not exercised solely at the platform’s unreviewable discretion.

## VI. Dworkin and the Problem of Expressive Freedom

Any proposal to treat platforms as quasi-public actors must confront the danger of suppressing expression in the name of fairness. Dworkin’s theory of expressive freedom provides a central constraint. For Dworkin, freedom of speech is connected to the equal moral status of persons and to the requirement that government treat citizens as responsible agents capable of forming and expressing their own judgments. The state must not silence viewpoints merely because it regards them as false, offensive, or dangerous to prevailing political interests.

Dworkin’s principles do not necessarily entail that private corporations have an unlimited right to control all forms of communication. But they do warn against state rules that impose a preferred ideological balance or punish platforms for hosting controversial speech. A platform may itself be an expressive association. Its decisions about curation, editorial tone, and community norms can communicate a viewpoint. If the state compelled every platform to carry every lawful message, it might undermine the platform’s own freedom of expression and the freedom of users to form communities with distinctive purposes.

This concern is especially acute when platforms use algorithmic ranking. Ranking is not merely a mechanical act of transmission. Choosing what to recommend, highlight, or suppress may involve editorial judgment. A public-law requirement that a platform give equal visibility to all viewpoints could be both impossible and normatively undesirable. It might amplify harassment, disinformation, or extremist propaganda while preventing platforms from developing communities governed by meaningful standards.

The appropriate response is to distinguish substantive editorial control from procedural accountability. Public law need not tell a platform which opinions to promote. It can instead require the platform to disclose the general principles governing recommendation and moderation, identify when automated systems have materially affected visibility, explain significant account sanctions, and provide avenues for review. Such requirements regulate the exercise of power without prescribing the outcome of every editorial decision.

Transparency itself must be carefully designed. Requiring disclosure of every technical feature of an algorithm could facilitate gaming, expose trade secrets, or create security risks. Public law should therefore distinguish among different audiences and levels of disclosure. Regulators and qualified independent auditors may receive confidential access to source code, training data documentation, impact assessments, and testing results. Users may receive intelligible explanations of the factors relevant to decisions affecting them. The public may receive aggregate reports concerning enforcement rates, error rates, demographic effects, political advertising, and changes in policy. This layered approach promotes accountability while protecting legitimate proprietary and expressive interests.

Dworkin’s concern for equal citizenship also supports some regulation. If private platforms systematically exclude certain citizens from effective participation in public discourse, the issue is not merely that a corporation has expressed a preference. It may be that the background conditions of equal citizenship have been distorted. Regulation designed to prevent viewpoint discrimination by governments, protect against discriminatory access to essential services, or ensure that users can contest errors can enhance rather than diminish expressive freedom.

The state must also avoid using transparency as a pretext for viewpoint control. A government that selectively investigates platforms because it dislikes their political orientation is not pursuing neutral accountability. It is attempting to control the communicative environment indirectly. Oversight institutions should therefore have independent appointment procedures, clear statutory mandates, judicial review, and public reporting. Rules should apply across ideological lines and focus on process, discrimination, fraud, privacy, and systemic risk rather than the truth or falsity of contested political claims.

Dworkin thus leads toward a constitutional principle of expressive pluralism. Platforms should generally retain the ability to create and enforce content policies reflecting their purposes, but they should not be able to exercise this power through secret, discriminatory, or unreviewable systems when they have become central to citizens’ communicative lives. Protecting expression requires both freedom from government censorship and protection against institutional arrangements that make some citizens’ participation precarious or effectively invisible.

## VII. What Public Law Should Require

The preceding theories converge on a model of public-law regulation that is substantial but limited. The aim should be to constitutionalize the conditions of platform governance rather than to constitutionalize every content decision.

First, platforms with significant market and civic power should be subject to transparency duties. They should publish clear moderation and recommendation rules, disclose material changes, report enforcement statistics, identify the use of automated decision-making, and explain how advertising, ranking, and data practices operate at a level intelligible to ordinary users. Transparency should include information about error rates and disparate impacts, not merely descriptions of formal policies.

Second, users should receive procedural protections. Before a serious sanction, they should ordinarily receive notice of the relevant rule and an explanation of the alleged violation. They should have an opportunity to appeal, with enhanced review for decisions affecting journalism, political participation, public-interest speech, or access to livelihood. Emergency action may be justified in cases involving credible threats or rapidly spreading harm, but it should be followed by prompt review. Procedural protections do not guarantee reinstatement; they make the exercise of power contestable.

Third, public law should address discriminatory and unreasonable access practices. Platforms that function as essential gateways to employment, political participation, or public services should not deny access on prohibited grounds or use automated systems that produce unjustifiable disparate effects. This does not require neutrality toward all content. A platform may prohibit harassment or misinformation, but its rules should not be applied as a disguised means of excluding groups or viewpoints.

Fourth, regulation should improve exit. Data portability, interoperability, access to open standards, and limits on anti-competitive acquisitions can reduce dependence on dominant platforms. Meaningful exit is not merely a consumer benefit; it is a condition of non-domination and consent. If users can move relationships and data without losing their social world, platform power becomes more accountable through competition and association.

Fifth, algorithmic governance should be independently auditable. Audits should examine privacy, discrimination, security, systemic manipulation, and the relationship between stated rules and actual outcomes. Auditors must have sufficient access to data and systems, while safeguards should protect confidential information and prevent exploitation of vulnerabilities. In some contexts, regulators should require impact assessments before deployment of high-risk systems, especially those affecting political communication, employment, credit, health, or children.

Sixth, the law should protect researchers, journalists, and civil-society monitors who investigate platform effects. Without independent scrutiny, transparency may remain a corporate public-relations exercise. Researchers should have access to relevant data under privacy-preserving conditions and should not face retaliation merely for documenting harmful or discriminatory outcomes.

Finally, public law should establish institutional accountability without concentrating excessive power in the executive branch. Independent regulators, courts, legislative reporting, and multi-stakeholder review can distribute authority. Users should have remedies for serious violations, including correction, restoration of access where appropriate, compensation for unlawful data practices, and judicial review in narrowly defined circumstances. The design should reflect Pettit’s concern with contestability: no institution should be able to impose significant communicative penalties without being answerable to a body capable of independent review.

## VIII. The Limits of Quasi-Public Status

The concept of quasi-public actors should not become a general license for state intervention. Several limits are essential.

The first is scale and function. Small online communities, personal blogs, private group chats, and voluntary associations should generally retain broad discretion. The strongest duties should attach to platforms that are dominant, infrastructural, and socially indispensable. A single legal category may be less useful than a graduated system based on user numbers, market power, risk, and civic importance.

The second is the distinction between access and amplification. A platform may be required to give users a fair opportunity to participate and to explain major restrictions, but it need not guarantee that every lawful post receives equal distribution. There is a moral and practical difference between removing a person from a service and declining to recommend their content to millions of others. Public law should recognize that ranking and curation are forms of editorial judgment, while still addressing deceptive or discriminatory systems.

The third is the distinction between unlawful and harmful content. Platforms should not be forced to adopt the government’s preferred definition of truth, public morality, or political legitimacy. They should retain authority to establish community standards, provided that their administration is transparent, consistent, and compatible with general law. Public oversight should focus on whether the system is arbitrary or discriminatory, not on whether regulators approve of every moderation decision.

The fourth is the protection of privacy and trade secrets. Accountability cannot mean indiscriminate publication of personal data, security-sensitive information, or proprietary code. Nissenbaum’s contextual integrity requires that information disclosed for oversight not be repurposed in ways that violate its own context. Regulatory systems must themselves respect appropriate informational norms.

The fifth is institutional humility. Platforms are capable of serious error, but states are not uniquely wise or impartial. Governments may misuse algorithmic oversight to intimidate opposition, pressure companies to suppress dissent, or entrench incumbents. Public law should therefore be designed around general rules, independent review, and procedural safeguards rather than informal political demands.

## Conclusion

Digital platforms should be governed as quasi-public actors when they function as essential infrastructures of communication, association, economic opportunity, and political participation. Their private ownership remains morally relevant, but it cannot by itself settle the question of public responsibility. Institutions that exercise pervasive power over the conditions of citizenship may require public-law constraints even when they operate through contracts and markets.

Rawls shows why access to digital communication should increasingly be understood as connected to primary goods. Without meaningful access to communication networks, citizens may lack effective opportunities to exercise basic liberties, compete fairly, and maintain the social bases of self-respect. This supports public intervention to secure fair access and prevent arbitrary exclusion. Nozick reminds us, however, that such intervention must respect property, voluntary exchange, and the expressive autonomy of private associations. The public importance of a platform does not automatically give the state authority to dictate its message or abolish its ownership.

Nissenbaum demonstrates that formal consent is often insufficient where users cannot understand data practices or reasonably withdraw without sacrificing social and economic participation. Exit, portability, and contextual limits on information flows are therefore central to legitimate governance. Pettit’s republican theory explains why opaque moderation and algorithmic ranking can constitute domination: users may be subject to the platform’s uncontrolled will even when interference is occasional. The remedy is contestability, not necessarily government control. Dworkin finally establishes that accountability must be compatible with expressive pluralism. The state should not impose ideological neutrality or require platforms to disseminate all viewpoints, but it may insist that the exercise of communicative power be intelligible, non-discriminatory, and reviewable.

The appropriate constitutional settlement is consequently neither laissez-faire nor state administration. Public law should impose duties of transparency, procedural fairness, privacy protection, non-discrimination, independent auditing, and institutional accountability on platforms whose power is sufficiently extensive and indispensable. It should improve the possibility of exit through interoperability and portability. It should permit platforms to retain substantive editorial identities and enforce community standards, while preventing secret and arbitrary exercises of authority.

Such a framework does not violate expressive freedom because it does not require the state to determine what citizens must believe or what every platform must promote. Instead, it protects the background conditions under which individuals and communities can speak, associate, dissent, and participate as equals. The goal is not to transform corporations into governments in every respect. It is to recognize that when private institutions govern public life, their power must be answerable to principles that citizens can contest and reasonably accept. The constitutionalization of platform governance is justified not because all private power is illegitimate, but because unaccountable power over the means of collective expression is incompatible with equal freedom.
