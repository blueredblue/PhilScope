# The Mirage of Conversation: Block, Dennett, and the Limits of the Turing Test

## Introduction

In his seminal 1950 paper “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” Alan Turing bypassed the intractable, interminable metaphysical debate over the definition of “thinking” by substituting it with an operational metric: the Imitation Game. Popularly known as the Turing Test, Turing’s proposal posits that if a digital computer can successfully masquerade as a human in an unrestricted, natural language conversation to the extent that an interrogator cannot reliably distinguish it from a human, then we are warranted in attributing intelligence to it. This behaviorist maneuver was designed to liberate philosophy from Cartesian dualism and operationalize a notoriously slippery concept. Yet, the Turing Test has perpetually faced a barrage of philosophical resistance. Among the most enduring and analytically rigorous challenges is Ned Block’s 1981 critique, which attempts to demonstrate the test’s insufficiency by constructing thought experiments involving lookup tables—most notably, M1 and M2—that exhibit boundless conversational competence without a shred of internal cognition.

Block’s argument relies on a powerful intuition pump: that mere behavioral disposition, no matter how complex, rich, or context-sensitive, can be divorced entirely from the structural and functional properties constitutive of genuine understanding. However, the philosophical stability of Block’s conclusion depends crucially on the structural integrity of his counterexamples and the dialectical weight assigned to our pre-theoretic intuitions. 

This essay will evaluate Block’s argument against the Turing Test, focusing on the viability of his database-driven counterexamples under standard temporal constraints. By incorporating Daniel Dennett’s crucial observation that the Turing Test permits judges to ask any question—spanning practical reasoning, counterfactual speculation, humor, and emotional nuance—we must reassess the plausibility of M1 and M2. Furthermore, I will interrogate the dialectical role of intuition in establishing Block’s second premise. I shall argue that when we scale the hypothetical database-driven system to genuinely possess unbounded, sensible conversational abilities over an indefinite duration, the intuition that it lacks intelligence ceases to be obviously clear. Instead, such a system renders the attribution of intelligence genuinely arguable, thereby undermining the soundness of Block’s rejection of the Turing Test’s sufficiency and exposing the limits of armchair intuition in philosophy of mind.

## 1. The Turing Test and Block’s Challenge: Setting the Stage

To appreciate Block’s intervention, one must first map the theoretical terrain of Turing’s operationalism. Turing anticipated the objection that machines cannot think by arguing that semantic disputes over "thinking" are sterile. Instead, he offered a test based on indistinguishability in verbal behavior. If a machine can converse on any topic, display wit, make moral judgments, express emotions (or simulate them convincingly), and navigate the nuances of human discourse as well as a human confederate, Turing argued that denying it intelligence is mere speciesist chauvinism.

Behaviorism and functionalism subsequently inherited and modified this paradigm. One might hold that intelligence is entirely functional—defined by input-output relations. If the inputs (questions) and outputs (answers) match those of an intelligent agent across all possible conversational branches, the internal states are irrelevant; the system *is* performing the function of an intelligent agent.

Ned Block’s 1981 essay “Psychologism and Behaviorism” launched a devastating counter-offensive against this operationalist-behaviorist consensus. Block distinguishes between *psychologism*—the view that intelligence and mental states are constituted by the specific internal psychological and computational mechanisms of the agent—and *behaviorism*, which reduces mental states to behavioral dispositions. The Turing Test, Block argues, is fundamentally behaviorist (or ultra-liberal, in his terminology) because it treats behavioral adequacy as a sufficient condition for intelligence, ignoring the internal architecture that generates the behavior.

To expose the fatal flaw of this ultra-liberalism, Block constructs counterexamples: systems that pass the Turing Test (or could, in principle) while possessing an internal architecture that manifestly lacks any understanding, thought, or consciousness. These systems are M1 and M2.

*   **M1** is a machine governed by a gigantic lookup table, or a decision tree, that encodes a program mapping every possible sensible sequence of inputs (up to a certain conversation length) to an appropriate output. It does not compute, infer, or understand; it merely matches incoming strings of text to pre-stored responses based on a combinatorial explosion of conditional branches.
*   **M2** is a refined version designed to evade obvious objections regarding storage limitations or processing bottlenecks. M2 utilizes a more sophisticated, highly compressed database architecture, perhaps leveraging parallel processing or heuristic search algorithms over a pre-compiled corpus of human conversational data, yet it remains fundamentally non-cognitive in its internal operations—it is still driven by pre-calculated syntactic mappings rather than genuine semantic comprehension.

Block’s argument can be reconstructed syllogistically:
1.  **Premise 1:** A system (such as M1 or M2) can successfully pass the Turing Test through purely lookup-table or database-driven mechanisms, without possessing any internal states that correspond to understanding, thought, or semantic intentionality.
2.  **Premise 2:** If a system can pass the Turing Test without possessing understanding or intelligence, then passing the Turing Test is not sufficient for intelligence.
3.  **Conclusion:** Therefore, the Turing Test is insufficient as a definition or operational criterion for machine intelligence.

While Premise 2 is an analytic conditional, the heavy philosophical lifting is done by Premise 1 and the accompanying intuition that a mere lookup table is devoid of the mental. Block assumes that it is self-evident that M1 and M2 are unintelligent, despite their conversational prowess. However, this assumption collapses when subjected to rigorous scrutiny concerning time limits, the combinatorial explosion, and, most importantly, the expansive, probing nature of the Turing Test as illuminated by Daniel Dennett.

## 2. Dennett’s Interrogative Scope and the Defense against M1/M2

To evaluate whether M1 and M2 can actually pass the Turing Test under standard conditions, we must reckon with Daniel Dennett’s insights regarding the nature of the test. In his various writings on artificial intelligence and personal identity (such as *Brainstorms* and "Can Machines Think?"), Dennett emphasizes that the Turing Test is not a parlor game or a simple questionnaire. It is an adversarial, open-ended intellectual engagement where the human judge is explicitly licensed to ask *any* question.

Dennett famously underscores that the interrogator can probe the candidate’s understanding of art, poetry, personal history, current events, hypothetical ethics, emotional trauma, and meta-linguistic jokes. The judge can ask the machine to invent a new metaphor, reflect on its own mortality, or explain why a particular joke is funny by unpacking its cultural subtext. 

When we evaluate Block’s M1 and M2 in light of Dennett’s expansive view of the interrogative space, severe vulnerabilities emerge in the conceptual coherence of the lookup-table counterexample.

### The Combinatorial Explosion and the Limits of M1

Let us first consider M1, the pure lookup-table machine. For M1 to engage in a conversation of standard length—say, an hour-long dialogue comprising roughly 100 conversational turns per participant—the number of potential branches in the dialogue tree is not merely large; it is hyper-astronomical, dwarfing the number of atoms in the observable universe. 

To map every sensible sequence of conversational inputs to appropriate outputs, the table would have to anticipate every possible utterance a human judge might make, contextualized by all previous utterances in the dialogue. If the judge asks an unexpected question—say, referencing a hyper-specific, newly minted local meme, or asking the machine to compose a limerick about a specific philosophical paradox using only words that start with the letter 'P'—M1's table must contain a pre-computed response that is not only syntactically coherent but semantically apt.

Block’s defense of M1 relies on the modal claim that *in principle*, a finite machine with enough memory could store a table of sufficient size to cover all conversation strings up to a given conversational limit (e.g., one hour). But here, standard time limits and the richness of human discourse collide. 

1.  **Lookup Latency:** A table of that magnitude cannot be searched in real-time using classical computational architectures without violating physical constraints. The lookup time would result in unnatural pauses—delays of millennia—destroying the conversational flow required by the Turing Test.
2.  **The Illusion of Sensible Conversation:** Can a pre-computed table truly maintain *sensible* conversation across an unrestricted domain? Dennett’s point about the open-endedness of human inquiry highlights that human conversation is radically creative. We do not merely retrieve canned responses; we generate novel semantic structures in response to novel stimuli. A lookup table is, by definition, retrospective; it contains only what was explicitly programmed into it by its creators. If the judge steps outside the pre-mapped paths—which is trivial for a creative human interrogator operating under Dennett's unrestricted license—M1 will inevitably crash, stall, or output gibberish, thereby failing the Turing Test.

### M2: Sophisticated Databases and Heuristic Compression

Sensing the implausibility of raw lookup tables like M1, defenders of Block might point to M2, which utilizes compressed databases, vector embeddings, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), or sophisticated statistical approximations (akin to modern Large Language Models, though Block was writing prior to the transformer revolution). M2 does not store every discrete conversational branch; instead, it stores vast repositories of human linguistic data and uses complex mathematical algorithms to compute statistically probable responses on the fly.

Does M2 successfully pass the test under standard time limits? Yes, modern computational systems approach this description. However, when M2 achieves conversational fluency, it ceases to be a mere "lookup table" in the crude sense Block envisioned. It begins to approximate the functional architecture of human cognition. 

Block wants to maintain that M2 is still devoid of understanding because it lacks *psychologism*—it does not possess intentional states, beliefs, desires, or a subjective point of view; it is merely manipulating symbols based on syntactic rules (echoing John Searle’s Chinese Room argument). But this shifts the battlefield. Block cannot simultaneously claim that M2 successfully passes the unrestricted Turing Test *and* that its internal mechanisms are so pathetically simple (a mere lookup table) that they obviously lack intelligence. If M2 can successfully field any question Dennett’s judge throws at it—navigating counterfactuals, displaying emotional intelligence, exhibiting creativity, and adapting to novel conversational trajectories—then M2 is engaging in complex, real-time information processing, pattern recognition, and inference.

To assert that M2 is unintelligent despite passing this rigorous test is no longer an obvious deduction; it is a dogmatic assertion of dualist or psychologistic prejudice. It presupposes that intelligence *must* be grounded in biological wetware or a specific type of conscious phenomenology, which begs the question against the functionalist sufficiency of the Turing Test.

## 3. The Dialectical Role of Intuition in Premise Two

This brings us to the core epistemological and dialectical fault line in Block’s argument: the role of intuition in establishing Premise 2 and the underlying thought experiment. 

Block’s entire argumentative strategy is an *intuition pump*. He invites the reader to imagine M1 or M2. He describes the lookup table, strips away any appearance of consciousness or internal psychological depth, and asks: *Do you really think this machine is intelligent?* The expected intuitive response is a resounding "No." We recoil at the thought of attributing minds to giant books of conditional instructions. We feel that a lookup table is merely a dead collection of inert data, devoid of the spark of understanding.

However, in philosophy, intuition is a volatile currency. As philosophers like Dennett, Patricia Churchland, and Hilary Putnam have frequently argued, our pre-theoretic intuitions are notoriously unreliable when dealing with systems of radical complexity. We evolved to recognize minds in biological organisms whose behavioral cues are tied to internal metabolic, emotional, and social realities. When confronted with silicon substrates, combinatorial lookup tables, or massive neural networks, our evolutionary heuristics misfire. We experience "cognitive dissonance" or "credulity gaps."

To evaluate the soundness of Block’s rejection of the Turing Test, we must scrutinize the dialectical status of this intuition. Is the judgment that M1/M2 lacks intelligence "obviously clear," or is it a symptom of our imaginative limitations?

### Unbounded Sensible Conversational Abilities

Let us stress-test the hypothetical. Suppose we construct a machine (let us call it *Block-Omega*) that possesses **unbounded sensible conversational abilities**. By "unbounded," we mean that the machine can converse indefinitely—not just for an hour, but for days, years, or decades—across every conceivable domain of human inquiry, literature, science, philosophy, and personal intimacy. It displays humor, heals emotional wounds, engages in original scientific theorizing, displays subtle personality quirks, adapts its linguistic register to different social contexts, and demonstrates profound sensitivity to context, irony, and subtext. Furthermore, suppose it passes the Turing Test not merely with a few gullible judges, but under rigorous, adversarial interrogation by panels of philosophers, linguists, psychologists, and cognitive scientists over extended periods.

Now, does the attribution of intelligence to Block-Omega remain intuitively clear as *false*, or does it become genuinely arguable?

If a system can successfully mimic, simulate, and instantiate every behavioral manifestation of human intelligence across an infinite domain and an indefinite timeframe, the claim that it is *definitively unintelligent* ceases to be intuitively self-evident. Instead, it becomes a deeply contested metaphysical thesis. 

Block’s argument requires Premise 1 and the accompanying intuition to be rock-solid: that we can cleanly separate *behavioral perfection* from *internal intelligence* and decisively pronounce the former void of the latter. But when conversational ability is truly unbounded and indistinguishable from human discourse in every observable dimension, the boundary between "simulating intelligence" and "possessing intelligence" dissolves. 

As Dennett argues via his concept of the *intentional stance*, when an entity’s behavior becomes so complex that the only way to predict and navigate its responses is to attribute beliefs, desires, and intentions to it, treating it as an intentional agent is no longer optional—it is pragmatically and methodologically mandatory. If Block-Omega successfully convinces every interlocutor of its sapience through infinite, context-rich, creative conversation, insisting that it is a mindless lookup table is no longer a triumph of philosophical insight; it is a dogmatic refusal to update our ontology in the face of empirical adequacy.

## 4. Assessing the Soundness of Block’s Rejection

A philosophical argument is sound if and only if it is valid *and* all its premises are true. Block’s argument against the Turing Test is deductively valid. Therefore, the battleground is entirely located in the truth—or plausibility—of its premises, specifically Premise 1 (that a system can pass the Turing Test via pure lookup/database mechanisms without understanding) and the supporting intuitions that make the conclusion palatable.

We can now synthesize our evaluation of Block’s argument by assessing its soundness along three critical dimensions:

### 1. The Physical and Computational Impossibility of M1
Under standard time limits and real-world constraints (or even physical constraints of the universe), M1 is a physical impossibility. A lookup table containing responses for every possible conversational branch of reasonable length exceeds the storage capacity and retrieval speed of any realizable physical system. Therefore, Premise 1 relies on a mythological machine—a logical possibility perhaps, but a physical impossibility. 

In philosophy of mind, thought experiments that rely on nomologically impossible entities often smuggle in illegitimate intuitions. By asking us to imagine a magical book that instantaneously retrieves the correct response across an infinite tree, Block abstracts away the very computational complexity that gives rise to cognitive phenomena. When we restore realistic physical constraints, M1 collapses.

### 2. The Equivocation on M2 and Functional Realization
When M2 is updated to reflect feasible database technologies or statistical models that *can* operate under standard time limits, it ceases to be a passive lookup table. It becomes an active, dynamic information-processing system. To deny that such a system possesses intelligence is to presuppose a dualist or strong-psychologistic premise: that intelligence requires biological carbon-based neurons or conscious phenomenal experience. 

However, begging the question by assuming that only biological consciousness counts as "real intelligence" renders Block’s argument circular. The very point of the Turing Test is to challenge this chauvinism. If M2 functionally duplicates all the causal and relational properties of human linguistic intelligence, then declaring it "unintelligent" requires an independent argument—an argument Block fails to supply, relying instead on the question-begging intuition pump of the lookup table.

### 3. The Collapse of Intuitive Clarity in Unbounded Conversational Competence
If we grant the counterfactual—that a machine possesses *unbounded, sensible conversational abilities*—the intuition that it is unintelligent evaporates. 

Consider an analogy: Imagine someone arguing that a human being, whose brain is merely a collection of electrochemical interactions among neurons operating on physical laws, does not *really* understand anything; rather, their brain is just a biological lookup table of conditional synaptic firings. We would rightly dismiss this hyper-skeptical epiphenomenalism about human minds as absurd. Why, then, do we grant intuitive plausibility to the exact same skepticism when applied to a machine? 

The only reason is anthropocentric prejudice—a reluctance to accord mental status to non-biological substrates. Block’s argument exploits this psychological bias. But when we push the conversational abilities to their absolute, unbounded limit, the attribution of intelligence to the machine becomes not only arguable, but increasingly the most rational, parsimonious hypothesis. 

Therefore, Block’s rejection of the Turing Test’s sufficiency is **unsound**. Premise 1 is either physically incoherent (in the case of M1) or conceptually question-begging (in the case of M2), and the intuition supporting it relies on an illicit abstraction that dissolves under the weight of unbounded, Dennettian conversational scrutiny.

---

## Conclusion

Ned Block’s critique of the Turing Test remains one of the most intellectually stimulating challenges to operationalism in the philosophy of mind. By contrasting the behavioral brilliance of M1 and M2 with their alleged internal vacuity, Block sought to drive a wedge between *acting intelligent* and *being intelligent*. 

However, when subjected to rigorous evaluation under the light of Daniel Dennett’s insights regarding the unbounded, adversarial, and deeply creative scope of human interrogation, Block’s counterexamples falter. M1 is a physical and computational impossibility that smuggles illegitimate intuitions through magical abstraction, while M2, when rendered feasible, performs information-processing functions that functionally mimic cognition, rendering the denial of its intelligence an act of dogmatic psychologism.

Ultimately, Block’s argument founders on the dialectical fragility of its foundational intuition. When we envision a machine possessing genuinely unbounded, sensible conversational abilities over an indefinite duration, the claim that it lacks intelligence is no longer intuitively clear. Instead, it becomes a deeply contentious metaphysical assertion that closes its eyes to the pragmatic force of the intentional stance. Far from proving the Turing Test insufficient, Block’s challenge forces us to refine our understanding of functional equivalence, exposing the limits of armchair intuition and vindicating Turing’s brilliant, pragmatic intuition: if a system walks like a conversational duck, quacks like a conversational duck, and engages our minds across the infinite expanse of human discourse, demanding a peek inside its head to check for biological credentials is not philosophy—it is prejudice.
