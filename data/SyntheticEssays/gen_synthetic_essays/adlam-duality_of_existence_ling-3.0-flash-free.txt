The question of what it means for something to exist is the foundational inquiry of ontology. For centuries, philosophers have debated whether the word "exists" signifies a single, univocal concept, or whether it equivocates across different domains of being. The claim that existence is not univocal—that concrete entities and abstract entities require distinct existence criteria—rests heavily on the concept of mind-independence. While concrete entities exist independently of the mind in a spatiotemporal, causally efficacious sense, abstract entities exist independently of the mind in a logical, relational, or structural sense. This essay evaluates the claim that existence is not univocal by analyzing Rudolf Carnap’s linguistic frameworks, Peter van Inwagen’s defenses of univocity, and the inadequacy of W.V.O. Quine’s criterion of ontological commitment. Ultimately, it will be argued that the dichotomy between spatiotemporal location and location in relational abstract spaces reveals an irreducible duality in the meaning of existence, a duality that the Problem of the Many exposes with particular clarity.

Rudolf Carnap’s logical empiricism offers a seemingly elegant resolution to the equivocity of existence by dissolving it into the framework of language. Carnap argued that existence is not a property of things, but rather a device of quantification. In his *The Logical Syntax of Language*, Carnap distinguished between internal and external questions. Internal questions are those asked within a linguistic framework; they are meaningful and can be answered true or false. External questions, by contrast, ask whether a framework is "real" or "true," and Carnap treated these as pseudo-questions, neither true nor false, but merely expressions of preference or acceptance. 

For Carnap, the statement "tables exist" is an internal question within the physical framework, while "numbers exist" is an internal question within the mathematical framework. Because both statements function as abbreviations for complex logical formulas—such as $\exists x (x \text{ is a table})$—they are syntactically and semantically identical in their logical form. Carnap thus appears to defend a univocal conception of existence: to exist simply is to be the value of a bound variable within an accepted framework. However, Carnap’s pragmatic resolution paradoxically underscores the non-univocity of existence. By bracketing the ontological status of entities and focusing solely on their linguistic function, Carnap does not eliminate the difference between concrete and abstract entities; he merely stratifies them into separate frameworks. The physical framework is anchored in spatiotemporal experience and causal interaction, whereas the mathematical framework is anchored in logical consistency and structural relations. When Carnap asserts that existence is a uniform linguistic device, he ignores that the *criteria* for accepting a framework—and thus the criteria for what counts as an existent within it—differ fundamentally between the spatiotemporal and the abstract. Carnap’s frameworks accommodate the duality of existence by isolating them, but they do not unify the meaning of the word "exists" across them.

Peter van Inwagen offers a more robust defense of ontological univocity, arguing that existence is a single, uniform concept grounded in the category of material being. In his *Material Beings*, van Inwagen argues that to exist is to be a material being. He rejects the existence of abstracta—such as numbers, propositions, and sets—because they lack materiality. For van Inwagen, if abstract objects exist, they must be material beings, or we must accept that there are no abstract objects at all. He defends a form of nominalism, arguing that we do not need abstracta to explain the world; rather, we can paraphrase away talk of abstract objects into talk of concrete particulars and their material relations. Van Inwagen’s univocity is thus a monism of material being: existence is a single, unified criterion derived from the physical world.

Yet, van Inwagen’s defense of univocity faces a significant obstacle when confronting the concept of mind-independence across different domains. While van Inwagen successfully unifies existence under the criterion of materiality, he does so by eliminating the ontological legitimacy of abstracta, rather than by demonstrating that concrete and abstract entities share a common mode of existence. If we grant that abstract entities—such as the set of all sets or the logical relations of mathematics—genuinely exist, van Inwagen’s framework struggles to account for them without collapsing them into material particulars. The mind-independence of a concrete rock is causal and spatiotemporal; it persists whether or not anyone perceives it because it occupies a region of spacetime and exerts gravitational force. The mind-independence of an abstract mathematical truth, however, is logical and necessary; it is true regardless of human cognition because it is entailed by the relations of a logical system. Van Inwagen’s attempt to force abstracta into the category of material beings—or to deny them existence altogether—reveals that his univocity is purchased at the cost of a restrictive definition of existence. By defining existence so narrowly as "being material," van Inwagen sidesteps the equivocity rather than resolving it. The dual nature of mind-independence—causal versus logical—suggests that existence cannot be reduced to a single material criterion without losing the distinctiveness of abstract being.

The most compelling evidence for the non-univocity of existence lies in the disparate modes of location required by concrete and abstract entities. Concrete entities are located in spacetime; they possess spatial boundaries and temporal duration, and they interact causally with one another. Their existence is predicated on occupying a specific region of the physical universe. Abstract entities, by contrast, are located in relational abstract spaces. A number, for instance, does not occupy a point in physical spacetime; rather, it occupies a position in a relational structure defined by arithmetic operations. A set exists not as a physical container but as a logical totality defined by the relation of membership. 

Because the criteria for location differ so radically, the existence criteria must also differ. For a concrete entity, to exist is to be spatiotemporally located and causally efficacious. For an abstract entity, to exist is to be logically determined by a set of relations or to be a necessary truth within a formal system. The mind-independence of concrete entities is rooted in their causal separation from the mind; the mind cannot alter the physical laws governing a rock. The mind-independence of abstract entities, however, is rooted in their logical necessity; the mind cannot alter the fact that 2 + 2 = 4, not because the mind lacks causal power over mathematical truths, but because mathematical truths are constitutive of the abstract relational space itself. Spatiotemporal location and location in relational abstract spaces are not merely different ways of being in the same universe; they are fundamentally different modes of being that demand distinct existential criteria. To conflate them is to commit a category mistake, treating the logical space of relations as if it were a physical container.

W.V.O. Quine’s criterion of ontological commitment attempts to bypass the problem of equivocity by providing a uniform, logical criterion for existence. Quine famously declared, "To be is to be the value of a bound variable." In *On What There Is*, Quine argued that our ontological commitments are determined entirely by the existential quantifier in our best scientific theories. If a theory quantifies over numbers—asserting $\exists x (x \text{ is a number})$—then numbers exist, and we are committed to them. If a theory quantifies over tables, then tables exist. Quine’s criterion thus presents existence as a univocal, logical property: to exist is simply to be a value that satisfies the bound variable in a formal language.

However, Quine’s criterion and the existential quantifier fail to adequately capture ontological commitments across both the concrete and abstract domains. The existential quantifier $\exists x$ is syntactically blind to the nature of $x$; it treats a spatiotemporally located electron and an abstract mathematical constant as identical in their mode of existence. Both are merely values that render the formula true. But this syntactic uniformity masks a profound ontological disparity. The existential quantifier captures *that* something exists within a theory, but it completely fails to capture *how* it exists. The criteria for being a value of a bound variable in a physical theory (causal interaction, spatiotemporal location) are fundamentally different from the criteria for being a value of a bound variable in a mathematical theory (logical necessity, structural relation). By reducing existence to a uniform logical operation, Quine collapses the duality of existence into a single, thin logical syntax. The existential quantifier is an instrument of logical commitment, not an instrument of ontological analysis. It tells us what a theory assumes, but it does not tell us what it means for those assumptions to be real.

The inadequacy of Quine’s criterion becomes glaringly apparent when confronted with the Problem of the Many, a paradox that reveals an irreducible duality in the meaning of existence. The Problem of the Many, articulated by Peter Tallmon and later explored by David Lewis and Peter van Inwagen, arises when we consider a concrete object and its constituent parts. Consider a table and the wood particles that compose it. The table exists; the wood particles exist. But the table just is the wood particles. If existence is univocal, then we must count the table and the particles as distinct objects, resulting in an overabundance of entities—a proliferation of "many" where unity seems to reside.

Van Inwagen attempts to solve this paradox using his univocal criterion of material being. He argues that the table and the particles are the same material being; the table does not exist as a separate entity from the particles. However, the Problem of the Many reveals that our concept of existence fractures when applied to different ontological levels. When we say the table exists, we mean it exists as a unified spatiotemporal whole with emergent causal powers and a specific form. When we say the particles exist, we mean they exist as spatiotemporal parts governed by physics. The "existence" of the whole is not identical to the mereological sum of the parts; the whole possesses a holistic mode of existence that the mere sum of parts does not. 

Furthermore, the Problem of the Many exposes the duality of existence when we introduce abstract relational spaces. Consider the set of all wood particles that constitute the table. The set exists in an abstract relational space, defined by the membership relation. Does the set exist in the same way the table exists? The table exists spatiotemporally; the set exists logically. The paradox forces us to confront the fact that our ontological commitments are divided between spatiotemporal continuants and abstract totalities. The "many" are not just a problem of counting; they are a symptom of the fact that existence operates according to different logics for different kinds of being. The table exists as a concrete, spatiotemporal unity; the particles exist as concrete, spatiotemporal parts; the set exists as an abstract, relational entity. These are not merely different ways of instantiating the same property of existence; they are distinct senses of existence, each governed by its own criteria of mind-independence and location.

In conclusion, the claim that existence is not univocal withstands rigorous philosophical scrutiny. Carnap’s linguistic frameworks demonstrate that while existence can be treated as a uniform linguistic device, the frameworks themselves are stratified by fundamentally different modes of experience—spatiotemporal versus logical—revealing that the underlying existence criteria are distinct. Van Inwagen’s defense of univocity fails because it either denies the existence of abstracta or reduces them to material beings, thereby ignoring the unique mode of mind-independence characteristic of abstract relational spaces. Quine’s criterion of ontological commitment, while powerful as a tool of logical analysis, is too thin to capture the ontological diversity of spatiotemporal and abstract entities, treating them as identical in their mode of being. Finally, the Problem of the Many exposes the irreducible duality of existence by demonstrating that the criteria for being a concrete whole, a concrete part, and an abstract relational entity are fundamentally different. Because concrete entities require existence criteria rooted in spatiotemporal location and causal mind-independence, while abstract entities require existence criteria rooted in relational abstract spaces and logical mind-independence, the meaning of existence is not univocal. It is a fractured concept, reflecting the irreducible duality of the world it seeks to describe.
