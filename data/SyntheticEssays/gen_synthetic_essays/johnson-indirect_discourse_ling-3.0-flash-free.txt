The philosophy of language has long grappled with the peculiar logic of indirect discourse, where a speaker reports the words of another without directly quoting them. Donald Davidson’s paratactic theory of indirect discourse offered a revolutionary, if controversial, solution to this phenomenon by proposing that the embedded clause in an indirect report is not a subordinate proposition but rather the main assertion itself, stripped of its overt subordination. In Davidson’s framework, the sentence “John said that it was raining” is analyzed not as John asserting a subordinate clause, but as John asserting the proposition that it was raining, where the word “that” functions merely as a complementizer introducing the asserted content. However, Davidson’s theory faces a significant challenge: the ambiguity problem. When we substitute the proposition for the utterance in an indirect report, we risk collapsing the crucial distinction between what was said and what was meant, thereby failing to capture the referential richness of the original utterance. Gary Kemp has proposed a sophisticated modification to Davidson’s paratactic theory, arguing that by replacing static propositions with proposition-functions, we can successfully resolve this ambiguity. This essay evaluates Kemp’s proposition-function modification, assessing whether substituting propositions for utterances successfully resolves the ambiguity problem, whether Kemp’s account adequately handles instances of deliberate ambiguity in indirect discourse reports, and whether the deeper theoretical and empirical objections—particularly the treatment of the word “that” as a demonstrative rather than a complementizer—hold up under the scrutiny of cross-linguistic evidence from Hungarian.

To understand the significance of Kemp’s modification, one must first grasp the architecture of Davidson’s paratactic theory and the ambiguity it engenders. Davidson’s central insight was that indirect discourse can be understood without positing a separate language of thought or a complex theory of meaning for subordinate clauses. In his paratactic analysis, the surface structure of an indirect report is derived from a direct one. When a speaker says, “John said that Bill left,” Davidson proposes that this is logically equivalent to the conjunction of two assertions: John uttered the sentence “Bill left,” and Bill left. The paratactic conjunction collapses the distinction between the act of uttering and the content of the utterance, treating the embedded clause as a direct assertion of the proposition. The word “that” in this analysis is a complementizer; it does not refer to anything in the world but simply marks the boundary of the asserted proposition.

The ambiguity problem arises when we attempt to substitute the proposition for the utterance in this framework. Consider the classic example: “John said that Bill left.” If we substitute the proposition for the utterance, we might interpret this as John asserting the proposition that Bill left. However, ambiguity emerges when we ask what exactly was reported. Did John report the *event* of Bill leaving, or did John report the *sentence* “Bill left”? Davidson’s theory, by reducing indirect discourse to a paratactic conjunction of an utterance and its content, struggles to distinguish between the utterance-type (the sentence itself) and the proposition-type (the meaning of the sentence). The substitution of propositions for utterances risks stripping indirect discourse of its capacity to refer to the very words that were spoken. If “that” merely introduces a proposition, how do we account for cases where the specific wording matters, or where the ambiguity lies between the proposition and the utterance? Davidson’s theory, in its quest for ontological parsimony, inadvertently flattens the semantic space between the utterance and the proposition, leaving the ambiguity problem unresolved.

Gary Kemp’s proposition-function modification is a direct response to this flattening. Kemp argues that Davidson’s error lies in treating the reported content as a static proposition. Instead, Kemp proposes that the content of an indirect discourse report should be understood as a proposition-function—a function from contexts to propositions. On this view, the reported speech in “John said that it was raining” does not denote a single, fixed proposition, but rather a function that maps the context of the original utterance to the proposition that it was raining. By treating the reported content as a proposition-function, Kemp aims to capture the context-dependence and the referential richness that Davidson’s static propositions miss. The ambiguity problem, in Kemp’s view, is resolved because the proposition-function does not substitute the utterance for the proposition in a rigid, one-to-one manner. Rather, it preserves the relationship between the context of the utterance and the proposition expressed within that context. The function bridges the gap between the utterance and the proposition, allowing the indirect report to carry the semantic weight of the original act of speech without collapsing the distinction between the two.

Kemp’s modification is theoretically elegant and offers a compelling solution to the semantic ambiguity of static propositions. By introducing proposition-functions, Kemp successfully accounts for the context-sensitivity of indirect discourse. The proposition-function can vary its output depending on the context of the report, thereby allowing the same indirect sentence to carry different propositional content in different circumstances. This successfully resolves the ambiguity of what exactly is being reported: the proposition-function captures the proposition *as it was meant in the original context*, thus preserving the speaker’s intended meaning without requiring a direct reference to the utterance-type itself. In this sense, substituting propositions for utterances via the lens of proposition-functions successfully navigates the ambiguity problem that plagues Davidson’s original paratactic theory. The ambiguity is not a bug of the theory but a feature of the context-sensitive mapping between utterance and proposition, which the proposition-function elegantly encapsulates.

However, Kemp’s account falters when confronted with the phenomenon of deliberate ambiguity in indirect discourse reports. Deliberate ambiguity occurs when a speaker intentionally produces an utterance that is semantically ambiguous, intending the hearer to entertain multiple incompatible meanings simultaneously. For instance, a speaker might deliberately say, “The butler said that the master was a fool,” where the word “fool” is used in its dual sense of both a jester and a simpleton, and the butler is understood to have meant both. The question is whether Kemp’s proposition-function modification can adequately handle such cases.

Kemp’s account struggles here because proposition-functions, by their very nature, are designed to map contexts to *propositions*. A proposition, in standard philosophical semantics, is a truth-conditional entity; it is either true or false in a given world. When a proposition-function encounters a context of deliberate ambiguity, it must resolve the ambiguity by selecting a specific proposition for that context. Even if the proposition-function maps the context to a range of propositions, it does so by enumerating the possible semantic values, thereby treating the deliberate ambiguity as a mere logical disjunction of distinct propositions. This approach fails to capture the *deliberate* nature of the ambiguity. In a case of deliberate ambiguity, the speaker wants the hearer to remain suspended between the incompatible meanings; the ambiguity is not a failure of disambiguation but the very point of the utterance. Kemp’s proposition-function, by resolving the ambiguity into a function of contexts, strips the reported speech of its pragmatic richness. It converts a living, intentional ambiguity into a static, semantic list of possible propositions. Thus, while Kemp’s modification resolves the structural ambiguity between utterances and propositions, it does so at the cost of failing to account for the speaker’s intentional manipulation of semantic ambiguity. The account adequately handles cases of semantic indeterminacy, but it is structurally incapable of capturing the pragmatic force of deliberate, speaker-intended ambiguity in indirect discourse.

Beyond the internal limitations of Kemp’s account lies a deeper theoretical and empirical objection that strikes at the very foundation of the ambiguity problem he seeks to solve. The ambiguity problem in Davidson’s theory—and by extension, the problem Kemp attempts to solve—often relies on the premise that the word “that” in indirect discourse is a demonstrative rather than a complementizer. If “that” is a demonstrative, it functions much like the words “this” or “that,” pointing to an object in the discourse environment. In the case of indirect discourse, “that” would point to the utterance itself, thereby creating an ambiguity between the proposition expressed by the utterance and the utterance-type to which “that” refers. This is the root of the ambiguity: the demonstrative “that” ambiguously refers to either the proposition or the utterance. Kemp’s proposition-function modification attempts to resolve this by making the reference context-dependent, but it leaves intact the premise that “that” is a demonstrative. However, there are profound theoretical and empirical objections to treating “that” as a demonstrative, and cross-linguistic evidence from Hungarian provides a decisive refutation of this premise.

The theoretical objection to treating “that” as a demonstrative is rooted in its syntactic behavior. Demonstratives are typically determiners or pronouns that require an antecedent in the discourse or the environment to which they refer. They are indexical, pointing to specific entities. Complementizers, on the other hand, are function words that subordinate a clause to a main clause without referring to any external entity. In English, “that” in indirect discourse behaves perfectly as a complementizer. It does not refer to the utterance in the way that a demonstrative refers to a noun; rather, it introduces the clause as the content of the speech act. If “that” were a demonstrative, we would expect it to pattern with demonstratives in other syntactic environments, such as requiring a referent or participating in contrastive focus constructions typical of demonstratives. But “that” in indirect discourse lacks these properties. It is semantically empty, serving only to mark the complement clause. Therefore, treating it as a demonstrative is theoretically unmotivated and leads to a category mistake that generates the very ambiguity Kemp tries to solve.

The empirical evidence from Hungarian provides the most compelling argument against the demonstrative analysis of “that.” Hungarian offers a clear and rigid syntactic distinction between the complementizer and the demonstrative, which directly contradicts the premise that English “that” is a demonstrative. In Hungarian, the complementizer that introduces indirect discourse is “hogy,” while the demonstrative pronoun or determiner is “az.” Consider the following Hungarian sentence: “János azt mondta, hogy esett a eső.” (John said that it was raining.) Here, “hogy” is the complementizer introducing the proposition that it was raining, and “azt” (the accusative form of “az”) is a demonstrative pronoun referring to the utterance itself. Crucially, Hungarian syntax strictly prohibits the substitution of “hogy” for “az” or vice versa. The demonstrative “az” cannot introduce a complement clause, and the complementizer “hogy” cannot function as a demonstrative pointing to an utterance. 

This strict separation is highly significant. If English “that” were a demonstrative, as the ambiguity problem assumes, then English should pattern with Hungarian’s demonstrative system, allowing for the demonstrative to introduce the clause or for the complementizer to refer to the utterance. But English does not pattern this way. In Hungarian, the ambiguity between the utterance and the proposition is resolved not by a single ambiguous word, but by the rigid syntactic distinction between two separate words: the demonstrative “az” and the complementizer “hogy.” The fact that Hungarian uses two distinct morphemes for what English attempts to do with a single “that” strongly suggests that the ambiguity in English is not due to “that” being a demonstrative that ambiguously points to both the utterance and the proposition. Rather, it suggests that English “that” is strictly a complementizer, and any apparent ambiguity must be located elsewhere—perhaps in the semantics of the verb “say” or in the pragmatic enrichment of the reported content, not in the syntax of the subordinator. 

The cross-linguistic evidence from Hungarian thus undermines the very foundation of Kemp’s modification. Kemp’s proposition-function modification is designed to solve the ambiguity created by treating “that” as a demonstrative. By making the reference of “that” context-dependent, Kemp attempts to bridge the gap between the utterance and the proposition. But if Hungarian shows that “that” is not a demonstrative at all—if it is simply a complementizer that introduces a proposition—then the ambiguity problem Kemp is solving is a phantom problem. The ambiguity is not a structural feature of the language caused by a demonstrative “that,” but a pragmatic or semantic feature arising from the complexity of speech-act verbs. Therefore, Kemp’s proposition-function modification is an elaborate solution to a problem that does not exist in the syntax of the language. The substitution of propositions for utterances via proposition-functions is an unnecessary theoretical maneuver if the word “that” is not doing the double duty of referring to both utterances and propositions.

In conclusion, Gary Kemp’s proposition-function modification of Donald Davidson’s paratactic theory of indirect discourse represents a sophisticated and intellectually motivated attempt to resolve the ambiguity problem inherent in substituting propositions for utterances. By shifting from static propositions to context-sensitive proposition-functions, Kemp successfully accounts for the semantic richness and context-dependence of indirect discourse reports. His account elegantly bridges the gap between the utterance and the proposition, resolving the structural ambiguity that Davidson’s theory leaves unresolved. However, Kemp’s account is not without its limitations. It fails to adequately handle instances of deliberate ambiguity in indirect discourse reports, as proposition-functions inherently resolve ambiguity by mapping contexts to specific propositions, thereby stripping the reported speech of the intentional, pragmatic multiplicity that characterizes deliberate speaker ambiguity. More fundamentally, Kemp’s modification rests on the theoretical premise that the word “that” is a demonstrative, a premise that is empirically refuted by cross-linguistic evidence from Hungarian. Hungarian’s strict syntactic separation of the complementizer “hogy” and the demonstrative “az” demonstrates that “that” in English is a complementizer, not a demonstrative, thereby dissolving the ambiguity problem that Kemp’s proposition-functions were designed to solve. While Kemp’s modification is a valuable contribution to the philosophy of language, it ultimately constructs an elaborate theoretical apparatus to address a problem that is better explained by abandoning the demonstrative analysis of “that” altogether.
