# Actual Infinity, Beginningless Time, and the Limits of Craig’s Reductio

William Lane Craig’s philosophical case against an infinite temporal regress is one of the most influential contemporary defenses of the claim that the universe began to exist. His argument is not merely that an infinite past is difficult to imagine. Craig argues that an actual infinite is metaphysically impossible when instantiated in the concrete world. If the universe had no beginning, then the series of past events would constitute an actual infinite formed by successive addition. Since, he argues, an actual infinite cannot exist in reality, the universe must have begun. This conclusion has obvious implications for theological systems that allow some form of beginningless time, eternal matter, or uncreated intelligences, including aspects of Latter-day Saint or LDS cosmology.

Craig’s principal illustrations include the Principle of Correspondence, Hilbert’s Hotel, and the infinite library. These examples are intended to reveal absurdities generated by the existence of an actual infinite. Yet whether they establish genuine impossibility is controversial. The examples do show that infinite collections possess properties unlike those of finite collections. They do not, by themselves, demonstrate that such properties are contradictory. What appears absurd may reflect the limitations of finite human intuition rather than a violation of logic. The central philosophical question, therefore, is whether Craig’s arguments expose an incoherent concept or merely a counter-intuitive one.

The answer depends partly on what is meant by “actual infinite,” partly on the difference between mathematical and metaphysical possibility, and partly on whether a beginningless temporal series is analogous to an abstract set. Craig is right that a real, concrete infinite raises difficult questions that cannot be dismissed simply by appealing to mathematics. But his reductio arguments do not successfully demonstrate that all actual infinities are impossible. At most, they establish that certain conceptions of an actual infinite conflict with ordinary finite intuitions and perhaps that some models of an infinite past are metaphysically problematic. LDS eternalism or beginningless time can therefore remain philosophically coherent, although it must clarify what sort of infinity it affirms and how temporal succession is related to that infinity.

## Craig’s Basic Argument Against an Actual Infinite

Craig’s reasoning is closely connected with the Kalam cosmological argument. In its familiar form, the argument proceeds as follows:

1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
2. The universe began to exist.
3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

The first premise is defended through common experience and metaphysical intuition. The second is defended through both philosophical arguments against an actual infinite and scientific arguments concerning cosmic origins. The present essay concerns the philosophical side, especially the claim that an infinite past is impossible.

Craig distinguishes between a potential infinite and an actual infinite. A potential infinite is an unending process that is always capable of further extension. For example, one may continue counting without reaching a final number. An actual infinite, by contrast, is a completed totality containing infinitely many members. The set of natural numbers—0, 1, 2, 3, and so forth—is the standard mathematical example. Craig accepts that potential infinity may be coherent but denies that an actual infinite can exist in the concrete world.

He then argues that the past cannot be an actual infinite because the past is formed by successive addition. One event occurs, then another, and then another. A sequence formed by adding one member at a time, he contends, can never become actually infinite. No matter how many members have been added, there will always be only a finite number. Since an infinite temporal regress would require an actually infinite number of past events to have elapsed before the present, the past must be finite.

This argument combines two claims:

- An actual infinite cannot exist in reality.
- An infinite temporal series cannot be formed by successive addition.

The first claim is illustrated through paradoxes involving infinite sets. The second is directed more specifically at time. The illustrations are meant to show that actual infinites generate absurd results, while the successive-addition argument is meant to show that an infinite past could never be completed.

## The Principle of Correspondence and the “Paradoxes” of Infinity

Craig frequently appeals to the Principle of Correspondence, associated especially with Georg Cantor. The principle states that a set is infinite if and only if it can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself. For example, the set of natural numbers can be paired with the set of even numbers:

- 0 corresponds to 0,
- 1 corresponds to 2,
- 2 corresponds to 4,
- 3 corresponds to 6,

and so on. Every natural number has a corresponding even number, and every even number is paired with exactly one natural number. Yet the even numbers form only a proper subset of the natural numbers, since many natural numbers—such as 1, 3, and 5—are not even.

For finite collections, this is impossible. If a hotel has ten rooms and all ten are occupied, it cannot accommodate an additional guest without displacing someone. If a collection contains ten objects, a proper subset must contain fewer than ten. Infinite collections behave differently: a proper subset can have the same cardinality as the whole.

Craig does not claim that this violates formal logic within set theory. Rather, he argues that it is absurd when applied to concrete reality. If there were infinitely many objects, then removing some objects would not reduce the total number. An infinite collection could have the same number of members as it had before objects were removed. This seems to undermine ordinary arithmetic and the principle that the whole is greater than the part.

The philosophical force of this point depends on the assumption that ordinary finite arithmetic must govern infinite collections. But that assumption is precisely what Cantorian mathematics rejects. The claim that the whole is greater than the part is valid for finite sets, but it is not a universal law of cardinality. For infinite sets, “greater than” has a technical meaning that does not require the whole to have a larger cardinality than a proper subset. The natural numbers and the even numbers have the same cardinality, although the former contains the latter as a proper subset.

This is not a contradiction. A contradiction would involve affirming both a proposition and its negation in the same sense. For example, one could not coherently assert that the set of natural numbers has exactly the same cardinality as the set of even numbers and does not have the same cardinality. But no such contradiction appears in the Principle of Correspondence. The relevant propositions are:

- The even numbers are a proper subset of the natural numbers.
- The two sets have the same cardinality.

In finite arithmetic, these propositions would be incompatible. In infinite cardinal arithmetic, they are not. The concept of cardinality has been generalized so that it measures the existence of one-to-one correspondences rather than the result of ordinary subtraction. Thus the surprising feature is a departure from finite intuitions, not a formal inconsistency.

Craig may respond that the issue is not formal contradiction but metaphysical absurdity. A mathematical model can be internally consistent while failing to describe a possible concrete reality. This is a legitimate distinction. Mathematics alone does not establish that every mathematically coherent structure can be instantiated physically. The existence of a set-theoretic model does not prove that there are infinitely many actual stars, moments, or causal events. But to show metaphysical impossibility, Craig must identify a contradiction in the proposed concrete reality, not merely point out that it behaves differently from finite collections.

The Principle of Correspondence therefore establishes an important conclusion, but not necessarily Craig’s conclusion. It shows that an actual infinite cannot be understood as a very large finite collection. It has a distinct structure. If someone assumes that all collections must obey finite principles, then actual infinity will seem impossible by definition. But the argument risks becoming question-begging: the supposed absurdity results from applying finite rules to a domain in which those rules have already been shown not to apply.

## Hilbert’s Hotel

Hilbert’s Hotel is perhaps Craig’s most famous illustration. Imagine a hotel with infinitely many rooms, numbered 1, 2, 3, and so forth, and suppose every room is occupied. Despite being full, the hotel can accommodate one additional guest by moving the occupant of room 1 to room 2, the occupant of room 2 to room 3, and so on. Room 1 then becomes available. The hotel can accommodate infinitely many additional guests by moving each occupant to the room with twice the number: the occupant of room 1 moves to room 2, room 2 to room 4, room 3 to room 6, and so on. All odd-numbered rooms become vacant.

Craig argues that such a hotel is absurd. It would be full and yet capable of accepting more guests. It could accommodate one new guest, infinitely many new guests, or even infinitely many buses each containing infinitely many guests. In addition, if an infinite number of guests checked out, the hotel could still have exactly as many guests as before. The situation contradicts common sense and ordinary arithmetic. Therefore, Craig concludes, an actual infinite cannot exist in the real world.

Hilbert’s Hotel is undeniably counter-intuitive. Yet its counter-intuitiveness is exactly what the mathematics of countable infinity predicts. The hotel does not contain “more than infinitely many” rooms after new guests arrive. The number of rooms remains countably infinite. Nor does “full” mean that every possible numerical capacity has been exhausted in the finite sense. It means that every room is occupied. A hotel can be full in that sense while still allowing a rearrangement that creates vacancies.

The key is that the rearrangement is not a violation of logic. Each guest receives exactly one room, no two guests occupy the same room, and every room remains available to at most one guest. The assignment is a bijection. In the case of one additional guest, the new guest goes to room 1 and each previous occupant moves from room \(n\) to room \(n+1\). In the case of infinitely many new guests, one can use an appropriate bijection that places old guests in even-numbered rooms and new guests in odd-numbered rooms. There is no contradiction unless one assumes in advance that a full collection cannot be enlarged. That assumption is true for finite collections but false for infinite ones.

The Hotel does, however, expose a genuine metaphysical question. If one imagines an actual physical hotel with infinitely many rooms, each containing a physical occupant, the scenario may be impossible for physical reasons. The hotel would require infinite spatial extension, infinite matter, infinite energy, or some other physically problematic conditions. But this would not show that infinity is logically impossible. It would show only that an infinite hotel may be incompatible with the physical laws or resource constraints of our universe.

There is also a question about the temporal realization of the hotel’s rearrangements. If infinitely many guests must move one at a time, perhaps the process could never be completed. But Hilbert’s Hotel does not require a temporal process in which each guest moves successively. It describes a static arrangement or a mathematically defined transformation. One can specify the final arrangement all at once. If the hotel is treated as a dynamic physical system, additional questions arise, but those questions concern physical implementation rather than the coherence of the infinite structure itself.

Craig’s use of Hilbert’s Hotel is strongest as an argument against the idea that an actual infinite is merely an extremely large finite quantity. It demonstrates that infinity has unfamiliar arithmetic properties. It is weaker as a proof of contradiction. The analogy establishes that certain outcomes are surprising, but surprise is not the same as impossibility. A reductio succeeds only if the conclusion derived from the supposition is genuinely unacceptable, and “unacceptable” must mean more than psychologically difficult to imagine.

## The Infinite Library

The infinite library offers another version of the same challenge. Imagine a library containing infinitely many books. The books might be arranged in a sequence, or they might include infinitely many copies of every possible book. If half the books are removed, the library still contains infinitely many books. If the books are rearranged, the collection may have the same size as before. In some versions, every possible finite text appears somewhere in the library, perhaps repeatedly. The result seems to make distinctions between quantities collapse.

Again, the example illustrates the difference between finite and infinite cardinality. Removing every other book from a countably infinite library leaves a countably infinite remainder. But the fact that the remainder has the same cardinality as the original does not mean that no books were removed. The set-theoretic description preserves both facts: the remainder is a proper subset, and the two sets are equinumerous.

There is also an ambiguity between “number” as cardinality and “number” as a result of counting or measurement. If one asks how many books are in the library, the answer may be “countably infinitely many.” If half are removed, the answer remains “countably infinitely many.” This does not mean that the operation of removal had no effect. It means that cardinality is too coarse a measure to capture every difference between infinite collections. The original library and the reduced library differ in membership even though they share a cardinal number.

One might object that if the library has infinitely many books, it could never have acquired them by ordinary addition. No one could place the first book, then the second, then the third, and eventually finish placing an infinite collection. This objection anticipates Craig’s argument from successive addition. It is powerful if the library is assumed to begin empty and to become complete through a finite-duration process. But it does not follow that an infinite library cannot exist. It may exist without having been generated by a process that began at a first member and ended at a final stage. Alternatively, it may be regarded as a totality with no final completion event.

This distinction is especially important for time. An infinite past does not necessarily involve a moment at which infinitely many events had already been added to an initially empty temporal container. On a beginningless model, there is no first moment and no final moment of construction before the present. Every past moment is preceded by earlier moments, but the series need not have been “completed” by a final act of addition. The present is not a temporal endpoint that was reached after traversing an infinite interval in the same way that a runner reaches a finish line after traveling a road.

Whether that response succeeds depends on one’s theory of time. If time is dynamic and events genuinely come into existence successively, then the past may be understood as a series generated by successive becoming. If time is tenseless or eternalist, by contrast, all events are located in a four-dimensional structure, and the metaphor of “forming” the past may be misleading. LDS thought generally emphasizes real temporal progression, agency, and becoming, but it need not accept that reality contains a first moment from which all subsequent moments were successively generated.

## Reductio ad Absurdum: What Counts as an Absurdity?

Craig presents his examples as reductio ad absurdum arguments. The general form is:

1. Assume an actual infinite exists.
2. Derive consequences that are absurd.
3. Therefore, an actual infinite cannot exist.

The validity of the inference depends on the status of step two. If the consequences are formally contradictory, the reductio succeeds. If they are merely unusual, counter-intuitive, or incompatible with finite arithmetic, the argument does not succeed without additional premises.

The expression “absurd” has several possible meanings:

1. **Formal contradiction:** A proposition entails both \(P\) and not-\(P\).
2. **Logical incoherence:** The concepts cannot be jointly specified without contradiction.
3. **Metaphysical impossibility:** The scenario cannot exist in any possible world.
4. **Physical impossibility:** The scenario cannot occur under the laws of nature.
5. **Practical impossibility:** The scenario cannot be constructed or managed by finite agents.
6. **Psychological unintelligibility:** Human beings cannot form a clear mental image of it.

Craig’s examples clearly establish the sixth claim and may support the fourth or fifth in particular physical contexts. They do not straightforwardly establish the first or second. The metaphysical conclusion would require a further argument connecting the unusual properties of mathematical infinity to contradiction in any possible concrete instance.

The Principle of Correspondence does not supply such a contradiction. Hilbert’s Hotel does not supply one either. The infinite library is similarly consistent under standard set theory. Craig can argue that set theory itself is not an adequate guide to metaphysics. But then his case must explain why the properties of infinite sets cannot be instantiated outside mathematics. It is not enough to say that the results violate ordinary intuition, because ordinary intuition was formed in a finite environment and may have no authority regarding infinity.

This does not imply that every mathematically coherent infinity is metaphysically possible. Mathematical structures abstract away from causation, embodiment, physical law, and temporal realization. A consistent set-theoretic model of infinitely many particles does not show that a universe containing infinitely many particles can exist. But the burden is to identify the relevant nonmathematical contradiction. For example, an actual infinite of events might be impossible if every event requires a prior event and if causal dependence cannot be founded in a beginningless chain. That would be a causal argument, not an argument from the Principle of Correspondence alone.

## The Successive Addition Argument

Craig’s argument from successive addition is more directly relevant to beginningless time. He reasons that the present could not have been reached if there were infinitely many past events. Since the past is formed by successive addition, and an infinite number of events cannot be added one at a time, the past must be finite.

There are several ways to evaluate this argument.

First, it is important to distinguish “having an infinite number of predecessors” from “starting at a point and completing an infinite task.” Suppose time is represented by the integers:

\[
\ldots, -3, -2, -1, 0.
\]

The present is 0. It has infinitely many predecessors, but there is no first event and no process that began at negative infinity and advanced through every integer until reaching 0. The model does not describe an infinite task performed from a starting point. There is no starting point. The claim that one cannot traverse an infinite sequence from a first member to a last member is therefore not obviously applicable, because a beginningless sequence has neither a first member nor an infinite interval that must be completed from a beginning.

Craig may reply that, even without a first event, the present still has infinitely many events in its past. The question remains how those events could have elapsed. Yet “elapsed” is ambiguous. If it means that each past event was once present and then succeeded by another, then a beginningless series satisfies the condition: for every event, there is a later event, and for every event before the present, there is another later event. If it means that an infinite totality had to be completed before the present, then the argument presupposes that time has an initial point from which the totality was generated. That is precisely what the beginningless model denies.

Second, the argument may involve an equivocation between an infinite series as a whole and an infinite process. A set of all negative integers is an actual infinite totality. But the temporal relations among its members need not amount to a process of constructing that set. A sequence can be ordered without being generated by an act of sequential addition. The events in a beginningless past are successively related, but that does not entail that the entire infinite series was produced by a finite agent performing an infinite sequence of operations.

Third, if time has a present moment and extends infinitely backward, each finite interval before the present contains only finitely many events, assuming a discrete temporal structure. There is no finite interval from which infinitely many successive events must be traversed. The total past is infinite, but every segment leading up to the present from any selected earlier event is finite. A person living at time 0 does not need to start at a first point in order to have a finite distance from any particular earlier point.

The argument becomes more forceful if time is composed of discrete events and if there must be a first event for any succession to exist. But that premise requires defense. It is not a theorem of logic. One can have a sequence indexed by the integers, in which every member has a successor and a predecessor. If Craig’s claim is that such a sequence cannot be metaphysically instantiated, he needs an argument beyond the fact that it lacks a first member.

There are, of course, serious philosophical objections to beginningless temporal succession. Some argue that an infinite past cannot be “completed,” even if it has no beginning. Others claim that an actual infinite of prior events leads to causal dependence without a foundation. Still others argue that temporal passage requires a first moment of becoming. These positions may support a finite past, but each depends on substantive assumptions about causation, time, and metaphysical dependence. They are not established merely by the existence of infinite sets.

## Finite Human Intuition and Actual Infinity

The tension between finite intuition and actual infinity is unavoidable. Human beings encounter only finite collections in ordinary life. Every physical object has finite dimensions, every practical task has finite stages, and every familiar collection can in principle be counted or exhausted. Our basic arithmetic concepts are shaped by this experience. It is unsurprising that infinite cardinality seems paradoxical.

Yet intuitive difficulty has limited evidential force. Many accepted truths violate ordinary intuition. Relativity conflicts with the idea that time is absolute. Quantum theory conflicts with classical notions of definite trajectories. Non-Euclidean geometry conflicts with the idea that space must obey Euclid’s parallel postulate. The success of these theories does not show that intuition is worthless; it shows that intuition must sometimes be disciplined by precise conceptual distinctions.

Infinity is especially likely to challenge imagination because imagination is not the same as conception. We cannot visualize an infinite set as a completed image. But we can understand it through definitions and relations. The inability to picture all natural numbers at once does not make the set incoherent. Mathematical understanding often consists in grasping a rule that determines infinitely many cases, not in surveying every case individually.

Still, caution is needed when moving from mathematics to metaphysics. The fact that the human mind can define an infinite set does not show that reality contains one. The question is whether an actual infinite is merely a useful abstraction or can exist as a concrete totality. Here Craig’s challenge is legitimate. An infinite set in mathematics does not automatically become an infinite physical collection. But the failure of intuition does not settle the issue in Craig’s favor. A metaphysical argument must show either contradiction or a principled reason why physical reality excludes actual infinity.

The debate may therefore be framed as a conflict between two kinds of intuition. Craig relies on the intuition that a completed infinite is impossible in concrete reality. His critics rely on the intuition that a consistent mathematical structure may be metaphysically possible unless contradiction is shown. Neither intuition is decisive by itself. The crucial question is whether the concept of an actual infinite has a coherent application to time, matter, or events.

## LDS Eternalism and Beginningless Time

Latter-day Saint theology presents a distinctive challenge to traditional arguments against an infinite past. LDS doctrine rejects or substantially revises the classical Christian doctrine of creation ex nihilo. Human beings are not created from nothing in the sense of being brought into existence as wholly novel substances. LDS scripture and theology speak of intelligences as having no beginning, and of matter or elements as eternal. Human persons are understood as premortal intelligences who enter embodied existence, undergo mortal experience, and continue into postmortal life. God is supreme, powerful, and worthy of worship, but divine activity is often described as organizing, ordering, and perfecting rather than creating all reality from absolute nothingness.

This framework can accommodate several forms of infinity:

- an infinite or beginningless series of intelligences,
- eternal matter or elements,
- an unbounded history of divine and cosmic development,
- a temporal order with no first moment,
- or an eternal progression in which there is no absolute beginning to existence as such.

LDS doctrine does not necessarily require every version of these claims. It may allow ambiguity concerning whether the past is temporally infinite, whether “intelligence” is an individual substance or a primitive feature of reality, and whether all forms of time existed eternally in the same way. Nevertheless, the theological direction is substantially more hospitable to beginninglessness than classical theism.

Can LDS theology consistently affirm an actual infinite? It can, provided it distinguishes several questions that are often collapsed.

First, an infinite number of intelligences is not necessarily an infinite number of past events. A doctrine may affirm that there are infinitely many beings while remaining agnostic about whether temporal history has an infinite past. Conversely, it may affirm beginningless time without claiming that all possible beings coexist in a single countably infinite collection. The two claims should not be treated as identical.

Second, eternal matter need not mean that matter was produced through an infinite succession of transformations. If matter is metaphysically fundamental, it does not require an earlier event that caused it to exist. It may still undergo temporal changes, but the existence of the basic stuff is not explained by a prior creative act. Craig’s causal argument against an infinite series of dependent events may therefore have less force against a theology that denies that every reality is dependent in the same way.

Third, eternal progression should not be confused with the claim that an infinite task was completed. Progression may be an indefinitely extensible process. If LDS theology teaches endless development into the future, that is usually a potential infinite: there is always further progress available. The stronger question concerns the past. A beginningless past would be an actual infinite in the sense that the set of past events is infinite. But the theology can maintain that such a totality has no first stage without claiming that it was once incomplete and then completed.

## Arithmetic Operations and the Reality of Infinite Collections

Critics of actual infinity often emphasize that ordinary operations generate puzzling results. Subtracting infinitely many members from an infinite set may leave the same number of members. Adding members may not increase cardinality. Dividing an infinite collection into parts may yield parts equal in size to the original. These results seem to undermine the intelligibility of arithmetic.

The proper response is not that arithmetic becomes meaningless, but that different kinds of arithmetic apply. Finite cardinal arithmetic and infinite cardinal arithmetic have different rules because they measure different structures. For a finite set, adding one member increases its cardinality. For a countably infinite set, adding one member leaves its cardinality countably infinite. This does not make the operation incoherent. It means that cardinality does not record every quantitative difference.

Moreover, the identity of cardinality is not identity of objects. If one book is removed from an infinite library, the original and remaining collections may have equal cardinality, but they are not the same collection. One contains a book the other lacks. The cardinal number expresses the existence of a bijection, not an exhaustive inventory of all properties.

A similar point applies to time. If an infinite past exists, the number of past events may remain infinite even if some events are removed from a model. But this does not mean that temporal history is unaffected in every respect. Removing events changes causal relations, durations, and local sequences, even if it does not change the cardinality of the total set.

The deeper issue is whether arithmetic descriptions of infinite sets can be applied to concrete temporal reality. If time consists of events with intrinsic temporal order, cardinality alone may not be the appropriate measure. Two temporal histories can have the same cardinality while differing radically in duration, causal structure, density, and physical content. Thus a paradox based solely on cardinality may be too abstract to establish metaphysical impossibility.

## Does Successive Addition Rule Out LDS Beginninglessness?

Suppose LDS theology affirms a genuinely beginningless temporal history. Would successive addition make this impossible? The answer depends on how temporal events are individuated and how time is understood.

If every moment is a discrete event, then the past might be modeled by the negative integers. The present is not reached by counting from a first moment; there is no first moment. Every event has a finite number of successors before any selected later event, but there are infinitely many events in the total past. This is mathematically coherent.

If time is continuous, the issue becomes more complicated. The interval extending infinitely into the past contains infinitely many instants, but instants may not be “events” in the relevant causal sense. A continuous temporal interval is not formed by adding instants one at a time. Craig’s successive-addition argument is most applicable to a discrete event ontology, yet the discreteness required by the argument is itself controversial.

If time is dynamic, one might say that the present is continuously coming into being and that the past is generated through successive actualization. But a beginningless dynamic series can still be described as one in which every moment is preceded by another. The notion of “completion” may be inappropriate because the past is not an event that was completed before the present; it is the totality of events earlier than the present. The phrase “the present was reached” can suggest a race from an initial point, but temporal becoming does not necessarily include such a race.

An LDS thinker might therefore maintain:

1. Every temporal event has an earlier event.
2. Every temporal event is followed by later events.
3. The present is a real stage in an ongoing process.
4. There is no first stage of the process.
5. No contradiction follows from these claims.

This position may be metaphysically controversial, but it is not refuted by Hilbert’s Hotel. It requires a coherent account of causation, identity through change, and temporal passage. LDS theology already emphasizes process, agency, embodiment, and development, so it may have conceptual resources for such an account. On the other hand, if LDS doctrine is interpreted as requiring an infinite sequence of discrete past choices, births, deaths, and causal transitions, it must explain why such a series can exist without a first ground. The theology may answer that reality is not dependent upon a single absolute act of creation and that some features of existence are primitive or eternal. Whether that answer is satisfactory is a separate metaphysical question.

## The Strongest Case for Craig

A fair evaluation should recognize that Craig’s arguments are not simply appeals to ignorance or psychological discomfort. They express genuine philosophical concerns.

First, concrete infinities may differ from abstract mathematical infinities because concrete objects stand in causal and material relations. A mathematical set can exist timelessly within an axiomatic structure, whereas a physical collection may require resources, locations, and processes that cannot be infinite.

Second, the actual infinite may be incompatible with temporal becoming. If the past is an actually infinite collection of events and if the present is produced through the successive occurrence of those events, one may argue that an infinite regress of temporal actualizations lacks a foundation. The question is not merely how many events there are but how an event can occur after infinitely many prior events.

Third, some infinite scenarios can be made to generate conflicting outcomes depending on how objects are arranged or removed. If a hotel’s residents are immortal and no new rooms are added, the result of removing different groups may appear to depend on arbitrary rearrangements. An infinite library might contain different “numbers” of books after the same kind of operation. Craig uses this to argue that actual infinity permits incompatible descriptions of the same reality.

These concerns show that the issue is not settled by saying, “Mathematicians use infinity.” A metaphysical account must explain why mathematical infinitude is applicable to concrete temporal order. Craig is right to demand that proponents of beginningless time provide more than a formal model.

Yet these arguments still fall short of demonstrating impossibility. The fact that different bijections produce different decompositions does not imply that the underlying set is inconsistent. Infinite sets have structural properties that are sensitive to how subsets are defined, just as finite geometric figures can be divided in different ways without contradiction. The more serious challenge concerns causal and temporal structure, not cardinal arithmetic.

## Final Assessment

Craig’s arguments against the actual infinite successfully demonstrate that infinity is not simply an enormous version of finitude. The Principle of Correspondence, Hilbert’s Hotel, and the infinite library reveal that infinite collections have properties that violate ordinary finite expectations. They also warn against careless movement from abstract mathematics to claims about physical reality. These are important philosophical lessons.

They do not, however, establish that an actual infinite is logically impossible. The examples do not generate formal contradictions. They generate counter-intuitive consequences that are fully accommodated by standard set theory. To convert counter-intuitiveness into metaphysical impossibility, Craig needs additional premises about the nature of concrete objects, temporal succession, causal dependence, or the relation between possibility and construction. Those premises may be defensible, but they are not established merely by the examples themselves.

The argument from successive addition is more relevant to a beginningless past, but it also depends on disputed assumptions. A beginningless series need not be a task that began at a first point and was completed before the present. Temporal order can be represented by a sequence with no first member. Whether such a sequence can be physically or metaphysically instantiated remains an open question, but its coherence is not refuted by saying that one cannot count from negative infinity to zero.

LDS theology can therefore consistently accommodate an actual infinite, beginningless time, eternal matter, or an infinite population of intelligences, provided it distinguishes these claims and offers an account of their metaphysical grounding. It need not deny the validity of finite arithmetic; it must simply acknowledge that infinite cardinality follows different rules. It need not claim that an infinite past was completed through an infinite act of addition; it can understand the past as a beginningless ordered totality or as an ungrounded but coherent temporal series. Whether such a view is ultimately persuasive depends on broader questions concerning divine dependence, causal explanation, temporal passage, and the nature of intelligence.

Craig’s reductio arguments are therefore best understood as challenges rather than demonstrations. They effectively expose the limits of finite intuition and force defenders of eternalism to clarify their ontology. But they do not by themselves prove that LDS eternalism or beginningless time is impossible. The alleged absurdities show that actual infinity is strange. They do not show that it is contradictory. In philosophy, that distinction is decisive: what cannot be easily imagined is not thereby what cannot exist.
