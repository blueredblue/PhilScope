The ontological argument has long stood as one of the most audacious attempts in the history of philosophy to deduce the existence of God purely from the concept of the divine. St. Anselm of Canterbury, in his *Proslogion*, defined God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived," arguing that such a being must exist in reality, for a being existing only in the understanding would be less than a being existing in both the understanding and reality. However, Immanuel Kant delivered a devastating blow to this classical formulation by asserting that "existence is not a predicate." For Kant, to say that a thing exists is not to add a property or characteristic to the concept of that thing; rather, it is merely to posit the object as an element within the domain of experience. Thus, the concept of a supremely perfect being, no matter how rich in attributes, does not logically entail its actual existence. Charles Hartshorne, in the twentieth century, sought to rescue the ontological argument from Kant’s critique by revising it through the lens of modal logic. By shifting the argument from a definitional predicate to a modal necessity, Hartshorne attempted to demonstrate that the existence of God is not merely a conceptual augmentation but a logical necessity. However, a rigorous philosophical evaluation reveals that Hartshorne’s revision, while clever, fails to adequately address the internal contradictions of Anselm’s classical concept of *actus purus*, and that the central assumption of God’s possibility remains unproven when examined through the distinction between abstract being and concrete becoming. Ultimately, modal coextensiveness and the appeal to potentiality fail to bridge the unbridgeable gap between the a priori concept of perfection and the necessary existence of a perfect being.

To understand the necessity of Hartshorne’s revision, one must first grasp the severity of Kant’s critique. Kant’s argument against the ontological proof rests on the distinction between analytic and synthetic judgments, and more specifically, on the nature of predicates. A predicate, in Kantian terms, is a property that is contained within or can be derived from the concept of a subject. When we define a triangle, the predicate of having three angles is logically contained within the subject. Existence, however, is fundamentally different. Kant famously illustrated this with the example of a hundred thalers. Whether I have the concept of a hundred thalers or merely possess the coins themselves, the sum total of the concept remains identical. Existence does not add a new property to the hundred thalers; it merely indicates that the concept is instantiated in reality. Therefore, existence is not a real predicate. Kant argued that the ontological argument illicitly treats existence as a predicate by assuming that the concept of a supremely real being contains existence as a necessary component of its definition. This is a category mistake. To define a unicorn as an existing unicorn does not make unicorns exist; it merely posits the concept as corresponding to an actual entity. Kant’s critique successfully dismantles the classical ontological argument by demonstrating that one cannot deduce existence from a definition, as existence is not a logical property but an empirical or existential given.

It is precisely this vulnerability that Charles Hartshorne sought to exploit and transform. Hartshorne argued that Kant’s critique, while devastating to the classical formulation, does not apply to the modal revision of the argument. In Hartshorne’s formulation, the argument does not claim that existence is a property that enhances the concept of a being. Instead, it claims that *necessary existence* is a perfection, and that a maximally great being—defined as a being that possesses maximal excellence (omnipotence, omniscience, moral perfection) in every possible world—must exist necessarily.
