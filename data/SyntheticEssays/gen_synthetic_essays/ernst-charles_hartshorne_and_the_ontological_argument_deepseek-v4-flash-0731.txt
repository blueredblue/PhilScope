## The Necessary Shadow: Hartshorne, Kant, and the Unresolved Gap in the Ontological Argument

In the annals of philosophical theology, few arguments have proven as resilient and as vexing as Anselm of Canterbury’s ontological argument. Its audacious leap from a purely a priori concept to the concrete, necessary existence of a perfect being has haunted philosophers for nearly a millennium. The argument’s most famous detractor, Immanuel Kant, delivered what many considered a fatal blow with his critique that “existence is not a predicate.” Yet, in the twentieth century, the American process philosopher Charles Hartshorne resurrected the argument from the ashes, not by ignoring Kant, but by radically reconfiguring the logical landscape. Hartshorne’s revision—rooted in modal logic and his unique process metaphysics—seeks to transform the argument from a static proof of a purely actual being into a dynamic proof of a necessarily existing, dipolar deity. The central question, however, is whether Hartshorne’s modal coextensiveness truly resolves the internal contradictions inherent in Anselm’s classical concept of *actus purus*, or whether it merely relocates the problem. A rigorous evaluation reveals that while Hartshorne brilliantly circumvents Kant’s literal objection by shifting the argument from *existence* to *necessity*, his success is ultimately illusory. The argument’s keystone—the assumption that God’s existence is possible—cannot bear the weight placed upon it. When scrutinized through the lens of Hartshorne’s own distinction between abstract being and concrete becoming, the concept of potentiality fails to bridge the gap between the a priori idea of perfection and the necessary existence of a concrete perfect being. Consequently, Hartshorne’s modal argument does not resolve the contradictions of *actus purus*; it abandons *actus purus* altogether, and in doing so, it inadvertently confirms the fundamental truth of Kant’s critique: no amount of logical contortion can derive a concrete, existing subject from a mere concept.

### I. The Classical Stage: Anselm’s *Actus Purus* and the Kantian Objection

To understand Hartshorne’s project, one must first grasp the terrain he seeks to navigate. Anselm’s argument in the *Proslogion* is deceptively simple. God is defined as “that than which nothing greater can be conceived” (*aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari possit*). Anselm posits that even the fool, who says in his heart “there is no God,” understands this concept. Since it is greater to exist in reality than to exist merely in the understanding, the being than which nothing greater can be conceived must exist in reality; otherwise, a greater being—one that exists—could be conceived, contradicting the definition. In *Proslogion* III, Anselm adds a crucial modal dimension: this being must exist *necessarily*, for it is greater to exist necessarily than contingently. Thus, God is not merely a being that happens to exist, but a being whose non-existence is absolutely impossible.

Classical theism, particularly as systematized by Thomas Aquinas, synthesized this Anselmian conception with Aristotelian metaphysics to produce the doctrine of *actus purus*—pure actuality. God is devoid of all potentiality (*potentia*). He is the unmoved mover, the first cause, the absolute fullness of being. In *actus purus*, there is no distinction between essence and existence; God’s essence *is* His existence. He is immutable, impassible, and eternal, existing outside the flow of time. This concept is internally coherent in its own austere terms: if God is absolutely perfect, any potentiality would imply a lack, a capacity to be other than He is, which would constitute an imperfection. Therefore, God must be pure act.

It is precisely this concept of *actus purus* that Kant attacks, though he does so obliquely. In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Kant argues that the ontological argument fails because it treats “existence” (*Existenz*) as a real predicate. A real predicate is a determination that adds to the concept of a thing. For example, “The apple is red” adds the predicate “red” to the concept of “apple.” However, Kant argues, “The apple exists” does not add a new property to the apple; it merely posits the apple as an object of experience. His famous illustration of the hundred thalers is decisive: “A hundred real thalers do not contain the least coin more than a hundred possible thalers.” The concept of a hundred thalers is identical whether they exist or not. Existence is not a property that a thing can have or lack while remaining the same thing; it is the position of the thing itself. Therefore, to argue that God must exist because existence is a perfection included in the concept of God is a category mistake. We cannot define a being into existence, because existence is not a defining characteristic.

Kant’s critique is devastating to the classical formulation. If existence is not a predicate, then Anselm’s argument collapses into a tautology: “God exists” is merely an analytic proposition that tells us nothing about the world. The concept of a perfect being can be conceived, but its existence remains a synthetic question requiring empirical or other non-conceptual justification. The gap between the idea of God and the reality of God remains unbreached.

### II. Hartshorne’s Modal Revolution: From Existence to Necessity

Charles Hartshorne, deeply influenced by Whitehead’s process philosophy and the insights of modal logic, believed he had found a way around Kant’s critique. His key insight was to concede Kant’s point about *existence* but to argue that it does not apply to *necessary existence*. Kant was right that “existence” is not a predicate, but “necessary existence” is a predicate—it is a modal property that describes the *mode* of a being’s existence. To say that a being exists *necessarily* is to say something substantive about that being; it is to say that its non-existence is impossible. This modal property is not a mere posit; it is a logical feature of the being’s nature.

Hartshorne’s argument, formalized by Norman Malcolm and later refined by others, proceeds as follows:

1.  God is defined as the being than which nothing greater can be conceived (or, in Hartshorne’s terms, the being worthy of worship).
2.  If God exists, He exists necessarily. (This is a conceptual truth about perfection; a contingent God would be inferior to a necessary one).
3.  Either God exists necessarily, or He does not exist necessarily.
4.  If God does not exist necessarily, then His existence is contingent (or impossible). If contingent, then there is a possible world where He does not exist. But a being whose existence is contingent cannot be the greatest conceivable being. Therefore, if God exists, He must exist in all possible worlds.
5.  The only remaining question is whether God’s existence is *possible* (i.e., not self-contradictory). If it is possible that God necessarily exists, then, by the modal axiom S5 (◊□P → □P), God necessarily exists.

This argument is a masterstroke of logical manipulation. It completely sidesteps Kant’s critique of existence as a predicate because the argument does not rely on adding “existence” to a concept. Instead, it relies on the modal status of the proposition “God exists.” The argument hinges entirely on the premise that God’s existence is *possible*. If it is possible that a necessary being exists, then it is necessary that it exists. This is the crux of the entire enterprise. Hartshorne calls this the “modal coextensiveness” of perfection and necessity: the concept of God is coextensive with the concept of necessary existence. To conceive of God is to conceive of a being whose existence is necessary.

However, this brilliant maneuver merely shifts the battlefield. The question is no longer “Is existence a predicate?” but rather “Is the concept of a necessary being coherent and possible?” Hartshorne argues that it is possible because the concept is not self-contradictory. But this is precisely where the internal contradictions of *actus purus* re-emerge with a vengeance.

### III. The Central Assumption: The Possibility of God and the Incoherence of *Actus Purus*

The modal ontological argument is only as strong as its possibility premise. If it is impossible for God to exist (i.e., the concept is contradictory), then the argument fails. Hartshorne is acutely aware of this. He argues that the concept of God is possible because it is a coherent concept—it does not violate the laws of logic. But what does “coherent” mean here? For a classical theist, God is *actus purus*. Is *actus purus* a coherent concept? Hartshorne emphatically argues that it is not. In fact, he dedicates much of his philosophical career to demonstrating the internal contradictions of classical theism.

Hartshorne’s critique of *actus purus* is rooted in the problem of divine perfection and relationality. If God is pure actuality, with no potentiality whatsoever, then He cannot be affected by anything outside Himself. He cannot know the world in a genuinely relational way, because knowing the world would require receiving information, which is a form of potentiality (the potential to be informed). A purely actual God cannot respond to prayer, cannot change His mind, cannot experience the joy or sorrow of His creatures, because all of these involve a transition from one state to another—a transition that requires potentiality. Yet, the God of the Bible and of religious experience is precisely a God who loves, who is compassionate, who interacts with history. Hartshorne argues that the classical concept of *actus purus* is thus internally contradictory: it posits a being who is simultaneously perfect (and therefore immutable and impassible) and yet personal (and therefore relational and responsive). This is the internal contradiction Hartshorne seeks to resolve.

To resolve this, Hartshorne proposes a radical revision of the concept of God. He introduces the distinction between *abstract being* and *concrete becoming*. God, for Hartshorne, is dipolar. He has an abstract essence, which is necessary, eternal, and unchanging. This abstract pole includes properties like perfect love, perfect wisdom, and perfect goodness—the defining characteristics of deity. This pole is pure actuality in a sense; it has no potentiality because it is fully determinate in its essential nature. However, God also has a concrete actuality, which is His ongoing, ever-changing experience of the world. This concrete pole is in a state of perpetual becoming. It is contingent, temporal, and relational. God’s concrete experience grows as the world grows, incorporating new events into His divine life. This concrete pole is where potentiality resides; God has the potential to experience new things, to respond to new prayers, to know new facts about the unfolding cosmos.

This dipolar theism is Hartshorne’s solution to the problem of *actus purus*. He does not try to resolve the contradiction within the classical concept; he dissolves it by separating the necessary, abstract essence from the contingent, concrete actuality. The abstract pole is necessary and purely actual; the concrete pole is contingent and involves potentiality. But this dissolution comes at a tremendous cost for the ontological argument.

### IV. Evaluating Modal Coextensiveness: Does it Prove the Right God?

The modal argument, as formulated by Hartshorne, aims to prove the necessary existence of God. But which God does it prove? It proves the necessary existence of the *abstract essence* of God. The argument demonstrates that there must exist a necessary being—a being whose essence is existence. However, this necessary being is, by Hartshorne’s own admission, an abstraction. It is the “God of the philosophers,” stripped of all concrete, personal, relational attributes. It is the bare concept of perfection, the logical requirement that there be a ground of necessity.

The modal argument does *not* prove the necessary existence of the *concrete actuality* of God. The concrete pole of God—His actual experience of the world at any given moment—is, by definition, contingent. It could have been different. God’s experience of the world on Tuesday is not the same as His experience on Wednesday. The concrete actuality is a matter of fact, not a matter of necessity. Therefore, the modal argument proves only that the abstract essence exists necessarily, but it leaves the concrete actuality entirely unproven.

This is a fatal flaw. The God of religious worship, the God that Anselm sought to prove, is not a bare abstraction. He is a living, personal being who acts in history, who loves, who judges, who redeems. Hartshorne’s own dipolar theism insists that the concrete pole is essential to what God is. A God without His concrete actuality is not a complete God; He is merely a potential God, a set of abstract properties awaiting instantiation. The modal argument proves the existence of the *potential* for God, but not the actuality of God. It proves that there is a necessary abstract principle, but it does not prove that this principle is instantiated in a living, concrete reality.

To put it another way, the modal argument proves that the property “divinity” is necessarily instantiated. But it does not prove that the *subject* which instantiates this property has any particular concrete features. It is analogous to arguing that because the concept of a “unicorn” is coherent, and because a necessary unicorn is greater than a contingent unicorn, therefore a necessary unicorn exists. The argument would prove that there is some necessary being with the property of “unicorn-ness,” but it would not tell us anything about the color, size, or behavior of that being. In the same way, Hartshorne’s argument proves that there is a necessary being with the abstract property of “perfection,” but it says nothing about the concrete, personal life of that being. It fails to bridge the gap between the a priori concept of perfection and the necessary existence of a *perfect being* in the full, concrete sense.

### V. Potentiality as a Bridge: A Chasm, Not a Connection

The prompt specifically asks whether potentiality adequately bridges the gap between the a priori concept of perfection and the necessary existence of a perfect being. Hartshorne’s answer is that potentiality is the key to understanding God’s nature. The abstract essence of God contains the potential for concrete actualization. The concept of perfect love, for example, is a potential that is realized in the concrete experiences of God as He interacts with the world. The potentiality of the concrete pole is what allows God to be genuinely related to the world.

But does this potentiality bridge the gap? Let us examine the logic. The modal argument requires that God’s existence is *possible*. This possibility is grounded in the coherence of the abstract essence. The abstract essence is possible because it is not self-contradictory. However, the concrete actuality is not part of the abstract essence. The concrete actuality is a contingent realization of the abstract potential. Therefore, the possibility of God’s existence, as required by the modal argument, is only the possibility of the abstract essence. It is the possibility that there is a necessary being with the property of perfection.

But this is a trivial possibility. It is possible that there is a necessary being with the property of perfection because the concept of “necessary being” is coherent. The modal argument then concludes that this necessary being exists. But this conclusion tells us nothing about the *concrete* God. The potentiality of the concrete pole is not a bridge; it is a chasm. It is the gap between the abstract possibility (which is proven) and the concrete actuality (which is not proven). The modal argument proves that it is possible for God to exist in the abstract sense, and therefore necessary that He exists in the abstract sense. But it does not prove that the concrete, personal God exists.

Hartshorne might respond that the concrete actuality is included in the perfection of God. A God who is merely abstract is not perfect; a perfect God must be concrete, relational, and dynamic. Therefore, the concrete actuality is a necessary component of the divine perfection. But this response conflates the abstract and concrete poles. If the concrete actuality is necessary, then it is not contingent, which contradicts Hartshorne’s own process metaphysics. If the concrete actuality is contingent, then it is not proven by the modal argument. The argument cannot have it both ways. Either God’s concrete existence is necessary, in which case it is not subject to becoming and change, reverting to a form of *actus purus*; or it is contingent, in which case the modal argument fails to prove it. The distinction between abstract being and concrete becoming does not resolve the tension; it merely names it.

Furthermore, the concept of potentiality itself is problematic when applied to God. In classical theism, potentiality is a sign of imperfection. To have potential is to be able to become something one is not yet. Hartshorne embraces this for the concrete pole, arguing that God’s potential for growth in experience is a perfection—it allows God to be supremely responsive. But this redefinition of perfection is a departure from the Anselmian tradition. Anselm’s God is perfect because He is *actus purus*—lacking nothing, having no unrealized potential. Hartshorne’s God is perfect because He is *maximally open*—having infinite potential for new experiences. These are two radically different concepts of perfection. Hartshorne’s modal argument only works if one accepts his process definition of perfection. But if one accepts that definition, then one is no longer proving the existence of the classical God; one is proving the existence of a different being altogether. The ontological argument, in Hartshorne’s hands, becomes a proof of his own process theology, not a proof of classical theism.

### VI. Kant’s Critique Revisited: The Persistence of the Predicate Problem

Does Hartshorne truly escape Kant’s critique? Kant argued that existence is not a predicate. Hartshorne argues that *necessary existence* is a predicate. But is it? Let us examine this more closely. When we say “God necessarily exists,” what are we saying? We are saying that the proposition “God exists” is necessarily true. This is a *de dicto* necessity—it is a property of the proposition, not of the object. Hartshorne, however, wants to claim that necessary existence is a *de re* property—it is a property of the being God itself. He wants to say that God has the property of “necessary existence” in the same way that He has the property of “omnipotence.”
