**The Dialectical Impasse: Epistemological Disguise and the Unresolvable Debate Between Theism and Atheism**

The perennial debate between theism and atheism presents itself as a conflict over the existence of a supreme being, ostensibly a question of objective truth. Yet, a closer examination reveals that the debate rarely progresses beyond a foundational impasse. This impasse is not born of a lack of evidence or logical acumen, but rather from a profound dialectical failure—a failure rooted in the fact that the epistemological rules governing the debate are not neutral instruments for discovering truth, but rather disguised ethical maxims derived from specific historical and cultural contexts. The claim that this debate rests on such a failure is not merely plausible; it is demonstrably accurate when we analyze the philosophical strategies employed by both sides. By scrutinizing Scott A. Shalkowski’s critiques of the “presumption of atheism”—specifically his attacks on parsimony, verifiability, and burdens of proof—and Michael Scriven’s defense of atheism grounded in everyday reasoning and sense-certainty, we see that both rely on presuppositions that are anything but self-evident. G.W.F. Hegel’s account of the confrontation between Faith and Enlightenment in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* provides the definitive framework for understanding this failure, revealing that the atheist critic commits a communicative failure by disguising ethical assumptions—such as the value of autonomy and material utility—as purely epistemic principles. Consequently, the rules governing the philosophy of religion cannot transcend their contextual origins to provide a neutral arbiter; rather, all epistemic frameworks ultimately emerge as post-contextual ethical maxims, which means the debate cannot be resolved through logic alone, but only through a speculative recognition of the one-sidedness of both positions.

**I. The Presumption of Atheism and Shalkowski’s Critique of Epistemic Rules**

The contemporary debate is often framed by the “presumption of atheism,” a term popularized by Antony Flew, which posits that atheism is the default position until the theist provides sufficient evidence. This presumption is not merely a claim about the burden of proof; it is a foundational epistemological stance that dictates what counts as legitimate knowledge. Scott A. Shalkowski, in his incisive critiques of this presumption, dismantles the apparent neutrality of this stance by demonstrating that the rules used to justify it—parsimony, verifiability, and burden of proof—are not objective logical principles but are, in fact, pragmatic and ethical choices masquerading as necessary conditions for rationality.

Shalkowski’s critique of parsimony is particularly telling. The principle of parsimony, or Ockham’s Razor, is often invoked by atheists to argue that a worldview without God is simpler and therefore more likely to be true. The argument runs: given that the existence of the universe can be explained by natural laws, the addition of a supernatural entity is an unnecessary multiplication of entities. However, Shalkowski correctly points out that parsimony is a methodological preference, not an ontological proof. It tells us how to *prefer* explanations, but it does not tell us what *exists*. The universe might very well be complex and contain unnecessary entities. Simplicity is a virtue of theories relative to human cognitive convenience, not a criterion of truth inherent in the world itself. To claim that the simpler theory is *more likely true* is to import a value judgment—that the world ought to be simple, that reality ought to conform to human aesthetic or cognitive preferences. This is an ethical maxim—a prescription for how we should think—disguised as a descriptive law of nature. Shalkowski argues that there is no logical contradiction in a complex universe containing a deity; the preference for simplicity is a choice made by the atheist based on a prior commitment to naturalism, not a conclusion forced by logic.

Similarly, Shalkowski dismantles the verifiability criterion of meaning, which was historically championed by logical positivists and implicitly adopted by many atheists. The criterion holds that a proposition is meaningful only if it is empirically verifiable. Since claims about God are not empirically verifiable, they are deemed meaningless, and the debate is closed before it begins. Shalkowski’s critique here is devastating: the verifiability principle is itself unverifiable. It cannot be empirically verified that “all meaningful statements are empirically verifiable.” Therefore, by its own standard, the principle is meaningless. This self-referential incoherence reveals that verifiability is not a discovered rule of logic but a dogmatic assertion of empiricism. It is an ethical decree—a demand that we only accept as real that which is accessible to our senses and scientific instruments—dressed up as a neutral epistemic criterion. The atheist who relies on verifiability is not engaging in objective inquiry but is enforcing a particular worldview that privileges material, quantifiable reality over any other form of being. This is a communicative failure because it refuses to even acknowledge the possibility of a different mode of truth, such as the existential, moral, or spiritual truth that theism claims to offer.

The third pillar Shalkowski critiques is the assignment of the burden of proof. The presumption of atheism places the burden entirely on the theist. But Shalkowski rightly notes that the burden of proof is a legal and conversational convention, not a logical necessity. In logic, a proposition is either true or false; there is no inherent “burden” on anyone to prove it. The assignment of the burden is a *pragmatic* decision based on what we consider to be the default, or common-sense, position. Shalkowski argues that placing the burden on the theist is an *ethical* choice. It reflects a prior commitment to the idea that skepticism is virtuous, that it is better to withhold belief than to risk error, and that it is unfair to make the skeptic prove a negative. These are moral valuations about intellectual conduct. They are not derived from logic. One could just as easily argue, as some theists do, that the burden of proof lies on the atheist to explain the existence of morality, consciousness, or the intelligibility of the universe without a transcendent ground. Shalkowski’s point is that the choice of who bears the burden is arbitrary from a purely logical standpoint; it is determined by the ethical maxim that one should not assert what one cannot empirically demonstrate. This maxim is a post-Enlightenment ethical commitment to autonomy and empirical evidence, not a universal rule of reason.

Thus, Shalkowski’s critique reveals that the atheist’s epistemological toolkit is not a set of neutral instruments for discovering objective truth. Parsimony, verifiability, and burden of proof are all *ethical maxims*—prescriptions for how a rational person *ought* to believe—that have been abstracted from their context (modern empirical science) and reified into universal rules of logic. The debate, therefore, is not between reason and faith, but between two competing ethical frameworks: one that values empirical evidence and material explanation above all, and another that values existential meaning, tradition, and transcendent reality.

**II. Scriven’s Defense of Atheism and the Presupposition of Sense-Certainty**

If Shalkowski exposes the logical weakness of the atheist’s rules, Michael Scriven’s defense of atheism demonstrates how deeply these rules are embedded in a particular, unexamined mode of everyday reasoning. In his seminal work *Primary Philosophy*, Scriven argues that atheism is the rational default because we do not need to disprove God; the theist must prove him. His argument rests on a fundamental appeal to what he calls “everyday reasoning” and the reliability of our senses. He states that we live in a world where we trust our empirical observations, where we do not believe in invisible entities unless there is compelling evidence for them. We do not believe in unicorns, fairies, or invisible teapots orbiting Mars, and we should not believe in God for the same reason.

Scriven’s defense is powerful precisely because it appeals to a shared, common-sense understanding of the world. It seems undeniably rational to reject unsubstantiated claims. However, Scriven’s reliance on “everyday reasoning” and “sense-certainty” is precisely the kind of presupposition that Hegel identifies as the most impoverished form of knowledge. In the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, Hegel begins his investigation of consciousness with “sense-certainty,” which claims to be the richest and truest form of knowledge because it is immediate—it simply points to “this,” “here,” and “now.” Yet Hegel demonstrates that sense-certainty is actually the most abstract and empty form of knowledge. When we try to articulate what we know through sense-certainty, we find that the “this” and “now” are constantly changing and cannot be pinned down. The truth of sense-certainty is not the immediate particular but the universal—the concept of “being” itself, which is abstract and devoid of content.

Scriven’s atheism is stuck in this stage of sense-certainty. He assumes that the empirical world, as given to our everyday senses, is the ultimate standard of reality. He presupposes that what is real is what is observable, measurable, and subject to the laws of physics. This is a contextual assumption, born from the success of modern science and the Enlightenment’s rejection of metaphysical speculation. But Scriven does not recognize this as a context; he presents it as the very definition of rationality. He commits a communicative failure by assuming that the theist must justify their belief in a transcendent being according to the rules of empirical verification, which are the rules of his own particular context. He fails to see that theism offers a different ontology—one where the ultimate reality is not a physical object but a spiritual subject, where the highest truth is not discovered through sensory observation but through moral conscience, religious experience, or rational contemplation.

Scriven’s appeal to “everyday reasoning” is a sophisticated form of begging the question. He defines “everyday” as the empirical, naturalistic worldview, and then uses that definition to exclude theism. He does not engage with the theist’s claim on its own terms; instead, he imposes his own epistemic framework and demands that the theist conform to it. This is the essence of a dialectical failure. A genuine dialectic requires that both parties be open to the possibility that their own standards of justification might be inadequate. Scriven, however, treats the empirical standard as absolute, thereby foreclosing any possibility of genuine dialogue. He disguises an ethical commitment—the belief that human beings should be autonomous, self-sufficient, and rely only on their own senses and reason—as a neutral epistemic principle.

Furthermore, Scriven’s reliance on sense-certainty ignores the fact that our everyday reasoning is itself embedded in a web of beliefs that are not empirically verifiable. We trust the uniformity of nature, the reliability of our cognitive faculties, and the existence of other minds, none of which can be proven by sense-certainty. These are presuppositions that make everyday reasoning possible. The theist can argue that belief in God is a similar foundational presupposition, a necessary condition for making sense of the world as a coherent, moral, and meaningful whole. Scriven’s refusal to grant theism this status is not based on logic but on a prior ethical decision to privilege the material over the spiritual, the immanent over the transcendent.

**III. Hegel’s Dialectic of Faith and Enlightenment: The Communicative Failure**

To fully understand the nature of this dialectical failure, we must turn to Hegel’s profound analysis in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*. In the chapter on “The Enlightenment,” Hegel examines the confrontation between two shapes of Spirit: Faith and Enlightenment. This is not merely a historical account of the 18th century; it is a structural analysis of the very logic of the theism/atheism debate. Hegel shows that Enlightenment’s critique of Faith is not a rational triumph but a profound misunderstanding, rooted in a communicative failure where Enlightenment mistakes its own ethical project for pure reason.

For Hegel, Faith is a shape of Spirit that has grasped the truth of the world as a unity, a divine presence that transcends but also permeates finite reality. Faith is not mere superstition; it is a form of knowledge, albeit a knowledge that is mediated through representation, feeling, and communal practice. Faith knows that the finite world is not self-sufficient, that its truth lies in an infinite ground. However, Faith’s knowledge is not yet fully conceptual; it clings to images and dogmas.

Enlightenment, on the other hand, arises as a critique of this representational thinking. It sees Faith as a form of bondage to authority, tradition, and priestcraft. Enlightenment demands that Faith justify itself before the tribunal of reason. It demands that Faith provide empirical evidence for its claims, that it prove the existence of its God, and that it demonstrate the utility of its beliefs. Enlightenment claims to be the pure voice of reason, stripping away illusion and superstition to reveal the bare truth of the material world.

Hegel’s genius lies in his demonstration that Enlightenment’s critique is self-defeating and hypocritical. Enlightenment accuses Faith of being based on contingent, external authority, but Enlightenment itself is based on the authority of sense-certainty and utility. Enlightenment claims to be purely rational, but its standard of truth is *utility*—what is useful for human happiness, material progress, and the avoidance of error. This is not a pure epistemic principle; it is an ethical maxim. It is a prescription for how humans ought to live—autonomously, empirically, and for the sake of worldly flourishing. Enlightenment’s demand for verifiability is not a neutral logical rule but a weapon in a political and ethical battle against the authority of the Church and tradition.

The communicative failure occurs because Enlightenment fundamentally misrecognizes the nature of Faith’s claim. Enlightenment projects its own categories of finite understanding onto Faith’s object. It treats God as if God were a finite object, a “thing” that can be empirically observed or logically deduced. When it fails to find such an object, it declares that God does not exist. But Faith does not claim that God is a finite object; Faith claims that God is the infinite, the ground of all being, that which cannot be captured by the categories of finite understanding. Enlightenment’s critique is thus a category error. It speaks a language of utility and verification, and it demands that Faith respond in that language. But Faith’s language is one of devotion, worship, and spiritual certainty—a language that is incommensurable with Enlightenment’s utilitarian calculus. This is the communicative failure: Enlightenment insists on a dialogue, but it dictates the rules of the dialogue in advance, rules that are secretly ethical (the maxim of empirical autonomy) rather than purely logical.

Hegel shows that this confrontation is a deadlock. Enlightenment cannot defeat Faith because it cannot even understand what Faith is claiming. It reduces the infinite to the finite, the transcendent to the immanent, and then celebrates its own reduction as a victory. Similarly, Faith cannot defeat Enlightenment because it refuses to translate its insights into the conceptual language of reason, clinging instead to representation. The result is a dialectical failure—a standoff where neither side can claim victory because they are not even arguing about the same thing.

This analysis directly maps onto the contemporary debate. The atheist, like Hegel’s Enlightenment, demands that the theist provide empirical evidence, invoke parsimony, and accept the burden of proof. The theist, like Faith, responds with appeals to revelation, religious experience, and the moral argument. The atheist dismisses these as non-evidence, while the theist dismisses the atheist’s criteria as reductionist. Neither side engages the other’s fundamental presuppositions. The atheist’s epistemological rules are ethical maxims (the virtue of skepticism, the value of material explanation) disguised as universal logic. The theist’s appeals are ethical maxims (the value of spiritual meaning, the necessity of a moral ground) disguised as objective facts. The debate fails because it is a clash of ethical life-worlds, not a logical dispute.

**IV. The Resolution: Contextual Origins and Post-Contextual Ethical Maxims**

The central question posed by this analysis is whether the rules governing the philosophy of religion can ever transcend their contextual origins, or if all epistemic frameworks ultimately emerge from post-contextual ethical maxims. The answer, as suggested by the preceding analysis, is that they cannot transcend their origins as neutral rules, but they do function as post-contextual ethical maxims.

Epistemic rules like parsimony, verifiability, and burden of proof are not Platonic forms. They are historical products. They emerged from the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment, from a specific struggle against religious dogmatism and political absolutism. They were developed to govern inquiry into the natural world, and their success in that domain led to their being universalized. However, this universalization is illegitimate. The rules that work for physics do not necessarily work for metaphysics, ethics, or religion. To demand that theism conform to the rules of empirical science is to commit a category error, to assume that all truth is of the same kind.

However, these rules are not merely local quirks. They are *post-contextual* in the sense that they have been abstracted from their original context and elevated to the status of universal ethical maxims. They are ethical because they prescribe how we *ought* to believe. “One should not assert what one cannot prove” is a maxim of intellectual honesty. “One should prefer simpler explanations” is a maxim of cognitive economy. “The skeptic should not bear the burden of proof” is a maxim of fairness. These are values. They are not descriptions of how the world is, but prescriptions for how we should think. They are post-contextual because they claim to apply to all rational beings, regardless of their historical or cultural situation.

The implication of this is profound. If all epistemic frameworks are ultimately post-contextual ethical maxims, then there is no neutral ground upon which the theism/atheism debate can be resolved. The atheist cannot prove theism false using empirical criteria because the theist does not accept those criteria as ultimate. The theist cannot prove atheism false using revelatory criteria because the atheist does not accept those criteria. The debate is not about facts; it is about values. It is a conflict between two ways of life, two sets of ethical commitments about what constitutes a meaningful and rational existence.

Can we resolve this tension? Hegel offers a way forward, but it requires a radical shift in perspective. Hegel does not suggest that we find a neutral rulebook; he suggests that we recognize the one-sidedness of both positions. Faith is one-sided because it clings to representation and fails to grasp the conceptual truth of its own insight. Enlightenment is one-sided because it reduces truth to finite utility and fails to see the infinite that grounds the finite. The truth, for Hegel, is the whole—the speculative unity of the finite and the infinite, the immanent and the transcendent. Neither atheism nor theism as currently formulated captures this whole. The atheist is right that the finite world is real and that we must engage with it empirically. The theist is right that the finite world is not self-sufficient and that it points beyond itself to an infinite ground. The truth is not a simple negation of one by the other, but a synthesis that preserves the insights of both while overcoming their limitations.

This synthesis, however, cannot be achieved within the current dialectical framework. It requires a movement to a higher level of consciousness, what Hegel calls “Absolute Knowing.” This is not a knowledge of a particular object but a recognition of the unity of subject and object, of thought and being. In the context of the philosophy of religion, this means recognizing that the debate between theism and atheism is itself a moment in the self-development of Spirit. The conflict is not a failure to be lamented but a necessary stage in the journey towards truth. The epistemological rules that govern the debate are not eternal truths but finite shapes of Spirit that must be aufgehoben—cancelled, preserved, and elevated—into a higher understanding.

Therefore, the resolution is not to find a rule that transcends context, for no such rule exists. Instead, we must recognize that all rules are post-contextual ethical maxims and that the philosophy of religion must become a *hermeneutic of suspicion*—an inquiry into the ethical commitments that underlie our epistemic frameworks. We must ask not merely “Is God’s existence proven?” but “What ethical values are being served by demanding this particular kind of proof?” We must recognize that the atheist’s demand for empirical evidence is a demand for a particular kind of world—a world without mystery, without transcendence, without authority. And we must recognize that the theist’s appeal to revelation is an appeal to a different kind of world—a world where meaning is given, not constructed, where the ultimate reality is personal, not impersonal.

**Conclusion**

The claim that the debate between theism and atheism rests on a dialectical failure driven by epistemological rules rather than objective truth is not only valid but is the key to understanding why the debate remains so intractable. Scott A. Shalkowski’s critiques reveal that the atheist’s reliance on parsimony, verifiability, and burdens of proof are not neutral logical principles but are ethical maxims chosen to serve a naturalistic worldview. Michael Scriven’s defense of atheism demonstrates how deeply these maxims are embedded in a presupposed everyday reasoning and sense-certainty, a mode of knowing that Hegel showed to be the most abstract and unreflective. Hegel’s dialectic of Faith and Enlightenment exposes the communicative failure at the heart of the debate: the atheist (Enlightenment) cannot understand the theist (Faith) because it imposes its own utilitarian and empirical categories onto a claim that is fundamentally about the infinite, thereby reducing the divine to a finite object and declaring its non-existence.

The rules governing the philosophy of religion cannot transcend their contextual origins. They are not discovered in the nature of logic but are constructed from the ethical commitments of specific historical communities. They are post-contextual ethical maxims—prescriptions for belief that claim universal validity but are rooted in particular values.
