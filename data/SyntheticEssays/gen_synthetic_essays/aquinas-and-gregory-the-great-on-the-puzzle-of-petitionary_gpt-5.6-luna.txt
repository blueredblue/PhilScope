# Petitionary Prayer, Desert, and the Problem of Divine Foreordination

Petitionary prayer appears to occupy an unstable position in a theistic universe. A petitioner asks God for some good: healing, protection, forgiveness, success, consolation, or assistance for another person. Yet classical theism commonly attributes to God perfect knowledge, perfect goodness, and an eternal providential plan. If God already knows what will happen and has eternally disposed which goods to give, petitionary prayer cannot inform God of anything, alter his settled intentions, or persuade him to become more benevolent. The practice can therefore seem pointless. Why ask for what God will give anyway, or for what he will not give anyway?

Scott Hill’s desertist solution attempts to preserve the significance of petitionary prayer without denying divine foreknowledge or eternal providence. Drawing on ideas associated with Aquinas and Gregory the Great, Hill proposes that respectful and non-demanding petitionary prayer can generate a small amount of prima facie desert. God may eternally dispose to give a good on the condition that a person pray for it in an appropriate way. The prayer does not change God’s eternal will; rather, it is part of the eternally intended order through which the good is given. On this view, prayer matters because it can make the petitioner, or perhaps the beneficiary, more deserving of the good.

The proposal is philosophically attractive. It offers petitionary prayer a genuine causal and normative role while preserving a strong conception of providence. It also accords with an important intuition about prayer: the manner in which one asks matters. A humble request seems spiritually and morally different from a demand, an attempt at manipulation, or an assumption that one is entitled to divine assistance. Nevertheless, the desertist account faces a serious challenge. Is effort really a valid basis of desert, especially when the effort itself depends on unearned circumstances such as upbringing, religious formation, temperament, opportunity, or divine grace? Rawlsian arguments appear to threaten the notion that people deserve outcomes in virtue of traits or efforts for which they cannot ultimately take credit.

This essay argues that the desertist framework succeeds in showing that petitionary prayer need not be pointless, but it does not fully rescue prayer from dependence on controversial metaphysical and theological premises. Its strongest achievement is conditional: if one accepts a form of agent-relative desert, a distinction between strict and prima facie desert, and a providential theory according to which God eternally ordains goods partly through human responses, then petitionary prayer has a coherent and nontrivial point. But the framework does not establish these assumptions independently. In particular, it must explain why prayerful effort is morally relevant when the capacity and circumstances for prayer are themselves unearned. A modest, non-demanding desert may survive the Rawlsian objection, but only by becoming closer to a fittingness or response-dependent reason than to ordinary distributive desert. The view therefore offers a plausible theodicy of prayer within classical theism, but not a neutral philosophical solution available without controversial theological commitments.

## I. The Puzzle of Petitionary Prayer

The puzzle begins with a tension among three claims.

First, God is omniscient. God knows before the petitioner prays what the petitioner will request, and God knows whether the requested good will be given.

Second, God is providential. The goods and events of the world are not random but belong to an eternal divine order. On a strong Thomistic view, God’s willing is not a temporal process in which new information or changed feelings produce new decisions. God does not deliberate, discover, or revise. The divine plan is eternal and immutable.

Third, petitionary prayer purports to ask God to do something. Its ordinary grammar is practical and interpersonal: “Please heal her,” “Please help me,” “Please protect them,” or “Please forgive me.” The prayer is not merely a meditation on what God will do. It is an address intended to make a difference.

If the divine plan is fixed, however, prayer cannot make God decide differently than he would otherwise have decided. If the petitioner’s prayer is foreknown and included in the plan, then the prayer seems redundant. If the prayer is not included, then it cannot affect the outcome. The dilemma resembles a problem about causal efficacy under determinism. A person may still act as a cause within a determined order, but it is unclear whether petitionary prayer has the kind of efficacy believers ordinarily attribute to it.

Aquinas offers one important response. Divine providence does not eliminate secondary causes; it establishes them. God may ordain not only that a good occur but that it occur through prayer. In that case, prayer is not an attempt to alter God’s will from outside the divine plan. It is one of the means through which the plan is fulfilled. The fact that God eternally knows and wills the prayer does not make the prayer causally irrelevant, any more than the fact that God eternally knows and wills a medical treatment makes the treatment irrelevant to a cure.

This response solves one problem but leaves another. It explains how prayer might be a cause without changing God’s eternal intention. It does not yet explain why prayer should be among the causes God chooses. Nor does it fully explain the normative character of prayer. Why would God make a good conditional upon a request? What is the point of asking if God already intends the request itself as part of the route to the good?

Hill’s desertist proposal supplies an answer. The petitioner’s prayer can be relevant not merely as a causal condition but as a reason for giving the good. Prayer creates a small amount of prima facie desert. The person who asks respectfully and without presumption becomes, in a limited sense, more deserving of divine assistance than the person who does not make that effort. God’s giving the good in response to prayer is therefore not arbitrary. It reflects a normatively intelligible relationship between the petitioner’s conduct and the gift.

The proposal is not that prayer earns divine favor in a strict or commercial sense. Nor is it that a sufficiently eloquent or frequent prayer obligates God. The desert generated by prayer is “minuscule” and “prima facie”: it is a modest consideration that can be outweighed by competing goods, harms, justice-based claims, or the requirements of providence. Prayer does not put God in the petitioner’s debt. It gives God a reason, within the created order, to provide a good that God may already have intended to provide.

## II. Aquinas, Gregory, and the Shape of a Desertist Account

The historical background matters because the proposal depends on a particular synthesis of divine immutability and human participation.

Aquinas insists that prayer does not change the divine will. God’s will is eternal, simple, and unchanging. Yet Aquinas also treats prayer as genuinely useful. Prayer is not useful because it gives God information or causes him to acquire a new intention. It is useful because God wills that certain effects occur through human petitions. Prayer therefore belongs to the order of secondary causation. God causes both the event and the human action through which the event occurs.

Aquinas also associates prayer with merit, though the relation is complex. In the theological framework of merit, human acts can be pleasing to God and can be ordained as conditions of receiving divine goods. But merit is never independent of grace. Human action is possible because God first moves and sustains the creature. Thus, if prayer has merit, it is not a ground of autonomous self-creation or a basis for bargaining with God. It is a creaturely participation in an order whose ultimate source remains divine.

Gregory the Great is relevant for a somewhat different reason. Gregory’s reflections on prayer emphasize that prayer must be morally and spiritually formed. The efficacy of prayer is connected with humility, perseverance, purity of intention, and the transformation of the person who prays. Prayer is not simply the production of verbal requests. It is an act of dependence that can fit the petitioner for receiving what is asked. The prayer’s significance is therefore partly located in the petitioner’s posture toward God.

Hill’s desertist interpretation can draw on both strands. From Aquinas it takes the idea that prayer can be an eternally ordained means to a divinely intended effect. From Gregory it takes the idea that the moral quality of prayer matters: a respectful request is different from a demand, and persevering prayer can display a kind of effort or responsiveness. The resulting picture is not one in which prayer changes God but one in which God has eternally chosen to distribute some goods through practices that make the recipients—or those praying for them—fitting participants in the gift.

The word “desert” must be used carefully. In ordinary moral and political contexts, desert often suggests that a person has a claim to an outcome because of what she has done. If a worker performs exceptionally, she may deserve praise or a bonus. If a criminal commits a serious offense, she may deserve punishment. Such desert is often contrasted with mere entitlement, need, or utility. It is also typically thought to be proportional: greater achievement or fault warrants a greater response.

The desert at issue in petitionary prayer is much weaker. A prayer does not establish a right to success, and it does not imply that those who fail to pray deserve deprivation. Nor does it imply that people who pray are morally superior to people who do not, since failure to pray may result from ignorance, despair, incapacity, or lack of religious belief. Prima facie desert is best understood as a defeasible normative consideration. The prayer makes the gift more fitting in light of the petitioner’s conduct, but it does not settle the matter.

This limited conception is crucial. If prayer generated strict desert, the view would be implausible and spiritually objectionable. It would suggest that divine generosity is a wage paid for correct performance. If prayer generates only a small, defeasible desert, the account can preserve divine freedom and grace. God gives because God chooses to give, but the gift may still be appropriately related to the petitioner’s response.

## III. Why Respectful Prayer Might Generate Desert

The desertist argument depends on the thought that effort can be a basis of desert even when the outcome is not achieved. A person who sincerely and humbly prays has undertaken an activity involving attention, self-restraint, hope, vulnerability, and orientation toward another’s good. These features can make the person worthy of some response.

Consider a human analogy. Suppose someone asks a friend for help in a respectful and non-coercive way. The friend is not obligated to comply, especially if the request concerns a serious matter. But the manner of the request can provide a reason to respond favorably. The requester has not merely demanded a benefit; she has recognized the friend as an agent with authority to decide. Her respectful appeal may make assistance fitting, even if it does not make it owed.

Petitionary prayer can have a similar structure. To ask God rather than command God is to acknowledge dependence and divine sovereignty. The petitioner does not treat the desired good as a possession to which she has an automatic claim. She exposes a need and entrusts its resolution to another. This can be understood as a morally valuable exercise of humility.

The effort involved also matters. Prayer can require a person to turn attention away from distraction, formulate concern, sustain hope, confront fear, and continue asking despite uncertainty. In prayers for others, the petitioner may invest time and emotional energy in someone else’s welfare. Even where prayer does not produce an external result, the act can express solidarity and concern. It is therefore not difficult to see why a deity might regard prayer as a meaningful response to the conditions of creaturely life.

The strongest version of the desertist account does not say that God rewards prayer because God enjoys being praised or because divine vanity demands recognition. It says that prayer is a form of active participation in the good. A petitioner who asks for another’s healing, for example, does not cause the healing independently, but she does place herself in a relation of concern, hope, and willingness. If God gives the healing partly through that prayer, the petitioner is not merely a passive observer of providence. She has participated in the route by which the good arrives.

The notion of effort is especially important here. It allows the view to explain why a fleeting but sincere prayer might have some significance even when it produces no extensive moral transformation. The prayer need not make the petitioner virtuous in a comprehensive sense. It may simply display a small amount of responsiveness. That is why the resulting desert is minuscule. It is proportional to the limited action performed.

Still, the account must distinguish effort from mere exertion. Effort is not automatically meritorious. A person can expend great effort on an unjust or trivial project. What matters is the relation between effort, intention, and object. Respectful petitionary prayer has a plausible desert base because it is directed toward a good, acknowledges the proper authority of God, and does not attempt to coerce the outcome. A manipulative prayer, even if lengthy and emotionally intense, may lack the relevant moral quality.

This distinction also explains the negative condition in Hill’s proposal. Petitionary prayer must be non-demanding. A demand can be linguistically framed as prayer, but it may fail to express the relevant form of humility. If someone treats God as an instrument or assumes that divine assistance is owed, the action may not generate the same modest desert. The proposal thus concerns not prayer as a mechanical verbal performance but prayer as a mode of intentional agency.

## IV. The Rawlsian Challenge to Desert Bases

The main philosophical difficulty concerns whether effort can ground desert at all. Rawls famously argues that moral desert should not be based on factors for which people are not responsible, including native endowments and favorable social circumstances. A talented person does not deserve greater income merely because she was born with unusual abilities. Nor does someone deserve less merely because she was born into poverty or lacked educational opportunity. Since natural talents and social starting points are morally arbitrary, they should not determine distributive shares simply through the language of desert.

A Rawlsian argument can be directed at prayer in two ways.

First, the capacity to pray may itself depend on unearned circumstances. Some people are raised in religious communities that teach petitionary prayer. Others are exposed to traditions that discourage it or to secular environments in which it never occurs to them. Some have temperaments disposed toward trust and hope; others have psychological conditions that make sustained prayer difficult. If desert depends on prayer, then it appears to depend on religious upbringing, cognitive disposition, emotional stability, and social opportunity—precisely the kinds of arbitrary facts Rawls warns against.

Second, even the effort involved in prayer may not be ultimately attributable to the petitioner. A person’s ability to concentrate, care for others, resist despair, and form intentions is shaped by genes, education, relationships, and prior experiences. If all these conditions are unearned, then the effort cannot generate genuine desert. The person who prays successfully may be fortunate, not deserving.

This objection is powerful because it targets the desert base rather than merely the proportionality of the reward. It is not enough to say that prayer involves effort. The relevant question is whether the effort is attributable to the agent in a way that can justify a desert claim. If the agent’s effort is the product of factors she did not choose, why should God give her more than someone who lacked those factors?

The objection can be sharpened by considering prayer for others. Suppose two people suffer equally. One is prayed for by a community with a strong religious culture; the other is not. If the first receives healing because the prayer generates desert, the distribution appears to track the luck of being surrounded by people who pray. That may be a coherent divine policy, but it is not obviously just. The beneficiary may have done nothing to deserve the prayer, and the petitioner’s capacity to pray may be socially inherited.

A further problem arises if divine grace is included in the explanation. Classical theism often maintains that faith, hope, and charity are themselves gifts of grace. If God gives the grace that enables prayer, then God seems to be rewarding a gift God has already supplied. The desertist framework might appear circular: God gives the petitioner the capacity to pray, the petitioner prays through that capacity, and God then gives a further good because of the prayer. Why should this be called desert rather than divine generosity all the way through?

## V. Responses to the Rawlsian Objection

There are several possible responses, but none is cost-free.

The first response is to distinguish distributive desert from interpersonal or participatory desert. Rawls’s argument is directed primarily at the distribution of social primary goods. It says that natural talents and social circumstances should not determine who receives income, political power, or basic opportunities. The desert involved in prayer need not be a principle for distributing social goods. It may instead concern the fittingness of a personal response within a relationship. God can have reason to respond to a person’s act without treating that act as a basis for allocating shares in a public distributive scheme.

This distinction has force. Not every reason for praise, gratitude, or favor is a distributive principle. If a friend helps someone partly because that person asked with unusual honesty, the friend need not be endorsing a general theory that those with better communication skills deserve more assistance. Similarly, God may take prayer into account without making prayer a criterion of basic worth.

However, the distinction does not completely remove the concern. The good requested in prayer is often precisely a distributive good: health, safety, success, or rescue. If God heals one person because others prayed and does not heal another because no one prayed, the outcome still appears to be distributed partly according to unearned circumstances. Calling the reason “interpersonal” rather than “distributive” does not eliminate the unequal effects.

A second response is to adopt a compatibilist account of responsibility. An action can be attributable to an agent even if it has sufficient causal conditions in prior factors. The petitioner need not be causa sui, creating herself from nothing, in order for her prayer to be hers. If she understands what she is doing, acts from her own reasons, and is not externally compelled, the prayer may be attributable to her. Desert can therefore be grounded in responsible agency without requiring ultimate responsibility for one’s character and circumstances.

This response challenges the Rawlsian inference from unchosen conditions to the absence of all desert. We routinely distinguish between someone who voluntarily helps another and someone who is physically forced to do so, even though both actions have causal histories. A prayer that expresses the petitioner’s own concern can be meaningfully hers even if her ability to pray was shaped by factors beyond her control.

Yet Rawlsian thinking may resist this move because it questions the moral relevance of precisely those capacities that constitute agency. The distinction between voluntary and coerced action may be sufficient for praise and blame, but not for determining who deserves scarce goods. A compatibilist account can establish responsibility in a weak sense without establishing desert in a strong sense. The desertist must therefore explain why attributable effort warrants divine favor rather than merely making the action intelligible as an expression of the person.

A third response is to reduce the strength of the desert claim. The petitioner does not strictly deserve the good. Prayer generates only prima facie desert, a small reason that can be defeated by other considerations. This makes the view less vulnerable to the charge that unearned circumstances are determining outcomes. If prayer contributes only a minuscule amount, then it need not override need, justice, or the interests of others.

This is perhaps the most plausible response. Moral life contains many weak reasons grounded in conduct that are not absolute claims. A person may deserve a little gratitude for a small kindness even though her kindness was enabled by good upbringing. We need not infer that such gratitude is unjust merely because the benefactor did not choose her character. Likewise, prayer may provide a modest reason for giving without implying that the non-praying person deserves deprivation.

But the reduction in strength threatens the explanatory ambition of the theory. If the desert is minuscule, it may not explain why prayer is necessary or why God should condition major goods upon it. The account could say that prayer has some tiny normative relevance, but that may be insufficient to rescue the ordinary significance of petitionary prayer. A prayer that is only one negligible factor among countless providential considerations may still seem practically pointless to the petitioner.

The fourth response is theological: God’s grace can transform otherwise inadequate human actions into genuine participation in divine goodness. On this view, the petitioner does not deserve the good independently. God graciously chooses to regard the prayer as meritorious and to make it a condition of giving. The prayer’s value derives from its relation to divine initiative.

This response fits Aquinas well, but it is explicitly theological. It does not answer the Rawlsian objection on secular terms; it changes the framework in which desert is understood. If God is the source of both the capacity to pray and the good received, then divine generosity can underwrite the relation between prayer and gift. The petitioner’s prayer remains a real act, but its desert is derivative and participatory rather than self-grounding.

That answer may be entirely appropriate within Christian theology. It is less satisfactory if the aim is to show that desert provides an independently compelling solution to the philosophical puzzle. The theory then depends on assumptions about grace, divine authorship, and the legitimacy of God’s assigning normative significance to creaturely effort.

## VI. Does Prayer Really Deserve a Response?

Another objection concerns the alleged moral value of respectful petition itself. Is asking for a good really an achievement? A petitioner may spend only a few seconds uttering words. She may not sacrifice anything significant, change her character, or benefit anyone. If mere asking generates desert, the view risks making rewards depend on a low-cost ritual.

The desertist can reply that cost is not the only measure of desert. A brief act can be morally meaningful if it expresses a significant intention. A person who apologizes sincerely may perform a valuable act even if the words take seconds to say. A person who asks for help may display vulnerability and trust that are not reducible to physical exertion. Prayer’s moral significance lies in its intentional structure, not simply in its duration.

Still, this response suggests that the relevant base may be sincerity, concern, humility, or solidarity rather than prayer as such. If a nonreligious person silently hopes for another’s recovery, or takes practical action on the person’s behalf, why should a verbal petition to God generate a distinct desert? The account must either identify a special value in relating oneself to God or treat petitionary prayer as one instance of a broader category of morally responsive action.

The broader category may be preferable. Prayer can be seen as a form of effortful concern directed toward a good under conditions of dependence. Its desert base would then be not the ritual performance but the agent’s orientation and participation. This makes the theory less sectarian and avoids the suggestion that God rewards the correct verbal formula. At the same time, it weakens the distinctive role of petitionary prayer. If practical assistance, hope, meditation, and prayer all generate similar modest reasons, prayer is no longer uniquely necessary to divine action.

A related concern is that respectful prayer may be valuable primarily because it transforms the petitioner, not because it makes the petitioner deserve an external good. Gregory’s emphasis on prayer’s formative effects can be interpreted in this way. Prayer teaches humility, disciplines desire, and aligns the person with the good. It may therefore be worthwhile even if it has no influence on whether God grants the requested outcome.

This alternative preserves the point of prayer but abandons the desertist solution. It says prayer is not pointless because it changes the petitioner, expresses love, sustains community, or makes action possible. But it does not explain why God would give a requested good because of prayer. Hill’s view is more ambitious: it seeks to retain an objective relation between prayer and divine giving. The cost is that it must defend a desert base whose moral status is contestable.

## VII. Eternal Disposition and the Meaning of Conditional Gifts

The desertist framework is most coherent when combined with a Thomistic account of eternal conditionality. God can eternally will: if the petitioner prays respectfully, then the good will be given. The condition is temporal from the creature’s perspective but eternally present to God’s knowledge and will. The prayer does not change the divine plan; it fulfills the condition through which the plan is realized.

This model preserves both divine immutability and human efficacy. The petitioner’s prayer is not an intervention into an already completed causal sequence. It is one of the sequence’s intended causes. The eternal plan includes the petitioner’s freedom, effort, and response.

Yet this raises a problem about counterfactual dependence. Suppose the petitioner does not pray. Would God withhold the good? If yes, then the good is conditional upon prayer in a strong sense. The beneficiary’s fate depends on whether someone happens to perform an act that may be socially and psychologically accidental. This seems harsh, especially where the person needing help cannot influence whether others pray.

If no, and God would give the good anyway, then the prayer is not necessary. It may still be a cause or reason, but its practical significance becomes difficult to identify. The desertist needs a middle position: prayer should make a difference, but not in a way that turns God’s response into a crude transaction or makes non-prayer morally blameworthy.

The notion of prima facie desert supplies that middle position. God may give the good in either case, but prayer raises the probability or fittingness of giving it. Alternatively, God may give a different or additional good because of prayer: strength, consolation, spiritual benefit, or assistance to others. This allows prayer to be significant without making every unanswered prayer evidence of failure.

But the more flexible the account becomes, the less determinate it is. If any outcome can be described as the good eternally ordained in response to prayer, the theory risks becoming unfalsifiable. The prayer is said to matter because it is included in the plan, but the plan is inferred from whatever happens. A philosophical account should specify what kind of difference prayer makes and why.

The desertist answer need not offer empirical predictions, since the issue concerns metaphysical and normative explanation. Nevertheless, it should make a conceptual claim: prayer contributes a reason that God can take into account, even when the ultimate outcome is governed by wider providential purposes. The point is not that prayer guarantees its object but that it makes the object more fittingly given. This is a coherent position, though its coherence depends on accepting that God’s distribution of goods can appropriately track the petitioner’s conduct.

## VIII. Does the Framework Rescue Prayer from Pointlessness?

The answer depends on what “pointlessness” means.

If pointlessness means that prayer cannot make a causal difference because God’s will is eternal, the desertist solution succeeds. It shows that eternal providence does not entail causal irrelevance. God can eternally ordain that a good occur through a prayer. The prayer is then a real secondary cause. This is no stranger than the claim that God eternally ordains that a person be healed through medicine, or that a student succeed through study.

If pointlessness means that prayer cannot give God a reason to act, the solution also offers a coherent answer. Respectful petition can generate a small, defeasible desert. God’s response can therefore be intelligible as a fitting response to a creature’s effort, not merely as an arbitrary selection within an inscrutable plan.

If pointlessness means that prayer lacks any independent value because God already knew and intended it, the account again has a response. Foreknowledge does not make an action pointless. What matters is not whether the action surprises God but whether it occupies a real place in the order of causes and reasons. A person’s prayer can matter even if God eternally knows that she will pray.

However, if pointlessness means that petitionary prayer must be rationally warranted without assuming controversial theology, the solution is much less successful. The argument relies on at least four disputable premises.

First, it assumes that prayer can generate desert. This is not a conceptual truth. One may instead believe that God gives from grace, responds to need, or acts for the common good, none of which requires prayer-based desert.

Second, it assumes that effort is a desert base. The Rawlsian objection shows that this is not straightforward, particularly when effort depends on unchosen abilities and circumstances.

Third, it assumes that divine providence includes conditional relations between creaturely action and divine giving. This is a substantive metaphysical thesis, not a conclusion forced by the concept of God.

Fourth, it assumes that God may permissibly distribute goods partly in accordance with prayer, even when prayer is unequally available and the beneficiaries may not control whether it occurs. That assumption requires theological defense concerning grace, justice, and divine purposes.

The framework can therefore rescue prayer from one form of pointlessness only by embedding prayer in a rich theological picture. It does not offer a premise-neutral argument that anyone committed to omniscience and providence must accept.

## IX. A Qualified Defense

Despite these limitations, the desertist account should not be rejected. Its central insight is that petitionary prayer can occupy a morally meaningful position between entitlement and irrelevance. Prayer need not compel God, and it need not change God’s mind, to be a reason within the created order. A humble request can express concern, acknowledge dependence, and constitute an act through which a petitioner participates in a good. These are plausible grounds for a modest divine response.

The Rawlsian challenge should lead to qualification rather than abandonment. Prayer should not be treated as a basis for strict distributive desert. No one should infer that the sick, unprayed-for, or religiously unformed deserve less care. Nor should prayer be regarded as evidence of superior moral worth. The appropriate claim is weaker: given God’s choice to create a providential order involving free and responsive creatures, a sincere prayer can provide a small agent-relative reason for a fitting response.

This revised account treats desert as relational and defeasible. The petitioner’s action is attributable to her in the ordinary sense that it expresses her reasons and concerns, even though it depends on unearned circumstances. The effort does not make her ultimately responsible for deserving the good. Rather, it gives God a reason to honor her participation. The distinction is close to the difference between deserving a reward and being owed a gift. A person may not be owed healing because she prayed, but healing may be more fittingly given through her prayer.

Whether this should still be called desert is partly terminological. Critics may say that the account has abandoned desert and replaced it with fittingness, responsiveness, or grace. That criticism has force. If desert requires an independent claim grounded in responsible achievement, prayer does not plausibly generate it. But if desert includes weaker forms of merit or appropriateness, then “prima facie desert” accurately signals the limited status of the reason.

The theological advantage of the terminology is that it preserves the intuition that prayer matters to God without making divine action contractual. God can freely give a good because the petitioner has made herself a participant in its reception. The gift remains grace because the petitioner did not create the opportunity, the capacity to pray, or the good itself. Yet grace does not exclude all creaturely significance. It can elevate and incorporate human effort into the divine economy.

## Conclusion

Hill’s desertist solution provides an ingenious response to the puzzle of petitionary prayer. Drawing on Aquinas, it understands prayer as an eternally ordained secondary cause rather than an attempt to revise God’s will. Drawing on Gregory the Great, it emphasizes the moral quality of the petitioner’s posture: humility, sincerity, perseverance, and concern can make prayer more than a verbal request. Respectful and non-demanding prayer may generate a minuscule amount of prima facie desert, giving God a reason to provide a good already eternally disposed to be given.

The theory’s central weakness concerns the status of effort as a desert base. Rawlsian arguments rightly warn that natural capacities, religious upbringing, psychological dispositions, and social opportunities are unearned. Since these factors influence who prays and how successfully, prayer cannot serve as a strict basis for distributing goods. A more modest account can respond by distinguishing interpersonal from distributive reasons, adopting a compatibilist notion of attribution, and emphasizing that prayer generates only defeasible desert. But these moves do not eliminate the underlying dependence on theological assumptions about grace, providence, and divine freedom.

The best conclusion is therefore qualified. The desertist framework does rescue petitionary prayer from pointlessness within a Thomistic-Christian metaphysics. It explains how prayer can be causally efficacious without changing God and normatively significant without obligating God. It also captures something important about the practice: asking respectfully can be a form of morally meaningful participation in the good.

It does not, however, rescue petitionary prayer without controversial premises. Its appeal to desert is philosophically unstable if desert is understood as a strong distributive entitlement. The more defensible version treats prayer as generating a weak, relational, and grace-dependent reason for divine response. That may be enough to preserve the intelligibility and value of prayer, but it also reveals that prayer’s ultimate point cannot rest on desert alone. Prayer may matter because it is a cause, an expression of love, a discipline of humility, a transformation of desire, and a mode of participation in providence. The desertist idea illuminates one dimension of that significance, but it should be presented as a theologically embedded interpretation of prayer rather than as a self-sufficient philosophical solution.
