### The Eclipse of the Proposition: Propositional Attitudes, Computational Cognitive Science, and the Neuro-Computational Turn

#### Introduction

The trajectory of cognitive science over the past half-century can be read as a protracted interrogation of the ontological and explanatory status of the propositional attitude. From the vantage of folk psychology, mental life is populated by beliefs, desires, hopes, and fears—states that take propositions as their objects and exhibit semantic evaluability, intentionality, and causal efficacy. In the classical computationalism of the late twentieth century, epitomized by Jerry Fodor’s Language of Thought (LoT) hypothesis, these folk-psychological commitments were not merely tolerated; they were formalized. Fodor argued that genuine causal explanation in cognitive science requires a combinatorial, compositional syntax realized by internal symbols operating under syntactically governed causal laws. 

Yet, the hegemony of the classical paradigm has been severely contested by the rise of artificial neural networks (ANNs) and sub-symbolic processing. Modern connectionist and deep learning architectures model representation not through discrete, language-like symbols manipulated by rules, but through distributed representations in high-dimensional activation spaces transformed via continuous gradient descent. This paradigm shift exposes a profound dialectical tension. If cognitive capacities are successfully modeled, predicted, and explained by sub-symbolic dynamics that bypass propositional structures altogether, what explanatory work do propositional attitudes actually perform? Do they designate genuine causal-explanatory entities, or do they merely track an abstract, macroscopic level of behavioral regularity that systematically obscures the micro-structural neuro-computational mechanisms of cognition?

This essay evaluates the claim that propositional attitudes fail to serve as adequate causal-explanatory entities in computational cognitive science. It examines the classical defense of propositional realism anchored in Fodor’s LoT hypothesis, juxtaposes this with the neuro-computational alternative of distributed representations and dynamic systems, and analyzes the broader philosophical implications for folk psychology. I will argue that while propositional attitudes retain an indispensable heuristic utility for macroscopic behavioral prediction, they do not function as fundamental causal entities within a rigorous computational ontology. Consequently, while a hard eliminative reductionism may be overly abrupt regarding our socio-linguistic practices, cognitive science requires an explanatory framework grounded in non-propositional, high-dimensional dynamics to secure genuine causal-mechanistic adequacy.

#### Section I: The Classical Citadel – Fodor, Productivity, and the Language of Thought

To appreciate the stakes of the contemporary challenge to propositional attitudes, one must first reconstruct the fortress erected by Jerry Fodor to defend them. In his foundational works, particularly *The Language of Thought* (1975) and *Psychosemantics* (1987), Fodor set out to rescue folk psychology from the threat of elimination by offering a rigorous computational vindication of intentional states. The core of Fodor’s project rests on a dilemma for any non-representational psychology: cognitive science must account for the systematicity, productivity, and compositional semantics of thought, and it must do so in a way that preserves the causal efficacy of mental contents without violating physicalism.

Fodor argued that these cognitive phenomena demand a representational theory of mind (RTM) coupled with a specific architectural hypothesis: the Language of Thought. According to Fodor, thinking is computation, and computation is the mechanical manipulation of physical symbols based on their syntax. A propositional attitude—such as the belief that *John loves Mary*—is analyzed as a computational relation ($R$) between a subject and an internal mental representation (a sentence in Mentalese, say, $\text{LOVES}(\text{JOHN}, \text{MARY})$). 

Crucially, Fodor maintained that these mental representations must possess two defining features to underwrite psychological explanation:
1. **Compositionality:** The meaning of a complex mental representation is a function of the meanings of its primitive constituents and the syntactic rules governing their combination.
2. **Productivity and Systematicity:** A cognitive system can entertain an infinite (or indefinitely large) number of novel thoughts because it can recombine a finite stock of primitive symbols (Productivity); moreover, the capacity to entertain the thought that *P and Q* invariably entails the capacity to entertain the thought that *Q and P* (Systematicity).

For Fodor, associative networks and early connectionist models were fundamentally inadequate because they treated mental states as unstructured nodes. Without internal syntactic and semantic structure, an associative network cannot explain why cognitive capacities are systematically interconnected. 

Furthermore, Fodor insisted on a strict methodological and ontological principle: for propositional attitudes to figure in causal explanations, they must satisfy the criteria of **intentional realism**. Causal interactions among mental states must mirror their semantic relations. If a mental state causes another because of its semantic content (e.g., my desire for water causing me to walk to the tap), the physical realization of that state must respect its compositional structure. Fodor’s famous "Methodological Solipsism" and his arguments against radical holism were designed to protect this framework: causal laws in psychology must operate over the syntactic properties of symbols, which locally determine their semantic interpretations.

In this classical view, propositional attitudes are not loose folk-psychological fictions; they are bona fide causal-explanatory nodes. They participate in computational mechanisms that exhibit law-like regularity, bridging the gap between physical neurobiology and overt behavior. To abandon the propositional attitude, in Fodor’s estimation, is to abandon the possibility of a cognitive science altogether, reducing psychology to either neuro-physiological micro-reductionism or behavioristic black-box correlations.

#### Section II: The Sub-Symbolic Revolt – Distributed Representations and Gradient Descent

The classical citadel, however, was soon besieged from within computational cognitive science itself. The resurgence of connectionism in the 1980s (Rumelhart, McClelland, and the PDP Research Group) and the subsequent explosion of deep learning architectures in the 21st century have introduced an alternative paradigm of computation that fundamentally challenges the necessity—and the coherence—of the Language of Thought.

Modern artificial neural networks (ANNs) process information not by manipulating discrete, concatenated symbols via syntactical rules, but by propagating continuous vectors of activation across weighted connections. Within this sub-symbolic framework, the very architecture of representation is transformed:

1. **Distributed Representations:** Rather than storing a concept (e.g., *dog*) as a discrete symbolic token in a localist register, ANNs represent concepts as patterns of activation across high-dimensional vectors. Each dimension in the vector space corresponds to a latent feature or statistical regularity extracted from the training data. A single unit participates in the representation of multiple concepts, and a single concept is distributed across multiple units.
2. **Continuous Dynamics and Gradient Descent:** Learning in ANNs is not the application of symbolic rules of inference, but the iterative optimization of connection weights via backpropagation and gradient descent. The network minimizes an error function over a high-dimensional loss landscape, shifting from a state of high error to a local or global minimum.
3. **Implicit Compositionality:** Connectionists argue that systematicity and productivity do not require an explicit, language-like syntax. Networks can develop compositional generalization capacities implicitly through structural alignment in geometric spaces. Vector spaces allow for algebraic operations—such as vector addition, subtraction, and analogical projections (e.g., $\text{vector}(\text{king}) - \text{vector}(\text{man}) + \text{vector}(\text{woman}) \approx \text{vector}(\text{queen})$)—that capture semantic relations without recourse to Mentalese sentences.

This neuro-computational perspective exposes a profound explanatory friction with Fodorian realism. If an ANN can master natural language syntax, perform complex reasoning, translate languages, and generate novel inferences without possessing internal symbol structures that correspond to propositions, what is the theoretical justification for positing propositional attitudes as causal engines?

Critics of classical computationalism argue that propositional attitudes are merely macroscopic descriptive projections. When we attribute a belief to a human or an advanced language model, we are engaging in an interpretive stance (in Daniel Dennett’s sense) that tracks behavioral regularities. But structurally, the actual computational engine driving the output is a set of differential equations governing activation vectors across millions of parameters. To claim that a specific weight matrix *causes* behavior *qua* propositional attitude is, from the connectionist view, a category mistake. It treats an emergent, high-level geometric trajectory through a state space as a discrete symbolic actor.

#### Section III: The Dialectical Tension – Folk Psychological Realism vs. Neuro-Computational Dynamics

At the heart of contemporary cognitive science lies a deep dialectical tension between **folk psychological realism** and **neuro-computational dynamism**. This tension can be unpacked across three distinct dimensions: the nature of mental representation, the character of psychological laws, and the criteria for causal-explanatory adequacy.

##### 1. Representation: Local vs. Distributed, Discrete vs. Continuous
Fodorian realism demands that mental representations possess constituent structure. A propositional attitude requires a subject, a propositional operator, and a structured object. The constituents must be sharply demarcated so that operations can apply to them selectively (e.g., negating a clause or substituting a term). 

Conversely, high-dimensional activation spaces are fundamentally non-compositional in the classical sense. While vectors can be combined or superimposed, they do not decompose into neatly concatenable syntactic atoms. Concepts bleed into one another along continuous gradients of similarity. The meaning of a neural network's internal state is holistic, context-dependent, and defined relationally by its position in a multi-dimensional manifold. If mental representation is geometrically continuous and distributed, then the clean, combinatorial architecture demanded by the Language of Thought is empirically unsupported by our most successful computational models.

##### 2. Causation: Syntactic Manipulation vs. Vector Transformations
In classical computationalism, mental causation is semantic-via-syntactic. State $A$ causes State $B$ because the physical realization of State $A$ triggers the physical realization of State $B$ via a causal law sensitive to syntactic form. 

In neuro-computational systems, causation is dynamical and continuous. State transitions are governed by differential equations, activation thresholds, and multiplicative weight matrices. There are no "rules" in the sense of explicit algorithms being executed; there are only convergence dynamics, basins of attraction, and trajectories through phase space. When a deep network outputs a response, it is the cumulative result of non-linear transformations applied to continuous inputs. To isolate a single "belief" as the discrete causal antecedent of this output is to arbitrarily slice a continuous, interdependent activation manifold. The causal work is done by the entire dynamical system, not by discrete propositional tokens.

##### 3. The Normative Dimension: Rationality vs. Statistical Regularity
Folk psychology and classical computationalism are deeply wedded to normative constraints. Propositional attitudes are bound by norms of rationality; we attribute beliefs and desires to agents on the assumption that their internal states cohere logically and instrumentally. 

Neural networks, however, are fundamentally statistical engines. They optimize for predictive accuracy over empirical distributions, often capturing subtle statistical correlations rather than underlying causal mechanisms or logical deductions. While large language models can *mimic* the generation of propositionally structured arguments, their internal mechanics are driven by probability distributions over token sequences, not by adherence to logical rules of inference. This divergence raises a critical question: are human propositional attitudes genuinely governed by rational norms, or are our folk-psychological attributions merely post-hoc rationalizations of underlying sub-symbolic statistical processing?

#### Section IV: Are Propositional Attitudes Merely Abstract Summaries?

To evaluate the claim that propositional attitudes fail as adequate causal-explanatory entities, we must confront the possibility that they occupy a status analogous to centers of gravity in classical mechanics, or temperature in statistical thermodynamics. 

In physics, macroscopic properties like temperature do not exist as fundamental entities at the level of micro-particles; rather, temperature is an emergent statistical property (mean kinetic energy of molecules). While temperature is indispensable for macro-level thermodynamics, it is not listed as a fundamental force in the Standard Model. More importantly, temperature does not possess fundamental causal powers *over and above* the micro-causal interactions of the constituent molecules. It is an explanatory summary, a coarse-grained description of a complex micro-state.

Could propositional attitudes be similarly understood? Paul and Patricia Churchland, pioneers of **eliminative materialism**, argued precisely this. They contended that folk psychology is a stagnant, radically false theoretical framework that will eventually be replaced by neuroscience, much as caloric theory was replaced by thermodynamics or witchcraft by modern psychiatry. From an eliminativist perspective, propositional attitudes fail as causal-explanatory entities because they carve nature at the wrong joints. They are artifacts of folk taxonomy, ill-suited for integration with mature biological and computational sciences.

However, many philosophers and cognitive scientists resist full-blown elimination, opting instead for **functionalism** or **non-reductive physicalism**. Proponents of this view argue that macroscopic explanatory categories can be genuinely causal even if they are multiply realizable and higher-level. Hilary Putnam, Jerry Fodor, and later Tyler Burge defended the autonomy of special sciences by arguing that higher-level regularities (such as those captured by economic, sociological, or psychological laws) track real causal patterns that would be rendered invisible at the micro-level.

Yet, when applied to computational cognitive science, this defense of higher-level realism faces a severe challenge. If the lower-level computational models (ANNs, dynamical systems) are already providing powerful, predictive, and mechanistic explanations of cognitive phenomena *without* utilizing propositional structures, the functionalist justification for preserving propositional attitudes weakens. 

Consider how an ANN explains generalization, categorization, or language processing. It does so by mapping the topology of activation spaces, identifying cluster centroids, and measuring geometric distances. These models achieve high predictive success without ever computing whether an internal state satisfies the truth conditions of a proposition. If the science itself progresses by subverting propositional ontology in favor of continuous, sub-symbolic dynamics, then insisting on the causal efficacy of propositional attitudes looks increasingly like an ungrounded defense of folk-psychological chauvinism.

#### Section V: The Eliminative Imperative – Grounding Cognitive Science in Non-Propositional Dynamics

We must now address the final evaluative question: Is an eliminative program required to ground cognitive science in non-propositional dynamics?

To answer this affirmatively is to endorse the view that cognitive science must shed its folk-psychological baggage entirely to mature as a natural science. The eliminative imperative asserts that as neuro-computational models achieve greater fidelity to biological neural networks and demonstrate superior explanatory scope, the vocabulary of beliefs, desires, and propositional attitudes must be phased out of scientific discourse.

There are compelling methodological reasons to favor this eliminative direction:
1. **Mechanistic Adequacy:** Modern neuroscience operates at the level of synaptic plasticity, ion channels, oscillatory rhythms, and distributed network dynamics. None of these biological mechanisms bear any structural resemblance to symbolic sentences in a Language of Thought. If cognitive science is to achieve vertical integration with neuroscience, its computational constructs must be topologically and dynamically compatible with neural tissue. High-dimensional vector spaces and gradient descent algorithms can be mapped onto neural populations with far greater ease than symbolic string-manipulation rules.
2. **Eliminating Semantic Intractability:** The classical framework struggles deeply with the symbol grounding problem—explaining how purely syntactic, abstract symbols in Mentalese acquire determinate semantic reference to the external world. Connectionist and embodied cognitive science bypass this intractable philosophical hurdle by grounding representations directly in sensorimotor interactions, environmental contingencies, and continuous vector trajectories.
3. **Accounting for Sub-Cognitive Phenomena:** Much of human cognition—perception, motor control, implicit learning, emotional regulation—is fundamentally non-propositional, fast, and sub-symbolic. Classical computationalism was forced to over-intellectualize these domains by attempting to force them into propositional, rule-governed formats. A non-propositonal dynamic framework absorbs these phenomena naturally, treating explicit propositional reasoning not as the foundational architecture of the mind, but as a late-arriving, culturally scaffolded, and highly specialized mode of output.

However, an eliminative program faces significant counter-arguments, particularly concerning the pragmatics of scientific explanation and the irreducibility of intentionality.

First, **instrumental utility and predictive power** must not be lightly dismissed. Even if propositional attitudes do not correspond to discrete, fundamental causal mechanisms in the brain, they provide an indispensable matrix for social prediction, ethical evaluation, and macro-behavioral coordination. The intentional stance is so deeply wired into human social cognition that abandoning it may be practically impossible, even if scientifically revisionary.

Second, the **phenomenology of conscious thought** often presents itself as propositionally structured. When we deliberate, weigh arguments, or formulate plans, we experience our thoughts as having determinate contents and logical relations. While introspection is notoriously unreliable as a guide to micro-mechanisms, any comprehensive cognitive science must account for *why* our sub-symbolic, high-dimensional activation spaces manifest phenomenologically as discrete propositional episodes.

Third, one might argue for a **pluralistic, stratified ontology**. Rather than enforcing a ruthless eliminativism, cognitive science may operate with multiple levels of abstraction. Just as biology utilizes population genetics alongside molecular biology, cognitive science might utilize intentional-level folk psychology for macro-social interaction, functional connectionism for algorithmic modeling, and neurodynamics for physical implementation. On this pluralist view, propositional attitudes do not fail as causal-explanatory entities; rather, they operate at an abstract level of description that is pragmatically and methodologically insulated from—yet consistent with—underlying neuro-computational dynamics.

---

#### Conclusion

The claim that propositional attitudes fail to serve as adequate causal-explanatory entities in computational cognitive science captures a profound shift in our understanding of the mind. Jerry Fodor’s Language of Thought hypothesis attempted to secure the reality of propositional attitudes by anchoring them in a classical, combinatorial, rule-governed symbolic architecture. Yet, the ascendancy of artificial neural networks, distributed representations, and continuous gradient descent has demonstrated that sophisticated cognitive capacities can be modeled—and successfully explained—without invoking symbolic syntax or propositional structures.

As abstract summaries of complex behavioral regularities, propositional attitudes retain a powerful heuristic and predictive role in human social life. But when evaluated as rigorous causal-explanatory entities within a mature computational ontology, they buckle under the weight of neuro-computational reality. The continuous, high-dimensional, and statistically driven dynamics of modern connectionist models reveal that discrete propositional attitudes systematically obscure the underlying micro-structural mechanisms of cognition.

Whether this realization demands a radical eliminative program depends on one's philosophy of science. If cognitive science insists on strict mechanistic reduction and vertical integration with neuroscience, then the propositional attitude must indeed be eliminated as a fundamental explanatory unit, relegated to the status of a folk-psychological macro-projection. If, however, one adopts a pluralistic functionalism, propositional attitudes may be preserved as higher-level abstractions. Nevertheless, the dialectical momentum firmly favors the non-propositional perspective: the future of computational cognitive science lies not in the manipulation of sentences in Mentalese, but in the geometry of high-dimensional activation spaces and the fluid, continuous dynamics of the sub-symbolic mind.
