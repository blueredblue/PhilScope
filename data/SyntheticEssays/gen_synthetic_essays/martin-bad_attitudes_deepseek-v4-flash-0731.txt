## The Ghost in the Activation Space: Propositional Attitudes, Causal Efficacy, and the Architecture of Mind

**Introduction**

The cognitive revolution of the mid-twentieth century promised to rescue the mind from the black box of behaviorism by reinstating internal mental states as legitimate objects of scientific inquiry. Central to this project was the conviction that cognition is a form of computation, and that our everyday psychological vocabulary—beliefs, desires, intentions—refers to real, causally efficacious states. Jerry Fodor’s Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis stands as the most rigorous and influential articulation of this conviction, positing a discrete, syntactically structured system of mental symbols whose manipulation constitutes thought. For Fodor, propositional attitudes are relations to sentences in this internal language, and their causal role is secured by the formal, syntactic properties of these sentences. However, the subsequent rise of artificial neural networks (ANNs) and connectionist architectures has presented a formidable challenge to this paradigm. ANNs model cognition not through the manipulation of discrete symbols, but through the continuous, high-dimensional dynamics of activation patterns and the statistical optimization of connection weights via gradient descent. This essay evaluates the claim that propositional attitudes fail to serve as adequate causal-explanatory entities within this computational cognitive science. I will argue that while Fodor’s LOT provides a logically coherent and intuitively appealing model of cognitive architecture, it ultimately fails to offer a *causally specific* account of neurobiological processes. Propositional attitudes, as posited by folk psychological realism, operate at a level of abstraction that obscures the proximate mechanisms of cognition. Yet, I will contend that the eliminative program—which seeks to banish propositional attitudes from scientific ontology entirely—is too strong. The correct resolution lies not in elimination, but in a nuanced form of non-eliminative reductionism, where propositional attitudes are understood as real, high-level patterns that supervene on, and are causally grounded in, the non-propositional dynamics of neural networks, without possessing autonomous causal powers of their own.

**Section 1: The Language of Thought and the Promise of Causal Syntax**

Jerry Fodor’s Language of Thought hypothesis, first systematically presented in *The Language of Thought* (1975) and refined in *Psychosemantics* (1987), is the philosophical backbone of classical cognitive science. Its core claim is that mental representations are symbol structures—complex entities composed of primitive symbols combined according to syntactic rules. These symbols have both semantic properties (they are about things) and syntactic properties (they have formal, structural features). The crucial move for Fodor is to argue that mental processes are computational processes, which are defined over the *syntactic* properties of these symbols. In a computer, for example, a program does not "understand" the semantics of the data it manipulates; it merely shuffles bits according to formal rules. Similarly, the mind, according to Fodor, manipulates mental symbols according to their formal, syntactic features. The causal efficacy of thought is thus secured: a belief causes a desire, or a perception causes a belief, because the syntactic structures of the corresponding mental sentences stand in the right formal relations to trigger the next computational step.

This architecture provides a powerful solution to the problem of mental causation. Folk psychology posits law-like relations between propositional attitudes—for instance, if an agent desires P and believes that Q is a means to P, then, ceteris paribus, the agent will intend to bring about Q. For these laws to be genuinely causal, the mental states involved must be able to enter into causal interactions. Fodor’s formalism allows this. The syntax of the mental representation "I desire water" and the syntax of "Drinking from the tap leads to water" can be computationally combined to produce the intention "I will drink from the tap." The causal chain is mediated by the physical instantiation of these syntactic symbols in the brain, but the explanatory level is the symbolic level.

Furthermore, LOT explains two hallmark features of human cognition: systematicity and productivity. Systematicity refers to the fact that if a cognitive system can think "John loves Mary," it can also think "Mary loves John." This is because the symbols "John," "Mary," and "loves" can be recombined in new syntactic configurations. Productivity refers to the ability to generate an infinite number of novel thoughts from a finite set of symbols and rules. These features are deeply rooted in the compositional structure of language, and LOT provides a natural account of them. If thought is a language, then systematicity and productivity are simply consequences of its syntax.

The appeal of this view is immense. It vindicates folk psychological realism, suggesting that our everyday "belief-desire" explanations are not just useful fictions but are tracking real, causally active mental states. It offers a clean, mechanistic account of rationality, where logical inference is mirrored by computational transitions. However, the very strength of LOT—its abstract, formal character—also exposes its fundamental weakness when confronted with the actual physical substrate of cognition. Fodor is famously a multiple realizability theorist: the same mental state (e.g., the belief that snow is white) can be instantiated in a human brain, a silicon chip, or a Martian's hydraulics, provided they all implement the right syntactic operations. This is a virtue for explaining how cognition could be implemented in diverse hardware. But it also means that the causal story told by LOT is *hardware-independent*. It describes the causal structure of the *algorithm*, but it does not tell us how that algorithm is physically realized. When we ask *how* the brain actually causally produces a thought, LOT remains silent. It merely asserts that there *must be* a physical implementation of the syntax, but it does not identify what that implementation is. This gap becomes critically problematic when we consider an alternative cognitive architecture that does not use discrete symbols at all.

**Section 2: The Neuro-Computational Alternative: High-Dimensional Dynamics and Gradient Descent**

The connectionist revolution, culminating in the development of parallel distributed processing (PDP) models in the 1980s, offered a radically different vision of cognitive architecture. Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are composed of numerous simple, neuron-like units (nodes) connected by weighted links. Knowledge is not stored in discrete symbols or rules but is distributed across the entire pattern of connection weights. A representation is not a single symbolic token but a *pattern of activation* across a population of units—a high-dimensional vector in an activation space.

In this framework, cognition is not the manipulation of symbols but the transformation of activation patterns. Input patterns propagate through the network, being transformed by the weights and activation functions at each layer, until an output pattern is produced. The causal story here is entirely continuous and quantitative. There are no discrete steps, no syntactic tokens being shuffled. Instead, there are shifts in the geometry of a high-dimensional state space. A "belief" is not a sentence but a region or trajectory in this space. The similarity between concepts is determined by their distance in this space, not by shared syntactic primitives. For example, the network's representation of "dog" might share many overlapping features with "cat," leading to a shorter distance between their activation vectors.

The learning process in ANNs is equally non-symbolic. Through a process known as gradient descent and backpropagation, the network adjusts its connection weights to minimize the error between its predicted output and the target output. This is a statistical optimization procedure, guided by the mathematical gradient of the error function. The network does not "learn a rule" in the sense of encoding a proposition; it adjusts a continuous landscape of weights to approximate a function. This process is fundamentally different from the algorithmic application of syntactic rules. It is a process of continuous adjustment, driven by quantitative feedback, not by formal manipulation.

This architecture presents a profound challenge to the LOT hypothesis. Where are the symbols? Where is the syntax? In a standard ANN, there is no obvious way to identify discrete, recombinable constituents. The representation of "John loves Mary" is not composed of separate "John," "loves," and "Mary" tokens that can be recombined to form "Mary loves John." Instead, it is a distributed pattern of activations that is context-dependent and holistic. While some connectionist models attempt to incorporate localist representations or compositional structures (e.g., Smolensky's tensor product representations), the core paradigm of deep learning relies on distributed, non-symbolic representations. The causal efficacy of cognition in an ANN is entirely located in the specific, quantitative interactions of the nodes and weights. There is no "ghost in the machine" of a symbolic algorithm running on top of the neural hardware; the neural dynamics *are* the cognition.

**Section 3: The Dialectical Tension: Folk Psychological Laws vs. Neuro-Computational Mechanisms**

The tension between these two frameworks is not merely a technical disagreement about how to build a computer model; it is a deep philosophical conflict about the nature of mental causation. Folk psychology, which LOT seeks to vindicate, posits law-like, intentional relations between propositional attitudes. These laws are *semantic*—they operate on the content of the attitudes. For example, "If someone believes that P and knows that if P then Q, they will infer Q." This law works because of the logical relation between the contents P and Q. The neuro-computational perspective, by contrast, posits causal relations that are entirely *mechanical* and *non-semantic*. In an ANN, a unit fires because its weighted input exceeds a threshold. The cause is a specific quantitative summation of activations, not the semantic content of a proposition. The causal power is in the physics of the network, not in the meaning of the representation.

Fodor attempts to bridge this gap through his "formality condition": mental processes are sensitive only to the syntactic properties of representations, and syntax is a formal property that can be physically implemented. In a classical computer, the semantic content of a program is causally irrelevant; only the syntax matters. Fodor extends this to the mind: the brain manipulates symbols based on their shape (syntax), and this manipulation happens to mirror the semantic relations that folk psychology describes. This is a clever solution, but it relies on the existence of a physical system that implements discrete syntactic operations. In an ANN, there is no such system. The network's state space is continuous, and its transitions are governed by differential equations, not by rules for rewriting symbolic strings. There is no "syntax" in the classical sense, and therefore the formality condition cannot be satisfied.

This leads to a serious problem of causal relevance. If the brain is a neural network, then the proximate causes of behavior are the specific activation patterns and weight adjustments. A propositional attitude, such as "the belief that it is raining," if it exists at all, would be a high-level description of a complex, distributed pattern of activations. The question is: does this high-level description have any causal power? If we accept a principle of causal closure of the physical domain—that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause—then the activation pattern is the cause of the behavior. The propositional attitude, as a description of that pattern, does not add any causal force. It is, at best, an epiphenomenon, or at worst, a mere abstraction. Fodor's LOT avoids this problem by positing that the symbolic level is *causally autonomous*. The symbols themselves are the causal entities, and their physical realization is just the vehicle. But in an ANN, the vehicle is the entire story. There is no autonomous symbolic level operating independently of the activation dynamics. The causal story is told entirely in terms of the continuous, quantitative changes in the network.

**Section 4: The Level of Generality and the Obscuring of Neurobiological Mechanisms**

The claim that propositional attitudes operate merely at an abstract level that obscures underlying mechanisms is a serious charge. To evaluate it, we must distinguish between a legitimate scientific abstraction and an obscurantist reification. Consider the concept of "temperature." Temperature is a high-level abstraction that describes the average kinetic energy of molecules. It does not obscure the underlying mechanics; rather, it provides a useful summary of a vast number of microphysical states. Similarly, a propositional attitude like "believing it is raining" could be seen as a high-level description of a specific pattern of neural activity. This description is useful for predicting behavior and for communicating about mental states, but it does not tell us *how* the brain produces the belief. It abstracts away from the specific neurobiological details.

However, the charge of obscuring mechanisms becomes more damning when we consider the *causal* role of these abstractions. In the case of temperature, we can explain *why* a gas expands by citing its temperature, and this is a legitimate causal explanation because temperature is causally linked to the molecular dynamics. But in the case of propositional attitudes in an ANN, the causal link is severed. The propositional attitude is not a distinct causal entity that interacts with other entities; it is a pattern of activations. If we ask "Why did the agent reach for the umbrella?", a folk psychological answer is "Because she believed it was raining." But the *mechanistic* answer is "Because a specific pattern of activation in her visual and memory systems propagated through her motor cortex." The propositional attitude is a *description* of that pattern, but it is not the *cause* of the pattern. The cause is the pattern itself, or rather, the physical processes that generate that pattern.

Therefore, citing the propositional attitude as the cause of the behavior is akin to citing "the shape of the water" as the cause of a wave's movement. The shape is a property of the wave, but the cause of the movement is the underlying hydrodynamic forces. If we focus solely on the propositional attitude, we obscure the actual causal mechanism—the gradient descent dynamics, the weight adjustments, the continuous activation propagation. This is not to say that the propositional attitude is false or useless; it is to say that it is not a *causal-explanatory entity* in the same way that a symbol in LOT is supposed to be. It lacks the causal autonomy that Fodor's syntax provides. It is a high-level, macroscopic pattern, and its explanatory power is limited to prediction and generalization, not to the provision of proximate causal mechanisms.

**Section 5: Is Eliminativism Required? The Case Against and For a Non-Eliminative Reduction**

If propositional attitudes fail to be causally autonomous, and if they obscure the true neuro-computational mechanisms, does it follow that we must eliminate them from our scientific ontology? This is the radical conclusion drawn by eliminative materialists like Paul and Patricia Churchland. They argue that folk psychology is a false and outdated theory, akin to phlogiston or caloric. Just as chemistry replaced phlogiston with oxygen, neuroscience will replace beliefs and desires with activation vectors and weight matrices. The concepts of propositional attitudes, they claim, will be as obsolete as the concept of a witches' brew.

The eliminativist argument is powerful, but it is ultimately too hasty. It rests on a mistaken assumption that the only legitimate scientific entities are those that appear in the fundamental laws of physics or the proximate mechanisms of a system. However, science is replete with high-level, abstract entities that are not reducible to fundamental physics but are nonetheless real and causally relevant. Daniel Dennett's concept of the "intentional stance" is instructive here. Dennett argues that we can predict the behavior of a complex system (a chess computer, a person) by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires. This is a predictive strategy, but Dennett also argues that these patterns are *real*—they are patterns in the behavior of the system that are not visible at the lower level. Similarly, the propositional attitudes are real patterns in the high-dimensional activation space of a neural network. They are not fictions; they are stable, projectable regularities that allow us to make sense of the system's behavior.

Crucially, these patterns are *causally relevant*, even if they are not *causally autonomous*. Consider the concept of a "traffic jam." A traffic jam is not a physical object in the same way a car is; it is a pattern of car movements. Yet, a traffic jam can have causal effects—it can cause a driver to take a different route. The pattern itself is causally efficacious because it influences the behavior of the individual cars. In the same way, a propositional attitude, as a stable pattern of activation, can influence the subsequent processing in the network. The pattern is not a separate entity, but it is a *real feature* of the network's state. When we say "the network believes it is raining," we are describing a specific, stable pattern of activation that will, given certain inputs, produce a specific output. This description captures a genuine causal regularity in the network's dynamics.

Therefore, we do not need to eliminate propositional attitudes. Instead, we need to *reduce* them to the underlying non-propositional dynamics. This is a non-eliminative reduction: the higher-level entities are real, but they are constituted by, and identical with, the lower-level patterns. The propositional attitude is not a separate causal agent; it is the macroscopic manifestation of a microscopic process. This is analogous to the relationship between temperature and molecular motion. Temperature is not eliminated by thermodynamics; it is reduced to it. We still use temperature because it is a convenient and predictive summary. Similarly, we will continue to use propositional attitudes in psychology because they are a convenient and predictive summary of neural dynamics. But we must recognize that they are not the fundamental causal entities.

The eliminative program fails because it throws the baby out with the bathwater. It ignores the genuine predictive and explanatory power of intentional vocabulary. Folk psychological laws, while not fundamental, capture real regularities in the behavior of cognitive systems. A theory that eliminates beliefs and desires would lose the ability to make these predictions. Moreover, the eliminativist assumes that the ontology of cognitive science must be defined by the *proximate* mechanisms. But cognitive science is a multi-level enterprise. We can have a computational level (what the system does), an algorithmic level (how it does it), and an implementational level (the physical substrate). Propositional attitudes belong to the computational/algorithmic level, while the activation dynamics belong to the implementational level. The failure of LOT is not that it posits a computational level, but that it insists on a specific algorithmic level (syntactic symbol manipulation) that is not realized by the actual neural substrate. The correct approach is to acknowledge that the computational level is real, but that it is *realized* by the non-propositional dynamics of the network. The propositional content is not a symbol to be manipulated; it is the *content* of the activation pattern, the meaning that the pattern carries.

**Conclusion: A Pluralistic Verdict**

In conclusion, the claim that propositional attitudes fail to serve as adequate causal-explanatory entities in computational cognitive science is *largely correct*, but it requires careful qualification. If we demand that a causal-explanatory entity be a discrete, autonomous, syntactically structured symbol that is manipulated according to formal rules—as posited by Fodor's LOT—then propositional attitudes indeed fail. The neuro-computational perspective, grounded in high-dimensional activation spaces and gradient descent learning, reveals that cognition is fundamentally a continuous, quantitative, and holistic process. There are no discrete symbols being shuffled; there are only shifting patterns of activation. The causal story is told in terms of the dynamics of the network, not in terms of the syntax of an internal language. In this sense, propositional attitudes are not the proximate causes of behavior. They are high-level abstractions that, if mistaken for autonomous causal agents, obscure the underlying neurobiological mechanisms. Fodor's LOT, while a magnificent logical edifice, is a castle in the air, unmoored from the actual architecture of the brain.

However, this does not necessitate the eliminative program. To eliminate propositional attitudes is to deny the reality of the patterns they describe. This is a mistake. The patterns are real, stable, and projectable. They allow us to predict behavior and to understand the functional organization of cognitive systems. The correct move is a non-eliminative reductionism. Propositional attitudes are real, but they are not fundamental. They are macroscopic descriptions of the non-propositional dynamics of neural networks. They are causally relevant *qua* patterns, but they do not possess causal autonomy. The future of cognitive science lies not in choosing between LOT and ANNs, nor in eliminating the mental, but in developing a rich, multi-level understanding that connects the intentional language of folk psychology to the continuous dynamics of the brain. We must learn to see the ghost in the machine, not as a separate spirit, but as the intricate, high-dimensional shape of the machine itself. The propositional attitude is not a cause in the brain; it is a description of the brain's causal architecture—a description that, while abstract, is indispensable for making sense of the mind.
