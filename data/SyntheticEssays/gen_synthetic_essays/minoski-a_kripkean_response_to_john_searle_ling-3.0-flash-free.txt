The philosophy of language has long been preoccupied with the mechanism by which words hook onto the world, securing a relationship between linguistic signs and their referents. Nowhere is this puzzle more acute than in the theory of proper names. For much of the twentieth century, descriptivism—the view that the meaning of a proper name is constituted by a cluster of descriptions associated with it—dominated the philosophical landscape, championed by figures like Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell. However, Saul Kripke’s revolutionary *Naming and Necessity* (1980) dismantled the descriptivist orthodoxy, arguing that proper names are rigid designators that refer to their objects independently of any descriptive content. Yet, a persistent claim in the secondary literature asserts that Kripke’s theory is fundamentally descriptivist, or at least that it cannot escape descriptivism at its origin point. This claim is fueled by John Searle’s influential argument that baptism and causal chains inherently rely on descriptive content, and that the initial baptism of a proper name requires unique descriptive content to fix its referent. This essay evaluates the claim that Kripke’s theory is fundamentally descriptivist, analyzes Searle’s baptism argument, examines the distinction between referring through descriptive content and descriptive content merely fixing the reference of a rigid designator, and assesses the hospital twin thought experiment and cases of mistaken identity. Ultimately, this essay will defend Kripke’s view of rigid designation, demonstrating that the object itself, rather than descriptive content, is the true driving force behind reference.

The claim that Kripke’s theory is fundamentally descriptivist stems from a misreading of his account of the origin of reference. Kripke acknowledges that at the initial baptism, a descriptive content is often present. For instance, a parent might name a child "Elizabeth" because she is "the future queen" or "the little girl with the dark curls." Critics seize upon this to argue that Kripke’s entire semantic framework is built upon a descriptivist foundation, merely relocated to the moment of baptism. However, this conflates the psychological motivation for a naming act with the semantic content of the name itself. Kripke explicitly argues that once a name has been fixed in a community through a causal-historical chain, the descriptive content attached to it becomes epistemically irrelevant to its reference. A name like "Aristotle" refers to Aristotle in all possible worlds where Aristotle exists, regardless of whether the speakers using the name believe Aristotle was a student of Plato—a descriptively false belief. The rigidity of the designator ensures that the name picks out the same object across possible worlds, not the description that happened to accompany its introduction. Thus, Kripke’s theory is fundamentally about the object and the necessary connections that bind it to its name, not about descriptive clusters.

To fully appreciate why Kripke’s theory resists descriptivism, one must examine the crucial distinction between a proper name referring *through* descriptive content and descriptive content merely *fixing* the reference of a rigid designator. In a descriptivist framework, such as that advanced by Russell or even a moderate descriptivist like Searle, the name refers *through* its descriptive content. The meaning of the name is the description, and the reference is determined by satisfying that description. If the description fails to be satisfied, the name fails to refer or refers to a different object. In contrast, in Kripke’s account, descriptive content merely *fixes* the reference of a rigid designator at the moment of baptism. The description acts as a pointer, a heuristic that allows the baptizer to identify the object in the actual world. However, the semantic content of the name is the rigid designator itself—the name functions as a tag that is directly attached to the object via a causal chain. The descriptive content is contingent and potentially false, but the rigid designation is necessary. Once the name is fixed, the descriptive content can be entirely stripped away or proven incorrect without altering the reference. This distinction reveals that Kripke’s theory is not descriptivist; rather, it uses descriptive content as a pragmatic tool for initiating reference, but the metaphysics of reference belongs to the rigid designator and the object it designates.

John Searle’s argument against Kripke’s non-descriptivist account hinges on the role of baptism and causal chains in establishing meaning. Searle contends that the causal chain of reference is not sufficient on its own; it must be anchored by descriptive content at its origin. For Searle, baptism is not merely a causal hooking-up of a sound to an object; it is an act of identification that requires the speaker to have a descriptive cluster that picks out the object. The initial baptism, according to Searle, requires unique descriptive content to fix the reference of the name. Without such unique descriptive content, the baptism would be a mere causal event devoid of semantic content, akin to pointing at an object without any conceptual grasp of what one is pointing at. Searle argues that the descriptive content is what transforms a causal connection into a meaningful linguistic act. The name "Socrates" means something because the community associates it with a cluster of descriptions—"the Athenian philosopher," "the student of Plato," "the hemlock drinker." It is this descriptive content, Searle claims, that drives the reference and constitutes the meaning of the name.

However, Searle’s insistence that the initial baptism requires *unique* descriptive content to fix its referent is highly problematic. At the moment of baptism, the subject is typically an infant or a newborn. It is virtually impossible to possess unique descriptive content about an infant that successfully fixes its reference in a non-circular or non-arbitrary way. Descriptions like "the firstborn son of John and Mary" are not unique if the parents have had multiple children, nor are they essential to the child's identity. Furthermore, any description attached to the infant at baptism is contingent and subject to revision. If the baptism requires unique descriptive content, then reference is perpetually underdetermined at its origin, making the semantic stability of proper names precarious. Searle’s framework forces him to claim that speakers possess a unique cluster of descriptions at the moment of baptism, yet this is psychologically and metaphysically implausible. The requirement of unique descriptive content at baptism makes Searle’s descriptivism overly demanding and ultimately unnecessary for explaining how names secure their referents.

The distinction between referring through descriptive content and descriptive content merely fixing the reference of a rigid designator becomes starkly illuminated when we examine the hospital twin thought experiment and cases of mistaken identity. Consider the hospital twin scenario: a mother gives birth to twins, Baby A and Baby B. Due to a hospital mix-up, Baby A is mistakenly taken home by the parents who intended to name her "Sarah." The parents, believing Baby A is their biological daughter, baptize her "Sarah" with the descriptive content "the little girl with the button nose" (a description that actually fits Baby B). As the children grow, the descriptive content "the little girl with the button nose" is meaningless or incorrect with respect to Baby A, who has a freckled complexion. Yet, the name "Sarah" rigidly refers to Baby A, the biological child of the parents, because the baptismal causal chain attached the name to Baby A. Under a strict descriptivism like Searle’s, the name "Sarah" should refer to Baby B, because Baby B satisfies the descriptive content associated with the name. But intuitively, and under Kripke’s theory, "Sarah" refers to Baby A. The descriptive content is either meaningless or factually incorrect, yet the name still functions as a rigid designator anchored to the object. This demonstrates that descriptive content is not the driving force behind reference; the object itself, secured by the rigid designation and the causal-historical chain, is.

Cases of mistaken identity further reinforce this conclusion. Imagine a philosopher who believes that the famous playwright "Shakespeare" was actually a collective pseudonym used by a group of Elizabethan courtiers, rather than the individual William Shakespeare of Stratford-upon-Avon. The descriptive content the philosopher associates with the name "Shakespeare"—"the author of Hamlet"—is meaningful, but the specific descriptive belief that it was a collective pseudonym is false or, at the very least, does not uniquely identify William Shakespeare in the way the philosopher intends. According to a pure descriptivist, the name might refer to the collective or fail to refer altogether because the descriptive content is muddled. However, Kripke’s theory posits that the name "Shakespeare" rigidly designates William Shakespeare in all possible worlds where William Shakespeare exists, regardless of the philosopher’s false descriptive beliefs. The descriptive content in the philosopher's mind may be incorrect or meaningless regarding the true identity of the referent, but the rigid designator persists in picking out the actual object. This illustrates that the object itself, not the descriptive content, is the ultimate driving force behind reference. Descriptive content is merely the vehicle of thought, not the vehicle of reference.

When we assess the driving force behind reference in these scenarios, the object itself emerges as the true anchor, not the descriptive content. In the hospital twin case, the object—Baby A—continues to be the referent of "Sarah" despite the descriptive content being attached to the wrong baby. In the mistaken identity case, the object—William Shakespeare—remains the referent despite the philosopher’s erroneous descriptive beliefs. The causal-historical chain, initiated by baptism and sustained by communal usage, connects the name to the object directly. Descriptive content is epistemically useful; it helps us navigate the world and identify which object a name might refer to in a given context. But it is not ontologically fundamental to reference. Kripke’s rigid designation captures this perfectly: the name is a tag that is locked onto the object at baptism, and it travels with the object across all possible worlds. Descriptive content may change, be forgotten, or be proven false, but the rigid designator remains tethered to the object. Searle’s descriptivism, by contrast, makes reference hostage to the vagaries of human psychology and the accuracy of descriptive clusters. If descriptive content drives reference, then a community that forgets or distorts the original descriptions loses the reference entirely—a counterintuitive result.

Searle’s framework struggles to account for the persistence of reference in the face of false or meaningless descriptive content precisely because it conflates the descriptive motivation for a name with the semantic content of the name. For Searle, the meaning of a proper name is the descriptive cluster; therefore, if the cluster is false, the reference is broken or altered. But Kripke’s theory, and the broader causal-historical theory of reference it supports, treats the descriptive cluster as a psychological phenomenon, not a semantic one. The semantic content of a proper name is its rigid designation. The hospital twin thought experiment reveals this cleavage clearly: the descriptive content "the baby born to the Smiths" is attached to the name "Tom," but if Baby Tom is switched with Baby Dick, the name "Tom" still rigidly refers to Baby Tom, the object that was baptized, not the description that was applied. The object is the driving force; the description is merely the initial, often flawed, mechanism by which the speaker located the object in the actual world.

In conclusion, the claim that Saul Kripke’s theory of proper names is fundamentally descriptivist is fundamentally mistaken. While Kripke acknowledges that descriptive content is often present at the baptism of a proper name, he argues that this content is merely the catalyst for the causal-historical chain, not the semantic essence of the name. John Searle’s argument that baptism and causal chains rely on descriptive content, and that the initial baptism requires unique descriptive content to fix its referent, fails to account for the persistence of reference in the face of false, meaningless, or incorrect descriptions. The distinction between referring through descriptive content and descriptive content merely fixing the reference of a rigid designator is decisive: Kripke’s theory posits that names refer through the object itself, rigidly designated across possible worlds, whereas descriptivism posits that names refer through the satisfaction of descriptions. The hospital twin thought experiment and cases of mistaken identity demonstrate conclusively that when descriptive content is meaningless or incorrect, the object itself remains the true driving force behind reference. Therefore, I defend Kripke’s view of rigid designation. It is a metaphysically robust, epistemically independent account of how names secure their referents, anchoring language to the world through the necessary connections that bind objects to their names, rather than tethering reference to the fragile and fallible descriptive contents of the human mind.
