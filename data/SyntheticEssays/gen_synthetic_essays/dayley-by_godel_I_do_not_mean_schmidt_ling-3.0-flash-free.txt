**The Anchor and the Lens: Descriptive Intentionality, Causal Chains, and the Fate of Reference**

The problem of how proper names secure their reference to objects in the world stands as one of the most persistent and foundational puzzles in the philosophy of language. For much of the twentieth century, the dominant paradigm for solving this problem was descriptivism, the view championed by Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, and P.F. Strawson, which posits that the meaning of a proper name is constituted by a cluster of associated definite descriptions. However, the mid-century witnessed a radical challenge to this orthodoxy, spearheaded by Saul Kripke, whose Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment purported to demonstrate the fatal inadequacy of the descriptive account. While Kripke’s critique successfully dismantles strict descriptivism by decoupling reference from essential descriptive content, the philosopher John Searle mounted a robust defense of descriptivism, arguing that causal chains of reference rely essentially on descriptive intentionality and community-based definite descriptions. When we examine the dialectical tension between the attributive and referential uses of proper names, it becomes clear that while causal historical chains provide the necessary structural anchor for reference, it is the associated definite descriptions—understood through Searle’s lens of intentional content—that ultimately explain how names achieve reference to objects.

To appreciate the magnitude of Kripke’s challenge, one must first understand the architecture of the descriptivist theory as articulated by its primary proponents. For Frege, proper names possess both sense (Sinn) and reference (Bedeutung). The sense of a name is the mode of presentation of its referent; it is the conceptual content that allows us to identify the object in question. For Frege, the sense of a proper name is essentially constituted by the associated descriptions that differentiate it from other names. Bertrand Russell extended this tradition by arguing that proper names are, in effect, "disguised definite descriptions." On Russell’s account, when we assert "Aristotle taught Alexander," we are asserting the existence of a unique individual who satisfies a cluster of descriptions—"the teacher of Alexander," "the author of the Organon," "the resident of Stagira"—and we refer to that individual by picking out the object that uniquely satisfies these descriptions. P.F. Strawson, while critical of Russell’s truth-conditional theory of meaning, nevertheless remained a descriptivist regarding the *meaning* of proper names. Strawson argued that even if a speaker successfully refers to an object without possessing all the relevant descriptions (what he called the "mutilated" description), the *meaning* of the name is still constituted by the associated descriptions within the linguistic community. For Strawson, the descriptive function is a matter of the name's place in the web of language, even if the individual speaker’s referential act does not require exhaustive descriptive knowledge. Thus, for all three thinkers, the connection between a name and its referent is mediated by descriptive content; to know a name is to know what it is supposed to pick out.

It is precisely this mediation of reference by descriptive content that Kripke targeted in his devastating critique, most vividly illustrated by the Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment. In *Naming and Necessity*, Kripke imagines a scenario in which the logician Kurt Gödel discovers a contradiction in a formal system S and writes a manuscript proving it, but the manuscript is subsequently lost. Later, another mathematician, Schmidt, independently proves the same contradiction. Intuitively, Gödel is the person who proved the inconsistency of S; the description "the discoverer of the inconsistency of system S" applies to Gödel, not Schmidt. However, if the descriptivist theory is correct, the name "Gödel" means "the discoverer of the inconsistency of system S." Since Schmidt also satisfies this description, the descriptivist must conclude that "Gödel" could refer to Schmidt, or that the reference of "Gödel" is ambiguous. Yet, this is absurd. When we say "Gödel proved the inconsistency of S," we rigidly refer to Gödel himself, not merely whoever happens to satisfy the description. 

Kripke uses this thought experiment to introduce the concept of the "rigid designator." A rigid designator designates the same object in every possible world in which it designates anything at all. "Gödel" is a rigid designator; it refers to the actual historical individual Gödel, regardless of what descriptions he or others may associate with him. Descriptivists, by contrast, use "non-rigid" designators—descriptions that may pick out different objects in different possible worlds. The Gödel-Schmidt case demonstrates that the reference of a proper name is not determined by the descriptive content associated with it at the time of utterance, but by some other, non-descriptive factor. Kripke further reinforces this with examples involving the identity of substances: "Water is H2O" is necessarily true, even though the ancient Greeks used the word "water" without possessing the description "the stuff with the chemical composition H2O." If reference were determined by description, the ancient Greeks would not have referred to water at all. Therefore, Kripke concludes that the causal-historical theory of reference is superior. A name is initially "dubbed" onto an object and then passed along a causal chain of communication. The name "Gödel" refers to Gödel because of this historical chain, not because of the descriptive content anyone currently associates with the name. In this light, Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment successfully undermines strict descriptivism by demonstrating that reference can be rigid and independent of descriptive content, thereby severing the necessary link between meaning and description.

However, to declare the demise of descriptivism based solely on Kripke’s critique is to overlook the profound defense mounted by John Searle, who argues that the causal-historical theory is fundamentally incomplete because it ignores the role of intentional content and community-based definite descriptions. Searle’s defense operates on the premise that a causal chain of physical events—sound waves, ink marks, neural firings—is not, by itself, a chain of *reference*. For a causal chain to transmit semantic content, it must be governed by the intentional states of the speakers within a linguistic community. Searle argues that Kripke’s theory commits a category mistake: it confuses the *vehicle* of transmission (the causal chain) with the *mechanism* of reference-fixing (descriptive intentionality).

Searle’s crucial insight is that causal chains of reference rely essentially on descriptive intentionality. When a speaker utters a proper name, they do not merely emit a sound that triggers a causal response in the listener; they must have a specific intention to refer to a particular object, and this intention is constituted by a definite description. Even if the description is minimal or "that very object," it must be a description of some kind. Searle elaborates on this through the concept of community-based definite descriptions. The meaning of a proper name is not an individual psychological property, but a social fact. The community uses shared definite descriptions to *fix* the reference of names. When a name is passed along a causal chain, the recipient does not simply inherit a physical token; they interpret the token using their own descriptive understanding of the community's practices. Thus, the causal chain provides the historical continuity, but the descriptive intentionality provides the semantic content. Without the descriptive intentionality of the community, the causal chain would be a mere sequence of physical events devoid of referential power—akin to a domino effect that carries no meaning. Searle thus argues that Kripke’s causal theory fails to explain how the physical chain is *about* anything; it is the associated definite descriptions, embedded in the intentional states of speakers, that ultimately explain how names achieve reference to objects.

This brings us to the dialectical tension between the attributive and referential uses of proper names, a distinction famously articulated by Keith Donnellan. An attributive use of a proper name occurs when the speaker uses the name to describe an individual, effectively employing it as a disguised definite description. For example, if a speaker says "The murderer of Smith is insane," using the name "Smith" attributively, they are describing a particular person by reference to their actions. A referential use, by contrast, occurs when the speaker uses the name to point to a specific individual, regardless of whether the associated descriptions are accurate. If a speaker points to an innocent man in a crowd and says "The murderer of Smith is insane," they are referentially using the name to refer to that specific man, even though their descriptive content is false.

This tension creates significant problems for both pure descriptivism and pure causal theories. For strict descriptivism, the referential use is deeply problematic. If reference is constituted by descriptive content, how can a speaker successfully refer to an object they are fundamentally misdescribing? Russell’s theory seems to predict that if the description fails, the name fails to refer, yet Donnellan’s examples suggest that referential success can occur despite descriptive failure. Conversely, for pure causal theories, the attributive use is problematic. If reference is solely a matter of causal-historical chains, then the descriptive content the speaker brings to the utterance seems epiphenomenal; it does not contribute to the reference itself. Kripke’s rigid designator approach privileges the referential use: the name rigidly refers to its original bearer, regardless of descriptive error. Yet, Searle’s defense highlights the indispensability of the attributive use. When a speaker uses a name referentially, pointing to an object, they do so within a linguistic framework where the name carries descriptive weight. The community's shared definite descriptions provide the "dividing line" that allows the speaker to identify *which* object in the causal chain they are referring to. Without the attributive dimension—the descriptive content that guides the speaker’s intention—the referential act would be a blind gesture, unable to distinguish the intended object from other objects in the causal network.

Ultimately, determining whether causal historical chains or associated definite descriptions explain how names achieve reference requires a synthesis that acknowledges the distinct roles each plays in the semiotics of reference. It is my contention that while causal historical chains provide the necessary structural anchor for reference, it is the associated definite descriptions—understood through Searle’s intentionalist framework—that ultimately explain the *achievement* of reference. 

Consider the alternative: that causal chains alone ultimately explain reference. If we accept this, we must accept that a name is simply a physical tag attached to an object and passed along a historical continuum. But this leads to the "boomerang" problem of causal theories: the problem of error. If reference is purely causal, how do we explain misreference? If a speaker utters "Gödel" while thinking of Schmidt, causal theorists might argue that the name still refers to Gödel because of the historical chain. But this makes the speaker's intention entirely irrelevant to reference, reducing the name to a mere causal echo. Searle’s critique exposes the inadequacy of this view: a causal chain without descriptive intentionality cannot distinguish between a genuine speaker and a name-mimicker, or between a speaker who refers correctly and one who refers mistakenly. The causal chain provides the *persistence* of reference—ensuring that the name "Gödel" continues to refer to Gödel across time and possible worlds, even if no one alive remembers the original descriptive content. But it does not explain the *acquisition* or *fixation* of reference. 

It is in the fixation of reference that definite descriptions prove indispensable. When a name is first introduced into a language, it is introduced through a descriptive act. A speaker says, "Let us call this object 'Gödel'—the genius who discovered the inconsistency in system S." The descriptive content provides the cognitive scaffold that allows the community to anchor the name to the object. Once anchored, the name can be transmitted causally, and the descriptive content can become thinner or even forgotten (as in the case of the ancient Greeks and "water"), yet the reference persists. However, the persistence of reference is not the *achievement* of reference; it is the *maintenance* of a previously achieved reference. The achievement of reference—the moment the name first successfully picks out its object—requires descriptive intentionality. Furthermore, even in ongoing use, descriptive intentionality remains necessary for the *identification* of the referent within the causal chain. When a speaker hears the name "Gödel," they must employ some descriptive content to identify which object in the causal history the speaker intends to pick out. Even if this description is merely "that object that was originally dubbed 'Gödel' by the community," it is still a description, an intentional content that guides the speaker's reference.

Therefore, the dialectical tension between attributive and referential uses resolves into a complementary duality rather than a zero-sum conflict. The referential use relies on the causal-historical chain to ensure that the name remains tethered to its original object across time and modal space, satisfying Kripke’s demand for rigidity. The attributive use relies on the descriptive intentionality of the community to provide the cognitive content that allows the name to be fixed, identified, and communicated. Searle’s defense of descriptivism is successful not in reviving *strict* descriptivism—the view that the meaning of a name is exhaustively constituted by a specific set of descriptions—but in demonstrating that descriptive intentionality is the necessary mechanism that makes causal chains semantically meaningful. Without the community-based definite descriptions that govern the interpretation of the causal chain, the chain is merely a physical sequence, not a chain of *reference*.

In conclusion, Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment successfully undermines strict descriptivism by demonstrating that reference can be rigid and independent of the descriptive content associated with a name at any given moment. The modal arguments and the problem of misidentification show that descriptive content cannot be the sole determinant of reference. Yet, John Searle’s defense reveals that the causal-historical chain alone cannot account for the semantic richness of reference. Causal chains require the anchoring influence of descriptive intentionality to function as vehicles of meaning. When we navigate the tension between the attributive and referential uses of proper names, we see that the causal chain provides the enduring, rigid anchor, but it is the associated definite descriptions—the intentional content of the community—that ultimately explain how names are initially achieved, continually fixed, and communally transmitted. Reference is thus a dual phenomenon: causally anchored in the historical world, yet semantically constituted by the descriptive intentions of the minds that wield language.
