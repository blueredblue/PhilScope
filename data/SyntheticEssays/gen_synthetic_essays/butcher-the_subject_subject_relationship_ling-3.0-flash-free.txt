The claim that person-to-person communication requires moving beyond Jean-Paul Sartre’s ontological conflict of mutual objectification toward a model of mutual transcendence represents a profound critique of the existential condition as articulated in *Being and Nothingness*. Sartre famously posited that "hell is other people," a statement that encapsulates the agonizing reality of the Look: the moment one consciousness encounters another, the dynamic of mutual objectification is triggered. The Other, through the gaze, objectifies me, reducing my transcendence to a static facticity, just as I inevitably reduce the Other to an object in my world. Yet, to claim that authentic communication demands a wholesale abandonment of this dynamic in favor of mutual transcendence requires a rigorous examination of how the self navigates the necessary objectification of the Other-as-object with the recognition of the Other-as-subject. By examining Paul Tillich’s concept of indeterminacy within the subject-subject relationship alongside Luce Irigaray’s notion of "the two," we can evaluate whether genuine conversation successfully replaces the drive toward domination with a dynamic anticipation of mutual change, resolving the tension between the static approximations we prepare for interaction and the relinquishment required during actual dialogue.

Sartre’s ontology provides the foundational problem. In the phenomenological world, consciousness is always consciousness *of* something; it is directed toward objects in the world to fulfill its projects. When the Other enters this world, they become an object for me, an obstacle or a tool. Simultaneously, the Other’s gaze traps me into being an object for them. This mutual objectification creates an ontological conflict: a zero-sum struggle for mastery where one consciousness must dominate the other to escape being objectified. In Sartre’s framework, the Other is fundamentally alien, a wall that defines my existence negatively. Communication, under these conditions, is inherently fraught with the drive toward domination. To communicate is to attempt to master the Other’s gaze, to force them to see me as I wish to be seen, while simultaneously seeking to un-look them to reclaim my own freedom. This is the deadlock of the Sartrean subject: the Other is a necessary condition for my self-consciousness, yet their presence is a perpetual threat to my autonomy.

However, to evaluate the claim that communication requires moving beyond this conflict toward mutual transcendence, we must ask whether mutual transcendence is even possible if the Other remains fundamentally an object to be mastered. This is where Paul Tillich’s concept of indeterminacy offers a crucial philosophical intervention. Tillich, in his theological and philosophical explorations of the subject-subject relationship, argues that authentic encounter requires the recognition of the Other’s indeterminacy—their unconditioned, inexhaustible nature that resists totalization and objectification. For Tillich, the ground of being is not a static substance but a dynamic, unconditioned reality that allows for genuine subjectivity. When two subjects encounter one another, the subject-subject relationship is characterized by this indeterminacy; the Other is not a fixed object to be categorized and controlled, but a presence that participates in the unconditioned ground of being. 

Tillich’s indeterminacy fundamentally disrupts the Sartrean cycle of mutual objectification. If the Other is indeterminate, they cannot be fully mastered or objectified. The drive toward domination, which relies on reducing the Other to a knowable, controllable object, is rendered philosophically impossible in the presence of true subjectivity. Instead, the subject-subject relationship becomes a space of openness and ultimate concern. In this model, communication is not a battle for mastery but a mutual participation in the unconditioned. The self does not seek to possess or define the Other; rather, it acknowledges the Other’s irreducible freedom and depth. This recognition of indeterminacy provides the ontological foundation for mutual transcendence: because the Other is not a static object, the relationship itself is dynamic, always becoming, always open to the unexpected emergence of the Other’s true being.

Yet, Tillich’s indeterminacy, while dismantling the possibility of total objectification, does not fully account for the structural asymmetries of power and difference that pervade human interaction. This is the void that Luce Irigaray’s notion of "the two" seeks to fill. Irigaray critiques the phallogocentric tradition—encompassing Sartre’s ontology—which subsumes the feminine and the Other into the masculine and the Same, reducing the latter to a lack or an object. In her work, particularly in *Thinking the Difference*, Irigaray argues for a model of relation based on "the two": two distinct, irreducible, and mutually recognizing subjects. "The two" is not a dialectical synthesis that erases difference, nor is it a mere plurality of isolated individuals; it is a dyadic space where the difference between the subjects is positively affirmed and becomes the very medium of communication.

Irigaray’s concept of mimesis is central to this model. Mimesis, for Irigaray, is not passive imitation but an active, creative mirroring that allows each subject to recognize the Other’s distinct mode of being. In the subject-subject relationship of "the two," communication becomes a dialogic practice of mutual transformation. Each subject reflects the Other, not to assimilate them, but to allow the Other’s difference to illuminate and reshape one’s own subjectivity. This directly addresses the Sartrean conflict by refusing the premise that the Other is an object to be dominated. If "the two" is the structure of genuine communication, then the drive toward domination is replaced by a dynamic interplay of recognition and difference. The self does not seek to master the Other; it seeks to participate in a relationship where both are subjects, each bringing their irreplaceable difference to the encounter.

However, the transition from Sartre’s objectification to Tillich’s indeterminacy and Irigaray’s "the two" is not a simple ontological switch; it is a lived tension that the self must navigate. The self must balance the necessary objectification of the Other-as-object with the recognition of the Other-as-subject. In the practical, everyday world, we must treat others as objects to some degree. We navigate social spaces using functional roles, categories, and schemas. To cross the street, we must treat the pedestrian as an obstacle or a moving body; to order coffee, we must treat the barista as a role within a transactional system. This pragmatic objectification is a necessary condition for survival and social coordination. The danger arises when this pragmatic objectification bleeds into the existential realm, when we forget that the person occupying the role is an indeterminate subject, an irreplaceable "two" in Irigaray’s terms, rather than a static object.

This brings us to the crucial tension between preparing for interaction through static approximations and relinquishing those approximations during actual dialogue. Before we enter into conversation, we inevitably rely on static approximations: stereotypes, preconceptions, social scripts, and heuristic categories. These approximations are the cognitive and affective tools we use to prepare for interaction. They allow us to anticipate the Other’s behavior and to navigate the social world with a degree of predictability. Sartre’s ontology, in many ways, explains why we rely on these approximations: because the Other as a subject is terrifyingly free and indeterminate, we construct static approximations of them to mitigate the anxiety of the encounter and to maintain the illusion of mastery.

Yet, genuine conversation demands the relinquishment of these static approximations. When dialogue actually begins, the Other-as-subject disrupts the script. Their indeterminacy—Tillich’s unconditioned presence—asserts itself against our prepared schemas. The Other says something unexpected, reveals a vulnerability, or challenges our assumptions, forcing us to drop our approximations and encounter them as they truly are in the moment. This relinquishment is the crucible of mutual transcendence. It is the moment where the drive toward domination—the desire to force the Other back into our static approximation—is overcome by the dynamic anticipation of mutual change. If we cling to our approximations, the conversation becomes a performance of domination, a monologue where we project our schemas onto the Other. But if we relinquish them, the conversation becomes a genuine encounter.

Does genuine conversation successfully replace the drive toward domination with a dynamic anticipation of mutual change? The answer, when evaluated through the synthesis of Sartre, Tillich, and Irigaray, is a qualified yes. Genuine conversation does replace the drive toward domination, but it does so not by eliminating the tension between objectification and subject-recognition, but by transforming it. In the Sartrean model, the tension is a static conflict: I must dominate you or you must dominate me. In the model of mutual transcendence, the tension becomes a dynamic engine of growth. The necessary objectification of the Other-as-object provides the ground upon which the relationship takes place, while the recognition of the Other-as-subject provides the direction of the relationship’s movement. 

The dynamic anticipation of mutual change is the hallmark of this transformed tension. When we relinquish our static approximations, we do not enter a state of passive acceptance; rather, we enter a state of active, expectant openness. We anticipate that the Other will change us, just as we will change them. This is the core of Irigaray’s mimesis and Tillich’s ultimate concern. The conversation is no longer a contest to be won, but a co-creation to be inhabited. The self does not seek to fix the Other into an object, nor does it seek to be fixed by the Other into a static subject; rather, both subjects participate in an ongoing process of becoming. The drive toward domination is replaced by the drive toward mutual transformation, a dynamic where the Other’s irreducibility is celebrated rather than feared.

Nevertheless, the evaluation of this claim must account for the fragility of this model. The transition from static approximation to dynamic relinquishment is fraught with risk. The self is always tempted to retreat into the safety of objectification when the Other’s indeterminacy becomes too threatening. The Sartrean conflict does not disappear; it is merely suspended by the discipline of genuine dialogue. If the conversation fails—if the participants revert to the drive toward domination—then the static approximations reassert themselves, and the relationship collapses back into the hell of mutual objectification. Therefore, mutual transcendence is not an ontological given but an achievement, an ethical and existential labor that requires the constant relinquishment of the ego’s need for mastery.

In conclusion, the claim that person-to-person communication requires moving beyond Sartre’s ontological conflict of mutual objectification toward a model of mutual transcendence is philosophically robust when evaluated through the lenses of Tillich and Irigaray. Sartre accurately diagnoses the structural trap of the Look, where the Other is inevitably positioned as an object and the self is trapped in a struggle for mastery. However, Tillich’s concept of indeterminacy reveals that the Other cannot be fully objectified because true subjectivity participates in the unconditioned ground of being, and Irigaray’s notion of "the two" demonstrates that authentic relation requires the positive affirmation of irreducible difference rather than its subjugation. The self must balance the necessary objectification of the Other-as-object with the recognition of the Other-as-subject, navigating the tension between the static approximations we prepare for interaction and the relinquishment of those approximations during actual dialogue. Genuine conversation does indeed successfully replace the drive toward domination with a dynamic anticipation of mutual change, transforming the existential hell of mutual objectification into a space of mutual transcendence. Yet, this transcendence is perpetually precarious, requiring the continuous, willing surrender of the self’s desire for mastery in the face of the Other’s inexhaustible, indeterminate presence.
