**Beyond the Fixed Past: A B-Theoretic Defense of Libertarian Freedom Against Ostler’s Incompatibilism**

The problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom has long constituted one of the most intractable puzzles in philosophical theology. At its heart lies the challenge of reconciling the classical theistic commitment to an infallible God who knows the future infallibly with the libertarian commitment to human freedom, which requires that agents genuinely possess the power to do otherwise in identical circumstances. Blake Ostler, in his critical examination of this problem, mounts a formidable argument for incompatibilism. Ostler’s argument hinges on a specific mechanism: the inalterability of past beliefs and truth propositions. If God’s past beliefs are fixed, and if human freedom requires the power to bring it about that God held different past beliefs, then libertarian freedom is logically incompatible with divine foreknowledge. However, Ostler’s incompatibilism relies upon unexamined ontological assumptions regarding the nature of time, memory, and causation. By carefully analyzing the A-theory and B-theory of time, the problem of memory, and the nature of causal loops, it becomes evident that changing God’s past beliefs involves no logical impossibility once human temporal intuitions are separated from the divine perspective. The inalterability of the past, as Ostler conceives it, is an artifact of A-theoretic thinking, and once this is abandoned in favor of a B-theoretic, atemporal divine perspective, the compatibility of infallible foreknowledge and libertarian freedom is restored.

To understand Ostler’s incompatibilism, one must first grasp the precise mechanism by which he links divine foreknowledge to the fixity of the past. Ostler argues that if God possesses infallible foreknowledge, then God holds true beliefs about future human actions. For instance, at time t1, God believes that at time t2, agent S will perform action X. Because God’s beliefs are infallible, the proposition "S will perform X at t2" must be true at t1. Ostler’s crucial move is to treat the truth of this proposition as metaphysically tied to the unalterability of the past. If the past is fixed, then the truth of the proposition "S will do X" at t1 is immutable. 

Now, introduce libertarian freedom. If S is libertarianly free at t2, S could have done otherwise; S could have performed action Y instead of X. But if S performs Y at t2, the proposition "S will do X" is false, and the proposition "S will do Y" is true. Consequently, God’s belief at t1 would have had to be different; God would have believed that S would do Y, not X. Yet, Ostler argues, the past is inalterable. God’s beliefs at t1 are part of the past, and therefore, they cannot be changed. Thus, if S could have done otherwise, God’s past beliefs would have to be different, but God’s past beliefs cannot be different because the past cannot be changed. Therefore, S cannot do otherwise. 

Ostler concludes that human freedom requires the power to bring it about that God held different past beliefs. Because agents lack the power to alter the past—and specifically, the past beliefs of an infallible God—agents lack the power to do otherwise. This, Ostler contends, renders libertarian freedom incompatible with infallible divine foreknowledge. The argument is compelling precisely because it taps into a deeply intuitive metaphysical assumption: that the past is a fixed, unchangeable domain, and that to be free is to possess the power to alter what has already been settled. However, the logical force of Ostler’s argument depends entirely on the ontological assumptions underlying his conception of time and the divine mind.

The first major assumption underlying Ostler’s argument is the A-theory of time. The A-theory, also known as the theory of objective becoming, posits that time flows. Under the A-theory, the past is objectively gone, the future is objectively open, and only the present is real. The "pastness" of an event is an ontological feature, not merely a relational one. It is precisely because the past is ontologically distinct from the present—because it is irrevocably gone—that the past is, in Ostler’s view, unalterable. If the past is not real in the same way the present is, there is no ontological space in which to intervene or change it. The A-theory thus provides the metaphysical scaffolding for the inalterability of past beliefs and truth propositions. 

However, classical theism has historically adopted a fundamentally different temporal ontology when it comes to the divine. The classical doctrine of divine eternity, articulated most famously by Boethius and later refined by Aquinas, holds that God is atemporal. God does not experience the flow of time; rather, God apprehends all moments of time simultaneously in an eternal, instantaneous present. This atemporal perspective aligns far more closely with the B-theory of time. The B-theory, or the theory of eternalism, posits that time is a static, four-dimensional block universe. Under the B-theory, there is no objective flow of time; there is no ontological privilege accorded to the present. Instead, events are related by tenseless, B-relations: earlier than, later than, and simultaneous with. The "pastness" of an event is merely a relational fact concerning its position in the block, not an ontological feature that renders the event fixed or unchangeable in an absolute sense. 

If God is atemporal, then God’s perspective is inherently B-theoretic. From the divine perspective, there is no "past" in the A-theoretic sense of a fixed, gone domain. There is merely a location in the four-dimensional block, related by the B-relation of "earlier than" to other locations. When we project our A-theoretic, human intuitions onto God—assuming that God experiences a fixed, unalterable past in the same way we do—we commit a category mistake. Ostler’s argument for incompatibilism relies on this very projection. It assumes that God’s "past beliefs" are fixed in the same robust, A-theoretic sense that human past beliefs are fixed. But if God is atemporal, the "fixity" of God's beliefs at an earlier B-time is not an ontological barrier to freedom; it is simply a coordinate in the static block. 

To further dismantle Ostler’s incompatibilism, we must examine the problem of memory and the nature of causal loops, which are deeply entangled with A-theoretic assumptions about the past. Ostler’s argument implicitly relies on a temporal model of divine knowledge that mirrors human cognition. In human experience, memory is the mechanism by which we access the past. Memory is inherently A-theoretic: it records a fixed past, and if the past were to change, our memories would be false or in need of revision. This is the "problem of memory" for an omniscient being: if God remembers the future, and the future changes, God's memory must be false, which contradicts divine infallibility. Similarly, Ostler assumes that if God's past beliefs are to be different, God's memory or record of the past must be altered.

However, divine knowledge is not memory in the human, A-theoretic sense. Human memory is a temporal process of retrieval, dependent on the passage of time and the degradation or alteration of mental states. Divine knowledge, by contrast, is an eternal, simultaneous apprehension of the entirety of the timeline. God does not "remember" the future; God simply knows it. The B-theoretic block universe contains all events, and God's knowledge is a perfect, non-temporal apprehension of the entire block. Therefore, the problem of memory—how God can have true memories of a future that changes—is a pseudo-problem generated by A-theoretic thinking. In a B-theoretic framework, there is no "future" that changes into the "past"; there is only the static block, and God knows the whole block at once. 

When we apply this B-theoretic understanding to Ostler’s claim that human freedom requires the power to bring it about that God held different past beliefs, the logical impossibility Ostler identifies dissolves. The claim presupposes that God’s past beliefs are temporally fixed in a way that prevents them from being re-evaluated or recontextualized by later events. But if God is atemporal, there is no temporal "later" or "earlier" in which God's beliefs are isolated. God's knowledge at the B-coordinate of t1 is simply God's knowledge of the truth at t1, and the truth at t1 is determined by the entire block universe, including the events at t2. 

Consider the objection regarding causal loops. Ostler might argue that if a human agent at t2 brings it about that God held different past beliefs at t1, this creates a causal loop—a paradox where the effect (the changed past belief) precedes its cause (the agent's choice at t2). Causal loops are often cited as logically impossible because they violate our intuitive understanding of linear causation. If the past is fixed, and the agent's present choice is caused by their past beliefs, which were caused by God's past beliefs, how can the agent's present choice retroactively alter God's past beliefs without generating a paradox?

This objection, however, again relies on a rigid, A-theoretic model of causation. Under the A-theory, causation flows strictly from the fixed past to the open future. A retroactive change in the past is thus an incoherence. But under the B-theory, causation is simply a cross-temporal relation within the static block. There is no "retroactive" change; there is only a consistent, holistic determination of truth values across the block universe. If an agent chooses Y at t2 instead of X, this choice is part of the block universe. The truth of the proposition "God believes at t1 that S will do X" is simply false; the true proposition is "God believes at t1 that S will do Y." There is no logical impossibility in a block universe where the truth values of propositions at earlier B-coordinates are grounded in the events at later B-coordinates. The "loop" is not a paradox; it is a consistent, self-contained description of a four-dimensional reality. 

The core of the defense of the thesis rests on the separation of human temporal intuitions from the divine perspective. Human beings are temporally embedded, A-theoretic agents. We experience time as a flowing river, with the past as a fixed shore and the future as an open sea. Our intuitions about freedom are inextricably bound to this A-theoretic experience: we feel that freedom requires the ability to alter what has been settled. When we attempt to solve the problem of divine foreknowledge, we inevitably project these A-theoretic intuitions onto God, imagining God as a cosmic observer standing at the edge of a fixed past, looking out at an open future. Ostler’s incompatibilism is the direct result of this projection. By assuming that God’s "past beliefs" are fixed in the same A-theoretic sense as human memories, Ostler constructs a logical wall that libertarians cannot scale.

Yet, if we separate our human intuitions from the divine perspective, we recognize that God is not an A-theoretic observer. God is atemporal, perceiving the entire B-theoretic block universe in a single, eternal apprehension. From this perspective, the "past" is not an ontologically distinct, immutable realm; it is simply a different location in the block, related by the B-relation of "earlier than." The inalterability of the past, which Ostler treats as an absolute metaphysical constraint, is revealed to be a feature of human A-theoretic consciousness, not an absolute feature of reality as a whole.
