**The Illusion of Direction: Temporal Becoming, Psychological Indistinguishability, and the Limits of Common-Sense Evidence**

The metaphysics of temporal becoming has long been dominated by two primary contenders: Presentism, which holds that only the present exists, and the Growing Block theory, which posits that the past and present are real, while the future is not, and that reality expands by adding new moments to its ontological edge. However, the philosophical landscape of time is far more variegated than these traditional models suggest. Alternative frameworks, such as the Diminishing Block theory and the Backwards Block theory, challenge the intuitive directionality of temporal passage by proposing that the ontological edge of existence might shrink or retreat rather than grow. A profound philosophical question emerges: can these alternative models generate subjective experiences of temporal flow that are indistinguishable from those produced by the Growing Block model? Furthermore, how do the intricate relationships between a subject’s memory hierarchy, psychological scaling of importance, and the objective edge of temporal existence mediate this experience? This essay argues that the Diminishing Block and Backwards Block theories can indeed generate phenomenological experiences of temporal flow structurally identical to those of the Growing Block model. However, while the psychological indistinguishability of these temporal structures neutralizes the evidential weight that common-sense human experience lends to traditional ontologies, the coherence of the Backwards Block model is critically undermined by Michael Dummett’s critique of backward causation.

To evaluate the claim that alternative models of becoming can replicate the subjective experience of temporal flow, one must first rigorously define the ontological mechanics of the Growing Block (GB), Diminishing Block (DB), and Backwards Block (BB) theories. In the GB model, the universe is a four-dimensional block universe, but unlike eternalism, the future does not exist. Reality grows by the continuous addition of new moments at the leading edge of existence—the present. The subjective experience of temporal flow in GB arises from the continuous transition of events from the non-existent future, through the nascent present, and into the already-realized past. The "now" is a dynamic frontier, and the sensation of passage is the phenomenological correlate of this ontological expansion.

The Diminishing Block theory inverts this ontology. Rather than reality growing by adding new moments, the DB model posits that the past, present, and future all possess some form of ontological status, but the edge of temporal existence is a vanishing boundary. In the DB view, as time passes, the past does not simply become "past"; rather, it diminishes in its mode of existence, fading into a state of ontological decrepitude, while the present is the boundary where existence is actively waning. The subjective experience of flow in DB is not the arrival of new reality, but the sensation of reality receding and fading away. The "now" is the trailing edge of existence, and temporal passage is the phenomenological experience of the world dissolving into the past.

The Backwards Block theory presents a more radical challenge. In the BB model, the ontological edge of temporal existence does not grow forward nor diminish, but recedes backward. The "now" moves from the future into the past, meaning that the present is a retreating frontier. What we perceive as the future is the domain of the already-existing but unexperienced, while the past is the domain of the present edge that has already receded from. The subjective experience of flow in BB arises because consciousness is tethered to this receding edge; as the edge retreats, new regions of the block become the present, while the previously present becomes the past. 

At first glance, it seems impossible that these three radically different ontologies—growth, diminishment, and recession—could produce the same subjective experience. However, a deeper analysis of the relationship between a subject’s memory hierarchy, psychological scaling of importance, and the objective edge of temporal existence reveals that they can. The subjective experience of temporal flow is not a direct apprehension of ontological mechanics; rather, it is a constructed phenomenon generated by the interaction between cognitive architecture and the boundary of existence. 

Consider the memory hierarchy. Human memory is not a monolithic repository but a stratified system, ranging from the immediate sensory trace of the specious present to the deep, consolidated archives of remote episodic and semantic memory. In the GB model, as the objective edge grows, it passes over events, converting them from the present into the past and encoding them into memory. The memory hierarchy thus acts as a fossil record of the growing block. In the DB model, the memory hierarchy records the diminishing traces of what has faded from the present; the past is not merely stored but actively decaying in its ontological vividness. In the BB model, the memory hierarchy records the receding edge; what was once the present is now the past, preserved only because the edge has retreated from it. In all three models, the memory hierarchy functions as the repository of the objective edge's trajectory. 

Crucially, the psychological scaling of importance determines how these memories are weighted and how the "edge" is phenomenologically experienced. The scaling of importance—driven by emotional salience, cognitive load, and biological necessity—acts as a filter that selects which events leave a lasting imprint on the memory hierarchy. When an event is scaled as highly important, it resists the natural decay of the memory hierarchy, creating a "heavy" anchor in the subjective present. In GB, this scaling creates the sensation of a weighted present that is anchored to the growing edge; the important past feels solid and real precisely because the edge has grown over it. In DB, the scaling of importance slows the diminishment of important events; they linger in subjective experience longer than trivial ones, creating the illusion of a persistent past that is actively fading. In BB, the scaling of importance dictates how far the receding edge must retreat to leave an important event behind; important events create a deep psychological trench that the edge must traverse, making the retreat feel like a continuous, weighted passage. 

Because the psychological mechanisms of memory hierarchy and scaling of importance are functionally identical across all three models—acting as a filter that transforms the objective boundary of existence into a phenomenological "now"—the subjective experience of temporal flow is structurally indistinguishable. The mind cannot introspect upon the ontological direction of the edge; it can only register the transition from the unweighted future (or unexperienced past, in BB) to the weighted present, and from the weighted present to the archived past. The phenomenology of "passage" is a product of the psychological scaling of importance acting upon the memory hierarchy as it interacts with the objective edge, regardless of whether that edge is growing, diminishing, or receding. 

However, the coherence of the Backwards Block model is not secure merely because it can replicate phenomenology; it must also provide a metaphysically sound account of its own dynamics. This is where Michael Dummett’s critique of backward causation becomes profoundly relevant. Dummett, a towering figure in analytic philosophy, argued extensively that backward causation is fundamentally incoherent. His critique rests on the premise that causal relations are inherently temporal and directional: a cause must precede its effect to provide a rational basis for explanation. If an event E1 is caused by a subsequent event E2, then E2 cannot serve as an explanation for E1, because explanations require that the grounds for a phenomenon precede the phenomenon itself. To invoke a future event as the cause of a present state is to sever the link between rational inquiry and temporal order, rendering the concept of causation vacuous.

Applied to the Backwards Block model, Dummett’s critique strikes at the very heart of its explanatory coherence. The BB model posits that the ontological edge retreats from the future into the past. But why does it retreat? What causes the present to recede? If the BB model is to be more than a mere geometric postulate, it must offer a causal or explanatory mechanism for the retreat of the edge. If the retreat of the edge is caused by future states—meaning that the future determines the present's movement into the past—then the BB model is squarely in the crosshairs of Dummett’s critique. The future event (the ontological state of the retreating edge) would be the cause of the present event (the recession of the edge), violating the temporal asymmetry of rational explanation. 

One might attempt to salvage the BB model by denying that the retreat involves causation, positing instead that the edge's recession is a brute, acausal metaphysical fact. Yet, this move renders the BB model explanatorily inert. If the retreat of the edge is not caused by anything, then the BB model cannot explain why we experience temporal flow in the specific manner that we do. It merely asserts a geometric direction without providing a rationale for the phenomenology that accompanies it. In contrast, the GB model aligns seamlessly with Dummett’s constraints: the growth of the edge is driven by the passage of time itself, a forward-directed metaphysical process that does not violate the temporal order of explanation. The DB model, while positing a vanishing edge, can also be framed as a forward-directed process of ontological decay, consistent with Dummettian causality. The BB model, by requiring a retrocausal or acausal mechanism for its central dynamic, suffers from a fatal incoherence that its competitors avoid.

Despite the phenomenological equivalence between GB, DB, and BB, and the logical vulnerabilities of the BB model, a final and crucial question remains: does the psychological indistinguishability of these temporal structures subvert the evidential weight that common-sense human experience lends to traditional presentist or growing block ontologies? The answer requires a nuanced dissection of what common-sense experience actually provides as evidence.

Common-sense human experience strongly favors the Growing Block and Presentist ontologies. Our visceral, immediate sensation is that the present is ontologically privileged, that the past is fixed and real, and that the future is open and unreal. We feel as though we are standing on a growing frontier, with the past behind us and the future ahead.
