To begin, let us consider Plato's theory of forms, as presented in the *Phaedo*. According to Plato, the world we experience through our senses is merely an imperfect reflection of a higher, eternal realm of abstract Forms or Ideas. These Forms are the true and unchanging reality, and the physical world is simply a shadow or imitation of this realm. Plato argues that the Forms are the ultimate source of all being and knowledge, and that they are the basis for our understanding of the world. In the context of change and predication, Plato's theory of forms posits that particulars participate in or instantiate the Forms, and that this participation is the source of their properties and characteristics.

For example, when an individual grows taller, Plato would argue that they are participating in the Form of Tallness to a greater degree than they were previously. This participation is not a physical or material process, but rather a metaphysical one, in which the individual's essence or soul is connected to the eternal and unchanging Form of Tallness. However, this raises the question of how the individual's underlying substratum remains numerically one while admitting contrary qualities, such as being both short and tall at different times. Plato's response to this problem is to invoke the doctrine of recollection, which holds that the soul is immortal and has existed prior to birth, and that knowledge of the Forms is innate and can be recollected through philosophical contemplation.

In contrast, Aristotle's ontological framework, as presented in the *Categories*, takes a more nuanced and empirically grounded approach to the nature of reality. These substances are characterized by their essential properties and potentialities, and they undergo change and development through the realization of these potentialities. In the context of change and predication, Aristotle introduces the concepts of potentiality and actuality, which provide a mechanism for understanding how particulars transition between opposite states.

Aristotle's mechanism of potentiality and actuality provides a more cogent account of change and predication than Plato's theory of forms, as it allows for a more nuanced and empirically grounded understanding of the world.
