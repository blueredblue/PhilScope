# The Gene's-Eye View and the Lived Heart: On Whether Ultra-Darwinian Explanations of Emotion Transform Their Objects

## I. Introduction

A persistent and unresolved tension pervades contemporary debates over the nature of human emotion. On one side stands the ultra-Darwinian research program in evolutionary psychology, which claims to explain the full spectrum of human affective life—from the flush of romantic love to the searing immediacy of anger—as products of natural selection operating at the level of genes. On the other stands a tradition of philosophical reflection, most notably associated with Charles Taylor, which insists that emotions are irreducibly linguistic, evaluative, and constitutively tied to the subject's apprehension of what matters. The question that animates this essay is whether these two frameworks can coexist, or whether the ultra-Darwinian explanation, by translating emotional phenomena into the language of gene-level adaptation, fundamentally transforms—and thereby undermines—the very objects it purports to explain. If the translation of everyday emotional experience into neo-Darwinian terminology alters the qualitative nature of feelings such as anger or love, then the explanatory framework does not merely describe emotion but annihilates it, raising the troubling dialectical problem of whether an account that destroys its original target can ever be said to have successfully accounted for it at all.

This essay proceeds in four stages. First, it examines the ultra-Darwinian account of emotion as advanced by evolutionary psychology and the related designative-instrumentalist conception of language operative in sociobiology. Second, it articulates Charles Taylor's expressivist framework, with particular attention to his thesis that emotions involve subject-referring import-ascriptions and require language for their constitution. Third, it analyses the tension between these two frameworks, focusing on whether the gene-language translation alters the qualitative texture of emotional life. Finally, it resolves the dialectical problem by arguing that the ultra-Darwinian framework, while it may illuminate the causal history of emotional capacities, cannot substitute for an account of what emotions actually are for the subjects who experience them—and that this limitation is not a contingent failure of the program but a structural feature of any explanatory framework that refuses to engage with the expressivist character of emotional life.

## II. The Ultra-Darwinian Account: Emotion as Adaptive Strategy

The ultra-Darwinian approach to human emotion finds its most systematic articulation in the work of evolutionary psychologists such as Leda Cosmides and John Tooby, whose research program treats the mind as a collection of domain-specific adaptations shaped by the selective pressures of the ancestral environment. In this framework, emotions are not merely subjective states but functional mechanisms—sophisticated information-processing systems that evolved because they solved adaptive problems faced by our Pleistocene ancestors. Fear keeps organisms away from predators; disgust prevents ingestion of pathogens; jealousy guards against mate infidelity; and love facilitates pair-bonding and parental investment. The emotion is, in essence, a strategy encoded by genes and executed by neural circuitry, designed by natural selection to maximize inclusive fitness.

Richard Dawkins's seminal formulation in *The Selfish Gene* (1976) provides the metaphysical backdrop for this enterprise. On this view, organisms are vehicles for their genes, and the apparent purposefulness of biological behaviour—including the elaborate emotional lives of humans—is the product of gene-level selection. Emotions are not things in themselves but strategies of the genes that inhabit us, mechanisms by which genes ensure their own replication. The feeling of love, on this account, is not primarily about the beloved but about the propagation of shared genetic material; anger is not about injustice but about resource defence calibrated to reproductive advantage.

Crucially, this explanatory framework is accompanied by a particular conception of language that is central to how emotions are understood within sociobiology and evolutionary psychology. This is what may be termed the designative-instrumentalist view of language: language is understood as a tool—a cognitive instrument—used to designate objects, states of affairs, and goals in the environment. Language serves the organism by enabling more effective navigation of its ecological niche, by facilitating communication about resources, threats, and allies, and ultimately by enhancing reproductive success. The expressive or semantic dimensions of language are secondary to its designative function; words are labels, and sentences are commands or representations that map onto states of the world. Within this framework, the language of emotion—when we say "I am angry" or "I love you"—is itself an adaptive instrument, a signal evolved to manipulate the behaviour of others in ways that served our ancestors' fitness interests.

This designative-instrumentalist view of language has profound implications for how emotion is theorized. If language is fundamentally a tool for designating objects and serving instrumental goals, then emotions described in language are themselves instrumental states—cognitive appraisals calibrated to guide behaviour toward fitness-enhancing outcomes. The qualitative character of the emotion, the felt texture of what it is like to be angry or in love, is either epiphenomenal or is itself explained as an adaptive mechanism: the feeling motivates the behaviour that the gene needs the organism to perform.

## III. Taylor's Expressivist Framework: Emotion as Import-Ascription

Against this background, Charles Taylor's expressivist philosophy of emotion offers a radically different picture. In *Sources of the Self* (1989) and in his broader moral philosophy, Taylor argues that emotions are not merely physiological states or adaptive strategies but are constituted by language and by the subject's apprehension of significance. Emotions involve what Taylor calls import-ascriptions: the attribution of weight, value, and meaning to persons, objects, and situations within the subject's moral and evaluative horizon. To be angry is not merely to register a physiological arousal or to execute an evolved strategy for resource defence; it is to apprehend something as unjust, as having crossed a boundary that matters to one's sense of self and one's relations with others. To love is not merely to be motivated to invest in a partner's reproductive success; it is to ascribe profound significance to another person, to see the beloved as a source of meaning in a way that constitutes one's own identity.

Taylor's expressivism is grounded in a broader hermeneutic and moral philosophy that resists the naturalistic reduction of meaning to mechanism. In his account, the self is constituted by its orientations, its commitments, and its sense of what matters—these are not add-ons to a fundamentally biological organism but are the very fabric of human subjectivity. Emotions, on this view, are not causes of action in the straightforward causal sense but are expressions of the subject's evaluative framework, its understanding of what is good, what is threatening, what is precious. And this framework is not pre-linguistic or pre-conceptual; it is constituted through and by language. One cannot have the emotion of anger, in its full human richness, without the linguistic resources to articulate what one is angry about, to distinguish between justified and unjustified anger, to place one's anger within a narrative of self-understanding. Language does not merely report emotions after the fact; it is constitutive of them.

This thesis—that emotions require language for their constitution—has far-reaching consequences for the ultra-Darwinian project. If emotions are constituted by language, and if language is not merely a designative instrument but a medium through which subjects grasp significance, value, and import, then the ultra-Darwinian translation of emotion into gene-language is not merely a different level of description of the same phenomenon. It is a transformation of the phenomenon itself. The anger that the evolutionary psychologist explains as a mechanism for resource defence is not the same anger that a subject lives through when she feels that she has been wronged and that this wrong matters to her. The love that the gene-centred theorist describes as a strategy for pair-bonding and parental investment is not the love that a subject experiences as a source of meaning, as an orientation toward another person that constitutes who she is.

## IV. The Tension Between Designation and Expression

The tension between these two frameworks can be articulated with greater precision by examining the divergent roles assigned to language in each. In the designative-instrumentalist view operative in sociobiology and evolutionary psychology, language is a cognitive tool that enables the organism to represent and manipulate objects in its environment. The content of emotional language is determined by the adaptive problems that the emotion solves. When we say "I am afraid of the snake," the linguistic act designates a dangerous object, and the emotion is the mechanism by which the organism avoids it. The language of emotion is thus parasitic on a prior, non-linguistic affective state that is itself a product of genetic programming.

In Taylor's expressivist framework, by contrast, language is not merely an instrument for designating objects but the medium through which subjects constitute their emotional lives. The content of emotional language is not determined by the adaptive function of the emotion but by the subject's apprehension of what matters within her moral and evaluative horizon. When we say "I am angry," we are not merely designating a stimulus and registering a physiological response; we are articulating a sense of wrong, of violation, of something that matters to us and has been threatened. The language of emotion is not parasitic on a prior affective state but is constitutive of it. Without the linguistic resources to articulate the import of a situation—to ascribe significance—there is no emotion in the full human sense, only a physiological arousal or an animal response.

This divergence generates a fundamental philosophical problem for the ultra-Darwinian project. The ultra-Darwinian framework treats emotions as adaptive mechanisms that existed before and independently of the linguistic capacities that human beings later developed. In this picture, the emotion of anger predates the language in which it is expressed; language merely adds a communicative layer to an already existing neuro-chemical process. But Taylor's expressivism denies this picture entirely. For Taylor, emotions of the kind that human beings experience—emotions rich in evaluative content, moral significance, and self-referential meaning—are constituted by language. There is no human anger that is not already linguistically structured, no human love that is not already an articulated sense of the beloved's significance. The evolutionary psychologist's "anger" and the subject's lived anger are not the same thing at different levels of description; they are different things altogether.

This point is not merely a matter of semantic quibbling. It has direct consequences for how we understand the explanatory ambitions of evolutionary psychology. If the ultra-Darwinian framework explains anger as a gene-level strategy for resource defence, it explains something other than what the subject herself understands when she says she is angry. The framework explains the evolutionary origins of a capacity that, when actualised in human subjects, produces experiences that are qualitatively different from what the framework itself describes. The explanation, in other words, does not illuminate the target phenomenon but replaces it with a different phenomenon—one that shares causal ancestry with the target but lacks its essential character.

## V. Does Gene-Language Alter the Qualitative Nature of Feeling?

The question of whether translating emotional experience into neo-Darwinian gene language alters the qualitative nature of feelings such as anger or love can be approached through the lens of qualitative experience, or what philosophers of mind have called "phenomenology." When a subject experiences anger, there is something it is like to be angry—there is a felt quality, a subjective texture, that cannot be captured by descriptions of neural firing patterns or adaptive functions. This qualitative dimension of emotion is precisely what the ultra-Darwinian framework is most vulnerable to missing, because the framework is constructed precisely to render the qualitative dimension explanatorily irrelevant. In the ultra-Darwinian picture, the feeling is what it is because it motivates behaviour that served reproductive fitness; the qualitative character of the feeling is either a by-product of the neural mechanisms that implement the strategy or is itself part of the strategy, functioning to motivate the organism toward the adaptive response.

But this eliminativist gesture toward the qualitative dimension of emotion raises a deeper problem. If the qualitative feel of anger is nothing over and above its adaptive function, then we have no independent reason to think that the feeling of anger is what it feels like to the subject. The framework explains the behaviour but not the experience, and by treating the experience as epiphenomenal or as identical to the mechanism, it renders the subject's actual emotional life unintelligible on its own terms. The subject who says "I am angry because I have been wronged" is not reporting the output of an adaptive mechanism; she is articulating a moral conviction, an evaluative stance, a sense of what matters. The ultra-Darwinian framework cannot engage with this articulation because it has already translated the phenomenon into a register in which moral conviction, evaluative stance, and the sense of what matters have no place.

This is the core of the problem: the translation of emotion into gene language does not merely describe emotion differently; it removes the features that make emotion what it is for human subjects. The qualitative nature of anger—its felt sense of injustice, its connection to the subject's self-understanding, its place within a narrative of moral life—is not captured by the language of gene-level adaptation. And love, perhaps even more dramatically, is not captured by the language of pair-bonding strategies and parental investment. When a subject says "I love her," the import of this statement is not reducible to the adaptive function that the feeling of love may have served in our evolutionary history. The subject's love involves a commitment, an orientation, a sense of the beloved as irreplaceable and meaningful in a way that transcends any adaptive calculus. The neo-Darwinian language of genes and strategies cannot accommodate this because it operates within a vocabulary that excludes the very categories—meaning, significance, commitment—through which emotional life is lived.

## VI. The Dialectical Problem: Can an Explanatory Framework That Annihilates Its Target Succeed?

This brings us to the dialectical problem at the heart of this essay. The ultra-Darwinian framework, by translating emotional experience into gene language, appears to annihilate the phenomenon it seeks to explain. It explains the behaviour associated with emotion but not the emotion itself; it explains the adaptive history of emotional capacities but not the qualitative experience of anger, love, grief, or joy as lived by human subjects. If the framework annihilates its original target, can it be said to have successfully accounted for human emotional life? Or is it, by its own explanatory practices, self-refuting?

The dialectical structure of this problem can be made clearer by considering the following argument. Suppose we accept the ultra-Darwinian framework as providing the correct explanation of human emotion. Then, on this framework, what we call "anger" is really a gene-driven strategy for resource defence, what we call "love" is really a pair-bonding mechanism calibrated to maximise reproductive success, and what we call "grief" is really a mechanism for reallocating social investment after the loss of a significant other. But if this is what anger, love, and grief really are, then the everyday emotional life that subjects describe—their experience of being wronged, of being devoted to another, of mourning what has been lost—is not what these phenomena really are. The framework has, in effect, replaced the phenomenon with its evolutionary explanation and declared the replacement to be the genuine article.

The problem is that this replacement is not neutral. The framework does not merely offer a different perspective on the same phenomenon; it eliminates the phenomenon as it appears to subjects and replaces it with something that subjects do not recognise as their own experience. The subject who says "I love her" does not understand herself as deploying a pair-bonding mechanism; she understands herself as being oriented toward another person in a way that matters to her identity and her sense of the good. If the ultra-Darwinian framework insists that this self-understanding is false or merely epiphenomenal—merely a cultural overlay on the underlying gene-level reality—then it has not accounted for the subject's emotional life but has instead dismissed it as illusion or as a non-fundamental accompaniment to the real explanation.

This dismissal, however, generates a new problem. If the subject's emotional life is illusory or epiphenomenal, then the ultra-Darwinian framework has not explained emotion but has explained away emotion. It has shown why organisms behave as if they have emotions without showing that they actually have emotions in any sense that matters to them. The framework succeeds in explaining the behaviour but fails in explaining the experience, and by treating the experience as derivative or illusory, it fails to account for what is most central to human emotional life—its felt, lived, qualitative character.

## VII. Toward a Resolution: Complementarity Without Reduction

The resolution of this dialectical problem requires a recognition that the ultra-Darwinian framework and the expressivist framework operate at different levels and answer different questions, and that neither can substitute for the other. The ultra-Darwinian framework can explain the evolutionary origins of the capacities that make human emotional life possible: why human beings are capable of anger, love, grief, and joy in the first place, and why these capacities took the particular forms they did. This is a genuine and important explanatory achievement. It tells us about the causal history of our emotional lives, about the adaptive pressures that shaped the neural and physiological mechanisms that underpin our feelings.

But this explanatory achievement does not, and cannot, substitute for an account of what emotions are as lived experiences. The ultra-Darwinian framework explains the hardware; it does not explain the software, the user experience, the meaning that emotions have for the subjects who feel them. And this meaning—the sense that anger is about injustice, that love is about the significance of another person, that grief is about the irreplaceability of what has been lost—is precisely what Taylor's expressivist framework captures and what the ultra-Darwinian framework cannot reach.

The resolution, then, is not to choose one framework over the other but to recognise that they are complementary in a way that precludes reduction. The ultra-Darwinian explanation of the causal origins of emotional capacities does not contradict the expressivist account of what emotions are for the subjects who experience them; it merely operates at a different explanatory level. The danger—and the dialectical problem—arises only when the ultra-Darwinian framework attempts to pass from the explanatory level of causal origins to the level of lived experience, when it claims that the gene-level explanation replaces rather than supplements the subject's own understanding of its emotional life.

This is not to say that the expressivist framework is immune to its own problems. Taylor's account must grapple with the question of why emotions evolved at all, why they take the particular forms they do, and how the linguistic and evaluative dimensions of emotion relate to the biological substrates that make them possible. But these are questions of integration, not of elimination. The expressivist framework does not need to deny that emotions have biological bases or evolutionary histories; it needs only to insist that these bases and histories do not constitute the whole of what emotions are.

## VIII. Conclusion

The claim that ultra-Darwinian explanations of human emotion undermine and transform the very objects they attempt to explain is, on the analysis offered here, substantially correct—but only when the ultra-Darwinian framework oversteps the legitimate bounds of its explanatory ambition. When evolutionary psychology restricts itself to explaining the causal origins and adaptive functions of emotional capacities, it offers a valuable complement to other approaches. When it claims that this explanation replaces or exhausts the phenomenon—to say that anger is really a gene-level strategy, that love is really a pair-bonding mechanism, that grief is really a resource reallocation process—it annihilates the very thing it purports to explain. It replaces the lived, qualitative, meaning-laden emotional life of human subjects with a causal account that does not capture what it is like to feel.

The designative-instrumentalist view of language operative in sociobiology is structurally incapable of engaging with the expressivist character of emotional life because it treats language as a tool for designating objects in the service of adaptive goals, whereas Taylor's framework insists that language is the medium through which subjects constitute the significance, value, and import of their emotional worlds. The tension between these two views of language is not a contingent disagreement that can be resolved by empirical discovery; it is a fundamental philosophical divergence about what language is and what emotions are. And the resolution of this tension lies not in choosing one view over the other but in recognising that human emotional life is irreducibly dual: it is both a biological phenomenon with an evolutionary history and an expressive phenomenon constituted by language, meaning, and the subject's apprehension of what matters. An explanatory framework that refuses to acknowledge the second dimension does not account for human emotional life; it accounts for only one part of it while dismissing the other as illusion, epiphenomenon, or mere cultural superstructure. In doing so, it does not explain emotion but explains it away.
