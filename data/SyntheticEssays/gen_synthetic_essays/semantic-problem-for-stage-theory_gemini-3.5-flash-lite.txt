### 1. Introduction: The Metaphysical Metanarrative and the Linguistic Mirror

Analytic ontology has long been vexed by the problem of persistence through time: how an object can remain numerically identical while undergoing qualitative change. Endurantism posits that ordinary macroscopic objects are wholly present at every moment they exist, bearing temporal parts only derivatively, if at all. Perdurantism, or four-dimensionalism, counters that objects are four-dimensional spacetime worms extended along the temporal axis, composed of instantaneous or temporal parts. 

Within four-dimensionalism, however, a radical variant has emerged that shifts the ontological fundamentalism of the temporal worm down to the slice: *stage theory* (or *counterpart-theoretic perdurantism*), championed most rigorously by Ted Sider and Katherine Hawley. Stage theorists maintain that ordinary, everyday referring expressions—proper names like "Kant," natural kind terms, and count nouns—do not pick out temporally extended four-dimensional worms. Rather, they strictly and literally denote instantaneous temporal stages existing *exclusively* at the moment of utterance or evaluation. 

This ontological doctrine is not merely an exercise in speculative metaphysics; it makes direct, aggressive claims about the compositional semantics of natural language. Stage theorists argue that natural language is tailor-made for stage theory. They claim that surface grammar—our everyday use of tense, predication, and identity—reflects a deep underlying semantics where referring expressions denote temporary stages and trans-temporal relations are mediated by hidden, contextually restricted counterpart relations operating over moments.

Yet, this metaphysical program faces a catastrophic reckoning when confronted with the actual compositional machinery of natural language semantics. There is a fundamental, structurally ingrained mismatch between what the stage-theoretic semantic framework requires of referring expressions and what natural language syntax actually provides. Stage theory demands that referring expressions be semantically rigid, instantaneous indexicals or rigid designators of temporal slices. Natural language syntax, conversely, provides referential architecture capable of binding across vast temporal domains, integrating multiple predicates, and supporting complex aspectual and tense structures. 

This essay evaluates the semantic claims of stage theory in relation to the compositional semantics of natural language tense. I will argue that the tension between stage-theoretic ontology and natural language syntax is not merely an incidental puzzle to be solved with clever paraphrases, but an intractable structural crisis. 

Focusing on the behavior of sentences containing multiple tensed verbs predicated of a single referring expression—paradigmatically exemplified by "Kant was born and died in Königsberg"—I will demonstrate that both traditional quantificational analyses and operator-based treatments of tense fail to reconcile stage theory with compositional reality. Finally, I will assess whether the stage theorist can escape this semantic impasse without abandoning the core metaphysical thesis that everyday objects are instantaneous temporal stages. I will conclude that they cannot: stage theory can preserve its ontology only by abandoning compositional compositionality, thereby reducing natural language semantics to a species of magical non-literalism.

### 2. The Semantic Architecture of Stage Theory: Sider, Hawley, and the Ephemeral Referent

To evaluate the semantic friction generated by stage theory, we must first map its foundational semantic and metaphysical commitments. Ted Sider, in *Four Dimensionalism* (2001), and Katherine Hawley, in *How Things Persist* (2001), deploy stage theory as an ontological package deal designed to solve the puzzles of temporary intrinsics and change. 

According to Sider, when we utter the proper name "Kant" at $t_1$, the referent of "Kant" is not a four-dimensional worm stretching from 1724 to 1804. The referent is the instantaneous temporal stage of Kant located precisely at $t_1$. When we assert "Kant is wise" at $t_1$, we are attributing the property of *wisdom* directly to that instantaneous stage. 

What, then, of our trans-temporal claims? How do we make sense of sentences like "Kant wrote the *Critique of Pure Reason*," uttered today in the twenty-first century, if the referent of "Kant" is strictly instantaneous and long dead? 

Here, the stage theorist invokes David Lewis’s counterpart theory. Trans-temporal predication is parsed not via internal relations holding between the subject and times it does not inhabit, but via *counterpart relations*. The sentence "Kant wrote the *Critique*" is semantically analyzed as containing a hidden or overt modalized quantificational operator over times and stages. It translates roughly as: *There is a past stage, a temporal counterpart of the present stage denoted by "Kant," located in the eighteenth century, and that stage wrote the Critique.* 

Crucially, for Sider and Hawley, this semantic framework requires that ordinary referring expressions are semantically inflexible. They denote *stages*. The temporal dimension of a tensed sentence must therefore be borne entirely by the predicate or by explicit temporal quantifiers, while the subject term anchors the proposition to a local, instantaneous base. 

Hawley refines this by emphasizing the pragmatic and contextual variability of counterpart relations. For Hawley, persistence is not a matter of objects enduring or worms stretching, but of our linguistic and cognitive practices tracking continuity across a series of distinct, transient entities. The truth-conditions of our tensed utterances depend on contextually salient counterpart relations that link the stage present at the time of utterance to other stages in the object's career.

This yields a striking semantic claim: the syntax of natural language, which treats proper names as invariant rigid designators across tensed clauses, is fundamentally misleading at the level of deep logical form. Surface grammar groups "Kant" with invariant constants, but stage-theoretic semantics treats the name as context-sensitive, shifting its referent depending on the temporal frame of the evaluation or binding it to a local stage that must then reach out to other times via counterpart theory. 

It is precisely at this juncture that the syntactic and semantic mismatch occurs. Natural language syntax does not merely tolerate trans-temporal reference; its compositional engines are built upon the presupposition that a single argument position can be saturated by a singular term whose referent persists, or at least remains identical, across distinct temporal evaluations, without requiring a machinery of instantaneous stage-swapping and counterpart-translation at every clausal seam.

### 3. The Mismatch: What Stage Theory Requires vs. What Syntax Provides

To appreciate the depth of the crisis, we must examine the architectural divergence between the semantic demands of stage theory and the structural realities of natural language syntax. 

In standard compositional semantics (following the Fregean-Montagovian tradition), a referring expression like a proper name denotes an entity (an individual, $e$) in the domain of discourse $D$. In extensional semantics, this individual is a constant. In intensional semantics, it is a constant function from possible worlds (and times) to individuals. Crucially, the syntactic category of a proper name is $\text{NP}$ (or $\text{DP}$), and its semantic type is $e$. 

When a sentence is tensed, syntax projects functional projections above the VP—specifically, $\text{TP}$ (Tense Phrase)—where tense features are checked and semantic temporal operators or quantifiers are introduced. The compositional rule of Application allows the predicate (of type $\langle s, \langle e, t \rangle \rangle$ or $\langle e, \langle t, t \rangle \rangle$, depending on whether times are arguments or evaluation points) to combine with the individual denoted by the subject.

```
                  TP
                 /  \
               DP    T'
             (Kant) /  \
                   T     VP
                 [PAST] / \
                       V   DP
                    (died)
```

In this standard tree, the subject DP "Kant" occupies the Specifier of TP (or VP-internal subject position, subsequently raised to Spec-TP), and it is semantically evaluated *once* as the argument of the complex predicate formed by Tense and the Verb Phrase.

What does stage theory require of this syntax? If proper names denote *instantaneous temporal stages*, then the semantic value of the DP "Kant" is not an eternal or enduring individual of type $e$ (or a constant function), but a stage bound to the temporal index of the utterance or the local evaluation parameter. If "Kant" is uttered at $t_{now}$, its referent is $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$. But $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$ is an entity that exists *only* at $t_{now}$. 

This creates an immediate syntactic bottleneck. Natural language syntax allows a single occurrence of a DP to saturate argument positions across complex syntactic structures, coordinate structures, and multi-clausal environments. A single syntactic node, $\text{DP}_{\text{Kant}}$, serves as the structural argument for multiple verbs bearing different tense features. 

The mismatch is stark:
1. **The Ontological Demand of Stage Theory:** The referent of a singular term must be an instantaneous slice, ontologically locked to the temporal coordinate of its semantic introduction or context.
2. **The Syntactic Provision of Natural Language:** Syntax provides a single, unified nominal node that can bind into, or be predicated over by, multiple temporal operators simultaneously, presupposing an invariant, persisting semantic anchor across distinct temporal intervals.

When the stage theorist attempts to map their semantics onto this syntax, they are forced to postulate a radically revisionary logical form. They must claim that what looks syntactically like a single referring expression with multiple tensed predicates is actually an elliptical or compressed structure involving covert counterpart quantification, indexical shifting, or multi-staged identification rules that find no structural motivation in the syntax of natural language. 

The syntax of natural language is thoroughly *endurantist* or *worm-theoretic* in its structural presuppositions: it treats nominal expressions as persistent pegs upon which various temporal properties are hung. Stage theory’s attempt to force instantaneous stages through this syntactic needle generates semantic paralysis.

### 4. The Intractable Problem: Multiple Tensed Verbs and a Single Referring Expression

To see this semantic paralysis in sharp relief, we must analyze sentences containing multiple tensed verbs with a single referring expression. Consider the classic historical sentence:

> (1) Kant was born and died in Königsberg.

Sentence (1) is syntactically straightforward: it consists of a single subject DP ("Kant") followed by a coordinate VP ("was born ... and died ...") containing two distinct tensed verbs, each locating the respective event in a different, non-overlapping past interval. 

Let us trace how this sentence must be evaluated under the two major semantic frameworks for tense: the **Quantificational Analysis** (treating times as explicit entities or intervals quantified over in the object language) and the **Operator Analysis** (treating tense as a propositional operator, akin to modal necessity or possibility, shifting the evaluation time of the proposition).

#### A. The Quantificational Analysis of Tense

Under a standard quantificational (or Neo-Davidsonian) framework, times are explicitly represented in the logical form as arguments. A sentence is true if there exist times $t_1$ and $t_2$ (both prior to the utterance time $t_0$) such that the subject stands in the relevant relations to those times and predicates. 

Let us apply the stage-theoretic translation to (1). 
* Step 1: "Kant" refers to an instantaneous stage. Which stage? If the utterance occurs today ($t_{now}$), the name "Kant" introduces a stage $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$ (or, if we allow the name to rigidly designate a stage via counterpart theory, it picks out a stage at some contextually salient time). Let us assume with Sider that the name refers to the stage existing at the time of utterance, or a stage tied to the name's introduction.
* Step 2: The predicate involves two past temporal quantifiers. 

Attempting to compose this quantification with the stage-theoretic subject yields a type mismatch or a trivialization. 
If the subject DP denotes an instantaneous stage $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$, then $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$ *does not exist* at the birth time $t_{born}$ (1724) nor at the death time $t_{died}$ (1804). 
Therefore, when we attempt to evaluate:
$$\exists t_1 [t_1 < t_0 \land \text{Born}(\text{Kant}, t_1)] \land \exists t_2 [t_2 < t_0 \land \text{Died}(\text{Kant}, t_2)]$$

The term $\text{Kant}$ in the argument position must be saturated by an entity that exists at $t_1$ and $t_2$. But $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$ does not exist at $t_1$ or $t_2$. 

To rescue this, the stage theorist must invoke counterpart theory *inside* the quantificational structure. The logical form must be massively expanded into something like:
> *There is a past time $t_1$, and there is a stage $x$ at $t_1$ which is a counterpart of the subject-stage, such that $x$ was born in Königsberg; and there is a past time $t_2$, and there is a stage $y$ at $t_2$ which is a counterpart of the subject-stage, such that $y$ died in Königsberg.*

This sounds plausible until we examine the compositional semantics of the coordination ("was born *and* died"). In natural language syntax, coordination reductions (Conjunction Reduction) operate on syntactic constituents that share a single subject. The syntactic tree is:

```
                      TP
                     /  \
                   DP    T'
                 (Kant) /  \
                       T     VP
                     [PAST] / \
                           VP  and  VP
                          /          \
                was born in K.     died in K.
```

If "Kant" denotes an instantaneous stage, how does the single DP node distribute across two distinct past temporal quantifiers binding different counterpart stages ($x$ at $1724$ and $y$ at $1804$)? 
If the subject DP provides a *single* variable or constant to the semantic derivation, that variable must be bound by or interact with the Tense heads. 
* If the subject is evaluated relative to the matrix tense, it gets one temporal slice. 
* If the Conjunction Reduction forces the predicate to distribute over two different past intervals ($t_{1724}$ and $t_{1804}$), the stage theorist's subject term must simultaneously supply a counterpart at $1724$ and a counterpart at $1804$. 

However, standard variable-binding and quantification rules in compositional semantics do not allow a single nominal argument to generate *simultaneous, distinct counterpart chains* to two different historical epochs without massive structural ambiguity or syntactic movement (such as multi-illed pied-piping or covert copying) for which there is zero morphological or syntactic evidence. The quantificational machinery stalls because the referent of the subject is point-like, while the predicates demand distributed historical access that a single point-reference cannot structurally coordinate.

#### B. The Operator Analysis of Tense

Consider now the Operator Analysis of tense (following Priorian tense logic). Here, tense is treated as an intensional operator ($\mathbf{P}$ for "It has been the case that...", $\mathbf{F}$ for "It will be the case that...") that shifts the temporal parameter of evaluation of the proposition it modifies.

Let $\phi$ be a proposition. $\mathbf{P}\phi$ is true at time $t$ iff there is some time $t' < t$ such that $\phi$ is true at $t'$.

Let us evaluate sentence (1) under a Priorian operator analysis, combined with stage theory:
> $\mathbf{P}(\text{Kant is born in Königsberg}) \land \mathbf{P}(\text{Kant died in Königsberg})$

Let us run this through a compositional semantics where the proper name "Kant" denotes an instantaneous stage. 
At the utterance time $t_{now}$, the sentence is evaluated. The first conjunct is $\mathbf{P}(\text{Kant is born in Königsberg})$. 
The operator $\mathbf{P}$ shifts the evaluation time backward to $t_{1724}$. 
At $t_{1724}$, we evaluate the embedded clause: "Kant is born in Königsberg." 

Here lies the fatal trap of the stage-theoretic operator analysis. *What does "Kant" refer to when evaluated at $t_{1724}$?*
1. **Option A (Rigid Stage Designation):** If "Kant" rigidly designates the stage $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$ (the stage existing at the utterance time), then when the operator $\mathbf{P}$ shifts the evaluation time to $1724$, the term "Kant" *still* picks out $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$. But $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$ does not exist at $1724$. Therefore, the proposition "Kant is born in Königsberg" evaluated at $1724$ contains a subject that does not exist at $1724$. Under standard semantic assumptions (free logic or Russellian presupposition failure), the proposition is either truth-valueless or false. Consequently, $\mathbf{P}(\text{Kant is born in Königsberg})$ evaluates to *false*. Sentence (1) comes out false, which is empirically disastrous.
2. **Option B (Context-Shifted / Counterpart Stage Designation):** If the stage theorist insists that the reference of "Kant" shifts or is mediated by counterpart theory when the temporal index shifts, so that at $1724$ "Kant" refers to $\text{Kant}_{1724}$ (the birth stage), and at $1804$ "Kant" refers to $\text{Kant}_{1804}$ (the death stage), how is this achieved compositionally? 

Option B requires that the proper name "Kant" is not a rigid designator in the Kripkean sense, but a radically indexical expression whose referent covaries with the temporal parameter shifted by the tense operator $\mathbf{P}$. 

If "Kant" covaries with the temporal parameter, then within a single sentence containing *multiple* tensed verbs, we encounter an intractable clash of scope and binding. Look again at the coordinate structure:
> "Kant [ $\text{TP}_1$ was born in Königsberg ] and [ $\text{TP}_2$ died in Königsberg ]"

If the tense operators in $\text{TP}_1$ and $\text{TP}_2$ shift the evaluation time to $1724$ and $1804$ respectively, and the subject "Kant" must covary with the evaluation time of its local clause, then:
* In $\text{TP}_1$, "Kant" refers to $\text{Kant}_{1724}$.
* In $\text{TP}_2$, "Kant" refers to $\text{Kant}_{1804}$.

This destroys the semantic unity of the referring expression. In natural language, "Kant" in "Kant was born and died" is a single syntactic antecedent binding or supplying the theta-role for *both* verbs. If "Kant" refers to $\text{Kant}_{1724}$ in the first conjunct and $\text{Kant}_{1804}$ in the second conjunct, then the coordination is not joining two predicates predicated of *the same object*, but is instead conjoining two completely distinct propositions about two entirely different instantaneous entities ($\text{Kant}_{1724}$ and $\text{Kant}_{1804}$). 

If they are distinct entities, then sentence (1) is semantically equivalent to:
> *This instantaneous stage at 1724 was born in Königsberg, and that (wholly distinct) instantaneous stage at 1804 died in Königsberg.*

This completely eviscerates the identity claim that makes sentence (1) a biographical statement about *one person*. In English, the sentence attributes birth *to the same entity* that later died. But on the stage-theoretic operator analysis, $\text{Kant}_{1724}$ and $\text{Kant}_{1804}$ are distinct stages. $\text{Kant}_{1724}$ never died (it ceased to exist instantly after birth); $\text{Kant}_{1804}$ was never born (it sprang into existence instantly before dying). The sentence does not express a life history; it expresses a disjointed sequence of ontological flashes.

Thus, whether tense is analyzed quantificationally or operator-theoretically, sentences with multiple tensed verbs and a single referring expression generate an intractable semantic paradox for stage theory: either the subject fails to refer because its designated stage does not exist at the shifted time, or the subject splits into multiple disconnected stage-referents, destroying the intra-sentential identity required for a coherent semantics of singular terms.

### 5. Evaluating Potential Defenses: Can the Stage Theorist be Rescued?

Confronted with this structural impasse, a resourceful stage theorist might attempt several defensive maneuvers. Let us examine the three most plausible escape routes and evaluate their viability.

#### A. The Ellipsis and Conjunction Reduction Defense

The stage theorist might argue that sentences like (1) are merely surface-level abbreviations—derived via syntactic ellipsis from a fuller, underlying structure. The deep structure, they claim, is not:
> *Kant was born in Königsberg and [Kant] died in Königsberg.*

Rather, it is a conjunction of two independent clauses, each containing its own covert or overt pronoun or counterpart term:
> *Kant-at-1724 was born in Königsberg, and Kant-at-1804 died in Königsberg, and these two stages are connected by the appropriate counterpart relation $C$.*

**Evaluation:** This defense commits a severe methodological and syntactic error. Conjunction reduction in natural language is constrained by structural identity and semantic coreference. When we say "John walked and talked," we do not mean that *John-at-the-beginning-of-walking* walked and *John-at-the-end-of-talking* talked as two disconnected entities; we rely on the persistence of John. 

To reduce "Kant was born and died" to independent stages linked by an external counterpart relation $C$ changes the truth-conditions of identity. Furthermore, it shifts the burden from semantics to syntax without any independent syntactic motivation. There is no morphological, phonological, or distributional evidence in English (or any other natural language) that proper names contain hidden temporal indices or that coordinate VPs trigger covert splitting of nominal referents into distinct stage-constants. This is *ad hoc* metaphysics masquerading as syntax.

#### B. The Adverbial / Predicate-Modification Defense

Another strategy is to relocate the burden of persistence from the subject to the verb. The stage theorist might claim that verbs themselves encode counterpart relations or temporal tracks, so that the predicate "was born" implicitly contains a quantifier over counterparts of the subject stage.

Under this view, the semantic type of the verb phrase is modified to take a stage and search across counterpart space. The derivation looks like this: the subject stage is plugged into the predicate, and the predicate's internal mechanics reach out to other times.

**Evaluation:** While this relocates the complexity, it does not solve the coordination problem. If the predicate "was born in Königsberg" reaches out to 1724 to find a birth-stage counterpart of the subject, and the predicate "died in Königsberg" reaches out to 1804 to find a death-stage counterpart of the subject, what happens when they are conjoined under a single subject? 

If the subject is an instantaneous stage $\text{Kant}_{t_{now}}$ (at 2024), then *both* predicates must project counterpart relations backward from 2024. 
* "was born" projects backward 300 years to find a 1724 counterpart.
* "died" projects backward 220 years to find an 1804 counterpart.

This requires the counterpart relation to be radically polyadic or contextually overloaded within a single coordinate structure. More damningly, it implies that the subject stage at 2024 is the *anchor* for both counterpart chains. But this makes the present stage epistemically and semantically privileged in a way that distorts historical truth. The truth of "Kant was born in 1724" does not depend on there being a living stage of Kant in 2024; people born in 1724 can have life histories even if their current descendants or temporal successors are non-existent or radically transformed. Stage theory’s tethering of all reference to the local stage makes historical statements hostage to the local evaluation frame in a way that compositional semantics cannot naturally compute.

#### C. The Supervaluationist / Plurivalency Defense

Drawing on supervaluationist semantics, a stage theorist might suggest that proper names are plurivalent: "Kant" does not denote a single stage, but supervaluates over the entire set of stages comprising the four-dimensional worm, and a sentence is true if it is true under all admissible precisifications (i.e., for all stages).

**Evaluation:** This defense is fatal to the core metaphysical thesis of stage theory. The entire motivation for stage theory—as advanced by Sider and Hawley—is to *reject* four-dimensional worms as the primary referents of ordinary terms. Sider explicitly argues against worm theory because of the problem of temporal parts and coincidence (e.g., the cat and the cat-minus-tail). If the stage theorist retreats to supervaluationism over an underlying set of stages that jointly constitute a worm, they have surreptitiously smuggled the four-dimensional worm back through the semantic backdoor. If "Kant" denotes a set of stages or a supervaluation over a worm, then the referent *is* a temporally extended entity (or a set thereof), and the instantaneous stage ontology is abandoned at the level of semantic composition.

### 6. Conclusion: The Ontological Cost of Semantic Realism

Our evaluation has revealed a deep, unbridgeable chasm between the semantic claims of stage theory and the compositional semantics of natural language tense. 

Stage theory requires that ordinary referring expressions denote instantaneous temporal stages, treating trans-temporal predication as an artifact of counterpart-theoretic maneuvering or context-sensitive shifting. Natural language syntax and compositional semantics, however, are built on the structural presupposition that singular terms designate persistent entities capable of serving as stable semantic anchors across multiple, distinct temporal evaluations and coordinate VPs.

When confronted with sentences containing multiple tensed verbs predicated of a single referring expression—such as "Kant was born and died in Königsberg"—both quantificational and operator analyses of tense break down under the weight of stage theory. 
* Quantificational accounts founder because an instantaneous stage cannot be the shared argument of distinct past temporal quantifiers without implausible, unmotivated syntactic splitting. 
* Operator accounts founder because shifting the temporal evaluation index either leaves the local stage stranded (referring to a non-existent entity at the shifted time) or forces the proper name to indexically fracture into mutually exclusive, disconnected stage-referents, thereby destroying the intra-sentential identity that makes a biography a single life.

Can the stage theorist resolve this tension without abandoning their core metaphysical thesis? The answer is a decisive **no**. 

Every attempt to reconcile stage theory with compositional semantics either:
1. Relies on unmotivated, *ad hoc* syntactic ellipsis (pretending natural language has hidden structures it lacks),
2. Overloads the counterpart machinery until compositionality collapses, or
3. Secretly reintroduces the four-dimensional worm via supervaluationism.

Ultimately, stage theory presents a profound cautionary tale about the limits of revisionary metaphysics. Philosophers like Sider and Hawley constructed an elegant ontology to solve the paradoxes of persistence, but they did so by paying insufficient attention to the semantic engine of natural language. 

Natural language is aggressively endurantist and perdurantist-worm-friendly in its structural bones; it assumes that when we speak of Kant being born and dying, we are speaking of *one and the same enduring or extended entity*. 

By insisting that our words hook onto nothing more than instantaneous flashes of being, stage theory renders the compositional semantics of everyday tensed discourse unintelligible. The stage theorist is thus faced with an uncomfortable dilemma: either abandon the instantaneous stage ontology in favor of a semantic framework that respects the temporal persistence built into natural language syntax, or accept that stage-theoretic semantics is not a description of how natural language works, but a revisionary, error-theoretic fiction that treats our linguistic practice as a systematic illusion. In the court of compositional semantics, stage theory stands convicted of structural mismatch.
