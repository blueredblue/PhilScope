## The Necessity of Higher-Order Thought: Defending Fact Awareness as the Constitutive Condition of State Consciousness

### Introduction

The problem of consciousness is famously resistant to easy resolution, and nowhere is this more evident than in the attempt to specify the precise conditions under which a mental state becomes conscious. We can distinguish between transitive consciousness—being conscious *of* an object—and intransitive consciousness—a state being conscious *simpliciter*, such that there is something it is like for the subject to be in that state. The central question of this essay concerns the necessary and sufficient conditions for intransitive consciousness. A prominent claim, rooted in the higher-order thought (HOT) theory, asserts that a mental state is conscious only if the subject is aware of that state in a particular way—namely, via a higher-order thought that represents the state as one's own. This essay will evaluate the claim that state consciousness requires *both* object awareness and fact awareness, where object awareness is a direct acquaintance with the intentional object of the state, and fact awareness is a higher-order cognition that one is in that state. Specifically, I will address whether a HOT, understood as fact awareness, is strictly necessary alongside direct acquaintance to confer intransitive consciousness.

My thesis is that a HOT is indeed strictly necessary. I will defend this thesis against Derek Browne’s powerful objection, which draws on Fred Dretske’s distinction between object and fact awareness. Browne argues that object awareness alone is sufficient for consciousness, making the addition of fact awareness redundant and potentially leading to an infinite regress. To assess this, I will examine the counterexamples of blindsight and unconscious driving. I will argue that these phenomena, rather than refuting the HOT theory, actually provide compelling evidence *for* it, demonstrating that object awareness without fact awareness yields only unconscious information processing. Ultimately, I will critique the sufficiency of object awareness and defend the thesis that consciousness is fundamentally a HOT addition, albeit one that operates non-consciously to avoid regress, and which constitutes the "for-me-ness" that distinguishes a conscious experience from a mere informational event.

### Section 1: The Conceptual Landscape – Object Awareness, Fact Awareness, and HOT

To navigate this debate, we must first clarify the key terms. Dretske’s distinction between object awareness and fact awareness is foundational. Object awareness (O-awareness) is the perceptual or epistemic relation a subject bears to an object. When I see a red apple, I am O-aware of the apple. This is a non-propositional relation—I am aware of the *thing itself*. Fact awareness (F-awareness), by contrast, is awareness that a certain proposition is true. I am F-aware that there is a red apple in front of me, or F-aware that I am seeing the apple. F-awareness is propositional and typically involves a conceptualization of the situation.

The HOT theory of consciousness, as advanced by David Rosenthal and others, maps neatly onto this distinction. According to the standard formulation, a mental state M (e.g., a visual perception of an apple) is intransitively conscious if and only if there is a suitable higher-order thought (HOT) about M. This HOT is an F-awareness—it is the thought *that* I am in M. The HOT is not itself conscious (otherwise we would face a regress); it is a non-conscious mental state that takes M as its intentional object. The content of the HOT is something like "I am seeing a red apple." Thus, the HOT provides the fact awareness that Browne considers unnecessary.

The claim under evaluation is that state consciousness requires *both* O-awareness and F-awareness. O-awareness provides the content of the first-order state—the apple, its redness, its shape. F-awareness, in the form of the HOT, makes the subject aware of the state itself, thereby conferring intransitive consciousness upon it. The question is whether F-awareness is strictly necessary, or whether O-awareness alone can do the job.

Browne’s objection is a direct challenge to the necessity of F-awareness. He argues that when I see a red apple, the seeing is conscious simply because I am O-aware of the apple. The conscious character of the experience—its "what-it-is-likeness"—is fully determined by the relation between the subject and the object. Adding a HOT is a redundant, epiphenomenal addition. It is a separate cognitive act that comes *after* the perceptual experience, and it cannot be what makes the experience conscious, because the experience is already conscious at the moment of perception. To claim otherwise, Browne suggests, is to confuse the content of the experience with the experience itself, and to invite a regress where every HOT requires a further HOT to be conscious.

### Section 2: Browne’s Objection – The Sufficiency of Object Awareness

Browne’s argument is intuitively attractive. Consider the phenomenology of perception. When I look at a sunset, I am absorbed in the colors, the shapes, the movement. I am not thinking about my perception; I am simply perceiving. The experience feels immediate and unmediated. Browne capitalizes on this intuition: the consciousness of the perception is intrinsic to the perception itself. It is a property of the O-awareness. The fact that I might later think "I saw a beautiful sunset" is a separate, higher-order cognitive event that does not alter the fact that the perception was conscious when it occurred.

Browne draws on Dretske’s work to argue that perceptual consciousness is a form of O-awareness. Dretske himself argued that perception is a non-conceptual, non-propositional relation to the world. When I see a tree, I am aware of the tree, not of the fact that I am seeing the tree. Dretske famously stated that "seeing is not believing." To see a tree does not require me to have any beliefs or thoughts about the tree. Browne extends this to consciousness: if seeing is a form of O-awareness, and seeing is conscious, then O-awareness is sufficient for consciousness. The F-awareness—the thought that I am seeing—is a distinct cognitive achievement, typically requiring introspection or reflection, and is not a prerequisite for the perceptual state to be conscious.

Browne’s objection can be formalized as follows:
1. A mental state M is conscious iff it has a certain phenomenal character (a "what-it-is-like").
2. The phenomenal character of M is fully determined by its O-awareness content (e.g., the redness of the apple).
3. Therefore, O-awareness is sufficient for M to be conscious.
4. Adding a HOT (F-awareness) does not add any new phenomenal character; it merely adds a thought *about* M.
5. Therefore, the HOT is unnecessary for M to be conscious.

The crux of Browne’s argument is premise 2. He assumes that the phenomenal character is a property of the *content* of the state—the redness, the shape—rather than a property of the *state itself* as a vehicle of representation. This conflation is precisely where I believe Browne errs, and the counterexamples of blindsight and unconscious driving will illuminate this error.

### Section 3: The Counterexamples – Blindsight and Unconscious Driving

To test the sufficiency of O-awareness, we must look for cases where O-awareness is present but consciousness is absent, or vice versa. The phenomena of blindsight and unconscious driving provide crucial test cases.

**Blindsight**: Patients with damage to the primary visual cortex (V1) often exhibit blindsight. They report being blind in a particular region of their visual field, yet when forced to guess, they can accurately discriminate the location, orientation, and even the color of stimuli presented in that "blind" region. They can reach for objects they claim not to see, and they can navigate around obstacles. The question is: do these patients have O-awareness of the stimuli?

Browne might argue that they do not—that their ability to guess is merely a form of unconscious information processing, lacking the phenomenal character that defines O-awareness. However, this response is problematic. If we define O-awareness as necessarily involving phenomenal character, then Browne’s argument becomes trivially circular: O-awareness is sufficient for consciousness because O-awareness is defined as conscious awareness. To avoid this circularity, we must allow that O-awareness can be a purely functional or informational relation—a representation of the object without any accompanying subjective experience.

If we adopt this non-circular definition, then blindsight patients *do* have O-awareness of the stimuli. Their visual systems process the object, extract its features, and use that information to guide behavior. They are O-aware of the object in the sense that their perceptual systems represent it. Yet they are not *conscious* of it. They report no subjective experience. There is nothing it is like for them to see the stimulus. This directly refutes the sufficiency of O-awareness. The presence of O-awareness (information processing) does not entail consciousness. What is missing is the HOT—the F-awareness that would make the state conscious. The patient has a first-order perceptual state representing the object, but no higher-order thought that "I am seeing this." Without that HOT, the perceptual state remains unconscious.

Browne might retort that blindsight patients lack O-awareness because they lack the *phenomenal* O-awareness. But this is to concede the point. If O-awareness is defined as phenomenal, then it is trivially sufficient for consciousness, but it is also trivially absent in blindsight. The interesting question is whether a non-phenomenal O-awareness (information processing) can generate consciousness. Blindsight shows it cannot. The missing ingredient is precisely the F-awareness that the HOT theory posits.

**Unconscious Driving**: Consider the phenomenon of "highway hypnosis" or unconscious driving. You are driving on a familiar route, lost in thought. You approach a red light, and you brake. You stop at the intersection, and then you continue driving. Later, you have no recollection of the red light, the braking, or the intersection. Were you conscious of the red light?

The HOT theory says no. Your visual system processed the red light, triggered the motor response of braking, but there was no HOT about the perception. You did not think "I see a red light." Therefore, the perceptual state of seeing the red light was not intransitively conscious. It was an unconscious mental state that guided behavior.

Browne would likely object that you *were* conscious of the red light, just not attentive to it. He might argue that you were O-aware of the red light, and that O-awareness is sufficient for consciousness, even if you don't remember it. But this response is weak. If you were conscious of the red light, why can you not recall it? Memory is not a perfect indicator of consciousness, but the total absence of any subjective trace suggests that the experience was not consciously registered. More importantly, the phenomenology of unconscious driving is that you "come to" and realize you were on "autopilot." You were not conscious of the road, the lights, or your own actions. The information was processed, but it was not experienced.

The case of unconscious driving is a perfect illustration of O-awareness without F-awareness. You are O-aware of the red light (your visual system represents it and guides your braking), but you are not F-aware that you are seeing it (no HOT). The result is a lack of intransitive consciousness. This directly supports the HOT theory. If O-awareness alone were sufficient, then the perception of the red light would be conscious, and you would have some subjective experience of it. But you don't. The absence of the HOT explains the absence of consciousness.

These counterexamples reveal a crucial insight: O-awareness is a relation between a subject and an object, but intransitive consciousness is a property of the *mental state itself*. The fact that a state represents an object does not make that state conscious. The state must itself be represented as being in the subject. This is the role of the HOT.

### Section 4: The Vehicle/Content Distinction and the Transitivity Principle

The failure of Browne’s sufficiency thesis can be traced to a fundamental confusion between the *content* of a mental state and the *vehicle* of that content. The content of a perceptual state is the object it represents—the red apple, the red light. The vehicle is the mental state itself—the neural or functional event that carries that content. Intransitive consciousness is a property of the vehicle, not the content. When we say a state is conscious, we mean that the state itself has a certain subjective quality, not that the object it represents is conscious.

O-awareness provides the content. It tells us what the state is about. But it does not tell us anything about the state itself. The state could be a conscious experience or a mere unconscious information process. The difference lies in whether the state is *represented as being in the subject*. This is the transitivity principle: a state is conscious iff the subject is aware of that state. The subject is aware of the state via a HOT, which is an F-awareness. The HOT represents the state as one's own, thereby making it conscious.

Browne’s mistake is to think that the phenomenal character of an experience is entirely determined by its content. He thinks that the redness of the apple, when represented, *is* the phenomenal character. But this is false. The redness is a property of the apple. The experience of redness is a property of the subject. The experience of redness involves a certain qualitative feel—a "what-it-is-like" to see red. This qualitative feel is not reducible to the content (redness). It is a property of the *state* that represents redness. And this property arises only when the state is the object of a HOT.

Consider the analogy of a photograph. A photograph of a red apple contains information about the apple—its color, shape, and location. But the photograph itself is not red, nor does it have any subjective experience of redness. It is a physical object that carries information. Similarly, a mental state that represents a red apple carries information about the apple, but it is not yet a conscious experience. To become conscious, the mental state must be "illuminated" by a higher-order thought. The HOT does not add new content; it adds a new relation—the relation of the state to the subject. It makes the state *mine*.

This is why the regress objection fails. Browne argues that if a HOT is needed to make a state conscious, then the HOT itself must be conscious, requiring a further HOT, ad infinitum. But the HOT theory explicitly denies this. The HOT is not conscious. It is a non-conscious mental state that takes the first-order state as its object. The regress is stopped because we do not require a HOT about the HOT. The HOT is simply a cognitive mechanism that performs the function of making the first-order state conscious. It is like a spotlight that illuminates a stage, but the spotlight itself is not illuminated by another spotlight.

### Section 5: Defending the HOT Addition – The Constitution of Subjectivity

Having established that O-awareness is insufficient, I must now defend the positive thesis that the HOT is strictly necessary and that consciousness is fundamentally a HOT addition. The HOT does not merely accompany the first-order state; it constitutes its consciousness. What does this mean?

First, the HOT provides the "for-me-ness" or subjectivity that is essential to consciousness. A conscious state is not just a state that represents an object; it is a state that is *experienced by a subject*. The HOT represents the state as being one's own, thereby creating the subjective perspective. Without the HOT, the state is a "blind" information process—a zombie state that guides behavior but has no inner life. The HOT is what turns this zombie state into a lived experience.

Second, the HOT explains the difference between conscious and unconscious perception. In blindsight and unconscious driving, the first-order state is present, but the HOT is absent. The result is unconsciousness. When the HOT is present, the state becomes conscious. This is not an epiphenomenal addition; it is a causal and constitutive one. The HOT changes the status of the first-order state from being a mere representation to being a conscious experience.

Third, the HOT resolves the problem of phenomenal character. Browne argues that the phenomenal character is given by O-awareness. But as we saw, the phenomenal character is a property of the state, not the content. The HOT is what generates this property. When the HOT represents the first-order state, it imbues that state with a qualitative feel. The redness is not just a property of the apple; it becomes a property of the experience. The experience of redness is a complex state consisting of the first-order representation of redness plus the HOT that represents that representation. The phenomenal character emerges from this combination.
