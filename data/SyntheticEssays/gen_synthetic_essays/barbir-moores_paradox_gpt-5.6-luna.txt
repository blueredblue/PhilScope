# Moorean Absurdity, Conversational Implicature, and the Rationality of Assertion

## Introduction

Moore’s Paradox concerns a peculiar kind of utterance that appears grammatically and logically intelligible but is nevertheless somehow absurd when sincerely asserted. The most familiar examples are:

> I believe that it is raining, but it is not raining.

and:

> It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining.

Neither sentence contains an obvious formal contradiction. It is possible for someone to believe falsely that it is raining, and it is possible for someone to fail to believe what is in fact true. Yet the first sentence is difficult, perhaps impossible, to assert sincerely in ordinary circumstances. The second is equally strange, though in a different way. The problem is not simply that the speaker’s beliefs are false or inconsistent. Rather, the problem seems to lie in the relation between what a speaker says, what the speaker thereby commits themselves to, and what the speaker represents as their own mental attitude.

One proposed resolution combines G. E. Moore’s conception of implicative relations with Paul Grice’s theory of conversational implicature. On this approach, an assertion of a proposition normally implicates that the speaker believes it; conversely, an assertion of “I believe that p” may conversationally implicate that the speaker is not asserting p outright, or that the speaker lacks certainty about p. The absurdity of a Moorean sentence can then be explained as a clash between propositions and the conversational commitments generated by their assertion. The sentence “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is” implicates, through the speaker’s assertion of the belief, that the speaker is committed to the proposition that it is not raining, while the second conjunct explicitly affirms its negation.

This proposal is illuminating, but it does not by itself settle the philosophical problem. It risks treating the distinctive absurdity of Moorean utterances as merely a violation of conversational etiquette or as a failure to observe Grice’s maxim of quality. That would be inadequate. A speaker can violate a conversational maxim without producing the distinctive first-person absurdity characteristic of Moore’s examples. Moreover, the relation between first-person belief ascriptions and assertions is not exhausted by conversational implicature. Wittgenstein’s use-oriented account of psychological language suggests that “I believe that p” is not always a report about an inner state. In many contexts it functions as a hesitant, qualified, or provisional assertion of p. This use creates a tension with Grice’s implicative account: if “I believe that p” is merely a weaker assertion of p, then its absurdity in combination with “not-p” cannot be explained simply by an implicature that the speaker believes p.

The best conclusion is therefore qualified. Gricean implicature helps explain how the social and communicative context of assertion contributes to Moorean absurdity, and Moore’s notion of an implicative relation identifies an important connection between asserting a proposition and presenting oneself as believing it. But the full phenomenon cannot be reduced to a conversational implicature. The central defect in a Moorean utterance is a conflict among the speaker’s first-personal commitments as manifested in speech. This conflict is best understood as a failure of practical rationality in the activity of assertion, not necessarily as a failure of theoretical rationality in the speaker’s underlying beliefs. A person may rationally or irrationally possess a false belief, but the characteristic Moorean fault arises when the person simultaneously gives voice to commitments that cannot coherently be adopted in the same act of sincere assertion.

## 1. The Structure of Moore’s Paradox

Moore’s Paradox is commonly divided into two forms. The first is:

> p, but I do not believe that p.

The second is:

> I believe that p, but not-p.

The first-person element is crucial. “It is raining, but John does not believe that it is raining” may be perfectly informative. The speaker can consistently assert both conjuncts because the first concerns the weather and the second concerns John’s psychology. But “It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining” is peculiar when asserted by the speaker. Likewise, “I believe that it is raining, but it is not raining” can be true as a description of a false belief, yet its sincere assertion is somehow defective.

The paradox therefore concerns more than truth conditions. There is no straightforward contradiction in the proposition expressed by either sentence. The first says that p and that the speaker does not believe p. The second says that the speaker believes p and that not-p. Each conjunction could, in principle, be true. The problem is instead that the speaker cannot ordinarily assert the conjunction without undermining the conditions under which assertion is a meaningful and sincere act.

Moore himself drew attention to the fact that asserting a proposition seems to involve a special relation to belief. To assert p is not merely to utter a sentence with the truth condition p. Assertion presents p as true and presents the speaker as committed to p. The speaker who asserts “It is raining” does not merely produce a sound that has a certain semantic content. The speaker undertakes a commitment to the truth of the proposition and normally represents themselves as believing or accepting it. This relation between assertion and belief is sometimes described as implicative: assertion of p implies, in some sense, belief in p.

The relevant notion of implication must be handled carefully. It is not ordinary logical implication. The proposition “It is raining” does not entail “The speaker believes that it is raining” in the way that “All humans are mortal” and “Socrates is human” entail “Socrates is mortal.” An assertion can be insincere, deceptive, mistaken, or mechanically produced. Nor is the relation merely causal. Beliefs may cause assertions, but an assertion can be caused by many things besides belief. The relation is instead connected with the norms of assertion: sincere assertion of p ordinarily requires that the speaker believe p, or at least that the speaker present themselves as committed to p.

The Moorean difficulty arises because the speaker’s utterance simultaneously generates and cancels this commitment. In “p, but I do not believe p,” the speaker asserts p while explicitly denying the belief that normally accompanies the assertion. In “I believe p, but not-p,” the speaker asserts a belief in p while explicitly asserting not-p. The latter may appear to describe a false belief, but the first-personal assertion of belief itself makes the speaker’s stance unstable. If the speaker knows and asserts not-p, then the speaker presents themselves as not committed to p at precisely the moment when they claim to be committed to it.

The paradox is thus fundamentally first-personal and performative. It concerns what the speaker does in asserting the sentence, not merely what the sentence describes. The same words can be unproblematic in indirect discourse, quotation, historical description, or dramatic performance. This fact already indicates why a purely semantic account is insufficient.

## 2. Moore’s Implicative Relation and Grice’s Conversational Framework

Grice’s theory provides a natural way to develop Moore’s insight. According to Grice’s Cooperative Principle, participants in conversation are expected to make their contributions appropriate to the purposes and direction of the exchange. The familiar maxims include quantity, quality, relation, and manner. The maxim of quality is especially relevant:

1. Do not say what you believe to be false.
2. Do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence.

An assertion of p normally communicates not only p but also something about the speaker’s epistemic position. If I say “The train has arrived,” I ordinarily communicate that I believe the train has arrived and that I have some basis for saying so. This need not be a strict semantic entailment. It can be a conversational implicature generated by the expectation that speakers observe the maxim of quality. If I knowingly say what I believe to be false, I violate the maxim. If I say “The train has arrived” while lacking evidence, I again violate or at least disregard the normal requirements of assertion.

This framework appears to explain Moore’s Paradox. Consider:

> It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining.

The first conjunct, asserted under ordinary conditions, implicates that the speaker believes that it is raining. The second conjunct explicitly says that the speaker does not believe that it is raining. The utterance therefore produces a clash between an implicature of the first conjunct and the explicit content of the second. The result is not a contradiction in what is said in the narrow semantic sense, but a pragmatic incoherence in what is communicated.

The second form can be treated similarly:

> I believe that it is raining, but it is not raining.

Here the first conjunct explicitly asserts that the speaker believes p. The second asserts not-p. If the speaker is following the maxim of quality, the assertion of not-p implicates that the speaker believes not-p. The speaker therefore represents themselves as believing both p and not-p. Alternatively, if the second conjunct is treated as a correction of the first, the speaker asserts a belief while simultaneously withdrawing the proposition that would make the belief intelligible as a rational commitment.

This Gricean approach has several strengths. First, it explains why the sentences are not ordinary contradictions. The conflict occurs at the level of what is implicated or pragmatically committed rather than at the level of truth conditions. Second, it explains why the first-person pronoun matters. The relevant implicature concerns the speaker’s own beliefs, not beliefs attributed to someone else. Third, it connects the paradox with the social norms governing assertion. The speaker is not simply describing a psychological state; the speaker is participating in a practice that presupposes some alignment between assertion and belief.

The theory also explains why the use of “I believe” may be pragmatically significant. If someone says “I believe that the meeting is at three,” rather than simply “The meeting is at three,” the additional words often suggest uncertainty, caution, or limited evidence. Under Grice’s maxim of quantity, the speaker has supplied information that would be unnecessary if they were fully prepared to assert the embedded proposition without qualification. The listener may infer that the speaker is less than certain. “I believe p” can thus function as a hedge: it puts forward p while reducing the force of the speaker’s commitment.

That observation, however, introduces an important complication. The expression “I believe that p” may serve either as a report of a mental state or as a cautious assertion of p. The Gricean analysis must distinguish these uses rather than treating them as one fixed construction.

## 3. Wittgenstein and the Use of First-Person Belief Utterances

Wittgenstein’s philosophy of language emphasizes that the meaning of an expression is inseparable from its use in a language-game. This perspective is particularly relevant to first-person psychological utterances. “I believe that p” does not have one uniform function across all contexts. It may be a report of a standing psychological condition, a disclaimer of certainty, a prediction, an admission, a concession, or a hesitant assertion.

Suppose a witness is asked when the train will arrive. She replies:

> I believe it will arrive at six.

In this context, she may not be reporting an introspected inner object called a belief. She is offering an answer while marking it as fallible. The sentence functions approximately as “My best judgment is that it will arrive at six” or “I am inclined to say that it will arrive at six, though I may be wrong.” It is therefore an assertion, but a qualified one. Wittgenstein’s use-oriented approach encourages us to attend to this function rather than assuming that the words describe a private mental event.

This is sometimes characterized by saying that first-person belief utterances can function as hesitant assertions. The phrase “I believe” does not always place a psychological state before the listener as an object of description. Instead, it can modulate the force of the ensuing claim. The speaker puts forward p while acknowledging that the claim is not fully secure. The expression resembles “I suppose,” “I take it,” or “As far as I can tell.”

This account puts pressure on a straightforward implicative theory. If “I believe p” is a hesitant assertion of p, then its relation to p is not merely that the speaker conversationally implicates a belief in p. The expression itself may be part of the assertion’s force. It does not first state a fact about the speaker and then indirectly communicate p. Rather, it presents p as the speaker’s considered but defeasible position.

The difference matters for Moore’s second form:

> I believe that it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is.

On a purely reportive interpretation, this may seem like an ordinary description of a false belief. A person can believe that it is not raining when it is raining. But on the hesitant-assertion interpretation, the first conjunct is a qualified assertion of not-raining. The speaker is putting forward not-raining as their answer while the second conjunct immediately denies it. The absurdity results from the speaker’s simultaneous attempt to assert and retract the same claim within a single practical act of assertion.

Wittgenstein’s emphasis on use also helps explain why the utterance can be acceptable in some settings. A person might say:

> I believed it was not raining, but in fact it was.

This is unproblematic if spoken retrospectively. The tense and context indicate that the speaker is describing a past error. Similarly, one may say:

> I believe it is not raining, but I have just learned that it is.

This can be a rational correction in progress. The speaker is not simply asserting both sides as settled commitments; they are acknowledging a change or conflict in their epistemic state. The Moorean absurdity arises most sharply when the utterance is offered as a single sincere, unqualified contribution, with no explanatory context that resolves the conflict.

The use theory therefore does not replace the Gricean account, but it shows why the account must be context-sensitive. The force of “I believe p” is not fixed by a single semantic or pragmatic mechanism. In some contexts it reports a belief; in others it serves as a hedge; in still others it may be a way of distancing oneself from responsibility for p. The explanation of Moore’s Paradox must identify which use is operative in the relevant case.

## 4. The Maxim of Quality and the Problem of “I Believe p”

The maxim of quality appears to generate two different implications that can pull in opposite directions.

First, if a speaker asserts p, the maxim of quality suggests that the speaker believes p. This supports the traditional Moorean diagnosis. In asserting “It is raining,” the speaker normally represents themselves as believing that it is raining.

Second, if a speaker says “I believe p,” the presence of the phrase “I believe” may suggest that the speaker is not willing to assert p outright. Why else would the speaker use the longer and weaker expression? The maxim of quantity can generate the implicature that the speaker lacks certainty or evidence sufficient for an unqualified assertion. In this sense, “I believe p” can implicate “I am not fully certain that p.”

These two implications must not be confused. The first concerns the relation between asserting p and believing p. The second concerns the pragmatic effect of explicitly mentioning belief rather than simply asserting p. “I believe p” normally commits the speaker to believing p, but it does not necessarily commit the speaker to the truth of p. Indeed, it can conversationally indicate that the speaker is aware of the possibility that p is false.

This distinction reveals a limitation in the claim that Moore’s Paradox can be resolved simply by combining Moore and Grice. Suppose the speaker says:

> I believe that it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is.

The first conjunct may report a belief without asserting not-raining as fact. The second conjunct may be an explicit correction. If so, there is no direct contradiction between the two propositions. The speaker is saying that a belief exists and that the belief is false. Grice’s maxim of quality does not prohibit speakers from reporting false beliefs. It prohibits them from asserting what they believe to be false. If the first conjunct is merely a report of the speaker’s belief, it may be true even though the belief is false.

The crucial issue is therefore whether “I believe p” functions as a report or as an assertion. If it is a report, then the sentence can be factually coherent. If it is a hesitant assertion, then the speaker is putting forward p while simultaneously denying p. The distinctively Moorean absurdity depends on the latter function, or at least on an interaction between the two functions.

Gricean implicature can help explain why the first conjunct is heard as more than a neutral psychological report. In ordinary conversation, a speaker who says “I believe that it is not raining” is often taken to be offering not-raining as the answer to the practical question “Is it raining?” The utterance therefore carries an implicature of endorsement. But an implicature is normally cancellable. The speaker could say:

> I believe that it is not raining, although I have no reason to think that my belief is true.

That sounds odd in some contexts but not impossible. Or:

> I believe that it is not raining—that is, I have the belief, although I know it is false.

In a suitably explanatory context, the apparent conflict disappears. If the Moorean absurdity could be reduced entirely to an implicature, it should be easily cancelled in this way. Yet even when the speaker tries to cancel the ordinary implication, the utterance may retain a peculiar first-personal instability. This suggests that something stronger than a conversational implicature is involved.

The maxim of quality is therefore relevant but not sufficient. It explains why assertion ordinarily carries a commitment to belief and why deviations from that pattern are communicatively significant. It does not explain why certain combinations of psychologically and factually compatible propositions are so difficult to assert sincerely.

## 5. Is the Absurdity Merely Pragmatic?

One might respond that this conclusion places too much weight on the word “absurd.” Perhaps Moorean sentences are not philosophically deep at all. They are merely conversationally defective. The speaker violates the maxim of quality or produces a misleading implicature, and the resulting oddness is no more mysterious than saying “The door is open, but I am not claiming that the door is open.”

There is some truth in this deflationary view. Moorean absurdity is context-sensitive, and many examples become acceptable when embedded in a larger narrative. Consider:

> I believe that the road is safe, but I have been warned that it is dangerous, and I am no longer sure what to think.

This is not absurd. It reports a conflict or transition in the speaker’s epistemic state. Likewise:

> I believe that the defendant is innocent, but I acknowledge that the evidence establishes guilt beyond reasonable doubt.

This might express a personal conviction that diverges from a legal conclusion. In such contexts, the speaker’s purpose is to describe a tension, not to present a unified and unqualified commitment.

Nonetheless, the Moorean phenomenon is not exhausted by misleadingness. Many violations of Gricean maxims are perfectly intelligible. A person who knowingly says “The door is open” when it is closed violates quality, but the utterance is not paradoxical. A liar can make a false assertion without producing the peculiar self-defeating effect of “p but I do not believe p.” A speaker who is excessively vague violates manner but does not thereby create a first-personal absurdity. What distinguishes Moore’s cases is that the utterance reveals a conflict between the speaker’s act of asserting and the speaker’s representation of their own attitude toward the asserted proposition.

The contrast with lying is especially instructive. A liar may assert p while believing not-p. This violates the norm of sincerity but is not necessarily absurd. The liar’s utterance can succeed as a deceptive act precisely because the audience assumes that assertion normally expresses belief. In “p but I do not believe p,” by contrast, the speaker announces the very condition that undermines the ordinary force of the assertion. The speaker is not merely violating a norm covertly; they are explicitly presenting the violation as part of the utterance.

The problem is therefore performative. To assert p is to undertake a commitment to p, or at least to present oneself as undertaking it. To say “I do not believe p” is to reject that commitment as one’s own. The speaker attempts to perform an act whose constitutive conditions they simultaneously deny. This resembles other forms of practical self-defeat. Saying “I promise to come, but I do not intend to come” is not merely false or impolite; it undermines the act of promising. Saying “I order you to leave, but I have no authority to order you” may similarly invalidate or compromise the act. Moorean utterances have a related structure, though assertion is not identical to promising or ordering.

Grice’s theory captures the communicative symptoms of this defect, but a theory of speech-act commitment is required to explain its source. Conversational implicature is a layer of the phenomenon, not the whole foundation.

## 6. The Distinctive Absurdity of “I Believe It Is Not Raining, but It Is”

The example “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is” is especially useful because it makes the problem vivid without containing a formal contradiction. Let p be the proposition that it is raining. The sentence has the form:

> I believe not-p, but p.

At the level of truth conditions, the sentence can be true if the speaker believes not-p and p is true. False beliefs are possible. So why does the utterance sound absurd?

There are at least four contributing factors.

First, the phrase “I believe not-p” is normally used to provide the speaker’s answer to the question whether not-p. In ordinary contexts it is not a detached report like “I once believed not-p.” It is an avowal of the speaker’s present position. The speaker is presenting not-p as the proposition they accept.

Second, the assertion of p in the second conjunct normally expresses the speaker’s commitment to p. The phrase “as a matter of fact” strengthens this function by contrasting the speaker’s belief with reality and presenting p as the correction that should govern the conversation.

Third, the conjunction “but” signals a contrast that is not merely accidental. It places the speaker’s belief and the fact in opposition. The sentence therefore calls attention to a discrepancy between the speaker’s attitude and the world.

Fourth, the first-person form makes the discrepancy reflexive. The speaker is not simply describing someone who is mistaken; they are using their own speech to put forward and undermine a commitment. The utterance says, in effect: “My position is not-p; nevertheless, the position I am now asserting is p.” The audience is left without a stable interpretation of what the speaker is doing—asserting not-p, reporting not-p, retracting not-p, or asserting p while refusing to own it.

A Gricean explanation can model much of this. The first conjunct conversationally suggests that the speaker endorses not-p; the second conjunct explicitly asserts p and, under quality, suggests that the speaker endorses p. The speaker therefore communicates incompatible commitments. But the use-oriented account adds that the first conjunct may itself be a weakened assertion rather than a neutral report. The phrase “I believe” allows the speaker to put forward not-p while insulating themselves from full certainty. In the Moorean sentence, the speaker then immediately cancels even that provisional endorsement.

The absurdity is thus not simply that belief and fact diverge. Belief and fact diverge constantly without absurdity. The absurdity lies in the speaker’s attempt to use one utterance both to endorse a proposition as their own position and to reject it as false. If the speaker says “I used to believe it was not raining, but now I know it is,” the temporal structure resolves the tension. If the speaker says “I believe it is not raining, although I have been told that it is,” the utterance expresses uncertainty or conflict. But if the speaker presents both claims as simultaneous, settled contributions, the act of assertion becomes unstable.

## 7. Theoretical Rationality and Practical Rationality

The rationality question requires a distinction between theoretical and practical rationality.

Theoretical rationality concerns the formation and maintenance of beliefs in accordance with evidence, logic, and epistemic norms. A person who believes that it is not raining when it is raining may be theoretically irrational if they have overwhelming evidence that it is raining. But a false belief is not automatically irrational. A person may have reasonable but misleading evidence. The rationality of belief depends on the evidence available to the believer, not simply on whether the belief is true.

Practical rationality concerns deliberation, action, intention, and the coherent management of one’s commitments. It includes the rationality of speech as an intentional activity. A speaker should not normally represent themselves as committed to p and not-p in the same context and at the same time. The problem in Moore’s Paradox is therefore more closely connected with practical rationality than with theoretical rationality.

This claim requires qualification. If a person sincerely believes p and not-p at once, that may constitute a failure of theoretical rationality. But the distinctive Moorean utterance does not require that the speaker possess contradictory beliefs. The speaker might have only one belief, namely not-p, while knowing or asserting that p is true. In the sentence “I believe not-p, but p,” the speaker could be reporting a false belief rather than holding both p and not-p. The problem is generated by the act of assertion, not necessarily by the person’s static doxastic state.

Indeed, a speaker could possess a false but epistemically justified belief that it is not raining. If asked, “Do you believe it is raining?” the speaker might sincerely answer, “No, I believe it is not raining.” If subsequently shown the rain outside, the speaker can rationally say, “I was mistaken; I believed it was not raining, but in fact it was.” There is no Moorean absurdity in this retrospective report. The speaker is distinguishing a past belief from present knowledge.

The absurdity arises when the speaker’s utterance is understood as a present assertion of not-p together with a present assertion of p. This is a failure of practical rationality because the speaker cannot coherently use the same conversational act to undertake and reject the same commitment. The issue resembles a conflict in intention. One may have competing desires, but one cannot straightforwardly form the intention to perform an act and the intention not to perform it under the same description at the same time. Similarly, one may be psychologically conflicted, but one cannot straightforwardly assert p and deny commitment to p without producing a defective performance.

Some philosophers might object that assertion is not an action in the relevant sense. If it were merely the expression of belief, the paradox might be a theoretical inconsistency after all. But assertion is governed by norms and can be evaluated as appropriate, sincere, warranted, informative, or defective. It is an intentional act with public consequences. The speaker undertakes responsibility for the proposition and invites the audience to rely on it. This makes practical rationality unavoidable.

The Gricean maxim of quality reflects this practical dimension. It tells speakers not to say what they believe false and not to assert without evidence. But the maxim should not be understood as a complete theory of rational belief. A person may violate quality while retaining internally coherent beliefs, as in lying. Conversely, a person may obey quality while holding a false belief, provided the belief is sincere and reasonably supported. The Moorean problem concerns the practical rationality of presenting one’s commitments, not merely the truth or justification of those commitments.

## 8. Can Moore and Grice Resolve the Paradox?

The combined account can be defended if “resolve” is understood modestly. It can explain why Moorean sentences are not formal contradictions yet remain pragmatically defective. Moore’s implicative relation identifies the connection between assertion and belief. Grice explains how that connection operates in conversation through the Cooperative Principle and the maxim of quality. Wittgenstein’s use theory then clarifies why the force of “I believe p” depends on context and why it may function as a hesitant assertion rather than a report.

Together, these ideas yield a layered explanation:

1. An assertion of p normally presents the speaker as believing p.
2. The maxim of quality helps generate this presentation as a conversational expectation.
3. “I believe p” may function as a qualified assertion of p, not merely as a report of an inner state.
4. A Moorean conjunction creates a conflict among these commitments.
5. The conflict is not necessarily a truth-functional contradiction but a practical failure in the act of assertion.

This is a substantial explanation. It tells us why first-person Moorean sentences differ from third-person reports and why their absurdity disappears under certain contextual transformations. It also explains why the use of “I believe” is not philosophically innocent: it can both express commitment and weaken the force of assertion.

Nevertheless, the combined account does not fully resolve the paradox if it treats the relevant relation as only a conversational implicature. There are three reasons.

First, conversational implicatures are typically cancellable. A speaker can often explicitly withdraw an implicature without contradiction. But the Moorean defect can survive attempts at cancellation because the problem concerns the act’s structure, not merely an optional inference drawn by the hearer.

Second, the maxim of quality is too general. Its violation explains lying, joking, irony, exaggeration, and error, none of which is identical to Moorean absurdity. The maxim indicates that assertion normally expresses belief, but it does not by itself explain why explicit denial of that relation produces a distinctive paradox.

Third, first-person belief utterances have multiple uses. A theory that treats “I believe p” as uniformly implicating a particular degree of certainty or commitment will misclassify ordinary reports of belief, hedged assertions, retrospective corrections, and expressions of uncertainty. Wittgenstein’s use relation is needed to determine which speech act is being performed.

The most defensible position is therefore that Gricean implicature contributes to, but does not constitute, the explanation. The Moore-Grice proposal succeeds as a pragmatic reconstruction of the conditions under which Moorean absurdity becomes salient. It fails as a reductive account if it claims that the paradox is nothing more than a clash between cancellable conversational implications.

## 9. A More Adequate Synthesis

A better theory should distinguish three levels.

The first is the semantic level: what propositions the sentence expresses. “I believe not-p, but p” expresses a proposition about the speaker’s belief and a proposition about the world. Those propositions may be jointly true.

The second is the pragmatic level: what the utterance communicates under ordinary conversational assumptions. The speaker normally communicates that they endorse, or at least provisionally accept, the relevant proposition. Grice’s maxims explain why an audience hears “I believe not-p” as more than a detached report.

The third is the performative or normative level: what commitments the speaker undertakes in making the assertion. Assertion is not merely the transmission of a proposition. It is an act that places the speaker under a norm of consistency, sincerity, and answerability. The Moorean defect arises primarily at this level.

Wittgenstein’s contribution is to show that these levels cannot be separated mechanically from use. The same sentence may function differently in different language-games. In a psychological interview, “I believe p” may report a belief. In an examination, it may be a cautious answer. In a debate, it may be a strategic qualification. In a retrospective narrative, it may describe a past mistake. The Moorean paradox appears when the utterance is used as a present assertion while simultaneously presenting the speaker as detached from, or opposed to, the asserted proposition.

This synthesis also clarifies the role of cancellability. Some aspects of the utterance are cancellable conversational implicatures. For example, “I believe p” may implicate uncertainty, but the speaker can cancel that implication by saying, “I believe p, and I am completely certain.” Other aspects are more deeply tied to the act of assertion. A speaker may cancel the suggestion of certainty, but they cannot straightforwardly cancel the basic fact that an assertion of p presents p as the speaker’s commitment. If they say, “p, but I do not in any sense endorse p,” they have not produced an ordinary assertion of p; they have perhaps quoted, imagined, or voiced a proposition.

The distinction between assertion and mere utterance is essential. Moorean absurdity concerns sincere assertion. The words can be uttered without absurdity in a theatrical performance, a language exercise, a quotation, or a report of another person’s speech. These cases lack the relevant first-personal commitment. What fails in the Moorean case is not the linguistic sequence but the speaker’s attempt to occupy an incompatible normative position through that sequence.

## Conclusion

The claim that Moore’s Paradox can be resolved by combining Moore’s conception of implicative relations with Grice’s conversational maxims is plausible but must be stated with care. Moore identifies an important connection between assertion and belief: to assert p is ordinarily to present oneself as believing or accepting p. Grice’s Cooperative Principle, especially the maxim of quality, explains how this connection operates in conversation. It also shows why an utterance may be defective without being formally contradictory. In a Moorean sentence, explicit content and conversational commitment come apart in a way that frustrates the normal purposes of assertion.

Wittgenstein’s use relation theory complicates and improves this picture. First-person belief utterances do not have a single fixed function. “I believe p” may be a report of an inner state, but it may also be a hesitant assertion of p, a qualification of confidence, or a provisional answer. When it functions as a hesitant assertion, the sentence “I believe it is not raining, but as a matter of fact it is” becomes absurd because the speaker tries to offer not-raining as their own position while simultaneously asserting that raining is the fact. The problem is not merely that their belief is false. False beliefs are ordinary and can be reported coherently. The problem is that one speech act attempts to establish and undermine the same commitment.

For this reason, conversational implicature alone does not fully explain the distinctive absurdity. Implicatures are often cancellable, whereas the fundamental conflict in Moorean assertion concerns the constitutive or normative relation between asserting and taking responsibility for what is asserted. Grice helps explain the communicative surface of the paradox; a theory of assertion and first-personal commitment explains its deeper structure.

Finally, the problematic utterances should not automatically be classified as violations of theoretical rationality. A person may have a false but justified belief, or may coherently report a belief that conflicts with reality. The Moorean defect occurs when the speaker’s present act of assertion is practically incoherent—when they publicly undertake and reject a commitment in the same conversational performance. It is therefore best understood as a failure of practical rationality in assertion, though it may coincide with theoretical irrationality if the speaker also holds contradictory beliefs or disregards decisive evidence.

The Moore-Grice proposal thus succeeds as part of a broader account. Moore supplies the normative connection between assertion and belief; Grice explains its conversational manifestation; Wittgenstein shows that its operation depends on use. The paradox is resolved not by reducing it to a single implicature, but by recognizing that semantic content, conversational communication, and practical commitment interact in first-personal assertion.
