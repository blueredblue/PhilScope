The problem of reference has long occupied the centre of analytic philosophy, from Frege’s distinction between sense and reference to Kripke’s modal arguments and the contemporary debates over the metaphysics of language. In the mid-twentieth century, the relationship between the meaning of a natural-language expression and the object it picks out in the world was most sharply illuminated by the debate between Bertrand Russell’s Theory of Descriptions and Keith Donnellan’s influential critique. Russell argued that definite descriptions—expressions of the form ‘the F’—do not refer in the way pronouns or proper names do. Instead, they are devices of qualification that carry a logical form involving an existential quantifier, a uniqueness condition, and a predicative clause. When one says ‘the present King of France is bald’, one is not referring to a hidden referent; one is making a claim about the world: there exists exactly one King of France, and that individual is bald. If no such individual exists, the sentence is false. This elegant semantic analysis revolutionized the philosophy of language by showing how languages with seemingly referential expressions could be interpreted without invoking hidden referents. Yet, as Keith Donnellan demonstrated in his landmark 1EXTENDED ESSAY ON PHILOSOPHY OF LANGUAGE 1966 paper “Reference and Definite Descriptions,” there is a distinction to be drawn between two ways of using a definite description: the attributive use and the referential use. In the attributive use, a speaker reports a fact about whoever (or whatever) satisfies the description. In the referential use, a speaker points to a specific object and predicates it of that object, regardless of whether the description applies uniquely or truthfully to it. The question is whether Donnellan’s distinction successfully refutes Russell’s theory, and how Saul Kripke’s subsequent objection—that referential uses are purely pragmatic and therefore fail to provide genuine counterexamples to the semantic account of reference—impacts this evaluation. This essay argues that Donnellan’s distinction does not refute Russell’s theory at the level of semantic truth-conditions, but it does demonstrate that Russell’s account is incomplete as a theory of reference by exposing the pragmatic mechanisms of speaker’s reference. Consequently, while Kripke is correct that referential uses are pragmatic, this very fact proves that Russell’s theory lacks a complete account of reference because it ignores the speaker’s intention to refer and conflates semantic meaning with the act of reference, which is insufficient for a complete philosophy of language.

Russell’s Theory of Descriptions provides a rigorous semantic framework for how many noun phrases in natural language function. According to Russell, a sentence containing a definite description like ‘the F’ is not a simple referring expression with a bearer; rather, it is an assertion of existence, uniqueness, and predication. The logical form of ‘The F is G’ is: Ex(Fx & Ay(Fy → y=x) & Gy). This means there exists at least one F, for all y if y is an F then y is an equal to x, and x is G. This analysis elegantly handles sentences like ‘The present King of France is bald’ by showing them to be false, since there is no present King of France, rather than requiring a hidden referent or a failure of the word ‘is.’ However, Russell’s theory was initially formulated to handle the attributive use of descriptions, where the speaker reports a property of the object that satisfies the description. The attributive use is semantically straightforward: the sentence asserts that there is a unique object with property F and that object has property G. If the uniqueness or existence fails, the truth-value of the sentence is false. Donnellan’s contribution was to show that this is not the only way definite descriptions are used. In a referential use, a speaker uses a description to pick out a specific object in a context to talk about it. For example, if Othello kills Desdemona, and I say ‘The murderer of Desdemona is a villain,’ I am attributively saying that there is a unique murderer and that murderer is a villain. But if I am uncertain who the murderer is and I use ‘the murderer of Desdemona’ to point to Othello, saying ‘The murderer of Desdemona is a villain’ is true if Othello is a villain, even if there is no unique murderer or if the description fails to uniquely identify him in the world. The speaker’s intention is to refer to Othello. Russell’s theory cannot handle this because it ties the truth-value to the description’s satisfaction, not the speaker’s intent. This is the core of Donnellan’s challenge: the referential use bypasses Russell’s semantic constraints.

The question of truth-values under intensional contexts further illuminates the issue. An intensional context is one where the substitution of co-referential terms can change the truth-value of a sentence, such as in propositional attitude contexts like ‘George IV wished that Scott had written Waverley’ is true, but ‘The author of Waverley wished that the author of Waverley had written Waverley’ is false if we substitute coreferential terms. The description ‘the author of Waverley’ fails to refer to Scott in the second sentence because it fails the uniqueness condition semantically, even though it refers to him. In Russell’s theory, the truth-value is determined by the logical form. If the form is false (no unique author), the sentence is false. But in the referential use, we are not evaluating the description; we are evaluating the object. When I say ‘The murderer of Desdemona is a villain’ referentially, I am pointing to Othello and saying he is a villain. The truth-value depends on Othello having the property ‘villain,’ not on the description uniquely identifying him. This suggests that Russell’s theory of descriptions is incomplete because it cannot capture the truth-conditions of referential uses. It assumes a direct semantic link between the description and the truth-value that ignores the speaker’s referential intention. The speaker’s reference is an act of picking out an object, which is a pragmatic act, but it has consequences for the truth-value of the utterance. In this sense, the referential use creates a hybrid semantic-pragmatic situation where the meaning of the sentence is Russellian, but the truth-conditions are determined by the referent, not the description. This challenges Russell’s theory at the level of what a complete theory of reference must handle.

Saul Kripke, in his paper “Speaker’s Reference and Semantic Reference,” argues that referential uses are purely pragmatic. The semantic meaning of ‘the F’ is still the Russellian meaning: it means a unique F. When a speaker uses it referentially, they are using it in a way that does not strictly obey the semantic meaning. The speaker’s intention to refer to an object despite the description failing to uniquely describe it is a matter of pragmatics, not semantics. Kripke’s objection is that Donnellan’s distinction does not provide a counterexample to the semantic theory of descriptions because the semantic content of the sentence is unchanged. The semantic value of ‘the murderer of Desdemona’ is the property of being the unique murderer, or the Russellian semantic meaning. The speaker’s use of the sentence to refer to Othello is an act of reference that is not captured by the semantic theory. Kripke argues that Donnellan confuses the semantic meaning of the description with the speaker’s referential use. The semantic meaning is what determines truth-conditions. The speaker’s intention to refer is a pragmatic matter.

This leads to the central mechanism of analysis: the distinction between speaker’s reference and semantic reference is pragmatic ambiguity, not semantic ambiguity. Semantic ambiguity occurs when a sentence has multiple meanings, like ‘bank’ meaning a riverbank or a financial institution. Pragmatic ambiguity occurs when the same semantic content is used for different speech acts or in different contexts. The attributive use of ‘The F is G’ is semantically ambiguous if we consider the unexpressed uniqueness condition as part of the meaning. However, Donnellan’s distinction is not about the sentence having two meanings; it is about the speaker having two distinct goals. In the attributive use, the speaker highlights the uniqueness and makes a claim about the world. In the referential use, the speaker uses the description as a singular term to refer to an object. The semantic meaning is the same, but the function differs. This is why pragmatic ambiguity is the right framework. The meaning of the sentence does not change; the use of the sentence changes. The description ‘The F’ has a constant semantic value, but the speaker’s act of reference is pragmatic. Defending this view requires showing that natural language is inherently context-dependent and that reference is a pragmatic achievement. Russell’s theory provides truth-conditions for the attributive use. The referential use requires an additional pragmatic layer. It is not that Russell’s theory is false; it is that it is incomplete. It does not account for the speaker’s intention to refer. The act of reference is not part of the semantics of the description; it is part of the pragmatics of the utterance.

Does distinguishing speaker’s reference from semantic reference prove that Russell’s theory lacks a complete account of reference? Yes, it does. A complete account of reference in natural language must explain how speakers successfully pick out objects. Russell’s theory only explains how descriptions pick out objects given uniqueness and existence. It does not explain how speakers can use descriptions referentially without those conditions. If the speaker’s reference is a distinct phenomenon, then Russell’s theory is incomplete as a theory of reference. By showing that we can refer successfully without the semantic constraints, Donnellan and Kripke prove that reference is not purely semantic. It involves the speaker’s intention and the context. Russell’s theory of descriptions is a semantic theory, not a theory of speaker’s reference. Therefore, it lacks completeness as a theory of reference. The distinction is not a refutation of Russell’s theory of descriptions, but an expansion of it. The refutation lies in the fact that Russell’s theory was meant to be a complete account of meaning and reference, but it ignores the pragmatic dimension. Thus, it is incomplete. The essay must conclude that Russell’s theory is not refuted in its semantic domain, but it is shown to be incomplete for a full account of reference.
