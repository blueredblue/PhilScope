### The Ghost of a Future: Evaluating Don Marquis’s “Future-Like-Ours” Against Identity, Consciousness, and Rights

The moral intuition that killing is a uniquely grave wrong is among the most firmly held in human ethical life. Yet, articulating *why* it is wrong, and precisely *what* is lost, has proven remarkably elusive. Don Marquis’s celebrated “future-like-ours” (FLO) argument offers a compelling and secular answer: killing is wrong because it deprives the victim of a valuable future—a future filled with the goods of life, such as experiences, activities, projects, and enjoyments. Because a fetus possesses such a future, Marquis concludes that abortion is morally equivalent to killing an adult human being, and thus constitutes a serious moral wrong. This argument has generated a vast literature, and its central insight—that the wrongness of killing lies in the privation of a valuable future—retains a powerful intuitive appeal. However, the FLO argument is not a monolith; it is vulnerable to devastating objections concerning the nature of personal identity, the continuity of consciousness, and the grounding of moral rights. In this essay, I will evaluate Marquis’s account by analyzing the specific objections raised by Peter McInerney, Robert Lovering, and Walter Sinnott-Armstrong. I will argue that these objections collectively reveal a fatal ambiguity in the concept of a “future” that Marquis fails to resolve. By engaging directly with the thought experiments of the isolated hermit and the paraplegic victim, I will demonstrate that the normative weight of killing rests not on the mere deprivation of *unrealized possibilities*, but on the privation of an *actualized* future—a future that is psychologically owned and narratively continuous with a present self. Consequently, the dialectical tension between actual future ownership and potential future possibilities cannot be resolved in Marquis’s favor. Ultimately, while FLO may explain the wrongness of killing conscious adults, it fails to establish that abortion constitutes an immoral loss.

#### I. The Architecture of the Future-Like-Ours Argument

To understand the force of the objections, one must first appreciate the structure and ambition of Marquis’s argument. In his seminal paper, “Why Abortion is Immoral,” Marquis begins with a methodological premise: a satisfactory account of the wrongness of killing must explain why killing an adult is wrong without relying on religious dogma or speciesism. He identifies the primary wrongness of killing an adult as the loss of that individual’s future. This future is not merely a temporal sequence of biological events; it is a *valuable* future, comprising the “goods of life” that make life worth living—the projects, relationships, pleasures, and intellectual pursuits that characterize a human life. When we kill an adult, we deprive them of all these goods. The wrongness is not primarily a matter of the pain of dying, nor the grief of survivors, but the *privation* of a future that would have been valuable to the victim.

Marquis then extends this logic to the fetus. He argues that a fetus, like an adult, has a future of value. The fetus, if allowed to develop, will eventually possess all the capacities for experiences, projects, and relationships that an adult has. Therefore, abortion deprives the fetus of a future-like-ours. Since the loss of such a future is the primary wrong-making feature of killing an adult, abortion is morally wrong for the same reason. This argument is elegant because it avoids the contentious question of when the fetus becomes a “person” with rights. Instead, it focuses on the *harm* done to the fetus—the loss of its future. The argument is a *privation* argument: it is the absence of a valuable future that constitutes the harm.

Marquis’s argument relies on a specific metaphysical assumption: that the fetus is the *same entity* as the adult it would become. If the fetus is not identical to the future adult, then it cannot be said to be deprived of that adult’s future. This assumption is crucial, and it is precisely here that the objections begin to bite. For the FLO argument to work, the fetus must have a *future* in a morally robust sense—a future that it *owns*, a future that is *its* future. The objections of McInerney, Lovering, and Sinnott-Armstrong each attack different aspects of this ownership claim, revealing that the fetus’s relationship to its future is fundamentally different from an adult’s relationship to its own.

#### II. McInerney’s Challenge: The Problem of Continuous Consciousness and Personal Identity

Peter McInerney offers a powerful critique of FLO by focusing on the conditions required for an entity to *have* a future in the morally relevant sense. McInerney argues that having a future is not merely a biological fact; it is a psychological one. To be deprived of a future, one must be the kind of being that can have a future—that is, a being with a continuous conscious self that can anticipate, plan, and value its own future. A fetus, McInerney contends, lacks this psychological continuity. It is not a self-conscious subject with a narrative identity that extends into the future. It has no desires, no projects, no sense of itself as a being that persists over time.

To illustrate this, McInerney introduces the thought experiment of the isolated hermit. Imagine a person who lives entirely alone on a remote island, with no social interactions, no family, and no community. Does this hermit have a valuable future? Marquis would say yes, because the hermit still has experiences, contemplations, and perhaps simple pleasures. The hermit’s future is valuable because it is a future of conscious experience. McInerney agrees that the hermit has a valuable future. However, he uses the hermit to highlight a crucial distinction: the hermit has a future because he is a *conscious* being with a continuous stream of experience. He is a subject of experiences. His future is *his* because he is psychologically connected to it through memory, intention, and a sense of self.

The fetus, however, is not a hermit. The fetus is not a conscious subject. It lacks the neural architecture necessary for consciousness, memory, or intention. It has no psychological connection to its future. McInerney argues that the fetus’s future is merely a *biological* potential, not a *psychological* possession. The fetus is not a being that *has* a future; it is a being that *will become* a being with a future. This distinction is not merely semantic. The wrongness of killing an adult, according to McInerney, lies in the disruption of a conscious, self-narrating life. The hermit is harmed because his conscious narrative is violently terminated. The fetus, by contrast, has no narrative to terminate. Its future is a blank slate, a set of unrealized possibilities, but not a future that is *owned* by a present self.

McInerney’s objection strikes at the heart of the “ours” in “future-like-ours.” Marquis assumes that the fetus’s future is like ours because it will eventually contain the same kinds of goods. But McInerney argues that a future is only morally significant if it is tied to a present self through psychological continuity. The isolated hermit demonstrates that a future can be valuable without social interaction, but it cannot be valuable without consciousness. The fetus lacks consciousness, and therefore lacks the capacity to be deprived of a future in the way that the hermit or any adult can be. This objection forces us to ask: is the wrongness of killing the loss of *experiences* or the loss of *a self that experiences*? Marquis’s argument conflates the two. The fetus is not a self that experiences; it is merely a potential self. Thus, McInerney concludes, FLO fails to account for the fact that killing is wrong because it harms a *subject*, and the fetus is not yet a subject.

#### III. Lovering’s Distinction: Actualized Futures versus Unrealized Possibilities

Robert Lovering extends McInerney’s critique by focusing on the distinction between an *actualized* future and an *unrealized* possibility. Lovering argues that Marquis’s FLO argument conflates the wrongness of killing (which deprives an existing being of a future it *has*) with the wrongness of preventing a potential being from coming into existence (which deprives a non-existent being of a future it *might have had*). To illustrate this, Lovering introduces the thought experiment of the paraplegic victim.

Consider a man who is involved in a terrible accident that leaves him permanently paraplegic. He loses the ability to walk, run, and engage in physical activities. He is deprived of a future of mobility. Is this deprivation morally wrong? Lovering argues that it is, but the wrongness does not stem from the mere fact that he could have had a future of mobility. It stems from the fact that he *had* a past of mobility, that he *desires* to walk, and that he *experiences* the loss of this ability. The paraplegic’s future of mobility was an *actualized* possibility—it was a future that was psychologically connected to his present self through his memories, desires, and self-conception. He is a subject who experiences the loss.

Now, consider a fetus. The fetus has the potential to walk, run, and engage in physical activities. But it does not *desire* to walk, nor does it *remember* walking, nor does it *experience* the loss of this ability. The fetus’s future of mobility is an *unrealized* possibility—a mere biological potential that has never been psychologically actualized. Lovering argues that the moral weight of killing rests on the *privation of actualized futures*, not on the *deprivation of unrealized possibilities*. The paraplegic is harmed because he loses something he actually had (a self-narrative that included mobility). The fetus is not harmed in this way because it never had a self-narrative to lose.

This distinction has profound implications for the abortion debate. If the wrongness of killing rests on the privation of actualized futures, then the fetus fails the test. The fetus is not a subject with a psychological connection to its future. It is more akin to a gamete—a potential person. Lovering argues that Marquis’s FLO argument, by focusing on the *content* of the future (the goods of life) rather than the *ownership* of that future, mistakenly treats the fetus’s potential as if it were actual. The paraplegic thought experiment shows that the loss of a future is only morally significant when the victim is a conscious being who can be *deprived* of something. The fetus, lacking consciousness and a self-narrative, cannot be deprived of anything. It merely fails to realize a possibility.

Lovering’s objection is devastating because it highlights the ambiguity in Marquis’s concept of “future.” Is the wrongness of killing the loss of a *valuable future* or the loss of *a future that is actually possessed*? Marquis seems to think that the mere potential for a valuable future is sufficient. But Lovering argues that this leads to absurd conclusions. If the deprivation of unrealized possibilities is morally wrong, then contraception would be morally equivalent to abortion, since contraception prevents a potential fetus from realizing its future. This is a reductio ad absurdum for Marquis, who explicitly wants to distinguish abortion from contraception. By grounding the wrongness of killing in the *actualized* future, Lovering avoids this absurdity but also excludes the fetus from moral protection.

#### IV. Sinnott-Armstrong’s Objection: The Necessity of Moral Rights

Walter Sinnott-Armstrong approaches the FLO argument from a different angle, focusing on the concept of moral rights. He argues that even if we grant Marquis that the fetus has a valuable future, this does not automatically entail that the fetus has a *right* to that future. The wrongness of killing an adult, Sinnott-Armstrong contends, is not merely that it causes a harm (the loss of a future), but that it violates a *right to life*. Rights are normative entitlements that impose duties on others. To have a right to life, an entity must possess certain moral properties, such as the capacity for agency, rationality, or self-consciousness.

Sinnott-Armstrong uses the isolated hermit thought experiment to clarify this point. The hermit, despite being isolated from society, clearly has a right to life. If someone were to kill the hermit, they would violate his right to life, even though no one would miss him or mourn him. Why does the hermit have this right? Because he is a moral agent—a being who can make choices, pursue goals, and have a conception of his own good. The hermit’s right to life is grounded in his rational and self-conscious nature.

The fetus, however, lacks these properties. It is not a moral agent. It cannot make choices, pursue goals, or conceive of its own good. Therefore, the fetus does not have a right to life. This does not mean that the fetus is not valuable or that its destruction is not regrettable. It means that the fetus does not possess the kind of moral status that generates a *claim* against being killed. Killing an adult is wrong because it violates their right to life. Abortion does not violate a right to life because the fetus has no such right.

Sinnott-Armstrong’s objection is significant because it shifts the focus from the *harm* done to the fetus to the *rights* of the fetus. Marquis’s FLO argument is a consequentialist or harm-based argument: it claims that abortion is wrong because it causes a harm (the loss of a future). But Sinnott-Armstrong argues that harm alone is insufficient for moral wrongness. We harm many things—we might harm a tree by cutting it down—but we do not violate its rights. The wrongness of killing an adult is not just that it harms them; it is that it violates their *right* not to be killed. This right is grounded in the moral agency that adults possess and fetuses lack. Thus, even if the fetus has a valuable future, it does not have a right to that future, and so abortion is not a violation of a moral right.

#### V. The Dialectical Tension: Hermit, Paraplegic, and the Fetus

The objections of McInerney, Lovering, and Sinnott-Armstrong collectively expose a fundamental tension within Marquis’s FLO argument. To see this tension clearly, we must place the isolated hermit and the paraplegic victim in direct dialogue with the fetus. The hermit shows us that a future can be valuable even in the absence of social relationships, but it must be a future of *conscious experience*. The hermit has a valuable future because he is a conscious subject who will continue to have experiences. The paraplegic shows us that a future can be valuable even when it is diminished or altered, but it must be a future that is *actualized*—a future that is psychologically connected to a present self through memory, desire, and intention. The paraplegic is harmed because he loses a future he *had*.

The fetus, however, is neither a hermit nor a paraplegic. It is not a conscious subject, so it cannot be deprived of a future of conscious experience in the way the hermit can. It has no past of mobility or self-narrative, so it cannot be deprived of an actualized future in the way the paraplegic can. Marquis wants to treat the fetus as a sleeping adult—a being whose future is temporarily suspended but will resume upon waking. But this analogy fails. A sleeping adult is a conscious being in a temporary state of unconsciousness. They have a continuous psychological history and will wake up with their memories and self-conception intact. The fetus has no such history. It has never been conscious, and its future is not a resumption of a prior state but an *emergence* of a new one.

This reveals the core dialectical tension: the normative weight of killing rests either on the *privation of actualized futures* or on the *deprivation of unrealized possibilities*. If we accept the former, then the fetus is not harmed because it has no actualized future. If we accept the latter, then we are forced to conclude that contraception is also wrong, since it deprives a potential fetus of an unrealized possibility. Marquis cannot have it both ways. He must either bite the bullet and accept that contraception is morally equivalent to abortion, or he must concede that the fetus’s loss is not the same as an adult’s loss.

The thought experiments of the hermit and the paraplegic are crucial because they demonstrate that the *source* of moral weight is not the mere existence of a valuable future, but the *ownership* of that future by a conscious self. The hermit owns his future because he is a conscious subject. The paraplegic owns his future because he has a self-narrative that includes mobility. The fetus owns neither. Its future is a mere biological potential, a set of unrealized possibilities that have never been actualized in a conscious self. Therefore, the normative weight of killing cannot rest on the deprivation of unrealized possibilities. It must rest on the privation of actualized futures—futures that are psychologically owned by a present self.
