In the realm of Platonic philosophy, the theory of forms has been a subject of intense scrutiny and debate. The middle dialogues, particularly *Phaedo* and *Republic*, have been at the center of this discussion, with scholars arguing over the nature and characteristics of the forms. One of the most pressing issues is the claim that Plato conflates two incommensurable theories of forms: universals (UF) and standards (SF). This essay will evaluate the textual evidence for this claim, assess the Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars Objection (IA) raised by Alexander Nehamas, and examine R.S. Bluck's defense of standards as a means of reconciling the tension between sensible particulars and universals.

To begin with, it is essential to understand the two theories of forms at play. Universals (UF) refer to the idea that forms are abstract, mind-independent entities that serve as the basis for predication. In this view, forms are the common characteristics that underlie multiple particular instances, allowing us to say that, for example, multiple things are beautiful because they all participate in the form of Beauty. Standards (SF), on the other hand, involve the notion that forms are paradigms or models that particulars strive to approximate but never fully attain. This perspective emphasizes the idea that forms are perfect, immutable, and eternal, while particulars are imperfect, changing, and ephemeral.

The textual evidence in *Phaedo* and *Republic* suggests that Plato does indeed conflate these two theories of forms. In *Phaedo*, for instance, Plato discusses the form of Beauty, arguing that it is a single, eternal, and unchanging entity that underlies all beautiful things (100b-101b). This passage implies a universal understanding of forms, where Beauty serves as a common characteristic that can be predicated of multiple particulars. However, in the same dialogue, Plato also describes the form of Beauty as a perfect, immutable, and eternal paradigm that particulars strive to approximate (74d-75d). This latter description aligns with the standards (SF) theory, emphasizing the idea that forms are models that particulars can never fully attain.

Similarly, in *Republic*, Plato discusses the form of Justice, arguing that it is a universal that underlies all just actions and institutions (433b-434d). Yet, he also describes the form of Justice as a perfect, eternal, and immutable standard that particulars can only approximate (472b-473b). This dual characterization of forms raises questions about the coherence of Plato's metaphysical framework. If forms are both universals and standards, how do we reconcile the tension between the idea that particulars participate in forms through predication and the notion that particulars fall short of forms due to their imperfection?

One of the primary objections to this conflation of theories is the Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars Objection (IA) raised by Alexander Nehamas. According to Nehamas, if forms are standards that particulars strive to approximate, it becomes impossible to determine which particulars are more or less approximate to the form. This indeterminacy arises because the notion of approximation implies a degree of proximity to the form, but it is unclear how to measure or quantify this proximity. As a result, the standards (SF) theory seems to undermine the universal (UF) theory, as it becomes challenging to say which particulars participate in the form and to what extent.

R.S. Bluck's defense of standards attempts to address this objection by arguing that the standards (SF) theory is not meant to provide a precise, quantifiable measure of proximity to the form. Instead, Bluck suggests that the standards theory is meant to emphasize the idea that forms are perfect, immutable, and eternal, while particulars are imperfect, changing, and ephemeral. According to Bluck, the notion of approximation is not meant to imply a degree of proximity to the form but rather to highlight the fundamental difference between the perfect, eternal form and the imperfect, changing particular.

Bluck's defense is partially successful in reconciling the tension between the universal (UF) and standards (SF) theories. By emphasizing the qualitative difference between forms and particulars, Bluck's account helps to clarify the nature of the approximation involved in the standards theory. However, this defense does not entirely resolve the Indeterminacy of Approximate Particulars Objection (IA). Even if we accept Bluck's interpretation of the standards theory, it remains unclear how to determine which particulars are more or less approximate to the form.

Furthermore, Bluck's defense raises questions about the logical status of universals in Plato's metaphysical framework. If forms are both universals and standards, it is unclear how the universal (UF) theory can be reconciled with the idea that particulars fall short of forms due to their imperfection. The universal theory implies that forms are common characteristics that underlie multiple particular instances, but the standards theory suggests that forms are perfect, immutable, and eternal paradigms that particulars can never fully attain. This tension between the two theories threatens to undermine the coherence of Plato's metaphysical framework.
