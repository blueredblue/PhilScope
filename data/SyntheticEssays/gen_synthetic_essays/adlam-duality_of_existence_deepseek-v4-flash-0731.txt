**The Univocity of Existence: Concrete Entities, Abstract Entities, and the Limits of Linguistic Frameworks**

The rock on my desk and the number two both seem to possess being. Yet, intuitively, their modes of being appear radically different. The rock is here, in space and time, causally interacting with the world, decaying, and ultimately vanishing. The number two is nowhere—it has no spatial coordinates, no temporal duration, and no causal powers. It is eternal, necessary, and impervious to physical change. This stark contrast has fueled a persistent metaphysical intuition: that existence itself is not univocal. Perhaps “exists” means something different when applied to a rock than when applied to a number. Perhaps concrete entities and abstract entities require distinct existence criteria, grounded in the concept of mind-independence. This essay evaluates that claim. I will argue that while the *criteria* for identifying, individuating, and applying the predicate “exists” are indeed domain-relative, the *meaning* of existence itself remains strictly univocal. The appearance of a duality in existence arises from a conflation of the epistemic and semantic conditions for determining what falls under the existential quantifier with the logical content of that quantifier itself. By analyzing Rudolf Carnap’s linguistic frameworks, Peter van Inwagen’s robust defense of Quinean univocity, and the specific challenge posed by the Problem of the Many, I will demonstrate that spatiotemporal location versus location in relational abstract spaces does not generate two separate senses of existence. Rather, these are merely different kinds of properties possessed by entities that all exist in exactly the same sense—a sense adequately captured by the existential quantifier.

**I. The Intuitive Duality and the Criterion of Mind-Independence**

The initial pull toward a dualistic conception of existence stems from the perceived ontological chasm between the concrete and the abstract. Concrete entities—tables, electrons, planets—are typically characterized by spatiotemporal location, causal efficacy, and contingency. Abstract entities—numbers, sets, propositions, properties—are typically characterized by their lack of spatiotemporal location, their acausal nature, and their necessity. This distinction is often buttressed by the concept of mind-independence. A standard criterion for reality or existence, derived from a broadly realist tradition, is that something exists if and only if it is mind-independent—that is, its existence does not depend on being thought, perceived, or constructed by any mind. The rock on my desk is mind-independent; it would exist even if no conscious beings ever existed. The number two, under a Platonist interpretation, is also mind-independent; its existence is not contingent on human cognition. However, under a nominalist or conceptualist interpretation, the number two is mind-dependent—it is a construction of our cognitive apparatus, a useful fiction, or a linguistic abstraction.

The question arises: does the criterion of mind-independence generate distinct existence criteria for concrete and abstract entities? If we define existence as *mind-independent reality*, then both the rock and the number (if Platonism is true) exist in the same sense—they are both mind-independent. If we define existence as *spatiotemporal causal efficacy*, then only the rock exists, and the number does not exist at all. Neither of these options yields a dual sense of existence. They yield a univocal sense applied to different domains, or a univocal sense applied to one domain and denied to another. To get a *dual* sense, we would need to say that the rock exists *in one way* (e.g., spatiotemporally) and the number exists *in another way* (e.g., abstractly), and that these are two distinct meanings of the word “exists.”

But this move is deeply problematic. The concept of mind-independence is a *metaphysical property* of entities, not a *criterion for the meaning* of existence. Existence is a precondition for having any properties at all, including the property of being mind-independent. To say that X exists is to say that X is something, that the world includes X. Whether X is mind-independent is a further question about X’s nature, not about the meaning of “exists.” If I say “the rock exists” and “the number exists,” I am making two assertions of the same logical form: “∃x (x is a rock)” and “∃x (x is a number).” The quantifier does not care whether the variable ranges over spatiotemporal particulars or abstract objects. It simply asserts that there is at least one thing satisfying the predicate. The difference between the rock and the number lies in the predicates they satisfy, not in the quantifier that binds them. Thus, mind-independence, while crucial for determining whether a particular entity is real in a robust metaphysical sense, cannot ground a distinction in the *meaning* of existence. It can only ground a distinction in the *kinds* of things that exist, or in the *epistemic access* we have to them.

**II. Carnap’s Linguistic Frameworks: Internal and External Questions**

The most sophisticated attempt to articulate a pluralistic conception of existence comes from Rudolf Carnap in his seminal essay, “Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology.” Carnap distinguishes between two types of questions concerning existence: *internal* questions and *external* questions. An internal question is posed *within* a linguistic framework—a system of rules governing the use of certain terms, such as the framework of arithmetic, the framework of physics, or the framework of everyday objects. For example, asking “Are there prime numbers greater than 100?” is an internal question within the framework of arithmetic. The answer is trivially yes, because the rules of the framework stipulate that numbers are objects of a certain kind, and the axioms of arithmetic entail the existence of such primes. Similarly, asking “Are there electrons?” is an internal question within the framework of physics, answered by empirical investigation guided by the rules of that framework.

An external question, by contrast, is posed *about* the framework itself. For example, “Do numbers really exist?” or “Is the framework of arithmetic the correct framework to adopt?” Carnap argues that external questions are not theoretical questions with cognitive content. They are, rather, practical questions concerning the choice of a linguistic framework. We adopt frameworks based on their utility, simplicity, fruitfulness, and efficiency. The question “Do numbers exist?” when posed externally is a pseudo-question, because existence is not a property that can be predicated of a framework. Instead, we ask whether we should *use* the framework of numbers. Carnap’s central insight is that existence is framework-relative. There is no absolute, framework-independent answer to the question “What exists?” What exists depends on the linguistic framework we have adopted. Within the framework of physics, spatiotemporal entities exist. Within the framework of mathematics, abstract entities exist. There is no neutral vantage point from which to adjudicate between these frameworks, because any such vantage point would itself require a framework.

Does Carnap’s framework relativism imply that existence is not univocal? At first glance, it might seem so. If the existence of numbers is determined by the framework of arithmetic, and the existence of tables is determined by the framework of physics, then perhaps “exists” means something different in each framework. However, a closer reading of Carnap reveals that the *meaning* of existence remains constant. The existential quantifier, or the ordinary language “there is,” has a fixed logical meaning. What varies is the *domain of discourse* and the *criteria for individuation* within that domain. When I say “There are numbers,” I am asserting that the domain of the arithmetic framework contains numbers. When I say “There are tables,” I am asserting that the domain of the physical framework contains tables. In both cases, the logical form is the same: “∃x (x is a number)” and “∃x (x is a table).” The quantifier “∃” has the same meaning in both assertions. The difference lies in what counts as a member of the domain—that is, what satisfies the predicates “is a number” and “is a table.”

Carnap’s framework relativism, therefore, does not generate a dual sense of existence. Instead, it generates a *plurality of criteria* for applying the univocal concept of existence. The criteria for being a number (e.g., being a member of a certain recursively defined sequence) are radically different from the criteria for being a table (e.g., being a macroscopic physical object composed of wood). But the *meaning* of “exists” is the same: to be a value of a bound variable, to be a member of the domain of discourse. Carnap’s distinction between internal and external questions actually *reinforces* univocity. Internal questions are answered by checking whether the entity satisfies the criteria of the framework. The answer is either “yes, it exists” or “no, it does not exist.” There is no third option, no “it exists in a different sense.” The external question—whether to adopt the framework—is a practical matter, not a question about the meaning of existence. Thus, Carnap’s linguistic frameworks provide a powerful explanation for why we *feel* that abstract and concrete entities exist differently: we are switching between frameworks and noticing the different *criteria* for membership. But we are not switching between different *meanings* of “exists.”

**III. Van Inwagen’s Defense of Univocity and Quine’s Criterion**

Peter van Inwagen is perhaps the most forceful contemporary defender of the univocity of existence. His argument rests on a Quinean foundation: the meaning of existence is captured by the existential quantifier. Quine’s famous criterion—*to be is to be the value of a variable*—holds that our ontological commitments are exactly those entities over which our bound variables must range in order for our best theories to be true. If our mathematical theories require us to quantify over numbers, then we are committed to the existence of numbers. If our physical theories require us to quantify over electrons, then we are committed to the existence of electrons. The quantifier “∃” is univocal. It does not have different senses for different domains. When we say “∃x (x is an electron)” and “∃x (x is a number),” we are using the same “∃” in both cases. There is no such thing as “abstract existence” distinct from “concrete existence.” There is only existence, *simpliciter*.

Van Inwagen explicitly addresses the objection that spatiotemporal location versus location in relational abstract spaces generates two separate senses of existence. The objector claims that an electron exists *in space-time*, while the number two exists *in the sequence of natural numbers*. These are two different kinds of location, and thus two different kinds of existence. Van Inwagen’s response is devastatingly simple: location is a property of entities that exist, not a mode of existence. The number two is not *located* in space-time, but it is *related* to other numbers in a certain way—namely, by the successor relation. This relation is a fact about the number, just as the relation “being to the left of” is a fact about a table. Neither relation constitutes the *existence* of the entity; rather, both relations presuppose that the entity exists. To say that the number two exists is to say that there is a thing that has the properties of being even, being prime, and being the successor of one. To say that the table exists is to say that there is a thing that has the property of being brown, being wooden, and being located at a certain spacetime coordinate. In both cases, the entity is a bearer of properties. The difference lies in the *kinds* of properties they bear, not in the *kind* of existence they enjoy.

Van Inwagen also invokes the Kantian/Fregean insight that existence is not a predicate. If existence were a predicate, it would make sense to ask whether an object has the property of existence in addition to its other properties. But existence is not a first-order property; it is a second-order concept that asserts that a concept has an instance. The existential quantifier is the formal expression of this idea. “∃x (Fx)” asserts that the concept *F* is instantiated. It does not attribute a property to any particular object. Consequently, there is no room for a distinction between “existing concretely” and “existing abstractly.” To exist is to be an instance of a concept. Whether that concept is “being a table” or “being a number” is irrelevant to the meaning of existence. The quantifier ranges over all entities whatsoever, regardless of their ontological category.

Quine’s criterion, however, is not without its critics. Some argue that the existential quantifier is too coarse-grained to capture the nuances of existence. For instance, one might argue that the quantifier in modal logic—the possibilist quantifier—ranges over possible objects, and that this is a different sense of existence than the actualist quantifier. But van Inwagen would respond that this is a confusion between existence and *actuality*. Possible objects do not exist; they merely *might* exist. The quantifier ranges over what exists in the actual world. Similarly, one might argue that the quantifier in mathematics ranges over a different kind of object than the quantifier in physics. But this is to mistake the *domain* of the quantifier for the *meaning* of the quantifier. The domain of mathematics is the set of mathematical objects; the domain of physics is the set of physical objects. The quantifier itself is the same. It asserts that the domain is non-empty. Thus, Quine’s criterion, as defended by van Inwagen, adequately captures ontological commitments across both domains. It forces us to be explicit about what we are committed to—whether numbers or tables—but it does not force us to adopt a dualistic conception of existence.

**IV. The Problem of the Many and the Illusion of Duality**

A powerful challenge to the univocity thesis comes from a specific paradox: the Problem of the Many. Consider Tibbles the cat, sitting on a mat. Tibbles is composed of millions of individual hairs, each of which is shedding and regenerating. Now, consider the millions of “cat-candidates” that overlap with Tibbles—each one consisting of Tibbles minus a single hair, or Tibbles minus a different hair, and so on. Each of these cat-candidates has an equal claim to being a cat. They all satisfy the predicate “is a cat” in the relevant sense. But if there are millions of cat-candidates, then there are millions of cats on the mat. Yet we intuitively believe there is only one cat. How do we resolve this?

One might be tempted to argue that the Problem of the Many reveals a fundamental ambiguity in existence. If we cannot uniquely identify which cat-candidate is the *real* cat, then perhaps existence is relative to a conceptual scheme. The cat exists in the framework of everyday objects, but the cat-candidates exist in a more fine-grained framework of mereology. This would seem to support Carnap’s framework relativism and, by extension, a pluralistic conception of existence. However, this interpretation is mistaken. The Problem of the Many is a problem about *identity* and *composition*, not about *existence*. All of the cat-candidates exist. They are all values of bound variables. The quantifier “∃” applies univocally to each of them. The paradox arises when we try to determine which one is *the* cat—that is, when we try to apply the predicate “is a cat” in a way that yields a unique referent.

The standard solutions to the Problem of the Many involve either (a) adopting a vague identity relation, (b) positing that the cat-candidates are not distinct objects but rather overlapping parts of a single object, or (c) accepting that there are indeed many cats but that our counting practices are vague. None of these solutions requires abandoning the univocity of existence. In fact, they all presuppose it. If existence were equivocal, then the problem would not arise. We could simply say that the cat exists in one sense, and the cat-candidates exist in another sense, and be done with it. But this would be a cop-out. The problem is that they all exist in the same sense, and yet we cannot count them as one.

Van Inwagen’s response to the Problem of the Many is instructive. He argues that the problem arises from the vagueness of the predicate “is a cat” or, more precisely, from the vagueness of the composition relation. When we say that Tibbles is a cat, we mean that Tibbles is a composite object whose parts are arranged cat-wise. But there are many ways to carve up the matter on the mat into cat-wise arranged parts. Each carving yields a distinct composite object. However, van Inwagen argues that these composite objects are not distinct *things*; they are just different ways of describing the same underlying reality. The only things that exist, on his view, are simples (fundamental particles) and living organisms (if they have a special unity). The cat-candidates are not organisms; they are mere aggregates of simples. Therefore, they do not exist in the same sense as the cat. But this is not because existence has two meanings. It is because the cat-candidates do not satisfy the criteria for being an organism. They exist as aggregates, but they are not cats. The quantifier ranges over them, but the predicate “is a cat” applies only to the organism.

The Problem of the Many, therefore, does not reveal an irreducible duality in the meaning of existence. It reveals the vagueness of our *predicates* and the complexity of our *composition* principles. Existence itself remains univocal. The paradox is a problem for our theory of identity and parthood, not for our theory of being. If we try to solve the problem by positing that the cat-candidates exist in a “lesser” sense than the cat, we are making a category error. Existence is not a matter of degree. Either something exists or it does not. The quantifier has no degrees. The problem is that we have too many candidates for the predicate “is a cat,” and we need to decide which one, if any, satisfies it uniquely. This is a problem of semantics, not of ontology.

**V. Synthesis: The Univocity of Existence and the Relativity of Criteria**

Given the arguments above, we can now synthesize a coherent position. The claim that existence is not univocal—that concrete and abstract entities require distinct existence criteria—is false. The apparent duality arises from a failure to distinguish between the *meaning* of existence and the *criteria* for applying it. Carnap’s linguistic frameworks provide the correct account of the criteria. Within a given framework, there are rules for what counts as an entity. In the framework of physics, an entity must have spatiotemporal location and causal powers. In the framework of mathematics, an entity must be a member of a certain recursively defined structure. These are different criteria, but they are criteria for the *same* thing: being a value of a bound variable. When we ask “Do numbers exist?” we are asking whether the domain of the mathematical framework is non-empty. The answer is yes, if we adopt that framework. But the “yes” is not a different kind of “yes” than the one we give to “Do tables exist?” It is the same “yes,” affirming that the domain in question has members.

Van Inwagen’s defense of univocity, grounded in Quine’s criterion, provides the correct account of the *meaning*. Existence is captured by the existential quantifier. The quantifier is univocal. It does not vary in meaning from domain to domain. When we quantify over numbers, we are using the same logical operation as when we quantify over tables. The difference lies in the *domain* of quantification, not in the quantifier itself. The Problem of the Many, far from undermining this view, actually reinforces it. The paradox shows that our difficulties lie in the application of predicates and the principles of composition, not in the concept of existence itself. All the cat-candidates exist in the same sense; the problem is which one satisfies the predicate “is a cat.”
