# The Incommensurability of Objectivity and Subjectivity: A Philosophical Evaluation of Johannes Climacus's Thesis on Authentic Christian Faith

## Introduction

In the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript to Philosophical Fragments* (1846), Johannes Climacus—the pseudonymous architect of Søren Kierkegaard’s critique of speculative Christendom—presents a radical thesis: objective inquiry and authentic Christian faith are fundamentally, structurally, and existentially incommensurable. At a historical juncture when speculative philosophy, spearheaded by G.W.F. Hegel, sought to synthesize Christianity with systematic metaphysics, and when historical-critical scholarship endeavored to anchor eternal truths in empirical verifications, Climacus launched a counter-offensive. He argued that the pursuit of objective certainty is not merely unhelpful to Christian faith; it is its absolute annihilation.

Climacus’s thesis rests on a disjunctive view of existence. Christianity, as he understands it, is not a doctrine to be comprehended, a system to be integrated into speculative history, or a set of historical facts to be authenticated by historical-critical methods. Rather, it is a way of existing—a passionate, individual relationship to an eternal happiness that is conditioned by an absolute paradox. To introduce objective inquiry into this domain is to commit a category mistake that dissolves the very conditions under which faith becomes possible. 

This essay will critically evaluate Climacus’s thesis by exploring its structural mechanics and its existential implications. It will examine the **passion-venture argument**, analyzing how objective inquiry strips away the objective uncertainty required for the passionate inwardness of faith. It will evaluate the **inspiration argument**, detailing the epistemological limits of historical inquiry in proving the divine origin of Scripture. It will investigate the fundamental tension between attaining eternal happiness through subjective inwardness versus empirical proofs. Furthermore, it will analyze Climacus’s **philologist thought experiment** alongside his **two biblical verification scenarios** to clarify the gulf between historical approximation and eternal truth. Finally, it will construct a robust counter-argument, questioning whether increased historical certainty *inherently* destroys authentic Christian commitment, or if a more nuanced, integrated epistemology might reconcile historical inquiry with subjective faith.

## Part I: The Passion-Venture Argument and the Erasure of Uncertainty

To understand why Climacus views objective inquiry as lethal to faith, one must first grasp his definition of truth. Departing from the classical correspondence theory and the Hegelian identity of thought and being, Climacus famously declares in the *Postscript*: *"An objective uncertainty, held fast in an appropriation-process of the most passionate inwardness, is the truth, the highest truth there is for an existing person."* Truth is not an abstract proposition residing in a timeless realm; it is a quality of existence, a relation held by a subject.

This subjective definition of truth gives rise to the **passion-venture argument**. Passion, for Climacus, is not mere emotional intensity; it is the total, unreserved commitment of the self in the face of hazard. A venture—or risk—is essential to this passion. Where there is a guarantee, where the outcome is secured by objective evidence, risk evaporates. And where risk evaporates, passion dies.

Climacus applies this dynamic directly to the problem of faith. What is the object of Christian faith? It is the God-man, Jesus Christ—an absolute paradox in which the eternal, immutable God intersects with temporal, mutable humanity. Reason, operating objectively, recoils from this paradox as logically scandalous. Consequently, an objective approach to Christianity demands that we neutralize the scandal by demanding proofs, historical evidence, and systematic coherence. 

However, Climacus argues that if Christianity could be objectively proven—if its historical and metaphysical credentials could be established with the indubitable certainty of a mathematical theorem or an empirical law—then faith would be rendered logically superfluous. If a person stands before an objective certainty, they do not "believe"; they *know*. And to know is to rest in intellectual acquiescence, not in passionate inwardness.

The passion-venture argument exposes the fatal consequence of introducing objective inquiry into the religious sphere. Objective inquiry seeks to transform an existential crisis into an academic result. It attempts to accumulate probabilities, weigh manuscripts, and scrutinize miracles to arrive at a conclusion: "Therefore, Christianity is historically probable." But Climacus maintains that *approximation* is the death of faith. Faith does not live in the realm of approximation (the 51% probability or the 99% probability); faith seizes upon the absolute paradox precisely *because* it is objectively uncertain. 

Objective inquiry labors to eliminate this uncertainty. By smoothing over the rough edges of the paradox with historical rationalizations and systematic categories, the objective investigator strips away the objective uncertainty. And by stripping away the objective uncertainty, the investigator removes the abyss over which faith must hover. Without the objective uncertainty, there is no longer any need for a venture. Without a venture, there is no risk. And without risk, the passion of inwardness—which *is* the relationship to the eternal—collapses into a mundane, bourgeois complacency. Thus, objective inquiry does not lead us deeper into faith; it replaces faith with epistemology, substituting the living, trembling soul with the dispassionate academic observer.

## Part II: The Inspiration Argument and the Limits of Historical Inquiry

If the passion-venture argument addresses the psychological and existential dynamics of faith, the **inspiration argument** targets its epistemological foundations, specifically regarding the authority of Scripture. During the nineteenth century, historical-critical theology was rising to prominence, seeking to secure the truth of Christian doctrine by establishing the historical reliability, authorship, and divine inspiration of the biblical texts through rigorous historical analysis.

Climacus dismisses this entire enterprise as a category error of monumental proportions. The core of his inspiration argument is deceptively simple: historical inquiry can never, under any methodological conditions, yield an eternal, infinite result. 

History deals with the realm of the contingent, the particular, the empirical, and the approximate. Every historical conclusion is provisional, subject to revision upon the discovery of new manuscripts, archaeological finds, or shifts in hermeneutical paradigms. Historical evidence can, at best, establish probabilities. It can tell us that a man named Jesus lived in first-century Palestine, that he was crucified under Pontius Pilate, and that his early followers made extraordinary claims about his resurrection. But historical inquiry can *never* bridge the infinite qualitative distinction between the finite and the infinite, the temporal and the eternal.

To claim that a historical text is *divinely inspired* is to make an absolute, qualitative claim that transcends the horizontal plane of empirical history. Inspiration is not a property that can be observed under a historical microscope. One cannot deduce the divine authorship of a text by counting sources, verifying historical dates, or analyzing literary styles. As Climacus points out, even if a historian could prove with 100% certainty every single historical assertion in the Bible—even if every event recorded were corroborated by independent contemporary archives—this would still only prove that the events happened and that the texts are historically accurate. It would *not* prove that the text is the Word of God.

To bridge this gap by treating historical probability as a substitute for faith is what Climacus calls a "comic" misunderstanding. People delude themselves into thinking that if they can just gather enough historical evidence, they will have a rational justification for believing the Bible is inspired. But Climacus argues that this approach rests on a misunderstanding of what eternity demands. An eternal happiness cannot be hitched to the shifting sands of historical research. If my eternal salvation depends on the results of historical-critical scholarship, then my faith is held hostage by the academic guild. If a new scroll is unearthed that complicates our understanding of a Pauline epistle, does my eternal salvation suddenly waver? Such a scenario is absurd to Climacus. 

The inspiration argument thus exposes the impotence of historical inquiry before the demands of faith. History can offer approximations, but faith requires an absolute relationship to the absolute. By attempting to use historical inquiry to prove the divinity of Scripture, the theologian commits a category error, treating an eternal, existential demand as if it were a historical hypothesis awaiting peer review.

## Part III: Eternal Happiness, Subjective Inwardness, and the Rejection of Empirical Proofs

At the center of Climacus’s philosophical anthropology lies the concept of eternal happiness (*Salighed*) and the absolute difference between how one relates to mundane truths versus ultimate truth. This framework forms the bedrock of his rejection of empirical proofs.

Climacus famously distinguishes between two types of truth and two corresponding orientations of the self: the objective orientation and the subjective orientation. 

When dealing with objective truths—such as mathematics, empirical sciences, or historical data—the object is primary. The goal of the knower is to abstract themselves from the equation, to look away from their own subjective state, and to let the object dictate the conclusion. In this realm, approximation, mediation, and accumulation are virtues. If I want to know the distance to the sun, I do not gaze into my own soul with passionate inwardness; I deploy telescopes, calculate parallax, and accumulate empirical data. My subjective passion is entirely irrelevant to the truth of the calculation.

However, when the truth in question is *eternal happiness*—the absolute good that concerns the entirety of an existing human being across eternity—the rules of the game undergo an inversion of Copernican proportions. Eternal happiness is not an object that can be placed on a laboratory table, dissected, weighed, or logically deduced. It is an existential reality that concerns the subject’s absolute relationship to the absolute.

Because eternal happiness is infinite and subjective, it cannot be approached through the accretion of empirical proofs. Climacus argues that if eternal happiness were dependent on empirical proofs, then access to the divine would become a matter of intellectual privilege. The brilliant scholar, the erudite historian, and the speculative philosopher would have a monopoly on salvation, while the uneducated, the simple, and the intellectually disabled would be disenfranchised. Christianity, however, is radically egalitarian; it addresses every human being *as* an existing individual, regardless of their cultural, intellectual, or historical positioning.

Empirical proofs fail because they operate on the plane of immanence. They try to sneak up on eternity by way of finite increments. Climacus insists that eternity can only be entered through an absolute break—a leap of faith that abandons the safety net of objective verification. Subjective inwardness is the very antithesis of empirical proof. It is the intensification of the self’s awareness of its own sinfulness, finitude, and absolute dependence on the eternal. 

To seek empirical proofs for Christianity is, for Climacus, a symptom of spiritual immaturity or evasion. It is a cowardly attempt to avoid the terror and the glory of existing before God without a safety net. The person who demands empirical proofs wants to make a calculated business transaction with the Almighty: "Show me the audited financial statements of the universe, provide me with undeniable historical notarizations of the resurrection, and then I will invest my faith." But Climacus counters that God refuses to negotiate on those terms. The divine incognito requires that the objective support be relentlessly withdrawn, forcing the individual back upon themselves, into the agonizing and exhilarating solitude of subjective inwardness.

## Part IV: The Philologist Thought Experiment and the Two Verification Scenarios

To dramatize the absurdity of grounding eternal faith in historical inquiry, Climacus constructs a series of brilliant thought experiments in the *Postscript*, most notably the **philologist thought experiment** and the **two verification scenarios** regarding Christ’s contemporaneity and historical witness.

### The Philologist Thought Experiment

Imagine a philologist, a scholar of ancient languages and texts, who devotes a lifetime to the exhaustive study of the New Testament. This scholar applies the most rigorous historical-critical methods available. They master Koine Greek, analyze textual variants, reconstruct historical contexts, and successfully demonstrate, with an unprecedented degree of historical probability, that the Gospels are authentic, reliable documents composed by eyewitnesses or their close associates. 

What has the philologist achieved? They have achieved a *historical result*. They have transformed a collection of ancient documents into a highly probable historical account. But does this scholarly triumph bring the philologist one millimeter closer to eternal happiness? Does historical erudition constitute authentic Christian faith?

Climacus answers with a resounding negative. The philologist may know more about the historical background of first-century Palestine than the Apostles themselves did, yet remain entirely untouched by the existential demand of the Gospel. Indeed, Climacus suggests a profound irony: while the scholar is busy examining the historical wrapping, they may completely miss the eternal gift inside. The philologist treats the Gospel as an *object* of study rather than the *subject* of existence. The scholar stands outside the text, evaluating it, judging it, and categorizing it, whereas faith requires standing *inside* the text, letting oneself be judged, challenged, and transformed by it. The philologist thought experiment thus illustrates the profound disconnect between historical scholarship and existential appropriation.

### The Two Verification Scenarios

Climacus further sharpens his critique by examining what happens when people dream of having lived contemporaneously with Jesus. A common sentiment in religious reflection is: *"If only I had lived in first-century Palestine. If only I had walked the dusty roads of Galilee, seen Jesus heal the sick, watched him calm the storm, and witnessed him emerge from the tomb with my own eyes—then I would have no trouble believing. Then I would have the ultimate empirical proof!"*

Climacus exposes the radical self-deception in this longing through his analysis of historical contemporaneity. He argues that being contemporary with Jesus does not make faith *easier*; it actually makes it infinitely *harder* or, at the very least, introduces the exact same qualitative demand for subjective inwardness.

Climacus outlines two scenarios regarding historical witnesses to Christ:

1. **The Contemporary Witness:** Imagine an individual who stands at the foot of the cross, watching Jesus suffer and die. What does the naked eye see? They see a broken, bloodied criminal executed by the Roman state. Objectively, empirically, historically, there is nothing divine to be seen. The incognito of God is absolute. The man on the cross looks like any other crucified insurrectionist. To move from seeing a dying man to confessing *"Truly this man was the Son of God"* requires precisely the same leap of faith—the same overcoming of objective uncertainty through passionate inwardness—as it does for someone living two thousand years later reading a disputed text. The contemporary witness has no advantage over the modern believer because the divine essence is, by definition, invisible to empirical observation.
2. **The Subsequent Believer:** The person living centuries later possesses only historical reports, which are infinitely mediated, fragmented, and uncertain. Yet, this temporal distance actually strips away the seductive distractions of immediate physical presence. The subsequent believer is confronted with the pure, naked paradox: an eternal claim mediated through radically uncertain historical reports. Because the historical certainty is so minimal, the space for subjective inwardness—for the passion-venture of faith—is maximally opened.

Therefore, Climacus argues, the dream of empirical verification through contemporaneity is an illusion. If physical sight could produce faith, then the soldiers who mocked Jesus, the Pharisees who plotted his death, and the crowds who shouted "Crucify him!" would have been believers *par excellence*. But physical sight produced only cynicism, indifference, or hostility. Faith is never the product of empirical observation; it is always and everywhere an act of inward appropriation, an existential uprising against the tyranny of the visible.

## Part V: Counter-Argument: Can Historical Certainty Coexist with Authentic Commitment?

Climacus’s thesis is breathtaking in its consistency and its existential rigor, but it invites a profound philosophical challenge. We must ask: **Does increased historical certainty *inherently* destroy authentic Christian commitment?** Is Climacus’s disjunction between objective inquiry and subjective faith a false dichotomy, or does it rest on an overly narrow, hyper-fideistic epistemology?

### The Critique of Hyper-Fideism

Critics of Climacus—ranging from moderate rationalists to historical apologists—argue that his absolute separation of history and faith creates a dangerous form of fideism that severs Christianity from the real world. If faith requires objective uncertainty to the degree that any historical grounding is destructive, does faith not collapse into irrationalism or arbitrary projection? 

Consider the structure of historical knowledge. Historical events *do* matter to Christianity. As the Apostle Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 15:14, *"And if Christ has not been raised, our preaching is useless and so is your faith."* Christianity anchors its truth claims not in timeless myths or abstract philosophical principles, but in specific, real-world historical events: the incarnation, the crucifixion, and the resurrection. 

If these events are entirely detached from historical inquiry—if historical-critical research can neither support nor undermine them without threatening faith—then Christianity begins to look less like an engagement with reality and more like a subjective therapeutic fiction. A faith that is indifferent to whether its foundational events actually occurred in space and time risks degenerating into a purely internal psychological state, a form of spiritual romanticism.

### A Reformed Epistemological Integration

To counter Climacus without falling into the trap of Hegelian rationalism, one can adopt a more nuanced epistemological framework, such as the one advanced by contemporary Reformed epistemologists (e.g., Alvin Plantinga) or historical theologians who reject Climacus’s stark dichotomy.

One can argue that **objective inquiry and subjective faith are not mutually exclusive, but exist in a dialectical partnership.** Historical inquiry does not need to *prove* Christianity with mathematical certainty to be of service to faith. Rather, historical inquiry can function as an *excavator* and a *clearcutter*, removing false historical claims, dismantling fraudulent mythologies, and establishing a baseline of historical plausibility. 

When a historian establishes that the early Christian movement erupted with extraordinary speed in Jerusalem just weeks after the crucifixion, proclaiming a risen Lord, this historical datum does not *compel* faith (thus preserving Climacus’s insight that faith cannot be logically coerced). However, it *supports* the rationality of faith by showing that the Christian narrative is not a late, gnostic myth manufactured centuries later, but a primitive testimony rooted in the eyewitness experience of real people.

Furthermore, one can challenge Climacus’s assumption that certainty instantly kills passion. Is it true that knowing something with a high degree of historical or intellectual assurance makes passionate appropriation impossible? 

Let us consider an analogy from human relationships. Imagine a person deeply in love. Do they love their spouse *better* if they maintain a posture of radical, objective uncertainty regarding whether their spouse actually loves them back or even exists? Does the passionate commitment of marriage require that the husband constantly doubt whether his wife is an actual human being or an elaborate hallucination? Of course not. In interpersonal relationships, objective knowledge—knowing someone’s history, verifying their actions, observing their character over years—deepens, rather than destroys, passionate commitment. 

Why should the divine-human relationship be constructed on a fundamentally different model? Why must God hide behind a veil of radical historical ambiguity to be loved passionately? A robust counter-argument suggests that God’s self-revelation in history can invite trust precisely because it is historically grounded. The more we discover about the historical integrity of the biblical witness, the more the believer can see God’s fingerprints not in a vacuum of unknowing, but in the concrete, messy tapestry of human history.

### Re-evaluating Climacus in Light of the Counter-Argument

While this counter-argument successfully exposes the limitations of Climacus’s hyper-fideistic extreme, it must be acknowledged that Climacus’s primary target was not moderate historical inquiry, but the *arrogance* of speculative system-builders who claimed to have mastered Christianity through conceptual and historical totalization. 

Climacus’s profound warning remains permanently valuable: Christianity is never safely possessed as an academic trophy. Even if historical research provides a high degree of plausibility, the final step—the transition from historical information to personal, existential transformation—cannot be performed by the historian. The historian hands over a dossier; the individual human soul must still decide whether to stake its eternal happiness on the scandalous claim of a crucified Savior. Thus, while increased historical certainty may not *inherently* destroy faith, Climacus is eternally correct in insisting that *salvation cannot be outsourced to the historian*.

---

## Conclusion

Johannes Climacus’s thesis in the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*—that objective inquiry and authentic Christian faith are fundamentally incommensurable—stands as one of the most provocative and existentially demanding critiques in the history of philosophy. Through the **passion-venture argument**, he demonstrated that objective certainty strips away the risk required for passionate inwardness. Through the **inspiration argument**, he exposed the methodological bankruptcy of using historical inquiry to validate the eternal, divine authority of Scripture. By contrasting the pursuit of eternal happiness through **subjective inwardness** with the sterility of **empirical proofs**, and by illustrating his points through the **philologist thought experiment** and the **two verification scenarios**, Climacus dismantled the nineteenth-century illusion that Christianity could be validated by objective, academic consensus.

Yet, as our evaluation through the **counter-argument** has shown, Climacus’s thesis pushes the critique of objectivity to an extreme edge, risking a separation between faith and historical reality that threatens to turn Christianity into an unanchored fideistic abstraction. A mature philosophy of religion must hold these tensions in creative balance: recognizing with Climacus that eternal truths cannot be conquered by empirical calculation or historical methodology, while acknowledging that faith is not afraid of history, but finds within the concrete, uncertain events of the past an invitation to an existential leap.

Ultimately, Climacus’s voice remains an indispensable antidote to any age—including our own—that seeks to domesticate the Gospel into a manageable set of historical propositions or intellectual data points. He reminds us that before the face of the eternal, scholarship is silent, systems crumble, and the individual human being is left standing alone in the terrifying, magnificent freedom of subjective inwardness.
