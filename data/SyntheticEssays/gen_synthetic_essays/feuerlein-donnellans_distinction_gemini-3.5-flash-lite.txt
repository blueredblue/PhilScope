# Meaning, Intent, and Reference: Evaluating the Semantic Status of Donnellan’s Distinction

## Introduction

In the philosophy of language, the semantic-pragmatic divide serves as a crucial boundary line between the meaning conventionally encoded by linguistic expressions and the contextual inferences generated by their deployment in specific communicative acts. At the center of this terrain lies Keith Donnellan’s 1966 distinction between the referential and attributive uses of definite descriptions. 

For decades, philosophers have debated whether Donnellan’s distinction reveals a deep semantic ambiguity in definite descriptions—implying that a single linguistic expression can possess two fundamentally different types of semantic content or truth-conditional profiles—or whether it is merely a pragmatic epiphenomenon, a byproduct of how speakers use univocal semantic structures to achieve communicative ends. 

To evaluate this question, one must trace the development of the theory of descriptions from Bertrand Russell’s classical paradigm through Donnellan’s challenge, and subsequently engage with Saul Kripke’s influential critique distinguishing *speaker’s reference* from *semantic reference*. 

This essay will argue that while Donnellan’s distinction undeniably highlights a fundamental cleavage between speaker intentions and linguistic meaning, attempts to internalize this distinction into the semantic machinery of natural language founder on systematic ambiguities, the proliferation of unnecessary semantic polysemy, and the robust counter-arguments marshaled by Kripke and defenders of a modified Russellian view. 

Ultimately, Donnellan’s distinction remains a pragmatic feature of speech acts, albeit one that forces a rigorous re-evaluation of how context interacts with truth-conditional content.

---

## I. The Russellian Orthodoxy: The Theory of Definite Descriptions

To understand the disruptive force of Donnellan’s insight, one must first examine the orthodox view it sought to challenge: Bertrand Russell’s Theory of Definite Descriptions (TDD), originally articulated in his 1905 paper "On Denoting." Before Russell, Gottlob Frege and the early Russell (in *The Principles of Mathematics*) posited that definite descriptions—expressions of the form "the $\phi$"—functioned as logical proper names, directly denoting an object if one existed that satisfied the description. 

This view, however, led to severe philosophical puzzles, most notably regarding *Meinongianism* and identity statements. If "the present King of France is bald" is meaningful, and if meaning requires an object to be meant, then the phrase "the present King of France" must denote *something*, even if that something merely subsists in the realm of non-existent objects. Furthermore, identity statements like "A is B" would either be trivial (if A and B have the same meaning) or uninformative/false (if they have different meanings but the same denotation).

Russell’s TDD dissolved these puzzles by unmasking the grammatical illusion of definite descriptions. For Russell, a definite description is not a referring expression at all, but rather an *incomplete symbol* that achieves meaning only within the context of a proposition as a whole. Syntactically, a sentence of the form "The $\phi$ is $\psi$" is analyzed not as a subject-predicate proposition ($Fa$), but as a complex existential quantification coupled with uniqueness and universality claims. 

Formally, a sentence like "The author of *Waverley* is Scottish" is analyzed as the conjunction of three distinct propositions:
1. **Existence:** There is at least one entity that authored *Waverley* ($\exists x (Ax)$).
2. **Uniqueness:** There is at most one entity that authored *Waverley* ($\forall y (Ay \to y = x)$).
3. **Predication:** That unique entity is Scottish ($Sx$).

Thus, the canonical Russellian analysis of "The $\phi$ is $\psi$" is rendered in first-order logic as:

$$\exists x ((Ax \land \forall y (Ay \to y = x)) \land Sx)$$

The philosophical elegance of this theory is immediately apparent in its handling of empty descriptions—sentences containing descriptions that fail to pick out any object in the actual world, such as Russell’s famous example, "The present King of France is bald." 

Under Russell’s analysis, the sentence is parsed as:

$$\exists x ((Kx \land \forall y (Ky \to y = x)) \land Bx)$$

Because the existential clause ($\exists x (Kx)$) is false in the actual world (there is no present King of France), the entire conjunction evaluates to *false*. This avoids the Meinongian commitment to non-existent objects while preserving the Law of Excluded Middle: the negation of the sentence—"It is not the case that the present King of France is bald"—is naturally parsed as the negation of the entire existential proposition, rendering the negation *true*. 

Crucially, for Russell, the semantic value of a definite description is entirely exhausted by this quantificational structure. The description does not "pick out" an individual directly; rather, the truth conditions of the sentence obtain if and only if the world contains a unique satisfier of the matrix $\phi$, and that satisfier also satisfies $\psi$.

## II. Donnellan’s Challenge: Referential vs. Attributive Uses

In his seminal 1966 paper, "Reference and Definite Descriptions," Keith Donnellan argued that Russell’s theory—and indeed, standard semantic theories of the time—overlooked a fundamental duality in how speakers use definite descriptions. Donnellan claimed that a definite description can be used in one of two distinct ways: the *attributive* use or the *referential* use.

### The Attributive Use
In an *attributive* use, the speaker uses a definite description to state something about whoever or whatever happens to fit that description. The description is integral to the statement in the precise manner Russell envisioned. 

Consider Donnellan’s classic example: "Smith’s murderer is insane." If a speaker utters this upon discovering the gruesome, ritualistic mutilation of Smith’s body, without having any antecedent suspect in mind, they are using the description attributively. The speaker wishes to assert that *whoever* committed this crime possesses the property of insanity. 

If it turns out that Jones committed the murder, the speaker has said something true of Jones. If no one murdered Smith, the description fails to denote, and the Russellian analysis dictates that the proposition is false (or truth-valueless, depending on how one treats empty descriptions). The attributive use aligns neatly with Russellian semantics: the description is used to predicate something of *whatever* uniquely satisfies the description.

### The Referential Use
In contrast, in a *referential* use, the speaker uses the definite description as a "tool" to direct the audience's attention to a specific individual, whom the speaker has already singled out by other means. Here, the description functions more like a demonstrative or a proper name. 

Imagine a trial where Jones is sitting in the defendant’s dock, looking entirely unhinged. A spectator whispers to their companion, "Smith’s murderer is insane." In this scenario, even if Jones is *not* Smith’s murderer (perhaps Smith died of natural causes, and Jones is on trial for an entirely different crime), the spectator is using the description to refer directly to Jones. The speaker intends to predicate insanity of *Jones*, using the description merely as a vehicle to latch onto him. 

If Jones is indeed insane, Donnellan argues, the speaker has said something *true* about Jones, despite the fact that Jones is not Smith’s murderer, and thus the Russellian existential presupposition/entailment is technically false.

This creates an immediate tension with Russell’s theory. If a speaker can use a definite description to successfully refer to an individual $x$, and make a true statement about $x$, even when $x$ does not satisfy the description, then Russell’s truth conditions—which demand that the unique satisfier of the description possess the predicate—seem inadequate. 

Donnellan’s insight suggests that descriptions do not inherently function as quantifiers; rather, speakers can exploit them referentially. The central philosophical battleground then becomes: Is this duality a matter of *semantics* (ambiguity within the language system itself) or *pragmatics* (how a univocal semantic structure is deployed in context)?

## III. Semantic Ambiguity vs. Pragmatic Epiphenomenon

To classify Donnellan’s distinction as a *semantic* phenomenon is to claim that definite descriptions are lexically or structurally ambiguous. On this view, the English sentence "The $\phi$ is $\psi$" has two distinct lexical or logical entries in the grammar:

1. **The Referential Entry:** "The $\phi$" functions semantically as a singular term (akin to a Russellian individual constant or a free variable), directly picking out an object $x$ that the speaker has in mind, and the sentence ascribes $\psi$ to $x$.
2. **The Attributive Entry:** "The $\phi$" functions semantically as a Russellian quantifier, asserting the existence and uniqueness of an entity satisfying $\phi$ which also satisfies $\psi$.

Proponents of the semantic view would argue that this ambiguity is analogous to lexical ambiguity (e.g., "bank" as financial institution vs. riverbank) or structural ambiguity (e.g., "Visiting relatives can be boring"). 

However, attributing semantic ambiguity to definite descriptions faces immediate and devastating objections. First, as many critics have noted, virtually *any* definite description can be used either referentially or attributively depending entirely on the conversational context, speaker intentions, and shared background knowledge. 

To posit a semantic ambiguity for every definite description would result in a massive and unprincipled explosion of lexical entries for every noun phrase preceded by the definite article. Semantic ambiguity is typically localized and idiosyncratic, whereas the referential/attributive distinction is systematic and productive across the entire class of definite descriptions.

Second, tests for semantic ambiguity generally fail when applied here. For instance, the *coordination test* (or "identity test") for ambiguity—as in "He went to the bank, and so did I," where one means a financial institution and the other a riverbank—results in zeugma or infelicity. Yet, we do not find the same linguistic strain when a speaker uses a description referentially in one clause and attributively in the next, or when an audience understands a description without knowing whether the speaker has a particular individual in mind.

Consequently, most contemporary philosophers of language—including Donnellan himself, in later reflections—concede that the distinction is not a matter of semantic ambiguity. The linguistic meaning (semantics) of the sentence "The $\phi$ is $\psi$" remains constant across both uses. What varies is the *pragmatic* exploitation of that meaning. 

The description’s semantic contribution is exhausted by its Russellian quantificational structure (or a Fregean sense, depending on one's foundational framework), but speakers routinely use this semantic machinery to perform distinct types of speech acts. 

Just as a speaker can use the declarative sentence "It’s cold in here" not merely to state a meteorological fact, but to pragmatically request that someone close the window, a speaker can use a Russellian description to pragmatically draw attention to an individual who may or may not satisfy the description. The utterance has a strict semantic truth condition, but the *speaker’s communicative intention* generates a distinct truth-conditional intuition regarding what the speaker *said* about a specific object.

## IV. Kripke’s Critique: Speaker’s Reference vs. Semantic Reference

The most formidable defense of the pragmatic nature of Donnellan’s distinction—and the most rigorous defense of a modified Russellian/Fregean semantic framework—was mounted by Saul Kripke in his 1977 paper, "Speaker’s Reference and Semantic Reference." Kripke argues that Donnellan’s observations, while psychologically and conversationally acute, do not threaten the semantic theory of definite descriptions because Donnellan conflates two distinct phenomena: *semantic reference* and *speaker’s reference*.

### Semantic Reference vs. Speaker’s Reference
* **Semantic Reference:** The referent of a singular term (or the denotational contribution of a description) determined by the conventional linguistic rules of the language. For a definite description "the $\phi$," the semantic reference (if any) is the unique object that satisfies the condition $\phi$. If no such object exists, the description has no semantic referent.
* **Speaker’s Reference:** The object that a speaker *intends* to talk about, or intends to direct the audience’s attention to, by using a particular linguistic expression on a specific occasion of utterance.

Kripke illustrates this distinction with numerous non-descriptive examples to show that speaker’s reference and semantic reference frequently diverge without implying any shift in the semantic content of the words used. 

Consider the man who sees Jones shaking hands with Smith across the street and whispers to his companion, "Look at that man with the martini in his hand." Suppose the glass actually contains water, not a martini. 

In this case, the semantic reference of the description "the man with the martini in his hand" fails (or picks out no one drinking a martini), but the *speaker’s reference* is clearly Jones. The speaker successfully directs the audience’s attention to Jones by using a description that is semantically false of him.

Do we conclude from this that "the man with the martini in his hand" is semantically ambiguous, or that its truth conditions have magically shifted? Of course not. 

Kripke argues that the exact same mechanism is at play in Donnellan’s Smith-murderer case. When the spectator points to Jones in the dock and says, "Smith’s murderer is insane," the semantic reference of "Smith’s murderer" (assuming Jones is innocent of Smith’s murder) is null or points elsewhere, but the *speaker’s reference* is Jones. 

The audience understands who the speaker is talking about because of contextual salience, visual cues, and shared assumptions, not because the semantic meaning of the phrase has somehow altered to mean "that guy over there."

### Pragmatic Intentions and Gricean Implicature
Kripke aligns this account with H.P. Grice’s distinction between what is *said* and what is *implicated* (or communicated). When a speaker uses a description referentially, what is *semantically said* is determined by the standard truth conditions of the sentence (which may be false or fail to denote), but what is *communicated* to the audience is a proposition concerning the speaker’s referent. 

The audience performs a pragmatic inference: they recognize that the speaker intends to refer to Jones, and they evaluate the speaker’s communicative success based on Jones’s actual properties (e.g., whether Jones is insane), even though the literal semantic content of the utterance failed to strictly apply to Jones.

Kripke’s intervention successfully relocates Donnellan’s distinction from the semantic lexicon to the pragmatic theory of utterance interpretation. It preserves the compositionality and systematicity of semantics: words have stable, rule-governed meanings, and contexts generate fluid, intention-driven conversational implicatures and speech-act potentials. To allow speaker’s reference to dictate semantic reference would be to abandon semantics altogether, reducing language to a chaotic array of idiosyncratic psychological intentions.

## V. Speaker Belief, Truth Conditions, and the "Smith’s Murderer" Dilemma

Despite Kripke’s powerful defense, the philosophical tension surrounding Donnellan’s examples does not vanish entirely. The core difficulty that motivates Donnellan’s challenge—and continues to trouble pure Russellians—revolves around the interplay between *speaker belief*, *semantic entailment*, and *intuitive truth values*.

Let us return to the courtroom scenario. The spectator sincerely believes that the man in the dock (Jones) is Smith’s murderer. The spectator utters, "Smith’s murderer is insane." 

* **The Russellian/Kripkian Verdict:** Strictly speaking, the sentence uttered is false (assuming Jones did not murder Smith). The semantic content entails the existence of a unique murderer of Smith, who is insane. Since the existential presupposition fails for Jones, the proposition expressed is false. Whatever truth-value intuition the listener feels ("Yes, what they said is true, Jones *is* insane!") is merely a pragmatic illusion driven by the fact that the *speaker’s reference* (Jones) possesses the property of insanity.
* **The Donnellanian/Intuitive Verdict:** When we evaluate the speech act, we feel a strong pre-theoretic intuition that the speaker has stated something *true*. The speaker succeeded in referring to Jones, and predicated insanity of him, and Jones is indeed insane. To dismiss this as a mere "pragmatic illusion" is to do violence to ordinary linguistic practice, where truth-value intuitions are often tied to the success of the speech act rather than the abstract satisfaction of hidden quantifiers.

This tension forces us to examine the role of *speaker belief* in determining the functional profile of a description. In the attributive use, the speaker’s belief about *who* satisfies the description is generally indifferent: the speaker does not care *who* murdered Smith, as long as someone did. 

In the referential use, however, the speaker’s belief is anchored to a specific object. The speaker believes that *that particular object* ($x$) satisfies the description, and this belief guides their communicative intention.

However, Kripke’s framework handles this variance effortlessly without semantic capitulation. Speaker belief is a psychological state, not a semantic property. A speaker can falsely believe that Jones is Smith’s murderer, just as a speaker can falsely believe that the man drinking water is drinking a martini. 

These false beliefs explain *why* the speaker chose that particular description to achieve their referential goal, but they do not alter the semantic contribution of the description itself. The mismatch between the speaker’s belief and the actual state of the world results in semantic falsehood (or referential failure), which is precisely what we should expect when speakers harbor false beliefs.

## VI. Evaluating the Semantic-Pragmatic Status: A Comprehensive Verdict

To synthesize the arguments and definitively answer our guiding question—whether Donnellan’s distinction carries semantic import or remains solely pragmatic—we must evaluate the criteria that demarcate semantics from pragmatics in contemporary philosophy of language.

### 1. Systematicity vs. Context-Dependence
Semantics deals with context-invariant rules of combination and truth-conditional compositionality. While definite descriptions are context-sensitive in the sense that their denotations vary across possible worlds and contexts of utterance (owing to descriptive content), the *mechanism* of that variation is purely quantificational or denotational. 

The referential/attributive distinction, by contrast, is entirely parasitic on the specific, contingent intentions of the speaker in a given conversational setting. Because *any* description can be used referentially given the right bizarre or mundane circumstances, the distinction lacks the closed, systematic grammar characteristic of genuine semantic ambiguity. It is a paradigm case of pragmatic modulation.

### 2. Truth-Conditional Divergence vs. Pragmatic Enrichment
Does the referential use generate a distinct *semantic* truth condition? Defenders of semantic internalism must argue that when a description is used referentially, the proposition expressed lacks the existence and uniqueness conditions of Russell’s analysis, functioning instead as a singular proposition (akin to a Russellian proposition containing an individual constant, $\langle \text{Insane}, \text{Jones} \rangle$). 

Yet, if we adopt this view, we run into severe compositional hurdles. How does the compositional semantics of the sentence "The $\phi$ is $\psi$" abruptly shed its quantificational shell and morph into a singular term merely because the speaker is pointing at someone or has a specific mental target? 

Standard compositional semantics cannot countenance such radical, context-driven structural mutations without collapsing the distinction between what an expression means and what a speaker uses it to convey. 

Post-semantic frameworks, such as those advanced by relevance theorists (e.g., François Recanati or Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson), handle this via *pragmatic enrichment* or *free enrichment*. The semantic content provides a skeletal logical form, which is pragmatically modulated to yield the proposition actually communicated. 

Thus, what the speaker *says* in the pragmatically enriched sense differs from the semantic content of the sentence uttered, yet the semantic content remains strictly Russellian. Donnellan’s referential use is best understood as a prime example of pragmatic enrichment or speaker-intent-driven reference transfer.

### 3. Cross-Linguistic and Formal Evidence
In formal semantics and cross-linguistic typology, definiteness markers (such as articles in English, German, or Romance languages) exhibit distributional patterns that track uniqueness, familiarity, or anaphoricity, rather than a built-in lexical switch for referential vs. attributive deployment. 

Formal semanticists working within dynamic semantics (such as Irene Heim or Hans Kamp with Discourse Representation Theory) treat definite descriptions as introducing discourse referents that must find a familiar anchor in the discourse context. 

Within these sophisticated frameworks, referential uses correspond to cases where the discourse referent is successfully bound to a salient entity in the conversational context, while attributive uses correspond to cases where the descriptive conditions themselves introduce or constrain the search for a satisfier. 

Crucially, these frameworks account for Donnellan’s insights *without* abandoning a unified semantic core; the semantic rules governing definiteness remain uniform, while the contextual updating of the discourse model accounts for the divergent cognitive profiles of the two uses.

---

## Conclusion

Keith Donnellan’s distinction between the referential and attributive uses of definite descriptions remains one of the most provocative and illuminating contributions to twentieth-century philosophy of language. It permanently shattered the complacency of simple-minded semantic accounts by demonstrating that linguistic expressions can be marshaled by speakers to achieve communicative ends that outstrip, bypass, or even contradict their literal semantic content.

However, as Saul Kripke decisively demonstrated, recognizing the divergence between *speaker’s reference* and *semantic reference* reveals that Donnellan’s distinction does not carry semantic import. To internalize the referential use into the semantic structure of natural language would be to conflate the stable, compositional rules of linguistic meaning with the fluid, intention-driven mechanics of speech acts. 

The tension between Russell’s entailment criteria and our intuitive truth-value judgments in referential utterances is successfully dissolved once we distinguish between the strict semantic truth conditions of the sentence uttered and the pragmatically enriched proposition communicated by the speaker. 

Therefore, Donnellan’s distinction remains steadfastly a pragmatic feature of speech acts—a testament not to the ambiguity of our words, but to the remarkable flexibility and intentional depth of human communication.
