### The Architecture of the Passions: Hursthouse, Davidson, and the Challenge of Arational Action

#### Introduction: The Orthodoxy of Desire and the Affective Dissident

For decades, the philosophy of action has operated under the shadow of a monolithic architecture: the belief-desire model of intentionality. Formulated with canonical precision by Donald Davidson, this framework asserts that to act intentionally is to act in the light of reasons, and to act for a reason is to be guided by a primary reason composed of a pro-attitude (a desire) and a belief that the action will satisfy that pro-attitude. Within this Cartesian-Humean synthesis, human agency is fundamentally cognitive and teleological. Emotions, when they feature at all, are relegated to the sub-basement of causation—either as the raw fuel that intensifies a pre-existing desire, as the cognitive appraisal mechanisms that feed into belief formation, or as the irrational noise that disrupts the pristine machinery of practical syllogism.

It is precisely this orthodoxy that Rosalind Hursthouse challenges in her seminal work on arational actions. Hursthouse invites us to look at the tempestuous landscape of emotional outbursts—the slamming of doors, the weeping in frustration, the triumphant gloating, the petty snapping—and asks a radical question: Are these mere sub-rational spasms, or are they intentional actions that stubbornly refuse to fit into the Procrustean bed of the belief-desire matrix? 

Hursthouse introduces the category of *arational actions*—actions that are done for an emotion rather than a belief-desire pair, lacking any future-directed goal, instrumental calculation, or cognitive formulation. This essay evaluates whether Hursthouse’s theory successfully undermines Davidson’s belief-desire paradigm. 

To achieve this, we must map the mechanics of Davidsonian orthodoxy against the phenomenological reality of emotional outbursts, closely examining the distinction between *pre-named* and *post-named* epistemic states. We must interrogate how pre-named actions challenge standard causal accounts by lacking both a named emotion and a corresponding belief-desire pair at the moment of execution. Furthermore, we must navigate the treacherous normative dilemma of moral culpability: If an agent performs an unintentional or arational pre-named action, how can they be held responsible without illicitly smuggling in Davidsonian desires? Can the capacity for *intentional preventative emotion moderation* bridge the normative gap, rendering agents indirectly accountable for outbursts that bypass cognitive calculation? Finally, we must resolve the underlying tension: Do actions driven purely by occurant emotion demand a revolutionary framework of intentionality, or do they inevitably collapse into the category of unintentional reflexes, physiological vents, or mere bodily movements?

---

#### Section I: The Davidsonian Citadel and Its Mechanics

To understand the magnitude of Hursthouse’s challenge, one must first dismantle the machinery of Davidson’s theory of action. In his foundational essays, particularly *Actions, Reasons, and Causes* (1963), Davidson sets out to answer a deceptively simple question: What is the relation between a reason and an action when the former explains the latter by giving the reason for which it was done?

Davidson’s answer is unapologetically causalist and cognitivist. He argues that the primary reason why an agent performs an action consists of a pair of elements: a *belief* about the world and a *pro-attitude* (which includes desires, urges, impulses, moral duties, aesthetic principles, and goals) directed toward a certain state of affairs. For Davidson, to have a reason to act is to have a cognitive representation of the world combined with a desiderative orientation toward a specific outcome. 

Crucially, Davidson posits that this reason *causes* the action in the right sort of way. If an agent walks across the room to open a window, Davidsonian analysis decomposes this event into:
1. A desire for fresh air (the pro-attitude).
2. A belief that walking across the room and pushing the window open will result in fresh air (the belief).
3. A causal chain initiated by this belief-desire pair that results in the bodily movements constitutive of opening the window.

This model carries several structural implications:
* **Teleology:** Intentional action is inherently forward-looking. It aims at an end, and the agent sees the action as a means to that end.
* **Rationalizability:** To explain an action is to rationalize it—to display the internal consistency between the agent’s beliefs and desires from the agent’s own point of view. Even if the reasons are foolish or morally abhorrent, they must possess a formal coherence.
* **Cognitive Mediation:** Desire sets the vector, and belief plots the coordinates. Emotion, if it enters the equation, acts merely as a catalyst that alters the intensity of the desire or the salience of the belief, but it never constitutes the primary reason on its own.

Davidson’s framework is elegant, parsimonious, and deeply intuitive for calculated, instrumental human behavior. However, its imperialism becomes apparent when it attempts to colonize the domain of the passions. When a grieving man kicks a chair, or a humiliated woman storms out of a room slamming the door, the Davidsonian must furiously reconstruct a hidden belief-desire pair. The analyst must posit that the man *desired* to express his grief and *believed* that kicking the chair would achieve this expression, or that the woman *desired* to signal her displeasure and *believed* that door-slamming was the optimal instrument. 

Hursthouse argues that this reconstructive maneuver is a Procrustean distortion. It forces spontaneous, non-instrumental, and purely emotional expressions into an intellectualized mold, mistaking a post-hoc rationalization for the actual etiology of the action.

#### Section II: Hursthouse’s Arational Actions and the Anatomy of Outbursts

Rosalind Hursthouse steps into this theoretical battleground armed with the phenomenology of human emotional life. In her seminal paper *A Rational Action* (1991), she identifies a vast class of human actions that are undeniably intentional, yet completely devoid of the beliefs and desires that Davidson insists are mandatory for intentionality. 

Consider the following scenarios:
* *Scenario A:* Out of sheer jealousy, an agent maliciously tears up a photograph of a rival.
* *Scenario B:* Out of grief, a mother violently throws her arms around a pillow and sobs uncontrollably.
* *Scenario C:* Out of anger, a man slams his fist against the wall, breaking his knuckles.

In each of these cases, the agent is not acting *for the sake of* a future state of affairs. The jealous person does not tear up the photograph *in order to* eliminate the rival, nor does the grieving mother hug the pillow *in order to* cure her grief. These actions do not serve as instruments for the realization of a desired end. Instead, they are done *out of* an emotion—jealousy, grief, anger. 

Hursthouse calls these *arational actions*. They are "arational" not because they are irrational (in the sense of violating logical consistency or defying common sense), but because they are logically independent of reasons in the Davidsonian sense. They do not issue from a belief-desire pair; they issue directly from an *occurant emotion*. 

To defend this category against Davidsonian assimilation, Hursthouse introduces a crucial set of diagnostic criteria. Arational actions possess a characteristic profile:
1. **Intentionally Performed:** The agent is not having a spasm, a reflex, or a seizure. The agent deliberately kicks the door, slams the fist, or tears the photograph. The bodily movements are under executive motor control.
2. **Non-Instrumental:** They are not performed as a means to an end. The agent does not calculate probabilities or weigh options.
3. **Emotion-Driven:** The *only* answer to the question "Why did you do that?" that captures the intrinsic nature of the action is emotional: "Because I was furious," "Because I was heartbroken," "Because I was consumed by jealousy."
4. **Intelligible, Not Rationalizable:** While they cannot be cast into the Davidsonian syllogism of belief and desire, they are entirely *intelligible* to anyone who understands human psychology. We do not need to be told the agent’s underlying goals to understand why a rejected lover burns old letters; the action makes sense as an expression of occurant heartbreak.

By isolating these actions, Hursthouse poses a direct threat to the Davidsonian citadel. If intentional action can occur without a belief-desire pair, then Davidson’s thesis—that a primary reason consisting of a belief and a pro-attitude is a *necessary condition* for intentional action—collapses.

#### Section III: Pre-Named vs. Post-Named Epistemic States in Emotional Outbursts

To evaluate the strength of Hursthouse’s assault, we must descend deeper into the phenomenology of emotional outbursts, interrogating a subtle temporal and epistemic distinction that frequently obfuscates the debate: the distinction between *pre-named* and *post-named* epistemic states.

When an agent undergoes an intense emotional episode—say, a sudden volcanic rage—their internal landscape is epistemically chaotic. In many instances of emotional outbursts, the agent does not enter the fray with a fully articulated, conceptually tagged emotional state. They do not think to themselves, *“I am now experiencing state-type Anger-7, and therefore I shall slam the door.”* 

Instead, the emotional state at the moment of execution is *pre-named* (or pre-conceptualized). The agent feels a visceral, somatic boiling, a constriction of the chest, a surge of adrenaline, and a sudden, irresistible impulse to discharge this physiological and psychological pressure through a specific bodily movement. The conceptual label "anger," "humiliation," or "grief" is frequently applied *after* the fact—it is *post-named*. The agent slams the door, pauses, breathes heavily, and only then conceptualizes what just happened: *"Good grief, I was so angry."*

This distinction between pre-named and post-named epistemic states deals a devastating blow to standard causal accounts, particularly those that rely on cognitive appraisals. 

##### 1. The Challenge to Cognitive Appraisal Theories
Standard causal accounts (such as those advanced by cognitive psychologists and cognitivist philosophers like Robert Solomon or Martha Nussbaum) argue that emotions are fundamentally cognitive judgments or appraisals. On this view, to be angry is to judge that one has been wronged. Therefore, anger already contains cognitive components—beliefs and evaluations—that can easily be mapped onto Davidsonian belief-desire pairs.

However, the phenomenon of *pre-named* outbursts shatters this cognitivist gloss. In the heat of a pre-named outburst, the agent often has *no* conscious cognitive judgment formulated. There is no proposition endorsed, no conceptualized appraisal running through the mind. There is only a raw, unmediated affective surge. If the action issues directly from this pre-named, non-cognitive, purely somatic-affective state, then standard causal accounts fail because the necessary mental states (beliefs, desires, or even named conceptual emotions) simply do not exist in the agent’s consciousness at the time of the action.

##### 2. The Absence of the Belief-Desire Pair
Davidson’s model requires that the reasons explaining an action must be consciously accessible or at least psychologically real to the agent in a propositional format (even if tacitly). But in pre-named emotional outbursts, the agent cannot produce a belief-desire pair, even under retrospective interrogation. 
* *Interrogator:* "Why did you throw the vase?"
* *Agent:* "I don't know! I just felt this overwhelming black wave of panic and fury, and my hand just did it."
* *Interrogator:* "What did you desire to achieve?"
* *Agent:* "Achieve? Nothing! I didn't want to achieve anything. I was just beside myself."

If the Davidsonian insists on imposing a belief-desire pair here—claiming that the agent *must* have desired to shatter the vase and *believed* throwing it would do so—they are engaging in a dogmatic fiction. They are flattening a rich, inarticulate, pre-named affective storm into a tidy, bloodless calculation of means and ends. Hursthouse’s theory succeeds precisely because it respects the phenomenological reality of these pre-named states, acknowledging that agency can be driven by a raw, occurant passion that has not yet been domesticated by language or conceptual labeling.

#### Section IV: The Normative Dilemma: Culpability and Preventative Emotion Moderation

If Hursthouse is correct—if arational actions are intentional yet lack beliefs and desires—we are immediately thrust into a profound normative dilemma regarding moral culpability. 

This dilemma strikes at the heart of our practices of holding people responsible. Our legal and moral frameworks are built upon the assumption of intentionality. We hold agents blameworthy for intentional actions because those actions flow from their cognitive states—their reasoned choices, their endorsed values, their calculated risks. But if an agent performs an arational action—such as striking a colleague out of a sudden, pre-named burst of jealousy—and that action is *not* guided by a belief-desire pair, how do we evaluate their moral culpability?

##### 1. The Exculpation Threat
If arational actions are purely emotion-driven surges that lack cognitive mediation, does the agent become a passive victim of their own passions? Does Hursthouse’s theory inadvertently provide an exculpatory shield, reducing outbursts to irresistible impulses that invite the plea of temporary insanity or diminished capacity? 

If an agent does not choose their emotion, and the arational action flows directly from the occurant emotion without the mediation of a Davidsonian choice, then it seems unjust to hold the agent morally responsible. Blame appears to require agency, and agency appears to require the capacity to have acted otherwise based on reasons. If reasons were absent, culpability evaporates.

##### 2. The Bridge of Indirect Accountability: Preventative Emotion Moderation
To rescue moral responsibility from this precipice without capitulating to Davidsonian orthodoxy, we must examine the agent’s long-term relationship with their emotional life. This leads us to the crucial concept of **intentional preventative emotion moderation**.

While an agent may not be able to prevent a specific arational outburst *at the exact micro-second of its occurrence* (since it wells up pre-named and unbidden from an occurant passion), agents *do* possess diachronic agency over their emotional dispositions. We are not moral vegetables tossed about by the waves of our passions. We have the capacity for:
* **Cultivation:** Nurturing virtues and dampening vices (e.g., working to reduce one's proneness to jealousy through therapy, reflection, or self-monitoring).
* **Avoidance:** Recognizing the triggers that lead to explosive emotional states and steering clear of them (e.g., knowing that one becomes violently angry when sleep-deprived and avoiding high-stress negotiations in that state).
* **Second-Order Regulation:** Exercising control over our emotional focus—deciding whether to dwell on a slight, fan the flames of resentment, or calm our minds through meditation.

This introduces a sophisticated bridge across the normative gap. An agent may not be directly blameworthy for the *local* arational action considered in isolation from its history, but they are **indirectly accountable** (or accountable *in virtue of* their broader character and failures of emotional self-governance) for allowing themselves to cultivate an emotional ecology prone to such violent, unmoderated outbursts.

Consider an agent who knows they have a volatile temper, who has repeatedly failed to practice emotion moderation, and who subsequently flies into a blind rage and destroys someone’s property. Even if that specific act of destruction was an arational outburst lacking a Davidsonian belief-desire pair at the moment of execution, the agent is fully culpable. Why? Because the outburst is the predictable downstream consequence of their culpable negligence regarding their own emotional character.

Thus, Hursthouse’s theory does not destroy moral responsibility; rather, it shifts the locus of culpability from *synchronic* practical reasoning (calculating means to ends in the moment) to *diachronic* character stewardship and emotional self-management.

#### Section V: Resolving the Tension: Redefining Intentionality or Collapsing into Reflexes?

We have now arrived at the philosophical crucible of our essay. We face a stark fork in the road, a foundational tension that must be resolved if Hursthouse’s theory of arational actions is to survive scrutiny.

* **Path A (The Davidsonian Reduction):** If we insist that all intentional actions must be governed by belief-desire pairs, then Hursthouse’s arational actions must either:
  1. Be exposed as covertly harboring hidden beliefs and desires (the cognitivist reduction).
  2. Be stripped of their status as intentional actions and relegated to the status of involuntary reflexes, physiological spasms, or mere bodily movements (the deflationary collapse).

* **Path B (The Hursthousian Revolution):** If we maintain that arational actions are genuinely intentional yet genuinely devoid of beliefs and desires, we are forced to revolutionize our very definition of intentionality, breaking the monopoly that the belief-desire model has held over philosophy for over half a century.

Let us evaluate the danger of Path A’s deflationary collapse. Can an arational action truly be distinguished from a knee-jerk reflex or an epileptic seizure? 

If someone’s leg jerks forward when the doctor taps their patellar tendon, this is a bodily movement, but it is not an intentional action. The patient does not *intend* to kick the doctor; they cannot help it, and they do not exercise executive motor control in the service of an internal state. 

Now, compare this with the agent who slams a door out of rage. Is this door-slamming more like the knee-jerk reflex, or more like walking to the window for fresh air? 
* It shares with the reflex its spontaneity, its lack of deliberation, and its direct tethering to a physiological-affective state. 
* Yet, it shares with the calculated action its **executive motor control**, its goal-directedness (in a loose, non-instrumental sense), and its responsiveness to the agent’s holistic psychological state. The agent *chose* to slam that specific door, with that specific force, in that specific direction. They could have inhibited the action had their self-control been slightly stronger or the social stakes been catastrophically higher (e.g., if a police officer had been standing right there).

This reveals that arational actions occupy a rich, nuanced middle ground between mechanical reflexes and intellectualized instrumental calculations. They are **non-instrumentally intentional**. 

To accommodate this middle ground without collapsing into incoherence, we must develop a **revised framework of intentionality**. We must sever the conceptual umbilical cord that ties "intentionality" exclusively to "acting for reasons" in the Davidsonian sense. 

A revised framework of intentionality recognizes two distinct species of intentional action:
1. **Rational (Teleological) Intentionality:** Actions guided by primary reasons (belief-desire pairs), aimed at future states of affairs, characteristically human, calculative, and justificatory.
2. **Arational (Expressive) Intentionality:** Actions guided directly by occurant emotions, aimed at the *expression* or *discharge* of affective states, non-calculative, non-instrumental, but nonetheless under the intentional control of the agent.

When an agent slams the door out of anger, they are not acting *for a reason* (i.e., they don't have a goal in mind), but they are acting *intentionally*. The intentionality resides not in the projection of a future end (the belief-desire pair), but in the **active embodiment and execution of a present emotion**. The emotion itself functions as the primary driver, bypassing the cognitive appraisal machinery while remaining fully anchored in the agent’s active, responsible self.

---

#### Conclusion: Beyond the Belief-Desire Orthodoxy

Rosalind Hursthouse’s theory of arational actions delivers a profound and successful challenge to Donald Davidson’s belief-desire orthodoxy. By dragging philosophy out of the sterile operating room of cold practical syllogisms and into the messy, passionate theater of emotional outbursts, Hursthouse exposes the Procrustean violence of the Davidsonian reduction. 

Through our investigation, we have demonstrated that:
* The Davidsonian attempt to reconstruct pre-named emotional outbursts into hidden belief-desire pairs is a dogmatic distortion of psychological reality.
* The distinction between pre-named and post-named epistemic states proves that agents frequently act out of raw, occurant affective states before any cognitive judgment or conceptual label has been formulated.
* The normative dilemma of moral culpability is successfully resolved not by abandoning responsibility, but by shifting our focus from synchronic instrumental calculation to diachronic *preventative emotion moderation*, holding agents indirectly accountable for the emotional ecologies they cultivate.
* Far from collapsing into involuntary reflexes or physiological spasms, arational actions demand a *revised framework of intentionality*—one that broadens our philosophical vocabulary to embrace non-instrumental, expressive, emotion-driven agency.

Ultimately, human beings are not merely calculating machines running algorithms of belief and desire; we are passionate, feeling creatures whose bodies and minds are deeply entwined in the kinetic expression of our emotions. By validating the category of arational action, Hursthouse rescues our emotional lives from theoretical erasure and restores psychological authenticity to the philosophy of action.
