# Emergent Dualism and the Persistence of the Self Through Hiatuses in Consciousness

William Hasker’s emergent dualism is an attempt to occupy a middle position between reductive materialism and traditional Cartesian dualism. It rejects the materialist claim that mental life is nothing more than brain activity while also rejecting the Cartesian picture of a complete, independently existing soul that merely happens to interact with a body. On Hasker’s view, the conscious self is an emergent entity: it arises when a sufficiently complex physical organism, especially a human brain, reaches an appropriate level of organization. Once emergent, however, the conscious self is not reducible to the physical system from which it emerges. It possesses its own causal powers and can exercise “downward” influence upon the brain and body.

This proposal is attractive because it attempts to preserve the reality of consciousness, mental causation, and first-person subjectivity without positing an immaterial substance that exists wholly apart from the body. Yet the view faces a particularly sharp problem when consciousness ceases for a period and later returns. Cases of resuscitation after cardiac arrest, temporary coma, deep anesthesia, and hypothetical cryonic suspension raise a question that is more exacting than the question of whether consciousness can be restored: is the consciousness that returns numerically identical with the consciousness that previously existed? If personal identity consists in the persistence of an emergent field of consciousness, then a genuine hiatus seems to threaten identity. But if identity instead follows the continuing physical brain, emergent dualism appears to surrender one of its central advantages over materialism. And if identity is restored by divine intervention, the view risks reintroducing the very interaction and explanatory difficulties that its emergent framework was designed to avoid.

The difficulty can be formulated as a trilemma. Hasker must either abandon the field of consciousness as the criterion of personal identity in favor of physical brain continuity; accept that numerical identity is lost across any genuine hiatus in consciousness; or invoke some special divine act that preserves identity across the gap. Each option carries a serious cost. The first weakens the distinctively dualist character of the theory. The second entails an implausibly revisionary account of ordinary survival. The third threatens to collapse emergent dualism into a Cartesian-style interaction problem, since it requires an explanation of how a conscious field that has ceased to exist can be restored or reattached to a later physical state.

The problem is not merely one of terminology. It concerns the metaphysical structure of the person. What makes a later subject the same subject as an earlier one? Is it a continuing field, a continuing brain, an uninterrupted causal process, a preserved pattern, a divine decree, or something else? The magnet analogy that motivates emergent dualism helps illuminate the dependency of consciousness upon its physical basis, but it also makes the hiatus problem more difficult. A magnet can generate a magnetic field when the relevant physical conditions obtain; if the magnet is destroyed, the field disappears. If a new magnet later generates an indistinguishable field, the new field is not numerically the old field. Similar considerations appear to apply to an emergent field of consciousness.

## I. The Structure of Emergent Dualism

Emergent dualism begins with the rejection of two forms of reduction. First, it denies that conscious experience is simply identical with neural processes. Neural activity may be necessary for consciousness, but the experience of pain, the awareness of one’s own thought, and the unity of a subject are not exhausted by descriptions of electrochemical events. Second, emergent dualism denies that the conscious self is a pre-existing soul externally attached to a body. Instead, consciousness emerges from the organization and activity of the biological brain.

The term “emergence” is doing substantial philosophical work here. In a weak or merely epistemological sense, a property is emergent if it is surprising or difficult to predict from lower-level facts. But Hasker’s position requires a stronger form of emergence. Consciousness is not merely a novel way of describing neural complexity. It is an ontologically novel reality, with properties and causal powers that are not reducible to or identical with the properties of the physical base. The brain generates, or gives rise to, a field of consciousness. That field then acts upon the brain, especially through unified mental agency, intentionality, and the direction of neural processes.

The resulting picture is neither simple identity theory nor independent substance dualism. Consciousness depends upon the brain, but it is not identical with the brain. The dependence is generative rather than merely correlational: in appropriate conditions, the physical base produces the emergent field. Yet the emergent field is not passive. It can influence its base. Mental intentions can help guide bodily movement; conscious deliberation can affect neural activity; the whole person can act in ways that cannot be adequately captured by treating the brain as a collection of disconnected micro-events.

The theory therefore involves two asymmetrical relations. The brain is prior in the order of generation: without the appropriate physical organization, no conscious field arises. The conscious field is prior in the order of agency: once it exists, it can operate as a unified causal subject. The physical base produces the field, while the field exercises downward causation upon the base.

This structure is central to Hasker’s response to materialism. If the field is a genuine entity, then consciousness is not eliminated by explaining its dependence upon the brain. A field may be dependent without being identical to its source. But this same structure generates the identity problem. If the field is genuinely distinct from the brain, what happens when the brain ceases to produce it? Does the field remain in existence in a dormant state? Does it cease to exist? Does it become attached to another physical base? Or does God preserve it in some special way? The answers determine whether emergent dualism can account for ordinary judgments about survival.

## II. The Magnet Analogy and Its Ambiguities

The magnet analogy is useful because it depicts an emergent entity as dependent upon, but not identical with, its physical source. A magnet generates a magnetic field. The field is not simply another name for the magnet, yet neither is it ordinarily present without the magnet. It has a real structure and can enter into causal relations with other objects. In this sense, the analogy captures the intended relation between the brain and the field of consciousness.

The analogy also helps explain how downward causation might be possible. A magnetic field generated by a magnet can exert forces on other physical objects. Likewise, a field of consciousness generated by the brain might exert causal influence upon neural events. The emergent field would not be an epiphenomenal byproduct but an active feature of the world.

Yet the analogy has limits. A magnetic field is not a subject of experience. It does not possess a first-person perspective, autobiographical memory, or a claim to personal identity. More importantly, the conditions under which a field exists are relatively clear. If the magnet is removed or loses its relevant physical properties, the magnetic field dissipates. The analogy therefore tends to support a model of conditional existence: the field exists when its generator possesses the right configuration and ceases when that configuration is destroyed.

This consequence is harmless for magnetic fields but troubling for persons. Suppose a magnet is temporarily demagnetized and later remagnetized. The later magnetic field may have the same shape, strength, and causal powers as the earlier one, but it is not obviously numerically identical with the earlier field. It is a new field produced by a later state of the magnet. Numerical identity requires that there be one and the same entity existing at different times, not merely two entities with identical properties or a continuous causal relationship between them.

The same distinction applies to the emergent field of consciousness. If the brain enters a state in which it no longer generates consciousness, and later returns to a state that generates a field with the same memories, personality, and dispositions, then the later field may be qualitatively continuous with the earlier one. But qualitative continuity is not numerical identity. A duplicate can possess the same psychological characteristics without being the same individual.

One might respond that the field does not disappear during the hiatus. Perhaps it remains present but inactive, like a magnetic field that persists in a latent form even when it is not manifesting force. But this response changes the conception of emergence. If the field is produced by the brain’s organized activity, then the absence of that activity appears to entail the absence of the field. A field that continues independently while its generating base is inactive begins to look less like an emergent property and more like an independently existing substance. The theory may stipulate that the field persists in a dormant condition, but it must then explain what makes it the same field, how it remains individuated, and why it does not count as an independently existing self.

The analogy thus generates a dilemma. If it is taken seriously as a model of dependence, it suggests that the field ceases when its base no longer supports it. If it is weakened to allow the field to persist independently of its base, it loses much of its explanatory force and approaches Cartesian dualism.

## III. Hiatuses in Consciousness

A hiatus in consciousness is a period during which the subject has no conscious experience. The relevant cases differ in medical and metaphysical detail. In ordinary sleep, many philosophers will deny that consciousness entirely ceases, since there may be dreams or minimally conscious states. But deep anesthesia, certain comas, cardiac arrest, and hypothetical cryonic suspension may involve a more substantial interruption. The crucial issue is not whether the person exhibits outward behavior but whether there is any continuing field of consciousness.

Consider resuscitation. A person’s heart stops, brain activity falls below the threshold required for consciousness, and after several minutes medical intervention restores circulation and neural functioning. The person later awakens with memories, character traits, and intentions continuous with those held before the arrest. Ordinary language says that the same person has been revived. The person did not merely resemble the individual who died; the person survived a temporary interruption.

A coma presents a less decisive case because some forms of coma may involve residual brain activity or an ongoing, though inaccessible, consciousness. If the emergent field continues at a minimal level, then the hiatus may not be genuine. But severe coma can be stipulated to involve a total cessation of conscious experience. The same question then arises: does the person persist through the period of unconsciousness?

Cryonic suspension makes the issue especially vivid. Suppose a brain is preserved in a state that prevents consciousness for centuries, and later advanced technology repairs and reactivates it. Memories and psychological dispositions return. If the person is judged to have survived, the relevant continuity cannot be ordinary conscious continuity. It must involve either physical continuity, informational continuity, a persisting self, or a divine act of preservation.

The emergent dualist cannot simply appeal to psychological continuity without further analysis. Psychological continuity is a relation between mental states, memories, intentions, and dispositions. But if no subject exists during the hiatus, then there is no continuous chain of mental states across the interval. There are earlier mental states, a period with no mental states, and later mental states. The relation between the earlier and later states may be causal and informational, but it does not by itself establish that the same field existed throughout.

This is the distinction between continuity and identity. A process may be causally continuous even if one of its temporary manifestations ceases to exist. A flame can go out and later be rekindled from embers, but the later flame is not numerically identical with the earlier flame. A river may preserve its course while its water is replaced, but the identity of a river is not identical to the identity of a particular quantity of water. Whether a person is more like a flame, a river, an organism, or a continuing subject is precisely what must be determined rather than assumed.

## IV. The Field as the Criterion of Personal Identity

The problem becomes acute if Hasker’s account treats the field of consciousness as the criterion of personal identity. A criterion of identity is not merely a condition that is correlated with being a person. It specifies what makes an individual at one time the same individual at another time. If the field itself is the persisting subject, then the identity of the person depends upon the identity of that field.

There are two possible interpretations of this claim. On the first, the field is identical with the self. In that case, a person exists exactly when the field exists, and the person’s persistence requires the field’s persistence. If the field ceases, the person ceases. A later field, even if generated by the same brain and possessing the same memories, is a numerically distinct subject.

On the second interpretation, the field is not identical with the person but is the principal bearer of consciousness and agency. The person may be a larger composite of brain, field, body, and history. In that case, the field may not need to persist continuously for the person to persist. But this response requires a separate account of what unifies the composite across the hiatus. If physical continuity does the work, then the field is not the criterion of identity. If psychological continuity does the work, then the theory must explain how psychological continuity survives when there is no intervening subject. If divine preservation does the work, then the theory incurs the theological and interactionist costs discussed below.

The appeal to the field as the identity criterion is initially attractive because it seems to preserve the unity of the person. The brain is a complex physical system, composed of changing parts and susceptible to division or duplication. A field of consciousness, by contrast, is holistic. It belongs to one integrated point of view. If the brain generates one field, then the field can explain why the many neural processes constitute one subject rather than a mere collection of experiences.

But the very holism that helps explain synchronic unity does not automatically explain diachronic identity. A field can be unified at a given time without persisting through time. The fact that a field is one subject now does not establish that a later field is the same subject after an interval of nonexistence. The theory needs a persistence condition, not merely a unity condition.

This is where numerical identity is more demanding than similarity. Suppose a brain is repaired after cryonic suspension so perfectly that every neural relation is restored. The resulting field has the same memories and character as the earlier one. From the first-person perspective, the person may report uninterrupted survival. Yet that report itself is part of the later field. It cannot establish that the earlier field has persisted rather than that a new field has inherited the earlier field’s memories. The later consciousness may sincerely remember being the earlier person without actually being numerically identical to that person.

Memory is therefore not sufficient by itself. A later subject’s apparent memory of an earlier experience may be caused by the earlier person’s brain, but memory content does not guarantee identity. If it did, the possibility of duplication would produce contradictions. Two later beings could each remember the same earlier life, but they could not both be numerically identical to the one earlier person. Psychological continuity may be evidence of identity in ordinary cases, but it cannot be the whole metaphysical foundation unless the theory accepts a reductionist account of personal identity.

Emergent dualism, however, is not naturally reductionist. It posits a real subject over and above the pattern of mental contents. Thus it cannot simply define identity in terms of memory continuity without explaining how one and the same field is preserved.

## V. The First Horn: Abandoning the Field for Brain Continuity

The first horn of the trilemma is to say that personal identity follows the continuing physical brain. On this account, the field of consciousness may disappear and later re-emerge, but the person remains the same because the underlying organism or brain has remained numerically continuous. The field is analogous to a temporary activity or manifestation of a persisting physical system. Its interruption does not destroy the person any more than temporary unconsciousness destroys the identity of a biological organism.

This solution has considerable intuitive force. We ordinarily identify people by the continuity of their bodies and brains. Medical and legal practices treat a comatose patient as the same person who entered the coma. A patient who is resuscitated is not usually regarded as a replacement individual. Cryonic survival, if possible, would presumably be grounded in preservation of the brain’s physical organization. The field might be absent, but the generator remains.

The difficulty is that this solution makes the brain, rather than the field, the fundamental bearer of personal identity. If the brain’s numerical continuity is sufficient for the person’s numerical continuity, then the emergent field does not perform the identity-making role. It may explain conscious functioning when present, but it is not what makes the subject persist. The person survives because the physical base survives.

This result need not reduce consciousness to the brain. An emergent dualist could consistently claim that consciousness is an irreducible field while maintaining that the person is a brain-field composite whose identity is anchored in the brain. But the view would then have to explain why the person is identified with the base rather than the emergent subject. If the field is the conscious agent—the entity that thinks, chooses, and experiences—then it is natural to say that the field is the person. If the field is not the person, then the theory risks an odd division: the brain is the bearer of identity, while the field is the bearer of consciousness and agency.

The proposal also creates a problem in cases of brain duplication or radical replacement. Suppose the physical brain is scanned, destroyed, and reconstructed elsewhere. If physical continuity is interrupted, the field criterion may deny survival. But if a brain is gradually replaced neuron by neuron, physical continuity may remain, even though the original biological matter is eventually gone. If two brains are produced from one original pattern, which one is the same person? The field theory might answer by pointing to the unique field, but the brain-continuity theory must specify whether identity follows material continuity, functional continuity, spatiotemporal continuity, or some privileged causal history.

Moreover, physical continuity alone may not be enough. A corpse can preserve much of the brain’s structure while no longer supporting consciousness. If a damaged brain is repaired after a long period, does the corpse remain the person throughout? If so, what distinguishes a temporary absence of consciousness from irreversible death? The answer will likely involve the brain’s capacity to generate the field, not its bare material persistence. But then the field has returned as a condition of identity, and the theory must explain whether the relevant condition is actual consciousness, potential consciousness, or the continued capacity of the physical base to produce consciousness.

Thus, brain continuity can save ordinary judgments about coma and resuscitation, but only by revising the role of the emergent field. The field ceases to be the criterion of identity and becomes an episodic manifestation of a physically persisting organism. The view remains non-reductive about consciousness, but its account of personal persistence becomes fundamentally biological.

## VI. The Second Horn: Accepting Loss of Identity

The second horn accepts the metaphysical consequence of taking the field seriously: whenever the field genuinely ceases, the person ceases. A later field produced by the same brain is numerically distinct, even if it has all the memories and traits of the earlier field. Resuscitation would therefore not restore the same person but create a successor who is psychologically continuous with the deceased individual.

This position has a certain logical clarity. It treats the field as a genuine entity with genuine conditions of existence. Just as a magnetic field disappears when its generating conditions cease, so the field of consciousness disappears when the brain no longer generates it. When the brain becomes active again, a new field arises. The theory avoids equivocation and does not smuggle physical continuity into the identity criterion.

It also exposes an important metaphysical possibility. Perhaps personal identity is not what matters in survival. Perhaps what matters is the preservation of psychological continuity, memory, character, and projects, even if numerical identity is lost. A later person who remembers one’s life and continues one’s commitments might be a practically adequate successor. On such a view, the metaphysics of identity could diverge from ordinary moral and emotional concern.

But this revision is extremely costly for emergent dualism when applied to ordinary cases. It implies that every deep, dreamless interruption of consciousness could involve the death of one self and the creation of another. If consciousness completely ceases during general anesthesia, then the waking person is not numerically identical with the person who entered the operating room. If a coma involves a genuine hiatus, the patient who awakens is a successor. If cryonic suspension is possible, revival would not be survival but replacement.

The theory might avoid this consequence by denying that these cases involve a complete cessation of the field. Perhaps the field persists at a low level during anesthesia, coma, or sleep, even when there is no reportable experience. This would preserve identity, but it would make the view depend on controversial empirical and conceptual claims about unconscious consciousness. More importantly, it would not solve the problem of a stipulated total hiatus. The philosophical question concerns what follows if there really is no field at all. A theory cannot answer that case simply by declaring that the field must have remained.

The loss-of-identity position also conflicts with the role of memory. The later field’s memories would be inherited rather than remembered in the strict sense. Yet the later subject would have every reason to regard itself as the earlier person. This creates a radical form of error embedded in ordinary self-consciousness. The revived subject would be systematically mistaken about its own past, despite possessing exactly the memories that normally ground first-person authority.

One might accept this error as a consequence of the theory, but then the cost is not merely counterintuitive. It affects responsibility, promises, relationships, and rational deliberation. If the person who awakens after resuscitation is not the person who made commitments before cardiac arrest, then the normative continuity of the life becomes difficult to explain. Perhaps successors inherit obligations through causal or social relations, but this would require a new account of moral identity.

The second horn is therefore coherent in a narrow sense but revisionary in a broad sense. It preserves the metaphysical integrity of the field by denying survival across its absence. Yet it makes the field an unsuitable criterion for personal identity if the theory aims to preserve the ordinary notion that a person can pass through unconsciousness and remain the same individual.

## VII. The Third Horn: Divine Preservation and Its Costs

The third horn invokes divine intervention. God could preserve the field of consciousness during the hiatus, perhaps by sustaining it in a non-actualized or disembodied state, and later reunite it with the restored brain. Alternatively, God could ensure that the field generated after revival is numerically identical with the earlier field, even if natural processes alone would produce only a new field.

This proposal has theological resources. A theistic metaphysics need not restrict existence to what is continuously supported by natural physical processes. God could preserve a person through death, coma, or cryonic suspension. The doctrine of resurrection, for example, already involves divine action that exceeds ordinary causal mechanisms. If the question is what is metaphysically possible rather than what natural emergence alone can explain, divine preservation may seem available.

However, the proposal changes the explanatory character of emergent dualism. The original attraction of the emergent model was that it offered a naturalistic or quasi-naturalistic account of how consciousness arises from the brain while avoiding reductionism. Once a hiatus occurs, the theory requires God to preserve or recreate the conscious subject. The field is no longer secured by the relationship between the brain and its emergent product; it depends upon an external agent that intervenes whenever the generative relation is interrupted.

This raises an interaction problem reminiscent of Cartesian dualism. If the field is preserved independently of the brain, then it must exist without the physical base that generated it. How does it remain a determinate individual during the interval? What grounds its identity? Where does it exist, and in what state? If God stores or sustains it, is the field a substance after all, capable of existing apart from the brain? If so, emergent dualism has moved toward the Cartesian conception of a mind that can exist independently of its body.

Alternatively, God might not preserve the original field but create a new field that is numerically identical by divine stipulation. Yet numerical identity cannot simply be conferred by labeling. If God creates a later field with the same memories and characteristics, that establishes qualitative and causal continuity, but it does not obviously establish that it is the same entity. God could ensure that the later field is the same field only if there is some metaphysical fact about the field that is preserved across the interval. The appeal to divine power does not itself explain what that fact is.

The theological option can also be formulated in terms of continuous divine cognition. Perhaps God retains the person’s identity in the divine mind and recreates that same person at revival. This resembles some historical theories of resurrection, in which divine memory grounds personal persistence. But this solution makes divine representation or intention the criterion of identity rather than the emergent field. The field remains important for conscious agency, but the continuity of the person is secured externally.

There is also a causal problem. If the revived field is to exercise downward causation upon the brain, how is the appropriate relation established? In the ordinary emergent model, the field arises from the brain and influences it as part of an integrated system. During the hiatus, however, the field is either absent or detached. On revival, it must again become coordinated with the brain’s neural processes. If God directly arranges the correspondence, then the relation is not explained by emergence but by divine coordination. This looks structurally similar to the interaction problem for Cartesian dualism: an immaterial or non-physical subject must be connected to a physical system in a way that produces unified agency.

Hasker’s emergentism may avoid some traditional interaction problems because the field is generated by the brain and is naturally integrated with it. But that advantage exists only while the generative conditions obtain. Across a genuine hiatus, the natural relation is broken. Divine intervention can bridge the gap, but the bridge is an exceptional act rather than a consequence of the theory’s ordinary metaphysics. The theory thereby becomes dependent upon the very kind of extra-natural mind-body coordination that it sought to avoid.
