**The Fragility of Undefeated Justification: Evaluating Peter Klein’s Fourth Condition Through the Lens of the Gettier Problem**

The Gettier problem remains one of the most formidable challenges in contemporary epistemology, having demonstrated that the traditional tripartite analysis of knowledge as justified true belief (JTB) is insufficient. Edmund Gettier’s seminal 1963 paper revealed scenarios wherein an agent possesses justified true belief, yet the truth of the belief is achieved through epistemic luck rather than through the justification itself. In response to this challenge, epistemologists have proposed various fourth conditions to complete the analysis of knowledge. Among the most prominent defenders of a defeasibility-based solution is Peter Klein. Klein argues that the fourth condition required for propositional knowledge is the absence of undefeated defeaters. According to Klein, if a belief is justified, true, and undefeated by any contrary evidence or considerations, it constitutes knowledge, regardless of whether the agent is aware of the defeaters or the specific chain of reasoning that led to the truth. 

However, Klein’s fourth condition—often referred to as the "No Defeaters" condition—faces significant scrutiny when examined through the complexities of unknown defeaters and the subsequent defeaters for those defeaters. The introduction of hidden background circumstances and the intricate web of defeat and undefeat raises profound questions about the necessity and stability of Klein’s epistemic requirement. This essay evaluates Klein’s fourth condition through the lens of the Gettier problem, focusing on whether unknown defeaters and subsequent defeaters for those defeaters undermine the necessity of Klein’s requirement. It further assesses the validity of Klein’s felicitous-coincidence principle by analyzing counterexamples involving hidden background circumstances, such as the unexecuted threat to the State Capitol Building and the delayed visit from the sister-in-law. Finally, it investigates whether Klein’s rejection of these counterexamples relies on an overly strong definition of knowledge that renders ordinary knowledge claims virtually impossible, and determines whether the existence of a defeater for a defeater preserves genuine knowledge in the absence of agent awareness.

**Klein’s Fourth Condition and the Gettier Problem**

To understand Klein’s response to the Gettier problem, one must first grasp his externalist framework and his specific taxonomy of defeaters. In his seminal work, particularly in *Certainty: A Refutation of Skepticism* and his subsequent papers, Klein argues that knowledge is equivalent to undefeated justified true belief. The first three conditions—truth, belief, and justification—are standard. The fourth condition, however, is the crux of his externalist solution: for S to know that P, there must be no undefeated defeater for S’s belief that P. 

A defeater, in Klein’s lexicon, is anything that, if S were aware of it, would undermine the justification or the truth of S’s belief. Klein distinguishes between objective and subjective defeaters. An objective defeater exists when there is a fact in the world that, if known, would defeat the belief. A subjective defeater exists when the agent actually possesses the defeating information. Klein’s central claim is that knowledge requires only the absence of objective, undefeated defeaters. The agent need not be aware of the defeater for it to function as such; conversely, the agent need not be aware of the absence of a defeater for the belief to count as knowledge. 

Under this framework, Gettier cases are not counterexamples to JTB + No Defeaters, but rather demonstrations that Gettier cases contain undefeated objective defeaters. In a classic Gettier scenario—such as the case where Smith has excellent evidence that Jones owns a Ford, and Jones owns a Ford, but Smith does not—the belief "Someone in the office owns a Ford" is true and justified, but it is defeated by the unknown fact that Smith himself does not own a Ford and the evidence was misleading. The hidden false lemma functions as an objective defeater. Thus, Klein argues, Gettier cases are not cases of knowledge precisely because they contain undefeated defeaters. The fourth condition is necessary because without it, justified true belief remains vulnerable to the kind of epistemic luck that Gettier exposed.

**Unknown Defeaters and Subsequent Defeaters: Undermining the Necessity of the Fourth Condition**

While Klein’s account elegantly dissolves standard Gettier cases by identifying the hidden defeaters, it encounters severe difficulties when we introduce unknown defeaters and subsequent defeaters for those defeaters. The core issue is whether the necessity of Klein’s fourth condition is undermined by the instability and infinite regress inherent in the defeat of defeaters.

Consider a scenario involving an unknown defeater (D1) and a subsequent defeater for that defeater (D2). Suppose an agent, S, forms a justified true belief that P based on evidence E. Unbeknownst to S, there exists an objective defeater D1 that, if known, would defeat the justification for P. However, D1 is itself defeated by D2. D2 is also unknown to S. According to Klein, because D1 is defeated by D2, D1 is not an undefeated defeater. Therefore, S’s belief that P is undefeated, and S knows that P.

This analysis, however, raises a troubling question: does the mere existence of D1, even if defeated by D2, undermine the necessity of Klein’s fourth condition? If the knowledge status of S’s belief oscillates based on the epistemic status of unknown facts in the world—facts that S has no cognitive access to and no way of verifying—then the fourth condition ceases to be a stable criterion for knowledge. The necessity of the condition is undermined because the truth of the belief and the quality of the justification remain entirely constant, yet the classification of the belief as "knowledge" fluctuates based on the contingent, hidden chain of defeat and undefeat. 

If D1 were not defeated by D2, S would not know. If D1 is defeated by D2, S does know. The justification E and the truth of P are invariant, but the epistemic status changes. This suggests that Klein’s fourth condition does not capture the *necessity* of knowledge so much as it tracks the objective, external fact of whether a defeater chain has been resolved in the actual world. By making knowledge contingent on the objective resolution of defeater chains that are entirely inaccessible to the agent, Klein’s requirement becomes epistemically disconnected from the agent’s cognitive situation. The necessity of the fourth condition is thus undermined: it is not that knowledge requires the absence of undefeated defeaters in a robust, necessary sense, but rather that knowledge is defined by whatever objective facts happen to be the case in the actual world, regardless of the agent's epistemic position.
