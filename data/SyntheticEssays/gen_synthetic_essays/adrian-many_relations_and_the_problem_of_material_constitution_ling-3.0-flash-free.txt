**The Unity of Matter and Form: Evaluating Hylomorphic Partism, the Five-Place Constitution Relation, and the Limits of Spacetime Co-Location**

The enduring puzzles of metaphysics—particularly the problem of the many and the statue and clay puzzle—have long tested the limits of mereological theory. When a sculptor fashions a lump of clay into a statue, we are confronted with the question of persistence and identity. What happens to the clay? Is the statue merely the clay, or is it something over and above the clay? Furthermore, if the clay and the statue occupy the exact same spacetime region, how do we avoid the problem of the many, wherein countless mereological sums (the lump, the statue, the lump-minus-the-nose, the statue-minus-the-nose) all vie for the status of ordinary objects? 

Hylomorphic Partism offers a sophisticated framework to navigate these waters. Drawing on Aristotelian metaphysics, it posits that ordinary objects are hylomorphic compounds—unities of matter (*hyle*) and form (*morphe*). Within this framework, recent developments have sought to refine the account through the introduction of a five-place constitution relation and the *in-F* (in-Form) relation. This essay evaluates this apparatus against the problem of the many and the statue-clay puzzle, specifically addressing whether the *in-F* relation successfully resolves the dialectical tension between proper and improper parthood that defeats Hud Hudson’s three-dimensionalist Partist View. Furthermore, it analyzes how form functions as a principle of unity distinct from mereological simples and region relativization, and critiques the proposal that multiple hylomorphic compounds can occupy the exact same spacetime region.

**I. Hud Hudson’s Three-Dimensionalist Partist View and the Dialectical Tension**

To appreciate the innovation of the five-place constitution relation, one must first understand the dialectical tension it seeks to resolve. Hud Hudson’s three-dimensionalist Partist View argues that ordinary objects are three-dimensional entities that persist through time by being wholly present at each moment. Crucially, Hudson adopts a Partist ontology: ordinary objects are nothing over and above the mereological sums of their proper parts. In Hudson’s framework, the lump of clay and the statue are identical; they are the same three-dimensional object, constituted by the same parts, merely arranged differently across time. This is a commitment to improper parthood: the whole (the statue) is strictly identical to one of its parts (the lump of clay).

However, this commitment to improper parthood generates a fatal dialectical tension. If the lump and the statue are strictly identical, they must share all properties, including their persistence conditions. Yet, intuitively, the lump can survive the destruction of the statue. If the statue is smashed, the clay remains. If the lump is strictly identical to the statue, the lump must be destroyed when the statue is. Hudson’s view struggles to account for this asymmetry without resorting to temporal parts (which would make him a four-dimensionalist, contradicting his three-dimensionalist commitments) or by claiming that the lump is not the statue, but merely a proper part of it. 

If the lump is merely a proper part of the statue, then the statue is a mereological sum, and the lump is just a subset of that sum. But if the lump is a proper part, it cannot be the whole statue. When the statue is destroyed, the proper part—the lump—might remain. Yet, if the lump is just a proper part of the statue, what is the lump in its own right? Hudson’s three-dimensionalist Partist View fails to give matter a principled ontological status independent of the organized whole. The dialectical tension lies in the inability to simultaneously maintain that the lump is the statue (improper parthood) and that the lump can persist through the destruction of the statue (which requires a distinction between the two). The *in-F* relation is introduced to break this symmetry.

**II. The *in-F* Relation and the Resolution of Proper and Improper Parthood**

The *in-F* relation is a non-mereological, formal-inclusion relation that holds between matter and form. To say that matter *m* is *in-F* form *f* is to say that *m* is organized according to the principle of unity that *f* constitutes. It is not a relation of composition, where parts are combined to form a whole; rather, it is a relation of formal determination. Matter *m* is *in-F* *f* just in case *m* is the material substrate structured by *f*.

This relation successfully resolves the dialectical tension that defeats Hudson’s view. By introducing *in-F*, the hylomorphic Partist distinguishes between the lump of clay (matter not yet *in-F* an artistic form, or matter *in-F* only its prime matter form) and the statue (matter *in-F* the artistic form). The lump and the statue are no longer identical through improper parthood. Nor is the lump a mere proper part of the statue. Instead, the lump is the matter *m*, and the statue is the hylomorphic compound constituted by *m* being *in-F* *f*. 

When the sculptor acts, the matter *m* transitions from being *in-F* no artistic form to being *in-F* the artistic form. The statue comes into being not by the creation of new matter, nor by the rearrangement of parts into a mereological sum, but by the imposition of a new formal principle upon the pre-existing matter. Because the *in-F* relation is asymmetric and directional, it grounds the distinct persistence conditions of the lump and the statue. The lump (matter) can persist through the loss of the artistic form (e.g., when the statue is melted back into a shapeless puddle), because matter does not essentially depend on a particular form in the same way a hylomorphic compound does. The *in-F* relation thus defeats Hudson’s three-dimensionalist Partist View by refusing to collapse the distinction between the organized and the unorganized into a mereological identity or improper parthood. It establishes a formal hierarchy that allows for the continuity of matter without the identity of the compound.

**III. The Five-Place Constitution Relation and the Problem of the Many**

The five-place constitution relation formalizes this hylomorphic ontology. Let us define the relation as *Constitutes(x, m, f, r, t)*, which is read as: "The hylomorphic compound *x* is constituted by matter *m*, form *f*, occupying spatial region *r* at time *t*." 

This five-place relation provides an elegant solution to the problem of the many. The problem of the many arises because, for any material object, there are countless mereological sums that share its spatial region (the statue, the statue-minus-a-finger, the lump, the lump-minus-a-finger, etc.). If mereological sums are ordinary objects, we face a devastating proliferation of objects, violating principles of ontological parsimony.

The five-place relation solves this by restricting ordinary objecthood to hylomorphic compounds that satisfy the formal condition *f*. The mereological sums—such as the statue-minus-a-finger—are not hylomorphic compounds; they are matter *m'* (where *m'* is a proper part of *m*) that is *in-F* the artistic form *f*. However, because *m'* is only a proper part of the matter required to instantiate the form *f* completely, the sum *m' + f* is not a fully constituted hylomorphic compound. It is a mereological artifact, a dependent entity or a mereological fiction, but not an ordinary object in its own right. 

By requiring the five coordinates—compound, matter, form, region, and time—the constitution relation ties the existence of an ordinary object to the unified actualization of a form in a specific matter at a specific place and time. The problem of the many is thus dissolved not by denying the existence of the mereological sums, but by denying them ontological status as ordinary objects. They are dependent formal-mereological constructs, but the only full-blooded entities are the hylomorphic compounds captured by the five-place relation.
