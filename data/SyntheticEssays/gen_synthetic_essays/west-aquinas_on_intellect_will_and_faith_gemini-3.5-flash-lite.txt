# The Volition of Belief: Aquinas, Metaphysical Goodness, and the Indirect Genesis of Christian Faith

## Introduction

In the architecture of medieval epistemology and theology, few problems possess the structural delicacy and enduring philosophical gravity of Thomas Aquinas’s account of Christian faith (*fides*). Modern epistemological concerns—obsessed as they are with internalist foundationalism, evidentialism, and the deontological duties of the epistemic agent—frequently look upon religious assent as an irrational leap, a suspension of critical faculties, or an arbitrary fideistic commitment. Yet Aquinas offers an account that resists these reductionisms, locating the genesis of faith not in a brute act of arbitrary will, nor in the coercive clarity of demonstrative vision, but in a complex, interior movement where the will, under the influence of divine grace, moves the intellect to assent. 

This essay evaluates the claim that Thomas Aquinas advances an **indirect descriptive volitionalism** regarding the acquisition of Christian faith. To understand this claim, we must investigate how the will can move the intellect to assent to propositions whose intrinsic evidence is insufficient to compel belief. This inquiry inevitably leads us to the metaphysical heart of Thomistic philosophy: the convertibility of being and goodness (*ens et bonum convertuntur*). It is through this ontological identity that Aquinas defends faith against charges of epistemic unjustifiedness and the apparent absence of subjective certainty. 

Finally, this essay analyzes the profound philosophical tension generated by proposing that faith operates through the will’s inclination toward the universal good rather than through direct intellectual demonstration. By resolving this dilemma, we illuminate the profound theological and moral implications of faith for the transformation of the human agent’s heart and desires, demonstrating that for Aquinas, believing is an act of the intellect moved by the will at the behest of love.

---

## I. Defining the Framework: Indirect Descriptive Volitionalism

To attribute a "volitionalism" to Aquinas requires immediate nuance. Direct volitionalism—the thesis that an agent can believe a proposition simply by deciding to do so, regardless of the perceived evidence—is psychologically implausible and foreign to Aquinas. The intellect is a natural faculty ordered to truth; it cannot be commanded to regard as true that which appears manifestly false or entirely unsupported by reasons, just as the eye cannot simply choose to see darkness as light. 

Aquinas explicitly denies that the will commands the intellect in a despotic manner (*imperium despoticum*), as a master commands a slave. Instead, the will moves the intellect in a political manner (*imperium politicum*), as a ruler governs free citizens who retain their own intrinsic nature and proper operations (*Summa Theologiae* [ST] II-II, q. 10, a. 2). The intellect retains its specification from its proper formal object: truth.

If direct volitionalism is untenable in Thomistic psychology, what does it mean to characterize Aquinas’s view as an **indirect descriptive volitionalism**? 
* It is **descriptive** because it seeks to explain the actual psychological and metaphysical mechanics by which an agent comes to possess the habit of faith, mapping the structural interplay between the cognitive and appetitive faculties. 
* It is **volitional** because the decisive tipping point in the acquisition of faith is not epistemic coercion by the evidence, but a movement of the human will. 
* It is **indirect** because the will does not manufacture evidence out of whole cloth, nor does it arbitrarily force the intellect to assent; rather, the will moves the intellect *indirectly* by inclining it toward the good, thereby altering the affective conditions under which the intellect evaluates the insufficient evidence before it.

In faith, the intellect is confronted with propositions that lack the luminous self-evidence of first principles (e.g., the principle of non-contradiction) or the conclusive demonstrative force of scientific demonstration. The articles of faith—such as the Trinity, the Incarnation, and the resurrection of the dead—are obscure to human reason. Why, then, does the intellect assent to them? Aquinas answers that the intellect assents not because it is forced by the weight of the evidence, but because the will commands it to do so. And why does the will command this assent? Because the will judges that believing these things is good, fitting, and ordered toward the ultimate human flourishing: union with God.

## II. The Mechanics of Assent: Moving the Intellect Without Evidence

To grasp how the will can move the intellect in the absence of compelling evidence, we must examine Aquinas’s psychology of the act of faith, particularly as articulated in *ST* II-II, q. 2, a. 9, and *De Veritate*, q. 14, a. 1. 

In any act of assent, two elements are present: the object (what is known or believed) and the subject (the knower). Assent can be brought about in two ways:
1. **By the movement of the object:** The intellect is necessitated to assent when the object is seen clearly and evidently. This occurs in scientific knowledge (*scientia*) and intellectual intuition (*intellectus*). Here, the truth of the proposition shines forth so brightly that the intellect has no choice but to yield.
2. **By the movement of the will:** When the object does not manifest its truth with self-evidence, the intellect remains suspended in doubt or mere opinion, unless the will intervenes. The will, considering the desirability, goodness, or utility of assenting, commands the intellect to give its firm assent to the non-evident proposition.

In the case of Christian faith, the propositions of faith lack the first kind of movement. As Aquinas notes, "Faith is the evidence of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1; cf. *ST* II-II, q. 1, a. 4). The believer does not possess a beatific vision of the divine essence, nor can syllogistic logic compellingly demonstrate the Trinity from empirical premises. Therefore, the intellect’s movement to assent must come from the second source: the will.

This presents a serious philosophical difficulty. If the intellect is ordered exclusively to truth, and if its proper motive is evidence, how can an affective faculty (the will) move a cognitive faculty (the intellect) without violating the integrity of the intellect? If the will forces the intellect to assent to something for which there is insufficient evidence, is faith not reduced to a form of epistemic wishful thinking, a subjective preference masquerading as knowledge?

Aquinas avoids this charge of irrationalism by distinguishing between *speculative* insufficiency and *practical* sufficiency. From a strictly speculative (or evidential) standpoint, the motives of credibility—such as miracles, prophecies, and the historical propagation of the Church—do not logically compel the intellect with demonstrative necessity. They render the act of faith *credible* (*credibile*), but not *evident* (*evidens*). 

However, from a practical standpoint, the will perceives a compelling reason to assent: the supreme authority of God revealing the truth, coupled with the realization that adhering to this truth is the sole pathway to human beatitude. The will says, in effect: "Although my intellect cannot see the intrinsic intelligibility of this mystery, I recognize that God is truth itself, that He cannot deceive or be deceived, and that to commit my life to His revealed word is the supreme good." Thus, the will moves the intellect not by altering the speculative evidence, but by supplying a non-speculative, affective reason for taking the leap of commitment.

## III. The Metaphysics of Being and Goodness (*Ens et Bonum*)

To defend Aquinas against the charge of epistemic unjustifiedness—the notion that faith is a blind, groundless leap into the dark—we must plunge into the deep waters of his metaphysics: the convertibility of being and goodness (*convertuntur ens et bonum*). 

In *De Veritate* (q. 21, a. 1) and the *Summa Theologiae* (I, q. 5, a. 1), Aquinas establishes that being and goodness are logically and ontologically identical in reality, differing only conceptually (*ratione*). Every being, insofar as it is actual, is a being, and insofar as it has actuality, it is desirable, perfect, and good. God is Subsistent Being Itself (*Ipsum Esse Subsistens*); therefore, God is Subsistent Goodness Itself.

This metaphysical principle is not an abstract ornament; it is the linchpin that saves Thomistic faith from epistemological subjectivism. If goodness were merely a subjective projection of human desire, disconnected from the ontological structure of reality, then the will’s movement of the intellect would be entirely subjective—a psychological mechanism untethered from truth. But because *ens* and *bonum* are convertible, a movement toward the ultimate good is simultaneously a movement toward ultimate reality and truth.

When the human will is moved by grace to embrace the object of faith, it is not retreating into a private fantasy world; it is aligning itself with the transcendent ontological gravity of God. The will recognizes that God—who is Truth and Goodness united—is the ultimate fulfillment of the human person. Therefore, the lack of *epistemic* evidence in the propositional content of faith is compensated for by the supreme *ontological* reality of the divine source. 

This dissolves the modern anxiety over the "absence of certainty" in faith. Modern epistemology equates certainty with Cartesian indubitability or empirical verification. For Aquinas, however, certainty is rooted in the firmness of the adherence to truth. In faith, the certainty is supreme, though its root is not the clarity of the vision, but the firmness of the will clinging to the supreme Good. As Aquinas writes:

> "The firmness of faith... arises from the authority of God revealing, which is an infallible rule... The cause of faith is not that which is seen, but that which is believed upon the authority of the revealer" (*ST* II-II, q. 4, a. 1).

The epistemological critic objects: *"How can you be certain of something you cannot see?"* 
Aquinas replies: *"I am not certain because of my intellectual inspection of the evidence; I am certain because I cleave to God, who is Truth Itself, through a will transformed by grace."* 

The metaphysical identity of being and goodness guarantees that the will's appetite for the good does not lead the intellect away from truth, but precisely into the heart of transcendent truth. The goodness of God is so overwhelming that when the will apprehends it through grace, it commands the intellect to assent with absolute firmness, bridging the gap left by the insufficiency of discursive evidence.

## IV. The Philosophical Tension: Universal Good vs. Intellectual Demonstration

Despite the elegance of this metaphysical reconciliation, proposing that faith operates through the will’s inclination toward the universal good rather than through direct intellectual demonstration generates a profound philosophical tension. 

The tension can be stated as follows: **The intellect’s primary and proper perfection is the apprehension of truth, whereas the will’s primary and proper perfection is the acquisition of the good.** If faith is an act of the intellect, yet its decisive moving principle is the will’s appetite for the good, does this not subordinate the intellect’s proper end (truth) to the will’s end (the good)? Does this not imply a form of intellectual violence, where the mind is coerced not by the intrinsic light of the object, but by the affective pressure of the heart's desires?

This tension touches upon the perennial medieval debate between intellectualism and voluntarism. 
* **Intellectualism** (associated by some interpreters with thinkers like Siger of Brabant or, in different ways, Aristotle) tends to prioritize the intellect, arguing that the beatific vision consists primarily in the act of knowing, and that the will merely follows the intellect's illumination.
* **Voluntarism** (found in varying degrees among Franciscan thinkers like Duns Scotus and William of Ockham) exalts the will as a radically free faculty that can transcend or even dictate intellectual judgments.

Aquinas steers a brilliant, precarious middle course. He maintains the primacy of the intellect in the order of specification and perfection (knowing God is higher than merely loving Him in this life, because knowing brings the object *into* the knower, whereas loving unites the knower *to* the object). Yet, in the specific genesis of *faith*, he grants the initiating, moving primacy to the will. 

Why does this generate a philosophical crisis? Because if the will moves the intellect to assent to propositions that are obscure, a critical mind must ask: *On what grounds does the will judge that this particular set of obscure propositions is worthy of assent, rather than another?* 

If the will judges arbitrarily, faith is reduced to irrational fideism. If the will judges based on natural evidence, faith collapses into natural theology (*scientia*). 

Aquinas resolves this dilemma by introducing the indispensable category of **Divine Grace**. The will does not move the intellect to faith by its own natural power alone. Natural volition could never suffice to bridge the infinite abyss between human cognition and the mysteries of the triune God. Instead, the human will is itself moved, healed, and elevated by prevenient and operative grace. 

In *ST* II-II, q. 6, a. 1, Aquinas addresses whether the act of faith is meritorious. He argues that because the assent of faith is an act of the intellect commanded by the will, and because that will is moved by grace, the act of faith possesses moral and spiritual value. Grace transforms the subjective orientation of the agent. It heals the wound of original sin, which clouds the intellect and disorders the affections, redirecting the will toward its true supernatural end: God as beatitude.

Thus, the tension is resolved: the will moves the intellect, but the grace of God moves the will. The intellect is not violated by brute force, but is elevated by love. As Augustine famously wrote—and Aquinas approvingly quotes—*"Nemo credit nisi volens"* (No one believes except of his own volition) (*ST* II-II, q. 2, a. 2). Yet, no one wills to believe savingly without the interior attraction of divine grace (*trahat a Patre*).

## V. Theological and Moral Implications: The Transformation of the Heart and Desires

To conclude our evaluation of indirect descriptive volitionalism, we must examine its broader theological and moral implications for the human agent. If faith is acquired and sustained through this intricate dance between intellect, will, grace, and goodness, what does this imply for the moral life and the transformation of the human heart?

First, it implies that **faith is fundamentally a moral and relational act, not merely a disembodied cognitive calculation.** Modern epistemology often treats belief as a cold, mechanical output of information processing: data goes in, probability is calculated, and belief is the resulting software state. Aquinas’s volitional account shatters this cold mechanistic model. Because faith is commanded by the will, and because the will is the seat of love (*amor*) and desire, faith is inextricably bound to the moral state of the person.

This explains why Aquinas insists that living faith (*fides formata*) is intrinsically united with charity (*caritas*). In *ST* II-II, q. 4, a. 3, Aquinas asks whether faith can exist without charity. He distinguishes between *fides informis* (formless faith, or dead faith, which can exist in a sinner) and *fides formata* (formed faith, living faith). While a sinner can possess a rudimentary intellectual assent commanded by a fear-stricken or unstable will, genuine, mature Christian faith is "formed" and vivified by charity. 

Charity is the perfection of the will. When the will is animated by charity—the supernatural love of God—it commands the intellect with a deep, harmonious conviction. The believer does not merely hold orthodox propositions in their head; they cleave to God with their whole heart. The moral transformation of the agent directly empowers and clarifies their epistemic posture. The pure in heart see God, not because their eyes are biologically altered, but because their desires are rightly ordered, allowing their will to command intellectual assent without the static of pride, concupiscence, or self-deception.

Second, this volitional-metaphysical framework radically redefines the human quest for truth. In a purely rationalist paradigm, truth is something conquered by the aggressive deployment of dialectic and empirical observation. In Aquinas’s indirect descriptive volitionalism, truth—especially transcendent truth—is something received through a posture of receptive love. One cannot bully one's way into the mysteries of the Trinity through syllogisms alone; one must be drawn by the goodness of God, yielding the will in trust, and allowing the intellect to be illuminated from above.

Third, this has profound implications for how we understand doubt and religious struggle. Far from viewing doubt as an immediate invalidation of faith, Aquinas’s framework accommodates the psychological reality that the human intellect, operating in this mortal life (*in via*), is constantly buffeted by non-evident darkness. Because faith does not rest on the luminous clarity of vision, doubts will inevitably arise. 

However, because the certainty of faith is anchored in the will’s steadfast adherence to the supreme Good through grace, the believer can maintain existential and theological stability even amid intellectual obscurity. Doubt is an intellectual friction; but faith is a volitional anchor. When the intellect falters in the fog of skepticism, the will—strengthened by charity—holds fast to the goodness of God, commanding the intellect to persevere until the dawn of the beatific vision, where faith will finally pass away into sight.

---

## Conclusion

Thomas Aquinas’s account of Christian faith as an indirect descriptive volitionalism is a masterpiece of philosophical synthesis. By carefully navigating between the rocks of rationalistic evidentialism and irrational voluntarism, Aquinas constructs a psychology of belief that honors both the intellectual dignity of human cognition and the affective primacy of the human will.

Through our investigation, we have seen how the will moves the intellect to assent to non-evident propositions not by arbitrary fiat, but by perceiving the supreme goodness and credibility of the divine Revealer. We have observed how the metaphysical convertibility of being and goodness (*ens et bonum*) rescues this volitional movement from the abyss of subjective wishful thinking, anchoring the believer’s certainty in the objective ontological reality of God. We have traced how the philosophical tension between the intellect's quest for truth and the will's inclination toward the good is harmonized by the transformative agency of divine grace.

Ultimately, Aquinas reveals that to believe is not merely to check a theological box or to calculate an epistemic probability. To believe is to undergo a profound moral and spiritual transformation of the heart and desires. It is an act where the will, enlivened by grace and formed by charity, gently urges the intellect to embrace the obscured brilliance of divine mystery, trusting that the God who is Being is also the supreme Good, and that the truth which eludes our finite sight is the very Love that holds the cosmos together.
