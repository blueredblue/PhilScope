## The Halo and the Horizon: Kant, Marion, and the Aporias of Selfhood

**Introduction**

The question of what constitutes the self is perhaps the most persistent and vexing problem in modern philosophy. For Immanuel Kant, the self is not a substance to be discovered but a condition to be presupposed—the transcendental unity of apperception that makes all experience possible. Yet this rigorous formal account, while securing the possibility of objective knowledge, seems to leave the *individual* self—the concrete, feeling, acting, and suffering subject—strangely absent. The claim under examination here is that Kantian selfhood, in its full articulation, requires a reconciliation between the restricted domain of *speculative possibility* (what can be known through the formal conditions of experience) and the demands of *practical reason* (the moral postulates that exceed those conditions), alongside the *supersensible experiences* theorized by Jean-Luc Marion—those saturated phenomena that exceed all conceptual grasp.

This essay will evaluate this claim by undertaking a fourfold analysis. First, I will demonstrate how Kant’s formal conditions of experience—space, time, and the categories—rigorously restrict speculative possibility, rendering any metaphysical knowledge of the self impossible. Second, I will contrast Kant’s restrictive, epistemic notion of possibility with Aristotle’s *dynamic* potentiality (dunamis) and Heidegger’s *existential* possibility (Seinkönnen), showing how these alternatives ground the self in ontological or existential processes rather than mere logical conditions. Third, I will assess the objection that ignoring the practical sources of possibility—the moral law and its postulates—leaves the Kantian account of the individual self incomplete, arguing that this objection is valid but that Kant’s own practical turn introduces a bifurcation that is itself problematic. Fourth, I will address the fundamental tension between inner sense (the receptive, temporal self-appearance) and apperception (the spontaneous, logical unity of the “I think”), a tension that Marion’s phenomenology of givenness exploits to its limit. Finally, I will examine whether Marion’s saturated phenomena *constitute* the self or merely appear *within* an individual halo of possibility. My conclusion will be that the claim of reconciliation is *necessary* but *aporetic*: the Kantian self cannot be complete without a phenomenological openness to excess, yet Marion’s saturated phenomena do not constitute the self; rather, they interrupt its horizon, revealing the self as a finite witness rather than a stable substance. The true selfhood lies in the productive tension between the formal conditions that make experience possible and the excessive givenness that shatters those conditions—a tension that neither Kant nor Marion alone can resolve.

---

**Part I: The Kantian Architecture—Formal Conditions and the Restriction of Speculative Possibility**

To understand the claim that Kantian selfhood requires reconciliation, one must first grasp the radical nature of Kant’s Copernican revolution. In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Kant argues that we can have a priori knowledge of objects only insofar as they conform to the structure of our cognitive faculties. The conditions of *possible experience* are thus the conditions of the *possibility of objects of experience*. Possibility, in the Kantian sense, is not a metaphysical property of things-in-themselves but an epistemic constraint on what can be *given* to us as an object. For something to be *possible* in the strict sense, it must be able to be intuited in space and time (the pure forms of sensibility) and subsumed under the categories (the pure concepts of the understanding, such as causality, substance, and unity). Anything that fails to meet these formal conditions is *speculatively impossible*—not necessarily logically contradictory, but incapable of being known or experienced.

This restriction is most evident in Kant’s treatment of rational psychology. The paralogisms of pure reason expose the fallacy of treating the “I” as a substance, a simple entity, or a person. The transcendental unity of apperception—the “I think” that must be able to accompany all my representations—is a *formal* condition of thought, not an object of intuition. We do not perceive the self; we *think* it as the logical subject of our representations. Consequently, speculative metaphysics, which seeks to know the soul’s immortality, its simplicity, or its substantiality, is entirely illegitimate. The self, as a thing-in-itself, is forever beyond the bounds of possible experience. Kant’s famous dictum, “I had to deny knowledge in order to make room for faith,” applies directly here: speculative reason cannot tell us *what* the self is; it can only tell us *how* the self must be thought as the condition of unified experience.

This restriction of speculative possibility is not a failure but a triumph of critical philosophy. It saves us from the dogmatic illusions of rationalism (Descartes’ res cogitans) and the skeptical despair of empiricism (Hume’s bundle of perceptions). However, it comes at a cost: the self is reduced to a mere logical function, a “transcendental subject” that is entirely devoid of content. The *individual* self—the self with a particular history, character, and embodiment—is relegated to the realm of inner sense, where it is known only as a series of temporally ordered representations, subject to the same causal laws as external phenomena. In this phenomenal realm, the self is determined, passive, and opaque. It is not a unified agent but a continuous flow of mental states.

The claim that Kantian selfhood requires reconciliation with practical reason arises precisely from this inadequacy. If the self is only a formal condition of knowledge, then it cannot account for moral responsibility, freedom, or the experience of obligation. The *Critique of Practical Reason* steps into this breach. Here, Kant introduces a different kind of possibility—not speculative but *practical*. The moral law, which we experience as the categorical imperative, grounds the possibility of freedom. We are not merely determined beings; we are agents capable of acting according to the representation of laws. Freedom, immortality, and God are not objects of speculative knowledge but *postulates* of practical reason—necessary conditions for the moral law to be coherent. These postulates are not *known* but *believed* (Vernunftglaube).

Does this practical turn reconcile the speculative restriction with a richer notion of selfhood? Partially, but at the cost of a deep bifurcation. The self is now split into a phenomenal self (determined, empirical, known through inner sense) and a noumenal self (free, rational, unknowable in itself). This bifurcation is the source of endless philosophical problems. How can a noumenal self act in the phenomenal world? How can freedom be reconciled with causality? Kant’s answer—that freedom is a transcendental idea that we must *think* but cannot *know*—is logically coherent but phenomenologically unsatisfying. The individual self, as a lived experience, does not feel like a mere logical point or a postulate. It feels like a concrete, embodied, and temporally extended being. This is where Marion’s phenomenology enters the picture, offering an account of experience that exceeds the Kantian categories.

**Part II: Contrasting Possibility—Aristotle’s Dynamic Potentiality and Heidegger’s Existential Possibility**

To fully appreciate the Kantian restriction, it is illuminating to contrast it with two other major philosophical accounts of possibility: Aristotle’s dynamic potentiality and Heidegger’s existential possibility. These alternatives reveal that Kant’s notion of possibility is *epistemic* and *formal*, whereas Aristotle’s is *ontological* and *substantial*, and Heidegger’s is *existential* and *temporal*.

For Aristotle, possibility (dunamis) is a fundamental mode of being. In the *Metaphysics*, dunamis is the capacity of a substance to change or to bring about change. An acorn has the potentiality (dunamis) to become an oak tree; a sculptor has the potentiality to shape a statue from marble. This potentiality is not a mere logical possibility but an ontological one—it is rooted in the *form* (eidos) of the substance. The self, for Aristotle, is the psyche, the form of a living body. Its possibilities are determined by its nature: a human being has the potential for rational thought, for moral virtue, for social life. These potentials are *dynamic*; they are actualized (energeia) through activity. The self is not a static logical point but a developing substance that realizes its essence over time.

Contrast this with Kant. For Kant, the self has no *essence* that can be actualized. The “I think” is not a substance with potentialities; it is a formal condition. Possibility, for Kant, is not about what a being *can become* but about what can *be known*. This is a decisive shift from ontology to epistemology. Aristotle asks, “What is the being of a human being?” Kant asks, “What are the conditions under which a human being can have objective experience?” The former grounds possibility in the nature of the thing; the latter grounds it in the structure of the mind. Consequently, the Aristotelian self is rich with content—it has a telos, a purpose, a set of virtues to cultivate—while the Kantian self is empty, a mere placeholder for the unity of consciousness.

Heidegger radicalizes this critique in *Being and Time*. For Heidegger, Dasein (the being that we are) is not a substance but a *possibility of being* (Seinkönnen). Dasein is not a thing that has possibilities; it *is* its possibilities. Existence is not a property but a mode of being that is always ahead of itself, projecting itself onto future possibilities. This is what Heidegger calls *existential possibility*—a possibility that is not merely logical or ontological but *temporal* and *finite*. Dasein is thrown into a world, finds itself in a situation, and must choose among its possibilities. The self is not a static unity but a dynamic project. Heidegger explicitly critiques Kant for reducing the self to a transcendental subject, divorced from its concrete existence. For Heidegger, the self is not a “worldless” ego but a being-in-the-world, constituted by its relations to things, others, and its own death.

Both Aristotle and Heidegger offer accounts of possibility that are richer than Kant’s formal restriction. Aristotle’s dynamic possibility grounds the self in a natural teleology; Heidegger’s existential possibility grounds the self in temporality and finitude. However, neither Aristotle nor Heidegger fully addresses the *excess* of experience that Marion theorizes. Aristotle’s potentiality is bounded by the form; a human being cannot become a god. Heidegger’s possibility is bounded by death; Dasein’s possibilities are finite. Marion’s saturated phenomena, as we shall see, break through these bounds, offering an *excessive* givenness that cannot be contained by any form or horizon.

**Part III: The Objection from Practical Sources—The Incompleteness of the Formal Self**

The objection that ignoring practical sources of possibility renders accounts of the individual self incomplete is a powerful one. The Kantian formal self, as described in the First Critique, is a mere logical condition. It has no character, no history, no projects, no moral identity. It is, as Kant himself admits, a “transcendental subject” that is “completely empty.” But the *individual* self—the self that I am—is not empty. I have a name, a body, a set of memories, a set of values, a sense of responsibility, and a future to anticipate. The formal account cannot explain any of these features.

Kant’s own practical philosophy attempts to fill this void. The moral law, as experienced through the categorical imperative, reveals the self as a free agent. This is a *practical* source of possibility: I am able to act not merely according to natural causality but according to the representation of laws. This freedom is the ground of my dignity and my moral personality. The postulates of practical reason—God, freedom, immortality—are not speculative possibilities but *practical necessities*. They are conditions under which the moral law can be coherently thought.

However, the objection deepens when we consider whether Kant’s practical sources are sufficient. The moral self is still a *formal* self in a different guise. It is the self insofar as it is subject to the moral law, but the moral law is universal and necessary. It does not tell me *who I am* as a unique individual. My particular talents, my particular loves, my particular suffering—these are not determined by the moral law. They belong to the phenomenal realm, where they are subject to causal explanation. The Kantian self, even in its practical dimension, remains a split subject: a noumenal, rational, moral agent on one hand; a phenomenal, empirical, determined being on the other. The *individual* self—the self that is both rational and embodied, both free and situated—is nowhere to be found.

This is precisely where Marion’s phenomenology offers a corrective. Marion argues that the Kantian reduction of possibility to the conditions of possible experience is a reduction of *givenness* to *objectivity*. For Kant, something is given only if it can be objectified under the categories. But what if there are phenomena that are given *without* being objectifiable? What if there are experiences that exceed the categories, that saturate intuition with an excess that the understanding cannot contain? These are what Marion calls *saturated phenomena*. They are not merely possible experiences; they are *hyper-experiences* that reveal the limits of the Kantian framework. By attending to these phenomena, we can recover a notion of the self that is not merely logical or moral but *sentient* and *receptive*—a self that is constituted not by its concepts but by its capacity to be overwhelmed by what is given.

---

**Part IV: The Tension Between Inner Sense and Apperception**

The tension between inner sense and apperception is the structural fault line in Kant’s account of selfhood. Inner sense (in Kant’s terminology) is the form of our intuition of ourselves. It is time. Through inner sense, we are conscious of our own mental states as a succession of representations. Inner sense is *receptive*: it does not produce representations but receives them. Apperception, on the other hand, is the *spontaneous* activity of the understanding. The “I think” is the pure, intellectual consciousness that accompanies all representations. It is the condition of their unity.

The problem is that these two aspects of the self seem irreconcilable. Inner sense gives us the self as *appearance*—a temporal flux of mental states. Apperception gives us the self as *thought*—a logical unity that is not in time. Kant insists that the “I” is not an intuition but a thought; it is not given in inner sense but is the condition of any intuition. Yet how can a thought be a self? How can a logical function have experiences, feel emotions, or make decisions? Kant’s answer is that the self is not an object; it is a subject. But this answer is unsatisfactory because it leaves the self as a mere “x” that we cannot know.

This tension is the root of the *paralogisms*. Rational psychology falls into error when it treats the apperceptive unity as if it were a substance. But even Kant’s critical solution leaves a gap: the self is known only as a formal condition, never as a concrete reality. The *individual* self—the self that is both the subject of experience and the object of inner sense—remains elusive. How can I be both the one who thinks and the one who feels? How can the spontaneity of apperception be united with the receptivity of inner sense?

Marion seizes on this tension. He argues that Kant’s account of the self is fundamentally *reduced* to its conditions. The self is not given to itself; it is only inferred as a condition of givenness. This is what Marion calls the “subject” as opposed to the “gifted” (le donataire). The subject, in the Kantian sense, is a *pole* of constitution—it actively synthesizes representations. But Marion proposes a different kind of self: the *gifted* is the one who *receives* givenness. The gifted is not the condition of possibility of experience but the *respondent* to what is given. This shift from constitution to reception is the heart of Marion’s phenomenology.

---

**Part V: Saturated Phenomena and the Individual Halo of Possibility**

Marion’s saturated phenomena are defined by four excesses: the event (excess of quantity), the idol (excess of quality), the flesh (excess of relation), and the icon (excess of modality). Each of these phenomena overwhelms the Kantian categories. The event cannot be comprehended because it has too many possible causes and effects; it is unforeseeable and unrepeatable. The idol saturates our gaze with an intensity that cannot be captured by a concept. The flesh is the pure experience of one’s own body, an auto-affection that precedes any objectification. The icon is the face of the Other, which exceeds all my attempts to reduce it to a representation.

These saturated phenomena are *supersensible* in a specific sense: they are not objects of possible experience in the Kantian sense, because they cannot be subsumed under the categories. Yet they are *given*—they appear to consciousness. Marion argues that givenness is more fundamental than objectivity. Before we constitute an object, we are given something. This givenness is not conditioned by the subject; rather, it *conditions* the subject. The subject is not the master of the phenomenon but its *witness*.

Now, the crucial question: Do saturated phenomena *constitute* the self, or do they merely appear *within* an individual halo of possibility? Marion’s answer is nuanced. He distinguishes between the *self* (the ego, the subject) and the *gifted* (the one who receives). The saturated phenomenon does not constitute the self as a substance; rather, it *interrupts* the self’s activity. The self is not the source of the phenomenon; it is the *recipient*. The “halo” is the finite horizon of the subject—the set of conditions (Kantian forms, Heideggerian existential structures) that allow the phenomenon to appear at all. But the saturated phenomenon *bursts* this halo. It exceeds the horizon and reveals its limits.

Does this mean that the self is constituted by the saturated phenomenon? No. The self is not constituted; it is *called* into being. The saturated phenomenon addresses the self, summons it to respond. The self becomes the *witness*—the one who bears testimony to the excess. This is not a constitution but a *transformation*. The self is not a stable substance but a *response* to givenness. The individual halo of possibility is the condition for the appearance of the phenomenon, but the phenomenon itself is not reducible to that halo. It is *given* in excess of it.

This has profound implications for the Kantian claim. Marion shows that the formal conditions of experience do not exhaust the realm of givenness. There are experiences that are *possible*—that are given—but that are not *objectively* possible in the Kantian sense. These experiences reveal the self not as a logical condition but as a *finite witness* to an infinite givenness. The self is not the master of its experience but its *servant*.

---

**Part VI: Evaluation and Conclusion—The Aporia of Selfhood**

We are now in a position to evaluate the original claim: that Kantian selfhood requires reconciling speculative possibility with practical reason and Marion’s supersensible experiences. My assessment is that the claim is *valid as a demand* but *aporetic in its resolution*.

First, the formal conditions of experience do indeed restrict speculative possibility. Kant is correct that we cannot *know* the self as a thing-in-itself. Any attempt to do so leads to paralogisms. This restriction is a permanent achievement of critical philosophy. However, this restriction does not exhaust the realm of *givenness*. Marion demonstrates that there are phenomena that are given without being objectified. These phenomena are not merely speculative possibilities; they are *experienced* possibilities. The saturated phenomena of the event, the idol, the flesh, and the icon are not objects of knowledge but events of givenness.

Second, the contrast with Aristotle and Heidegger reveals that possibility is not merely formal. Aristotle’s dynamic potentiality grounds the self in a natural teleology; Heidegger’s existential possibility grounds the self in temporality and finitude. Both offer richer accounts of the individual self than Kant’s formal restriction. However, neither Aristotle nor Heidegger can account for the *excess* of saturated phenomena. Aristotle’s potentiality is bounded by the form; Heidegger’s possibility is bounded by death. Marion’s saturated phenomena break through these bounds, revealing a givenness that is *unbounded*.

Third, the objection that ignoring practical sources of possibility renders the account of the individual self incomplete is valid. Kant’s practical philosophy introduces freedom as a postulate, but it does so at the cost of bifurcating the self into phenomenal and noumenal realms. The individual self remains elusive. Marion’s phenomenology offers a way to recover the individual self as a *witness*—a being who is addressed by givenness and who responds. This is a more complete account than either the formal or the practical Kantian self.

Fourth, the tension between inner sense and apperception is the structural aporia of Kantian selfhood. Kant cannot unite the receptive, temporal self with the spontaneous, logical self. Marion’s concept of the *gifted* offers a resolution: the self is not a unity of apperception but a *receptivity* to givenness. The self is not the condition of possibility of experience but the *response* to what is given.

Finally, do saturated phenomena constitute the self? No. They do not constitute the self; they *interrupt* it. They appear *within* the individual halo of possibility, but they burst that halo. The self is not constituted by the phenomenon but is *called* into being by it. The self is not a substance but a *witness*. This is the deepest insight of Marion’s phenomenology: the self is not a thing but a *response*.

In conclusion, the claim that Kantian selfhood requires reconciling speculative possibility with practical reason and Marion’s supersensible experiences is *necessary* but *unresolvable*. The Kantian self is a formal condition that cannot be known; the practical self is a postulate that cannot be experienced; the Marionian self is a witness that cannot be constituted. The individual self is the *tension* between these three—a tension that cannot be overcome but must be *endured*. The self is not a synthesis but an aporia. It is the horizon that is always exceeded, the halo that is always broken, the witness that is always addressed. To be a self is to be caught between the formal conditions of possibility and the excessive givenness of the impossible. This is not a failure of philosophy but its highest achievement: to recognize that the self is not a thing to be known but a mystery to be lived.
