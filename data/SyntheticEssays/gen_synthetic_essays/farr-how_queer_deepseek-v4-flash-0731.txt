### The Queerness of Values: A Critique of Mackie’s Metaphysical Prejudice

In the landscape of metaethics, few arguments have exerted as much dialectical pressure as J. L. Mackie’s “argument from queerness.” Presented in his seminal work *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, the argument serves as the primary positive support for his error theory—the view that all moral judgments are false because they presuppose the existence of objective moral values that do not, in fact, exist. Mackie contends that if objective moral values were real, they would be “entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe.” Furthermore, our knowledge of them would require “some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly different from our ordinary ways of knowing everything else.” Because these metaphysical and epistemic implications are so profoundly alien to our standard, scientifically-grounded worldview, Mackie concludes that we have strong grounds to reject moral realism. The argument is elegant in its simplicity and devastating in its apparent scope. However, a rigorous philosophical evaluation reveals that the argument from queerness is not the decisive refutation Mackie believed it to be. While it successfully highlights a genuine tension between objective morality and a strict naturalistic ontology, its efficacy rests entirely upon an unargued and contestable standard of plausibility. By equating “strangeness” with “non-existence,” Mackie commits a metaphysical prejudice that, if applied consistently, would undermine not only moral properties but also numerous other indispensable features of our conceptual and scientific schema. Consequently, the presumption against queerness does not successfully eliminate the possibility of objective moral facts; rather, it merely forces the moral realist to choose between an untenable naturalistic reduction and a robust, albeit metaphysically expensive, non-naturalism. The argument is a powerful challenge, but it is not a proof of moral error.

#### I. The Anatomy of Queerness: Metaphysical and Epistemic Strangeness

To properly evaluate Mackie’s argument, one must first understand its precise structure. Mackie identifies two distinct, though interrelated, prongs of queerness: the metaphysical and the epistemic. The metaphysical prong targets the intrinsic nature of objective values. According to Mackie, objective moral values—if they existed—would possess a unique and bizarre property: intrinsic prescriptivity, or what he calls “to-be-pursuedness.” Unlike natural facts, such as the atomic weight of carbon or the gravitational pull of a planet, a moral fact like “cruelty is wrong” would not merely describe a state of affairs; it would inherently demand action. The wrongness of cruelty would be an objective feature of the act itself, carrying with it an imperative quality that necessarily provides a reason for action to any agent who apprehends it. This is not a relational property, dependent on human desires, conventions, or institutions; it is a categorical, intrinsic property of the world. Mackie argues that such a property is “utterly different from anything else in the universe” because nothing in our empirical experience exhibits this combination of factual description and inherent normative prescription. We observe the world as a series of facts, but those facts do not, in themselves, tell us what we ought to do.

The epistemic prong follows logically from the metaphysical one. If objective moral values are non-natural, intrinsically prescriptive entities, then how could we possibly come to know them? Our standard epistemic faculties—sense perception, introspection, and rational inference based on empirical data—are all attuned to the natural world. They can tell us what *is*, but they cannot tell us what *ought to be* in the objective, categorical sense Mackie describes. To grasp these strange entities, we would require a distinct cognitive mechanism, a faculty of “moral intuition” that operates outside the causal nexus of the natural world. This faculty would allow us to directly apprehend the normative fabric of reality, a form of knowledge that is entirely disconnected from the empirical methods that have proven so successful in science. Mackie finds this epistemic apparatus deeply suspect. He argues that the postulation of such a faculty is ad hoc and lacks any independent justification. It is a “queer” way of knowing, and the burden of proof falls on the moral realist to explain how such a faculty works, how it is reliable, and how it avoids the massive sceptical problems that plague any non-causal account of knowledge.

It is crucial to note that Mackie’s argument is not merely an appeal to ignorance. He is not saying that because we don’t currently understand moral knowledge, it cannot exist. Rather, he is employing a principle of ontological parsimony. The postulation of queer entities and queer faculties is a theoretical cost. In the absence of a compelling explanatory need, we are justified in rejecting such costly postulations in favor of a more economical ontology. He contrasts this with scientific entities, which, while sometimes strange, are ultimately part of the causal fabric of the universe and are necessary for explaining observable phenomena. Moral properties, by contrast, are causally inert and explanatorily redundant. We can explain why people behave morally—and why they make moral judgments—through natural psychological and sociological mechanisms, such as social conditioning, empathy, and the internalization of societal norms. We do not need to posit a spooky realm of objective values to explain human moral behavior. Thus, the argument from queerness is designed to show that moral realism is not merely surprising, but theoretically bankrupt.

#### II. The Unwarranted Standard of Plausibility and the Prejudice of Naturalism

The central weakness of Mackie’s argument lies in its implicit reliance on a specific, contestable metaphysical framework: reductive naturalism or physicalism. Mackie’s standard of “strangeness” is not a neutral, ahistorical criterion of existence; it is a measure of how well a proposed entity fits within the ontology of the natural sciences. He assumes, without adequate justification, that the world is fundamentally composed of the entities and properties described by physics, chemistry, and biology, and that any putative entity that cannot be reduced to or integrated with these domains is automatically suspect. This is the “scientism” that underwrites the argument. But why should we accept this as the ultimate arbiter of reality? The history of philosophy is replete with arguments that the scientific image of the world is incomplete—that it fails to capture consciousness, intentionality, abstract objects, and modal truths (necessity and possibility). To dismiss moral properties because they do not fit neatly into a scientific ontology is to beg the question against any non-naturalist metaphysics from the outset.

The prompt asks whether dismissing properties simply on the grounds of strangeness rests on an unwarranted standard of plausibility. It does. The standard is unwarranted because “strangeness” is not an intrinsic property of an entity; it is a relational property between that entity and our current background beliefs. To a medieval peasant, the concept of a magnetic field or the curvature of spacetime would be utterly bizarre, a metaphysical absurdity. To a quantum physicist, the non-locality of entangled particles is profoundly strange, challenging our intuitions about causality and locality. Yet, we accept these phenomena because they are forced upon us by the weight of empirical evidence and their immense explanatory power. The history of science is a graveyard of “strange” ideas that turned out to be true. If Mackie’s presumption against queerness had been applied rigorously in the past, it would have precluded the acceptance of heliocentrism, evolution, and quantum mechanics, all of which were initially considered wildly counter-intuitive.

Mackie could respond that scientific entities, however strange, are ultimately *causal* and *empirically verifiable*, whereas moral properties are neither. This distinction is what separates the acceptable strangeness of science from the unacceptable strangeness of morality. However, this response only works if one already accepts a causal theory of knowledge and a causal criterion of reality. Consider abstract objects, such as numbers, sets, or logical laws. These entities are not causally efficacious; they do not exist in space or time, and we do not come to know them through empirical observation. Yet, they are indispensable to scientific practice. Mathematics is the language of physics. The law of non-contradiction is a precondition for any coherent thought or empirical inquiry. If Mackie’s criterion of queerness is applied consistently, then numbers and logical laws are just as queer as moral properties. They are non-natural, non-causal, and epistemically accessed through a non-empirical faculty of rational intuition. Mackie, to his credit, was aware of this and was willing to bite the bullet, adopting a form of nominalism and error theory about abstract objects as well. But this is a deeply radical and implausible position. It leads to a kind of global scepticism that undermines the very scientific realism Mackie wants to privilege. If we cannot trust our rational intuition to deliver truths about mathematics, we cannot trust our rational intuition to deliver truths about logic, and without logic, science itself collapses.

This reveals that Mackie’s standard of plausibility is not just unwarranted; it is self-defeating. By insisting that all genuine knowledge must be causal and empirical, he forces us to either reject the foundations of science or accept a broader epistemology that includes a priori rational intuition. If we accept rational intuition as a legitimate source of knowledge for mathematics and logic—as virtually all philosophers and scientists do—then the epistemic queerness of moral properties loses its force. The moral realist can simply argue that moral knowledge is a species of a priori knowledge, akin to mathematical knowledge. The faculty of moral intuition is no more mysterious than the faculty of mathematical intuition. Both involve the direct apprehension of non-natural, necessary truths. The burden of proof shifts to Mackie to explain why rational intuition is reliable in the mathematical domain but completely unreliable in the moral domain. He cannot simply assert that moral properties are queer without establishing a principled distinction between the two domains.

#### III. The Parallel with Science: Explanatory Power and Indispensability

The prompt specifically asks to analyze the parallel between the alleged strangeness of moral properties and scientifically accepted phenomena. This is the crux of the “companions in guilt” strategy. Mackie argues that science is the paradigm of objective knowledge, and its entities, however strange, are justified by their explanatory power. Quarks, black holes, and electromagnetic fields are accepted because they are indispensable to our best theories about the physical world. They explain a vast array of observable phenomena. The moral realist can adopt a similar strategy. They can argue that objective moral properties are indispensable to our best explanations of human experience and practical reasoning.

Consider the phenomenon of moral experience. We experience moral obligations as objective demands. When we deliberate about whether to tell a lie or keep a promise, we do not merely weigh our subjective preferences; we feel that there is a fact of the matter about what we *ought* to do, a fact that is independent of our desires. The moral realist can argue that the best explanation for this pervasive and robust feature of human experience is that there are indeed objective moral truths that we are apprehending. If there are no objective moral facts, as Mackie’s error theory claims, then our entire moral experience is a massive, systematic illusion. We are all systematically mistaken about a fundamental aspect of our own conscious lives. While illusions are possible, the burden of proof is on the error theorist to explain why this particular illusion is so universal, so deeply entrenched, and so resistant to correction. Mackie offers a projectionist account—we project our own attitudes and desires onto the world and mistake them for objective properties—but this account is notoriously difficult to flesh out in a way that explains the *specific* content and *normative force* of our moral judgments.

Furthermore, the moral realist can point to the indispensability of normative truths for practical reasoning itself. As Derek Parfit and David Enoch have argued, we cannot engage in any kind of rational deliberation without presupposing normative facts. When we decide what to believe, we assume that we have a reason to believe what is true. When we decide what to do, we assume that we have a reason to pursue what is good. These are not empirical claims about the world; they are normative claims about what we have reason to do. Even the scientist relies on normative principles, such as the principle that we ought to accept the theory that best explains the evidence. If we reject all objective normative facts, we cannot justify our own epistemic practices. We would be forced into a radical form of scepticism about rationality itself. This is the “normative indispensability” argument. Just as we are committed to the existence of mathematical entities because they are indispensable to our best scientific theories, we are committed to the existence of normative facts because they are indispensable to our best theories of rational agency.

Mackie’s response to this is to reiterate the queerness of these normative facts. He might grant that we presuppose them but argue that the presupposition is mistaken. However, this response is weak. The fact that a presupposition is necessary for a practice as fundamental as rational deliberation provides a powerful prima facie case for its truth. If the choice is between accepting a “queer” normative fact and abandoning the coherence of all rational thought, the rational choice is to accept the normative fact. The queerness is a price we are willing to pay because the alternative is intellectual suicide. This is precisely how we treat scientific entities. We accept the strangeness of quantum mechanics because rejecting it would mean rejecting the most successful explanatory framework we have. The moral realist is simply asking for the same intellectual charity.

#### IV. The Failure of Naturalistic Reduction and the Resilience of Non-Naturalism

While the argument from queerness fails to refute moral realism *simpliciter*, it does succeed in refuting a specific version of it: reductive naturalism. Some moral realists, known as naturalists, attempt to avoid the charge of queerness by identifying moral properties with natural properties. For example, they might argue that “goodness” is identical to “pleasure,” or that “rightness” is identical to “what maximizes well-being.” If moral properties are natural properties, they are not queer; they can be studied empirically, and we can know them through ordinary scientific means. This strategy, championed by philosophers like Richard Boyd and Peter Railton, seeks to naturalize morality.

However, as Mackie correctly intuits, this reduction fails to capture the essential feature of morality: its normative, prescriptive force. If “goodness” is simply identical to “pleasure,” then the statement “pleasure is good” becomes a tautology, equivalent to “pleasure is pleasure.” It loses its ability to guide action. The whole point of a moral judgment is that it tells us what we *ought* to do, regardless of our current desires. But if goodness is just a natural property, then the fact that an action is good gives us no more reason to perform it than the fact that it is yellow gives us a reason to look at it. The naturalist faces the infamous “open question argument” originally formulated by G.E. Moore. We can always meaningfully ask, “I know this action maximizes pleasure, but is it *good*?” If goodness were identical to pleasure, this question would be nonsensical, just as it is nonsensical to ask, “I know this is a bachelor, but is it unmarried?” The fact that the question remains open reveals that goodness is not identical to any natural property. The naturalist has either changed the subject, defining a new concept that is not recognizably moral, or they have failed to account for the normative “oomph” that makes morality what it is.

Mackie’s argument thus forces the moral realist into a corner. They cannot be naturalists without losing normativity, and they cannot be non-naturalists without embracing queerness. The non-naturalist, however, can and should embrace the queerness. Philosophers like Parfit and Enoch argue that the “queerness” of moral properties is not an objection to them but a fundamental feature of reality. The normative domain is sui generis; it cannot be reduced to the natural domain, and it is not accessible through empirical observation. Just as the physical world has its own fundamental laws and properties, the normative world has its own fundamental truths. The fact that these truths are “strange” from a scientific perspective is irrelevant. Why should we assume that reality is limited to what science can discover? Science is a method for investigating the natural world, but it is not a comprehensive metaphysics. There may be truths about reasons, values, and obligations that are simply beyond its scope.

Enoch, in his book *Taking Morality Seriously*, defends a robust version of non-naturalism against Mackie’s challenge. He argues that the argument from queerness is question-begging because it presupposes a naturalistic ontology. He offers a “third-factor” response to the epistemic objection. How do we know objective moral truths? Enoch suggests that we can have reliable moral knowledge if there is a correlation between our moral beliefs and the objective moral truths, and this correlation can be explained by a third factor—for example, the fact that we are rational agents who are responsive to reasons. We do not need a direct causal connection between moral facts and our beliefs; we only need a reliable co-variation, which can be explained by our rational nature. This account is no more mysterious than the reliability of our mathematical beliefs. We believe that 2+2=4 because we are rational beings who can grasp necessary truths. Similarly, we believe that cruelty is wrong because we are rational beings who can grasp categorical imperatives.

#### V. Resolving the Presumption: Does Queerness Eliminate Objective Moral Facts?

The final question is whether the presumption against queerness successfully eliminates the possibility of objective moral facts. Based on the preceding analysis, the answer is a definitive no. The argument from queerness is not a sound basis for rejecting moral realism because it rests on an unwarranted standard of plausibility. Mackie’s presumption is not a neutral epistemic principle; it is a substantive metaphysical commitment to a reductive naturalism. This commitment is neither self-evident nor universally accepted. It is a contested philosophical position, and to use it as the standard by which to judge the existence of moral properties is to beg the question against the non-naturalist.

The argument does, however, perform a valuable dialectical function. It exposes the high metaphysical and epistemic costs of accepting moral realism. It forces the realist to choose between a deflationary naturalism that loses the normative character of morality and a robust non-naturalism that posits a sui generis realm of normative facts. The naturalist option is a failure, as it fails to capture what we mean by “good” and “right.” The non-naturalist option is expensive, requiring us to expand our ontology beyond the physical and to accept a faculty of rational intuition that is not empirically verifiable. But expense is not impossibility. The history of philosophy and science is a history of paying high metaphysical costs for theories that are explanatorily powerful and practically indispensable. The moral realist can argue that objective moral facts are precisely such a theory. They are necessary to explain our moral experience, to justify our practical reasoning, and to make sense of our lives as rational agents.

Ultimately, the debate between Mackie and the moral realist is not a debate about queerness; it is a debate about the ultimate nature of reality. Mackie’s error theory is a coherent and internally consistent worldview, but it is a deeply pessimistic one, suggesting that our deepest moral convictions are all false. The moral realist’s non-naturalism is also coherent, but it is a more optimistic worldview, suggesting that our moral convictions can be true. The argument from queerness does not settle this debate. It simply highlights the fact that these two worldviews are fundamentally incompatible. The choice between them is not a choice between the plausible and the queer; it is a choice between two different metaphysical visions. Mackie’s attempt to privilege the scientific vision is an argumentative move, not a proof. It relies on a standard of plausibility that is itself a philosophical thesis, and theses can be rejected. Therefore, the presumption against queerness fails to eliminate the possibility of objective moral facts. It merely reminds us that if they exist, they are not like anything else in the universe—which, for the non-naturalist, is precisely the point.
