# Evolutionary Explanation and the Transformation of Emotion

## Introduction

Few claims in contemporary philosophy of mind and human nature are more provocative than the claim that evolutionary psychology explains emotions by showing what they are *for*. Anger becomes a system for deterring exploitation or correcting violations of reciprocal exchange. Jealousy becomes a mechanism for guarding paternity certainty or preventing the diversion of a mate’s reproductive investment. Love becomes an attachment strategy, a device for sustaining parental investment, pair-bonding, or cooperation. Fear becomes an alarm system designed to avoid fitness-reducing threats. In each case, the emotion is interpreted as an adaptation, or as the output of an information-processing system whose ultimate function was shaped by natural selection.

The attraction of this approach is evident. Human beings are biological organisms, and their emotional capacities have undoubtedly been formed through a long history of natural selection, development, learning, and social interaction. Evolutionary explanations can illuminate regularities in emotional response that otherwise appear arbitrary. They can explain why certain situations reliably provoke anger, fear, attachment, or disgust, and why these responses have characteristic physiological, cognitive, and behavioral profiles.

Yet the explanatory success of an ultra-Darwinian approach may seem to generate a philosophical problem. If anger is translated into the language of adaptive regulation, or love into the language of reproductive investment and kin-selected cooperation, has the original phenomenon survived the translation? The ordinary concepts of anger and love do not merely designate biological states. They involve what a situation means to a subject, what is felt as offensive, threatening, admirable, intimate, or worthy of devotion, and what normative significance the subject takes herself to have. A person who says “I am angry” does not ordinarily mean “a defensive motivational module has been activated in response to a fitness-relevant violation.” Nor does a declaration of love ordinarily function as a report about a mechanism selected for increasing reproductive success.

This tension can be formulated through a contrast between two conceptions of language. The first is a designative-instrumentalist conception, often operative in sociobiological and ultra-Darwinian explanation. On this view, language is primarily an instrument for naming, classifying, and communicating pre-existing states of affairs. Emotional vocabulary is a useful but theoretically dispensable coding system. The scientist can replace ordinary terms with more precise functional descriptions, and in doing so can reveal the real mechanisms behind folk categories. The second is Charles Taylor’s expressivist conception. For Taylor, human feelings are not simply inner objects waiting to be labeled. Human beings articulate what they feel through language, and the articulation does not merely report an already completed mental state. It helps constitute the kind of state it is. Emotions involve subject-referring import-ascriptions: anger is not just bodily arousal caused by a stimulus, but the apprehension of something as an insult, injustice, betrayal, or violation directed at me or at someone with whom I identify. Such emotional meaning is inseparable from a linguistically and culturally articulated form of selfhood.

The central question, then, is whether neo-Darwinian explanation merely explains emotional life from another level, or whether it transforms and perhaps annihilates the very objects it claims to explain. The answer requires avoiding two symmetrical errors. One error is reductionism: the assumption that because an emotion has an evolutionary function, its intentional, normative, and expressive meaning is unreal or theoretically irrelevant. The other is anti-naturalism: the assumption that because emotions are linguistically and culturally articulated, their biological history cannot illuminate them. The most defensible position is dialectical. Evolutionary explanation can explain the conditions, dispositions, and recurring architectures that make human emotional life possible. But when it replaces the subject’s meaningful emotional world rather than situating it within a broader account, it ceases to explain emotion and begins to describe a different object. The original phenomenon is not destroyed by every naturalistic explanation; it is destroyed only by a form of explanation that treats the explanatory model as ontologically exhaustive.

## I. Ultra-Darwinism and the Functional Re-description of Emotion

Evolutionary psychology generally distinguishes between proximate and ultimate explanations. Proximate explanations concern the immediate mechanisms producing a behavior or experience: neural processes, hormonal changes, perceptual appraisals, developmental learning, and social cues. Ultimate explanations concern why organisms possess those mechanisms in the first place, typically by appealing to natural selection. Ultra-Darwinism privileges the latter and often presents human psychological capacities as collections of specialized adaptations designed to solve recurrent problems in ancestral environments.

The language of design is crucial. A psychological mechanism is said to have a function because its structure was historically selected for producing certain effects. Anger, on this account, is not fundamentally a mysterious passion but an evolved bargaining device. It increases the likelihood that others will recognize the agent’s unwillingness to accept a loss, violation, or unequal distribution. It may mobilize aggression, focus attention on the offender, and sustain costly threats. Love may be analyzed as a suite of mechanisms coordinating attachment, sexual choice, parental investment, and reciprocal cooperation. These explanations do not necessarily deny that people experience anger or love. They claim, however, that the scientifically significant truth about those experiences lies in the causal architecture and evolutionary function beneath their subjective descriptions.

The designative-instrumentalist view of language supports this movement. If words are labels for pre-linguistic mental or biological states, then emotional language can be progressively refined by science. “Anger” is a folk term with vague boundaries; evolutionary psychology promises a more exact vocabulary of threat detection, social valuation, bargaining leverage, kin investment, mate retention, or norm enforcement. The ordinary emotional concept is treated as a rough pointer to a mechanism. Scientific translation thus appears to improve upon common sense by replacing expressive and morally laden terms with neutral functional ones.

There is an important truth in this picture. Human beings often misunderstand their own emotions. Anger may be experienced as righteous indignation while also serving self-protective or status-enhancing ends. Love may be experienced as pure concern for another while being entangled with attachment needs, sexual competition, or reproductive strategies. Evolutionary theory can reveal causal influences of which agents are unaware. It can also challenge the assumption that the conscious reasons people give for their emotions are complete explanations of those emotions.

Moreover, the fact that a phenomenon has meaning for a subject does not make it causally inert. A person’s interpretation of an insult may alter her physiology, guide her attention, and produce action. The biological mechanism is not an alternative to meaning; in human beings it is often organized by meaning. Anger at betrayal is not less embodied because it is about betrayal. Love is not less neurobiological because it is directed toward a particular person under a description such as “my child,” “my partner,” or “the one whose good matters to me.”

The problem begins when the functional vocabulary is treated not as a partial explanatory language but as the only legitimate one. A person’s anger is then redescribed exclusively as an adaptation for bargaining or deterrence. The fact that the person experiences another’s action as insulting or unjust is dismissed as a culturally decorative interpretation imposed on an underlying mechanism. The danger is not simply that something is omitted. The danger is that the object itself changes. “Anger” in the ordinary sense is a meaningful, subject-involving, normatively structured phenomenon. “A mechanism for regulating social exchange” is a functional-biological phenomenon. The two may be causally related, but they are not identical descriptions of the same thing unless a bridge between them is supplied.

## II. Taylor’s Expressivism and the Constitution of Emotional Meaning

Charles Taylor’s expressivist framework challenges the image of language as a transparent labeling system. Expressivism, in its broadest sense, holds that human beings do not first possess fully determinate inner states and then use language merely to announce them. Rather, language articulates a space of significance within which certain experiences become possible as the kinds of experiences they are. To express a feeling is not simply to point to an internal object. It is to give shape to an experience, to locate oneself in relation to what matters, and to disclose a world of significance.

This is especially important for emotion. An emotion is not exhausted by a physiological condition, a behavioral tendency, or a causal response to a stimulus. It has intentional and evaluative content. Fear is fear *of* something as threatening. Shame is shame *before* someone, under a description of oneself as exposed or unworthy. Grief is grief over a loss that matters. Anger is anger at an offense, an injustice, or a violation. Love is love of someone as precious, irreplaceable, or worthy of care.

Taylor emphasizes that emotions involve what can be called subject-referring import-ascriptions. An emotional experience does not merely represent an object as possessing a neutral property. It takes the object as mattering in relation to a subject or community. An insult is not merely a sequence of sounds or a social action; it is experienced as an affront *to me*. Betrayal is not merely a departure from a behavioral expectation; it is a violation of a relationship in which I understand myself as standing. Love does not merely register another person’s traits; it makes that person salient as someone whose good is bound up with my own practical identity.

The “subject-referring” dimension is essential. Human emotions often concern not just what happened but what happened to me, to us, or to someone who belongs within the circle of my concern. The anger of a person whose colleague is mistreated may be grounded in solidarity. The grief of a parent involves not only the absence of another organism but the rupture of a shared history and an identity constituted through care. The love of a friend is not reducible to a probability of reciprocal assistance, even if friendship may historically support cooperation. The emotional object is understood through a first-personal and relational framework.

Language plays a constitutive role because the significance of these relations must be articulated. A person learns to distinguish resentment from indignation, jealousy from envy, grief from disappointment, affection from love, shame from embarrassment. These distinctions are not merely labels attached to pre-existing physiological kinds. They provide forms in which experience can be organized and recognized. They enable a person to say not only “something unpleasant happened” but “I was betrayed,” “I feel guilty,” “I resent being treated as inferior,” or “I love her even though the relationship is not advantageous.” Each articulation locates the self in a normative space.

This does not entail that infants or nonhuman animals have no emotions. Nor does it imply that linguistic articulation creates all affect from nothing. Animals can fear, attach, compete, mourn, and perhaps display forms of anger. Human infants show affective responses before mastering language. Taylor’s claim is better understood as concerning the specifically human form of emotional self-understanding and the richer kinds of emotion that depend upon conceptual articulation. Language does not create biological arousal, but it can constitute the emotion as a socially and normatively intelligible experience. A preverbal infant may recoil from danger; an adult may experience fear as cowardice, responsibility, existential vulnerability, or a challenge to courage. These are not merely different labels for the same state.

Expressivism therefore resists the idea that emotion is an inner object whose nature is fixed independently of its expression. The expression of emotion can alter the emotion itself. Naming resentment may differentiate it from anger. Describing a loss as unjust may intensify indignation. Reinterpreting an event as a misunderstanding may transform anger into embarrassment or forgiveness. Emotional life is plastic because it is articulated within language, practices, narratives, and forms of self-interpretation.

## III. Does Gene Language Change the Feelings It Explains?

The question of whether neo-Darwinian translation changes the qualitative nature of emotion must be approached carefully. There are at least three different senses in which it might do so.

First, the translation may alter the *subject’s understanding* of the emotion. Learning that jealousy may have evolutionary functions can change how a person relates to it. She may see it as less authoritative, less morally pure, or less uniquely personal. She may become able to distance herself from the feeling: “This is an evolved mate-retention response, not proof that my partner has betrayed me.” Such reinterpretation can genuinely change the emotional experience. The feeling may weaken, become shameful, become intellectually suspect, or be redirected into a more reflective form. In this sense, theory is not external to emotion. Human beings are self-interpreting creatures, and scientific descriptions can enter the practical identity of those whom they describe.

Second, gene language may alter the *social meaning* of an emotion. If anger is widely described as an adaptive strategy for extracting concessions, displays of anger may come to be treated as manipulation rather than as protest against injustice. If love is described as a reproductive strategy, expressions of devotion may be heard as disguised self-interest. A vocabulary that foregrounds fitness can reshape institutions, expectations, and interpersonal judgments. It may encourage cynicism: every act of care is interpreted as investment, every sacrifice as signaling, every moral commitment as disguised advantage.

Third, the translation may alter the *theoretical object* without necessarily changing the lived emotion. A biologist can describe an emotion in terms of gene frequencies, selection pressures, neural circuits, and behavioral outputs while the subject continues to feel anger or love in the ordinary sense. The scientific account need not enter consciousness at all. In that case, the qualitative character of the feeling is not directly transformed; rather, the researcher has selected a different level of description.

The key philosophical distinction is between changing an emotion and redescribing it. A causal account of the conditions under which anger arises does not automatically change the anger. If I learn that visual perception depends on neural processing, I do not thereby cease to see tables and trees as tables and trees. Likewise, learning that attachment has an evolutionary history does not by itself make my attachment unreal. Gene language can be explanatory without being phenomenologically constitutive.

But the analogy has limits. Perception is often treated as revealing objects independently existing in the world, whereas emotion involves a relation between subject, object, and normative significance. The evolutionary account of vision can be combined with the ordinary perception of a tree without displacing the tree as perceived. An account of anger as a bargaining mechanism may be more disruptive because it competes directly with anger’s own self-understanding. Anger presents itself as a response to something wrong, not merely as an instrument for improving one’s bargaining position. To translate it entirely into strategic terms is to deny the internal sense under which the emotion is experienced.

Consider anger. Suppose an employee becomes angry when a supervisor publicly takes credit for her work. An ultra-Darwinian account might explain the anger as a mechanism that protects status, deters exploitation, and increases the probability of future resource access. This could be true at the level of evolutionary function. Yet it does not determine whether the supervisor’s act was unjust, whether the employee’s anger is proportionate, or whether she should confront, forgive, resign, or organize with colleagues. More importantly, the anger is not simply a bodily state caused by the prospect of lost status. It is an apprehension of being wronged. If the employee discovers that the supervisor’s action was accidental, her anger may dissolve into embarrassment. If she learns that the supervisor deliberately exploited a subordinate, her anger may intensify into moral indignation. The emotion changes as the meaning of the situation changes.

Now consider love. A reproductive account can explain why humans evolved attachment, sexual desire, parental care, and mechanisms for maintaining long-term bonds. It may illuminate why love is often associated with exclusivity, jealousy, sacrifice, and concern for another’s future. But the person in love does not ordinarily experience the beloved as a vehicle for reproductive success. She may love someone who cannot have children, someone outside her reproductive age, or someone whose interests conflict with her own. She may understand her love as a commitment to the other’s good even at considerable cost. The evolutionary account may explain the possibility or distribution of such attachment, but it does not capture the beloved as beloved.

Indeed, gene language can distort the emotion when it imports a suspicious interpretation into contexts where the subject’s reasons matter. To say that a parent’s care has evolutionary roots does not show that the parent’s concern is secretly selfish. A cause of a disposition is not identical with the content of the disposition. The fact that love may have been selected partly because it promoted offspring survival does not entail that loving one’s child means valuing the child merely as a vehicle of genetic continuation. Once love exists as a human practice, it can be extended, criticized, transformed, and detached from the original evolutionary conditions that helped produce its capacities.

## IV. The Problem of Reduction and the Multiplicity of Explanatory Levels

The apparent conflict between evolutionary psychology and expressivism becomes less severe if we distinguish levels of explanation. A human emotion can be described in at least four ways.

At the biological level, one can describe neural, endocrine, and physiological processes. At the functional level, one can ask what recurrent problem the capacity may have solved in ancestral environments. At the psychological level, one can describe appraisal, motivation, attention, memory, and action tendencies. At the personal and cultural level, one can describe what the emotion means to the subject, how it is articulated, and how it is embedded in norms and practices.

These levels are not necessarily rivals. A particular emotion may be simultaneously a bodily mobilization, an evolved disposition, an appraisal of significance, an action-oriented concern, and an articulated stance toward another person. The existence of one description does not eliminate the others. A complete account of human emotion would need to show how these levels are related rather than choosing one vocabulary as metaphysically final.

The difficulty is that ultra-Darwinian explanations often slide from a legitimate claim about function to an illegitimate claim about identity. From “anger has been shaped by selection to perform certain regulatory tasks,” the theorist moves to “anger is nothing but a regulatory mechanism.” From “love has effects that historically enhanced reproductive success,” the theorist concludes “love is a reproductive strategy.” But the first claim concerns the historical etiology of a capacity, while the second concerns the nature of the lived phenomenon. The inference is not valid without an argument showing that normative and expressive dimensions are reducible to biological function.

This is a version of the etiological fallacy: confusing the origin of a capacity with the meaning or present constitution of its exercise. The origin of language may lie in evolutionary pressures, but that does not mean that a poem is nothing but a fitness-enhancing signal. The origin of moral norms may involve coordination and group survival, but that does not settle whether a particular action is just. Likewise, the evolutionary origin of love does not exhaust what love is in a human life.

One might object that the expressive and normative dimensions are themselves biological products. Perhaps language, subjectivity, and value are all adaptations, and therefore the ultra-Darwinian framework remains ultimate. But an evolutionary genealogy of a capacity does not replace the standards internal to its use. Mathematics may be enabled by evolved brains, yet mathematical proofs are not explained by listing the reproductive advantages of numeracy. A legal judgment may occur in an evolved organism, yet its legal validity depends on institutional and normative structures not captured by describing neural mechanisms. The same point applies to emotion. Biological conditions make emotional articulation possible, but the articulated emotion has criteria of appropriateness, intelligibility, and sincerity that belong to a different explanatory order.

This does not grant emotional self-understanding infallibility. A person can be wrong about the object of anger, the grounds of love, or the significance of fear. Expressivism should not be confused with the claim that whatever someone feels is justified or true. Emotions are interpretive, and interpretations can be distorted by prejudice, trauma, ideology, or self-deception. Evolutionary psychology can help identify systematic distortions and recurrent biases. It can reveal why jealousy is easily triggered, why status threats command disproportionate attention, or why disgust becomes attached to culturally variable objects. Such knowledge can be emancipatory.

The proper conclusion is not that biological explanation is irrelevant, but that it must be integrated with an account of meaning. A theory of anger should explain both why human beings possess anger-like capacities and how particular situations become experienced as insults, injustices, or threats to identity. A theory of love should explain both the attachment system and the practices through which persons come to understand another as uniquely significant. Evolutionary psychology can contribute to the first task, but it cannot complete the second by simply replacing the second with the first.

## V. Does Expressivism Entail That Language Is Necessary for Emotion?

The claim that emotions require language for their constitution needs qualification. If “emotion” means any affective response or motivational state, then it is implausible that language is necessary. Many animals display fear, aggression, attachment, grief-like behavior, and perhaps forms of empathy. Human infants experience affect before acquiring a public vocabulary. An organism can be aroused, oriented toward an object, and motivated to approach or avoid without possessing linguistic concepts.

Taylor’s framework is strongest when applied to the distinctively human form of emotion: an emotion as a self-interpreted, normatively situated, and linguistically articulable way of being affected. Language may not be necessary for the raw capacity to respond, but it is necessary for many emotions as humans experience and understand them. Shame, guilt, resentment, indignation, romantic devotion, and solidarity depend upon social distinctions and forms of selfhood that are sustained through language. Even where an affective precursor exists in animals, the human emotion may be transformed by narrative and conceptual articulation.

This distinction also clarifies the relationship between biological and expressive accounts. Biological mechanisms can be necessary conditions for emotional experience without being sufficient conditions for its human meaning. A person must have a nervous system capable of arousal, attention, memory, and motivation. But those capacities become organized into anger, love, grief, or shame through participation in a world of language and shared practices. The body provides a repertoire of possibilities; culture and articulation specify, refine, and sometimes radically reorganize them.

There is also a difference between public language and internalized language. One need not be currently speaking or even consciously formulating a sentence in order to feel an emotion. An emotion may be pre-reflective and bodily. Yet the emotion can still be shaped by inherited categories, remembered narratives, and implicit distinctions learned through linguistic interaction. A person’s shame may be activated before she formulates the thought “I have violated a norm,” but the form of the shame depends upon a normative world in which such a violation is intelligible.

Thus the expressivist claim should not be interpreted as linguistic idealism. It does not say that physical reality waits for words to exist. It says that the human significance of affect is achieved through articulation. The evolutionary psychologist is therefore right to insist that emotional capacities are embodied and historically shaped. The expressivist is right to insist that the human emotions we care about are not fully specified by those capacities.

## VI. The Dialectical Problem: If Explanation Annihilates Its Object

We can now formulate the dialectical problem more precisely. Suppose a theory explains an apparent phenomenon by reducing it to mechanisms that lack the defining features of the phenomenon. If anger is nothing but a fitness-maximizing regulatory device, then the anger of a person who experiences herself as wronged has disappeared. If love is nothing but a strategy for securing reproductive investment, then the object of love as a person of unique value has disappeared. The theory appears to succeed by explaining away what it set out to explain.

There is a genuine paradox here. Explanations usually presuppose the reality of their objects. A meteorological account explains storms; it does not redefine storms as something other than weather events. But some theories are transformative. Psychoanalysis, Marxism, neuroscience, and evolutionary psychology can alter the self-understanding of the phenomena they address. Once people adopt a theory, their practices and experiences may change. Human beings are not passive objects like rocks; they are self-interpreting agents who can respond to descriptions of themselves.

This means that an explanatory framework can be performatively destabilizing. If everyone comes to regard love as strategic manipulation, the norms of trust and devotion may erode, thereby changing the phenomenon historically sustained by those norms. If anger is always interpreted as a dominance display, legitimate protest may be delegitimized. The theory can become part of the social world it describes and thereby help transform its object.

Yet the possibility of transformation does not imply annihilation. First, a theory can be reductive in its conceptual vocabulary without eliminating the causes and practices that sustain the phenomenon. People may continue to love and become angry even if they are told that these states have evolutionary functions. Second, the functional explanation itself often depends upon the phenomenon it replaces. To say that anger deters exploitation presupposes that agents can recognize exploitation, communicate demands, care about social standing, and respond to perceived violations. The social function of anger is not an explanation that bypasses meaning; it is a function performed through meaningful responses.

Third, an evolutionary explanation cannot easily make sense of maladaptive, excessive, or normatively self-defeating emotions if it treats them exclusively as optimally designed strategies. Human emotional life includes unrequited love, principled sacrifice, mourning for strangers, anger at historical injustice, and commitments to future generations. These may be shaped by capacities whose evolutionary histories are intelligible, but their specific forms often exceed ancestral fitness functions. Cultural evolution, imitation, institutions, symbolic thought, and reflective criticism enable emotional practices to be redirected toward ends not represented in the original selection environment.

The solution to the dialectical problem is therefore a distinction between *explanatory reduction* and *explanatory integration*. A reductionist framework annihilates its object when it denies the reality of the features by which the object is identified. An integrative framework explains how those features are embodied, enabled, and historically conditioned without treating them as fictitious. The question is not whether biology can explain emotion, but whether it can explain an emotion while preserving its intentional, expressive, and normative identity.

## VII. Gene Talk, Agency, and the Ethics of Emotional Interpretation

The issue has ethical and political consequences. Ultra-Darwinian descriptions can appear to naturalize existing patterns of behavior. If male jealousy is described as an evolved mechanism for guarding paternity and female choice as an adaptation for securing resources, social arrangements may be presented as inevitable. The language of genes can turn contingent norms into natural necessities. What is statistically common or historically selected may be treated as what ought to be accepted.

This is another version of the distinction between explanation and justification. An evolutionary account of aggression does not justify violence. An explanation of jealousy does not excuse control. An account of parental attachment does not determine what good parenting requires. In some cases, biological knowledge strengthens moral agency by showing that an impulse is not a command. To understand anger as a predictable evolved response may help one decide whether to endorse or resist it. To understand jealousy as an inherited vulnerability may make reflective self-regulation more possible.

But this emancipatory use depends upon preserving the first-personal meaning of emotion. If the person is told that her feeling is merely a gene’s strategy, she may be deprived of the language needed to evaluate it. The point is not to deny that she has been shaped by natural history; it is to enable her to ask whether the object of her anger is genuinely unjust, whether her love respects the other’s independence, and whether her fear accurately discloses danger. A purely functional vocabulary cannot answer these questions because it does not contain the normative predicates required to formulate them.

The same applies to social criticism. Anger at racism, exploitation, or humiliation may have biological preconditions, but its political significance depends upon an articulation of injustice. To say that protest anger serves coalition-building may be descriptively useful, but it does not capture why the protest is morally intelligible. Conversely, the fact that an emotion is morally intelligible does not prove that it is immune to biological influence. Human freedom is not freedom from causation; it is the capacity to inhabit, interpret, and transform the motives through which one acts.

## Conclusion

Ultra-Darwinian explanations of human emotion do not automatically undermine the phenomena they explain. They reveal that emotional capacities have biological histories, physiological bases, and functional regularities. Anger can indeed regulate social exchange; love can indeed support attachment, cooperation, and reproductive investment; fear can indeed protect organisms from danger. These truths matter, and any philosophy of emotion that ignores them is incomplete.

The problem arises when a functional explanation is elevated into an exhaustive ontology. The ordinary emotion is not merely a mechanism. It is a way in which a subject finds herself affected by something that matters. It has intentional content, normative significance, expressive form, and a relation to a personal identity. Taylor’s expressivism makes visible the role of language in constituting this human form of emotional life. Language does not simply label inner states; it articulates the distinctions through which a person experiences herself as betrayed, ashamed, devoted, resentful, courageous, or responsible. Emotional experience is therefore not a pre-linguistic biological object onto which culture later adds decoration.

Gene language can alter the qualitative nature of a feeling when it becomes part of the subject’s self-understanding or reshapes the social practices in which emotions are recognized. It can make anger seem like manipulation, love like disguised reproductive strategy, and moral commitment like genetic self-interest. But this transformation is not entailed by every evolutionary explanation. A biological account can remain a causal and functional account of the conditions under which meaningful emotions arise. It becomes destructive only when it denies that meaning is a real dimension of the phenomenon.

The dialectical problem is consequently resolved by refusing the choice between reduction and mystification. An explanatory framework annihilates its object if it explains away the intentional and expressive features that define it. It successfully accounts for human emotional life when it explains how embodied, evolved capacities become organized within language, social practices, and forms of self-interpretation. The task is not to decide whether anger is a mechanism or an experience of injustice, whether love is attachment or devotion, whether emotion is biological or linguistic. Human emotions are both, but not in the sense that one description can simply replace the other. They are biological capacities whose specifically human reality is articulated in a world of meanings.

Evolutionary psychology can tell us why creatures like us are capable of anger and love. Expressivism tells us what it is for anger and love to become ways of inhabiting a human world. Only together, and only if each respects the limits of its explanatory vocabulary, can they account for emotional life without either romanticizing it or explaining it out of existence.
