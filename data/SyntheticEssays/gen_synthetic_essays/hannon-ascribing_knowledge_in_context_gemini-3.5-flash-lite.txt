# The Elasticity of Epistemic Ascent: A Critical Evaluation of Contextualism in Response to Skepticism

## Introduction

The perennial clash between the Cartesian skeptic and the defender of ordinary empirical knowledge has long driven the engine of epistemological inquiry. The skeptic’s weapon of choice is an escalating dialectical maneuver: by introducing radical hypotheses—such as the Evil Demon, the Brain-in-a-Vat (BIV), or the modern digital simulation—the skeptic argues that our foundational everyday claims to know (e.g., that we have hands, that the sun will rise, that a physical world exists) lack the necessary warrant, because we cannot conclusively rule out these counter-possibilities. Traditional epistemological responses have oscillated between dogmatic foundationalism (which seeks to establish absolute certainty against the skeptic) and error theories (which concede that we do not strictly know, yet preserve our practices by other means). 

In the late twentieth century, epistemic contextualism emerged as a sophisticated diagnostic and therapeutic intervention. Championed in various forms by figures such as Stewart Cohen, Keith DeRose, and David Lewis, contextualism suggests that the verb "to know" (and its cognates) is indexical or context-sensitive, much like words such as "flat," "tall," or "here." According to this view, the truth-conditions of a knowledge ascription vary depending on the conversational context of the ascriber. In low-stakes, ordinary contexts, the epistemic standards are lax, allowing us to truthfully assert that we know we have hands. However, when the skeptic enters the dialectical arena, the mere consideration of a skeptical hypothesis shifts the conversational context to a high-stakes, high-standards environment. In this elevated context, the requirement for knowledge becomes so stringent—demanding the elimination of all skeptical scenarios—that our ordinary claims become false. Thus, contextualism purports to achieve a double victory: it preserves the validity of ordinary empirical knowledge in everyday life while accommodating the intuitive force and semantic plausibility of the skeptic’s conclusion.

This essay critically evaluates the contextualist solution to skepticism. I shall argue that while contextualism offers an attractive semantic diagnosis of the skeptical paradox, it ultimately founders upon severe internal and external difficulties. Specifically, I will examine the mechanics of context-sensitivity, address whether contextualism successfully reconciles ordinary knowledge with the skeptical threat, and evaluate three devastating objections: (1) the objection that the mere consideration of a skeptical hypothesis introduces an ad hoc, unusually restrictive standard that destabilizes ordinary knowledge rather than explaining it; (2) the failure of contextualism to provide a principled account of how context determines epistemic standards or how shifts in context are recognized and navigated; and (3) the criticism that contextualism begs the question against the skeptic by presupposing the very epistemic framework it seeks to defend.

## I. The Semantic and Epistemic Architecture of Contextualism

To understand the contextualist maneuver, one must first grasp the anatomy of the skeptical paradox it seeks to dissolve. Consider the following three propositions, which together form an inconsistent trilemma:

1. **(P1)** The ordinary person knows ordinary empirical propositions (e.g., that she has hands).
2. **(P2)** If a person knows an ordinary empirical proposition, she can know the negation of a skeptical hypothesis that is incompatible with it (e.g., that she is not a brain-in-a-vat).
3. **(P3)** A person cannot know the negation of a skeptical hypothesis.

Dogmatists reject (P3); skeptics reject (P1); invariantists of other stripes might deny (P2). Contextualism, however, targets the stability of the truth-conditions across these propositions. It posits that (P1) and (P3) can *both* be true because the word "knows" shifts its meaning (or, more precisely, its extension) between the contexts in which (P1) is asserted and the contexts in which (P3) is asserted.

In an ordinary conversational context ($C_{low}$), the standards for knowledge are modest. To say "S knows that $p$" is to require that S has justification sufficient for ordinary purposes, that $p$ is true, and that S believes $p$. In $C_{low}$, one need not rule out every logically possible, physically bizarre alternative to $p$; one need only rule out the contextually salient alternatives. Since brains-in-a-vat are not salient in a normal pub conversation or a physics lecture, they can be safely ignored. Thus, (P1) is true in $C_{low}$.

However, when the epistemologist or the Cartesian skeptic introduces the Brain-in-a-Vat hypothesis into the conversation, the conversational context shifts dramatically to $C_{high}$. In $C_{high}$, the standards for knowledge escalate. The set of relevant alternatives now expands to include the skeptical hypothesis. To know $p$ in $C_{high}$, one must possess evidence that actively eliminates the BIV hypothesis. Because empirical evidence is typically neutral between being an embodied human and being a envatted brain receiving identical sensory inputs, the subject fails to meet this hyper-stringent standard. Consequently, in $C_{high}$, the assertion "S knows that $p$" is false, and (P3) is established as true.

This semantic move relies on an analogy with paradigm context-sensitive expressions. Consider the word "flat." If I am grading the surface of a microscopic silicon chip, I might say, "This surface is not flat," because it contains microscopic bumps. Yet, if I am driving across the plains of Kansas, I might say, "This road is flat," even though it features gentle undulations and the curvature of the earth. The invariantist who insists that "flat" has a single, absolute semantic value—meaning strictly $100\%$ Euclidean flatness—would be forced to conclude that no road, table, or planet is flat, thereby committing an error theory about ordinary language. Contextualism applies this exact anti-invariantist insight to epistemology. Epistemic terms are radically indexical; hence, the skeptic and the common-person are both right *within their respective contexts*, and the appearance of a paradox is merely an illusion generated by equivocating on "knows" across conversational boundaries.

## II. The Reconciling Promise: Ordinary Knowledge vs. The Skeptic

Does this semantic architecture successfully reconcile ordinary empirical knowledge with the skeptical hypothesis? At first glance, contextualism delivers a profound psychological and linguistic peace. It validates the pre-theoretic conviction of the common person—who feels certain she has hands and knows she is sitting in a chair—while simultaneously granting the philosopher the intellectual integrity to acknowledge that, against the backdrop of Cartesian doubt, our ordinary justification falls short of absolute, infallible certainty.

Yet, this reconciliation rests on a fragile fault line. The contextualist reconciliation is bought at the price of what one might call "epistemic deflation." By rendering knowledge ascriptions relative to conversational contexts, contextualism strips knowledge of its trans-contextual robustness. If the truth of "S knows that $p$" can be wiped out simply by someone *mentioning* a skeptical hypothesis in the room, then knowledge becomes an astonishingly delicate, conversational artifact rather than a robust, objective relation between a cognizer and a mind-independent world.

Consider the implications for inquiry and action. We typically treat knowledge as a guide to action and a commodity that can be accumulated, shared, and transmitted across conversational divides. If John is in $C_{low}$ and truly knows he has hands, and Mary is in $C_{high}$ (because she has just been reading Descartes) and truthfully asserts that John does *not* know he has hands, we are confronted with a puzzling semantic friction. Contextualists handle this via semantic ascent: John's utterance is true relative to his context, and Mary's utterance is true relative to hers. There is no real contradiction, because the semantic content (the proposition expressed) has shifted.

However, this semantic peace fails to satisfy the deeper epistemological intuition that motivates our engagement with skepticism. The skeptic does not merely point out a quirk in our linguistic conventions; the skeptic challenges our *epistemic condition*. The skeptical anxiety is ontological and normative: *Are we radically deceived? Do we possess any genuine purchase on reality?* To respond that "knows" is indexical is akin to responding to Zeno’s paradox of motion by noting that the word "fast" is context-sensitive. While linguistic disambiguation may clear up conceptual muddles, it leaves untouched the underlying ontological worry. If our epistemic standing is so fragile that the mere voicing of a philosophical hypothesis strips us of our knowledge, then our ordinary knowledge is shown to be deeply superficial—held only by virtue of our collective refusal to think about skeptical alternatives.

## III. The Objection of Unusually Restrictive Standards

This brings us directly to the first major objection: the charge that the mere consideration of a skeptical hypothesis introduces an unusually restrictive, ad hoc standard that dissolves ordinary knowledge rather than vindicating it. 

Why should the mere *mention* or *contemplation* of a Cartesian scenario be capable of shifting epistemic standards upward? In the case of paradigm indexicals, context shifts are governed by objective features of the physical or conversational environment. "Here" shifts with the spatial location of the speaker; "now" shifts with the temporal moment; "flat" shifts with the practical stakes and pragmatic interests of the agents involved. If I am building a microchip, the practical consequences of a bump are catastrophic; hence, the high standards for "flatness" are pragmatically grounded in the practical requirements of the task.

In the epistemological case, however, what grounds the shift to $C_{high}$? Often, the contextualist appeals to the *salience* of the skeptical hypothesis. When the skeptic introduces the Brain-in-the-Vat hypothesis, it becomes psychologically salient to the interlocutors. But salience in a philosophical seminar is not a practical stake; it is a purely intellectual, contemplative state. Why should the psychological salience of an epistemic possibility alter the semantic extension of "knows"?

Critics argue that this mechanism is not only mysterious but pernicious. If the mere thought of a radical skeptical scenario raises the standards for knowledge to an unachievable height, then contextualism implies a bizarre epistemological fragility: *ignorance is contagious through imagination*. Merely entertaining a philosophical thought experiment is sufficient to destroy one's knowledge. This runs counter to our deepest intuitions about the nature of epistemic status. If I know that I am typing on a keyboard, my epistemic relation to that fact—grounded in my sensory perception, neurophysiology, and cognitive architecture—does not evaporate simply because someone in a distant seminar room utters the word "Descartes."

Furthermore, this objection highlights an internal tension within contextualism. If the standards for knowledge are elevated whenever a skeptical hypothesis is considered, then the epistemologist who studies skepticism is *ipso facto* operating in $C_{high}$. Consequently, whenever the epistemologist tries to evaluate whether we have knowledge, they are guaranteed to find that we do *not* have it (relative to their current context). The contextualist vindication of ordinary knowledge ($C_{low}$) becomes a domain that can only be inhabited by remaining unreflective. To analyze knowledge is to destroy it; to theorize about skepticism is to ensure that skepticism is correct within one's own framework. This renders contextualism self-defeating as a philosophical theory, for it relegates the very domain it seeks to defend (ordinary knowledge) to a pre-theoretical ghetto that cannot withstand philosophical scrutiny.

## IV. The Failure to Account for Context-Determination and Shift Recognition

A second, deeply technical vulnerability of contextualism lies in its failure to account for how context determines epistemic standards or how shifts in context are recognized and navigated by speakers. 

For contextualism to be a viable semantic theory, it must provide a systematic semantics that maps conversational contexts to specific epistemic standards. Yet, despite decades of development, contextualists have struggled to provide a non-ad hoc, rigorous account of the parameters that govern these standards. What exact features of a conversation determine whether an alternative is "relevant" or "salient"? Is it the subjective psychological states of the speakers, the objective practical stakes of the situation, the intentions of the conversational participants, or social norms of inquiry?

If epistemic standards are determined by the *psychological salience* of alternatives to the speakers, contextualism faces severe subjectivism. Suppose an agent is completely unaware of the BIV hypothesis, but a mischievous philosopher sitting nearby is thinking intently about it. Does the philosopher's private mental state shift the epistemic standards for the agent? If so, contextualism entails bizarre action-at-a-distance semantic dependence. If not—if only the agent's own salience matters—then the skeptic's public articulation of the hypothesis fails to shift the standards for the ordinary person unless that person fully comprehends and attends to it. But even if the ordinary person does attend to it, the contextualist must explain *why* this psychological shift alters the semantic content of "knows" rather than simply causing the agent to commit an epistemic error (i.e., failing to recognize that they lack knowledge).

Moreover, contextualism encounters severe difficulties regarding *inter-contextual communication and disagreement*. Imagine a dialogue between an ordinary person (in $C_{low}$) and a skeptic (in $C_{high}$):

*   **Ordinary Person:** "I know I have hands." (Uttered in $C_{low}$, true).
*   **Skeptic:** "You do not know you have hands, because you cannot rule out being a brain-in-a-vat." (Uttered in $C_{high}$, true).

According to contextualism, both speakers are speaking truly, because their utterances occur in different contexts and therefore express different propositions. But if they express different propositions, *they are not disagreeing with one another*. The skeptic is not actually denying what the ordinary person is asserting. 

This consequence is devastating for the dialectical ambitions of epistemology. When the skeptic challenges the common person, we naturally assume that a genuine dispute is taking place—that the skeptic and the anti-skeptic are locking horns over a shared proposition. If contextualism is correct, this entire debate is an equivocal talking-past-each-other. The skeptic says, "You don't know (by high standards)," and the ordinary person replies, "I do know (by low standards)." There is no substantive disagreement, only a semantic mismatch. 

Worse still, speakers are often oblivious to these alleged shifts in context. If semantic indexicality operates in mundane cases (like "here" or "flat"), speakers have immediate, intuitive mastery of the shift; I know when I have crossed a conversational boundary that makes "flat" inappropriate. But in epistemology, ordinary speakers are completely unaware that their use of "knows" is shifting its semantic content when the BIV hypothesis is introduced. They feel as though their pre-theoretic claim ("I know I have hands") is directly contradicted by the skeptic. Contextualism must posit a massive, systematic semantic illusion: speakers use an indexical term without having any conscious grasp of the indexical rules governing its variation, leading them to falsely believe they are engaged in a substantive debate when they are merely shifting semantic gears. A semantic theory that requires such widespread phenomenological blindness to its own mechanisms strains credulity.

## V. The Objection of Begging the Question Against the Skeptic

Perhaps the most philosophically profound objection to contextualism is the charge that it begs the question against the skeptic by presupposing the very epistemic framework it claims to vindicate.

To see why contextualism is accused of question-begging, we must examine the epistemic methodology it employs. Contextualism is fundamentally a *diagnostic* or *therapeutic* strategy. It does not refute the skeptic by producing new evidence, nor does it establish foundational certainty against the BIV hypothesis. Instead, it grants the skeptic's core semantic and epistemic claim—namely, that *if* the skeptical hypothesis is salient or considered, we do not know ordinary propositions—and uses this concession to defuse the skeptical threat to ordinary life.

In doing so, however, contextualism assumes that ordinary epistemic practices in $C_{low}$ are epistemically legitimate *without offering any argument to that effect*. The contextualist simply takes it as a datum that ordinary speakers have knowledge when they are not thinking about skepticism. But the skeptic's challenge is precisely to question whether our unreflective, low-standards practices have any genuine epistemic standing. The skeptic argues that our ordinary beliefs are unjustified because we lack a principled method for ruling out error possibilities. 

When the contextualist responds by saying, "Ah, but your high standards only apply in your high-standards context; in our low-standards context, our ordinary claims are true," the skeptic can rightly object that this is a cheap semantic evasion. It amounts to declaring victory by stipulation. It defines "knowledge" in such a way that everyday confidence counts as knowledge *by definition*, provided no one brings up philosophy. 

Consider an analogy. Suppose an astrologer claims to predict the future based on planetary alignments, and a scientist challenges this practice, demonstrating that planetary gravity has no causal bearing on human destiny and that the predictions fail rigorous tests. A "contextualist about astrology" might step in and argue: "There is no real conflict here! When people are chatting casually at a dinner party, 'prediction' means something modest, like discerning general personality traits, and in that context, the astrologer's claims are true. When the scientist is in a rigorous laboratory context, 'prediction' means something extremely stringent, requiring causal mechanisms and controlled trials, and in that context, the astrologer's claims are false. Thus, both parties are right relative to their contexts."

We immediately recognize that this defense of astrology is deeply unsatisfactory because it sidesteps the normative question. The normative question is not merely about how we happen to use the word "prediction" in different social settings; it is about whether astrological practices possess genuine epistemic warrant. Similarly, in epistemology, the core issue is whether our ordinary perceptual beliefs constitute genuine knowledge of an objective world. By retreating into semantic indexicality, contextualism insulates ordinary knowledge from skeptical critique by changing the subject from *normative justification* to *linguistic usage*. 

By assuming that ordinary ascriptions in $C_{low}$ are true simply because they are part of our ordinary linguistic practice, contextualism presupposes that our everyday cognitive interactions with the world are unproblematically knowledge-producing. But this is precisely the premise that the skeptic challenges. To assume it in the definition of contextual semantics is to beg the question against the Cartesian challenge at the most fundamental level.

---

## VI. Concluding Assessment: The Limits of Semantic Therapy

Epistemic contextualism represents one of the most ingenious attempts in contemporary analytic philosophy to disarm radical skepticism. By harnessing the resources of philosophy of language—specifically the thesis that epistemic terms are context-sensitive indexicals—contextualism provides a neat diagnosis of why the Cartesian paradox feels so compelling: it arises from an illicit, unannounced shift from ordinary, lax epistemic standards to hyper-stringent, philosophical standards.

Yet, as our evaluation has demonstrated, contextualism’s linguistic maneuver comes at an exorbitant philosophical cost. 

1.  **First**, it traps epistemology in a self-defeating loop: the very act of engaging in philosophical reflection or considering a skeptical hypothesis automatically elevates the standards to a level where knowledge is unattainable, rendering epistemology structurally suicidal.
2.  **Second**, it fails to provide a non-ad hoc, rigorous account of how context determines standards or how speakers navigate shifts in context, leaving its core semantic machinery mysterious and forcing it to posit widespread, unacknowledged semantic illusion among ordinary speakers who believe they are engaged in genuine disagreement with the skeptic.
3.  **Third**, and most critically, it begs the question against the skeptic. By substituting semantic arbitration for normative epistemological defense, contextualism insulates ordinary beliefs through linguistic fiat rather than vindicating them through rational argument. It treats as an unargued presupposition the very status of ordinary justification that the skeptic calls into question.

Ultimately, contextualism mistakes a semantic description of our linguistic habits for a solution to an epistemic problem. While it accurately captures the intuition that our conversational demands shift depending on our pragmatic and intellectual environment, it fails to show that these conversational shifts correspond to genuine variations in our underlying epistemic relation to the world. The Cartesian challenge remains unrefuted, and the fragile peace offered by contextualism dissolves under the pressure of rigorous philosophical scrutiny. To resolve skepticism, epistemology requires more than an indexical bandage; it demands a robust, normative account of how human cognition manages to bridge the gap between subjective appearance and objective reality—a task that semantic contextualism attempts to bypass, but ultimately leaves unfulfilled.
