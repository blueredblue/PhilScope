# The Modest Modality of Being: Parmenides, B6.1–2, and the Alleged Modal Fallacy

## Introduction

The opening lines of Parmenides’ fragment B6 have long served as a fulcrum for debates concerning the logical structure of the *Way of Truth*. The text, preserved by Simplicius, reads: *χρὴ τὸ λέγειν τε νοεῖν τ’ ἐὸν ἔμμεναι· ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι, μηδὲν δ’ οὐκ ἔστιν*. At first glance, these lines appear to be a straightforward injunction: one must speak and think of what is, because being is possible, whereas nothing is not. Yet beneath this surface simplicity lies a notorious philosophical problem. Critics have charged that Parmenides commits a modal fallacy—an illicit shift from the necessity of thought or speech about being to the necessity of being itself. If this charge stands, the foundational deduction of the *Way of Truth* collapses into logical invalidity, undermining the entire Eleatic project. However, this accusation depends heavily on how one translates and interprets the modal terms embedded in the fragment. Standard translations by John Burnet and G.E.L. Owen, which rely on a *de re* interpretation of modal statements, indeed render the argument susceptible to this critique. In contrast, alternative linguistic frameworks offered by Owen Goldin and T.M. Robinson—centered respectively on the notions of ascertainment and existential availability—provide a reading that exonerates Parmenides from the charge of illicit modal shift. In this essay, I will argue that while the standard *de re* reading does produce a modal fallacy, the alternative interpretations successfully dissolve the apparent invalidity by relocating the modality from the object of thought to the act of thinking or the truth of propositions. Consequently, Parmenides’ deduction does not collapse; rather, it emerges as a logically coherent—if radically counterintuitive—argument about the conditions of thought and being.

## Section 1: The Text and the Standard Interpretation

To assess the charge of a modal fallacy, we must first establish the precise text and its standard translations. Fragment B6.1–2 is conventionally punctuated as follows: *χρὴ τὸ λέγειν τε νοεῖν τ’ ἐὸν ἔμμεναι· ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι, μηδὲν δ’ οὐκ ἔστιν*. The syntax is deceptively simple. *Χρὴ* is an impersonal verb expressing necessity or obligation, often translated as “it is necessary” or “one must.” *Τὸ λέγειν τε νοεῖν τ’* are infinitives functioning as the subject of *χρὴ*, meaning “to say and to think.” *Ἐὸν ἔμμεναι* is an infinitive phrase meaning “that what-is is” or “being to be.” The second clause, *ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι*, is more ambiguous. *Ἔστι* can be read existentially (“there is”), veridically (“it is true that”), or copulatively (“it is”). *Εἶναι* is the infinitive “to be.” The final clause, *μηδὲν δ’ οὐκ ἔστιν*, negates the preceding: “but nothing is not” or “but it is not possible for nothing to be.”

John Burnet’s translation, which has been enormously influential, renders the passage as: “It is necessary to speak and to think what is; for being is, but nothing is not.” Burnet reads *ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι* as a straightforward existential claim: being exists, and nothing does not exist. The modal force of *χρὴ* is confined to the opening clause, while the justification for this necessity is the brute fact of existence. G.E.L. Owen, in his seminal essay “Eleatic Questions,” offers a more refined translation that explicitly introduces modal vocabulary: “It is necessary to say and think that what-is is; for it is possible to be, but it is not possible for nothing to be.” Owen’s translation is significant because it interprets *ἔστι* as expressing possibility—specifically, the possibility of being—and *οὐκ ἔστιν* as expressing impossibility—the impossibility of nothing. This reading aligns *ἔστι* with the Greek idiom *ἔστι + infinitive*, which often conveys possibility (e.g., *ἔστι μοι ἐλθεῖν* = “it is possible for me to go”).

On Owen’s reading, the argument in B6.1–2 can be formalized as follows:

1. Necessarily, one says and thinks that what-is is. (Premise, from *χρὴ*)
2. Possibly, what-is is. (Premise, from *ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι*)
3. It is not possible that nothing is. (Premise, from *μηδὲν δ’ οὐκ ἔστιν*)
4. Therefore, necessarily, what-is is. (Implicit conclusion, leading to the *Way of Truth*)

The alleged modal fallacy emerges when we scrutinize the inference from premises 1 and 2 to conclusion 4. Premise 1 is a *de dicto* necessity: it is necessary that the proposition “what-is is” be thought or said. This is a claim about the epistemic or linguistic act, not about the object itself. Premise 2 is a *de re* possibility: it asserts that the object “what-is” has the modal property of possible existence. The conclusion, however, asserts a *de re* necessity: that “what-is” necessarily exists. In standard modal logic, the inference from *□(S thinks p)* and *◇q* to *□q* is invalid. The necessity of a thought does not entail the necessity of its object. For example, it is necessary that I think “2+2=4” if I am to do arithmetic correctly, but this does not make the number 2 necessarily exist in a *de re* sense. Similarly, the possibility of being does not, by itself, yield the necessity of being. To bridge the gap from possibility to necessity, Parmenides would need an additional premise—such as the principle that what is possible is necessary, or that there is no middle ground between being and not-being. But B6.1–2 does not explicitly provide such a premise. Hence, the charge of a modal fallacy: Parmenides illicitly moves from a *de dicto* necessity of thought to a *de re* necessity of being, and from a *de re* possibility to a *de re* necessity.

This critique has been articulated forcefully by modern commentators. Jonathan Barnes, for instance, argues that Parmenides conflates the conditions of thought with the conditions of existence, treating the impossibility of thinking not-being as evidence for the impossibility of not-being itself. Patricia Curd similarly notes that the *Way of Truth* rests on a confusion between epistemic and metaphysical modalities. The standard reading thus renders Parmenides vulnerable to the objection that his deduction is logically invalid. If we accept Burnet’s and Owen’s translations as the definitive interpretation, the *Way of Truth* appears to be built on sand.

## Section 2: The De Re / De Dicto Distinction and the Nature of the Fallacy

To fully appreciate the force of the modal fallacy charge, we must clarify the distinction between *de re* and *de dicto* modality. A *de dicto* modal statement attributes a modal property to a proposition or a state of affairs. For example, “It is necessarily true that all bachelors are unmarried” is a *de dicto* necessity: the proposition “all bachelors are unmarried” is necessarily true. A *de re* modal statement attributes a modal property to an object or a thing. For example, “The number 2 is necessarily even” is a *de re* necessity: the object “2” has the property of being necessarily even. The distinction matters because the rules of inference differ. From *□(p)* we can infer *p*, but we cannot infer that the objects mentioned in *p* have any particular modal properties. Conversely, from *◇(a is F)* we cannot infer *□(a is F)* without additional premises.

In the standard reading of B6.1–2, the modal terms are distributed in a way that invites confusion. The opening *χρὴ* governs the infinitives *λέγειν* and *νοεῖν*, making the necessity *de dicto*: it is necessary that the act of saying and thinking be directed at the proposition “what-is is.” The phrase *ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι* is read as *de re* possibility: the object “what-is” is possible. The conclusion, which Parmenides draws throughout the *Way of Truth* (e.g., in B8), is that being is ungenerated, imperishable, and eternal—i.e., *de re* necessity. The fallacy lies in the tacit inference from the *de dicto* necessity of thought to the *de re* necessity of being, and from the *de re* possibility of being to its *de re* necessity.

Burnet’s translation, which omits explicit modal vocabulary in the second clause, might seem to avoid the possibility-to-necessity shift. If *ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι* simply means “being is,” then the argument is: It is necessary to think being; being is; nothing is not. This is a straightforward assertion of existence, not a modal claim. However, even this reading faces a problem. The necessity of thought (*χρὴ*) is not logically connected to the existence of being. Why must we think what is? Parmenides’ answer, presumably, is that we cannot think what is not. But the impossibility of thinking not-being is a *de dicto* impossibility about thought, not a *de re* impossibility about being. To conclude that being is and not-being is not, Parmenides must assume that the limits of thought are the limits of reality—a metaphysical assumption that is not logically entailed by the premises. Thus, even Burnet’s more austere reading leaves Parmenides open to a related, though slightly different, charge: the conflation of epistemic and metaphysical modalities.

Owen’s translation makes the modal fallacy more explicit by introducing the possibility language. Owen himself was aware of this tension and attempted to resolve it by arguing that Parmenides is not making a modal inference at all, but rather a point about the “availability” of being for thought. In his analysis, *ἔστι* does not express possibility in the modern sense but rather a kind of “being available” or “being present.” However, Owen’s own translation, which uses “it is possible to be,” undermines this intention by importing a modal term that invites the fallacy. The standard reading, therefore, is caught in a dilemma: either it is logically invalid (if we take the modal terms seriously) or it is philosophically ungrounded (if we take them as mere assertions of existence). This dilemma motivates the search for alternative interpretations.

## Section 3: Owen Goldin’s Ascertainment Reading

Owen Goldin offers a radical reinterpretation of the modal language in B6.1–2 that seeks to avoid the fallacy by denying that Parmenides is making *de re* modal claims about being. Goldin’s central thesis is that the verb *ἔστι* in Parmenides does not function as an existential or copulative “is” but rather as an “ascertainment” verb—a verb that indicates the presence of an object to thought or perception. In this framework, *ἔστι* is not a predicate but a marker of cognitive availability. It tells us that something is “there” for the mind to grasp, that it is “present” or “available” for consideration.

Goldin translates B6.1–2 as follows: “It is necessary to say and think that what-is is present (to thought); for it is possible for what-is to be present, but it is not possible for nothing to be present.” On this reading, the modal terms are not about the existence or non-existence of an object in the world, but about the possibility or impossibility of certain cognitive acts.
