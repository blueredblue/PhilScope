# The Architecture of Utterance: Strawson, Vagueness, and the Ontology of Non-Referential Reference

## Introduction

In the philosophical landscape of mid-twentieth-century analytic philosophy, the problem of referring to that which does not exist served as a primary testing ground for rival theories of meaning. When Bertrand Russell, in his seminal 1905 essay “On Denoting,” offered his theory of definite descriptions, he sought to exorcise the ghosts of non-existent entities from the logical domain. By analyzing a proposition such as “The present King of France is bald” as a complex existential claim—asserting that there is at least one, and at most one, King of France, and that he is bald—Russell dissolved the semantic paradox of non-referential subjects. He substituted a rigid logical grammar for surface grammatical form, rendering sentences containing empty designators straightforwardly false rather than nonsensical or logically indeterminate.

Nearly half a century later, P.F. Strawson launched a systematic and brilliant counter-offensive in his 1950 paper “On Referring.” Strawson argued that Russell had conflated the meaning of an *expression* with the *use* of that expression on a particular occasion. For Strawson, expressions themselves do not refer; rather, *speakers* use expressions to refer to particulars within specific contexts. Meaning, in Strawson’s framework, is not an entity standing in a direct relation to an object, nor is it a Russellian logical paraphrase that flattens pragmatic context. Instead, meaning is a set of general directions for the use of an expression in making statements about the world.

This essay evaluates Strawson’s theory of expressional meaning, testing its structural integrity and its vulnerability to vagueness when confronted with expressions that lack fixed referents—such as “the present Queen of America.” The dialectical core of this investigation hinges on a critical fault line: When an expression’s directions for use point toward a void, does Strawson’s theory collapse into an untenable subjectivity, or does it correctly locate the source of semantic failure in the speaker's pragmatic misapplication rather than the expression's structural incoherence? 

To resolve this tension, we must examine whether the implicit directions encoded in our language are inherently unreliable when stripped of existential anchors, or if speakers fundamentally misunderstand the rules governing proper application. Ultimately, by tracing the contours of this debate, we can adjudicate between Russell’s austere logical paraphrases and Strawson’s nuanced theory of denoting, revealing how the limits of reference expose the delicate interplay between semantic rules and pragmatic reality.

---

## I. Strawson’s Revolution: Expressions, Uses, and the Pragmatic Turn

To understand Strawson’s intervention, one must first grasp the target of his critique. Russell’s theory of denoting is fundamentally logocentric. It treats sentences as vehicles for propositions whose logical forms are hidden beneath the misleading surface of ordinary grammar. In Russell’s view, if a sentence contains a singular term that fails to pick out a unique object, the sentence as a whole fails to express a proposition that is true, because the existential presupposition (or rather, assertion) built into its logical form is false. 

Strawson identified a category mistake in this approach. He argued that Russell was asking of sentences a question that could properly be asked only of *uses* of sentences. According to Strawson, we must distinguish between:
1. A sentence (or expression).
2. A use of a sentence (or an utterance of an expression on a particular occasion).
3. A statement (what is made by a particular use of a sentence).

Expressions do not refer; people refer *by using* expressions. An expression like “the table” has a meaning that remains constant across different contexts, but its *reference* changes depending on who utters it, when, where, and to what they are attending. Meaning, for Strawson, is essentially instrumental and rule-governed: it is a set of general directions for making statements, for referring to particulars, and for characterizing those particulars. 

To use Strawson’s famous metaphor, a sentence and its meaning are like a tool in a toolkit. The meaning of a word or sentence is not some shadowy object or Russellian logical structure; it is the set of instructions for how the tool is to be employed in human discourse. If I hand you a saw and tell you it is meant for cutting wood, I have given you its general direction of use. If you attempt to use it to hammer a nail into concrete, you have not disproven the tool’s meaning or function; rather, you have made a misuse of it.

From this foundation, Strawson argued that statements made by using sentences containing empty definite descriptions—such as “The king of France is wise”—are neither true nor false. They are, instead, *spurious* or *void*, because the precondition for making a truth-evaluable statement has not been met. That precondition is the unique existence of the object referred to. Just as one cannot step into the same river twice, one cannot make a truth-evaluable statement about a non-existent particular using a definite description. The sentence *itself* has meaning (the general directions for use), but the *utterance* fails to state anything because the presupposition of existence is unsatisfied.

## II. The Architecture of Meaning as "Directions for Use"

Strawson’s definition of meaning as a set of general directions for use is deceptively simple, yet it carries immense theoretical weight. It shifts the locus of semantic analysis away from truth-conditions (in the strict, extensional sense) and toward the normative practices of language-users. However, this shift introduces immediate vulnerabilities. 

What does it mean for an expression to embody "directions for correct use"? 

If I consult a manual for assembling a piece of furniture, the instructions tell me to take panel A, align it with slot B, and secure it with screw C. The directions are functional, explicit, and bounded by physical constraints. Linguistic directions, however, are rarely so explicit. They are embedded in a complex, evolving network of conventions, social practices, and cognitive habits. 

An expression’s meaning-directions typically comprise:
1. **Sortal constraints:** What broad ontological category does the expression pick out? (“The table” requires a physical, artifactual object; “the number” requires an abstract mathematical entity).
2. **Uniqueness conditions:** Does the expression demand a single, identifiable item within a shared context? (Definite articles and singular demonstratives).
3. **Relational or contextual parameters:** How does the expression anchor itself to the speech situation? (Indexical elements like “here,” “now,” “this”).

When these directions are clear and the world cooperates, communication proceeds smoothly. But language is chronically susceptible to vagueness. Words bleed into one another; boundary cases proliferate; and speakers routinely deploy expressions in novel, analogical, or speculative ways. 

Strawson’s framework relies on the assumption that these directions for use are sufficiently determinate to distinguish between a *correct* use (one that successfully hooks onto the world) and an *incorrect* or *aberrant* use. But what happens when the directions themselves are structurally indeterminate, or when the expression points toward a domain where the conceptual geography is fundamentally fractured? This brings us to our test case: “the present Queen of America.”

---

## III. The Test Case: "The Queen of America" and the Anatomy of Semantic Vagueness

Consider the phrase “the present Queen of America.” 

From the perspective of ordinary English grammar and compositional semantics, the expression is structurally well-formed. It consists of a definite article (“the”), an adjective indicating temporal status (“present”), a noun denoting a monarchical office (“Queen”), and a prepositional phrase designating a territorial jurisdiction (“of America”). 

If we apply Strawson’s theory, what are the general directions for the use of this expression?
* Look for a unique person who currently holds the title of Queen.
* Ensure that this person’s sovereign domain is the United States of America.

When a speaker utters the sentence, “The present Queen of America is visiting London,” they are attempting to use the phrase to refer to a specific individual. Yet, the political and constitutional structure of the United States precludes the existence of a monarch. There is no office of the Queen of America; consequently, there is no occupant of that office.

Under a Russellian analysis, the proposition expressed is false, because the assertion—$\exists x (\text{Queen}(x, \text{America}) \land \forall y (\text{Queen}(y, \text{America}) \supset y = x) \land \text{Visiting}(x))$—fails at the existential clause. 

Under Strawson’s analysis, the speaker has failed to make a true or false statement because the presupposition of unique existence is unsatisfied. The utterance misfires. 

However, the problem runs deeper than a simple failure of presupposition. "The present Queen of America" exposes a profound vulnerability in Strawson’s notion of "directions for use." Is the phrase "the present Queen of America" suffering from a mere *absence of an object* (like "the present King of France" in 1950), or is it afflicted by a deeper sort of *conceptual vagueness and structural incoherence*?

To evaluate this, we must examine how speakers interact with such expressions. When someone encounters or utters "the present Queen of America," do they misunderstand the rules of language, or are the implicit directions in the language inherently unreliable when applied across political and cultural boundaries?

### The Two-Horned Dilemma for Strawson

1. **Horn 1: The Directions are Clear, but the Speaker Misuses Them.** 
   If Strawson insists that the directions for use are determinate and clear—requiring a monarch in America—then any speaker who utters the phrase is simply making a pragmatic error. They are violating the sortal or institutional presuppositions of the term. The fault lies entirely with the speaker’s willful or ignorant disregard for the constitutional realities of the United States. In this case, Strawson’s theory holds firm, treating the expression as having a clear meaning (directions for use that cannot be fulfilled) while dismissing the utterance as a semantic misfire.

2. **Horn 2: The Directions are Inherently Vague or Adaptable.** 
   If, however, language-users treat the expression as potentially meaningful in a metaphorical, counterfactual, or fictional context (e.g., in a dystopian novel like Philip K. Dick’s *The Man in the High Castle*, or in satirical political commentary), then the "directions for use" cannot be fixed solely by actual extralinguistic reality. They must be fluid enough to accommodate imaginative projections. But if meaning-directions are this fluid, where do we draw the line between a *failed reference to a non-existent actual object* and a *successful reference to an imaginative or contextual construct*? 

This is where vagueness threatens to dismantle Strawson’s neat distinction between expression-meaning and utterance-reference. Vagueness is not merely a matter of borderline cases (like "baldness" or "heap"); it is also a structural indeterminacy in how rules of reference apply when the institutional or ontological scaffolding required by an expression is absent.

## IV. Dialectical Evaluation: Are Implicit Directions Inherently Unreliable, or Do Speakers Misunderstand Application?

To resolve the tension exposed by our test case, we must directly confront the dialectical objective of this essay: *Are the implicit directions in language inherently unreliable when accounting for empty designators, or do speakers fundamentally misunderstand proper application?*

### The Case for Inherently Unreliable Directions

Proponents of a skeptical view regarding implicit linguistic directions might argue that natural language is riddled with category mistakes waiting to happen precisely because its grammar is decoupled from strict ontological inventories. 

Grammar permits the combination of any definite article with any noun modifier and any territorial specifier. We can construct "the present square circle," "the fastest prime number," or "the present Queen of America." If meaning is truly a set of directions for use, and those directions permit the generation of expressions that point into logical or institutional voids, then the directions themselves are profoundly unreliable guides to reality. 

In this view, Strawson’s metaphor of the tool breaks down. A saw is designed for wood because the physical properties of steel and wood interact in predictable ways. But language generates expressions via recursive syntax that can outrun any possible domain of objects. If the instructions ("Find the unique X such that...") can be routinely issued for non-existent entities, then the semantic engine of natural language is inherently prone to producing vacuous outputs. The "directions" do not warn the speaker that the road ends in a cliff; they blithely keep pointing forward.

### The Case for Speaker Misunderstanding and Pragmatic Competence

Conversely, a defender of Strawson must argue that language does not operate in an ontological vacuum, and that competent speakers understand the presuppositions embedded in expressions. 

When a speaker uses an expression like "the present Queen of America," they are either:
* Engaged in deliberate irony, fiction, or counterfactual hypothesis (where the pragmatic frame temporarily suspends actual-world reference).
* Displaying a cognitive or factual error regarding the political structure of the United States.

In neither case is the *meaning* of the expression flawed, nor are the directions for use inherently unreliable. The directions are conditional: *If* there were a constitutional monarchy in the United States, *then* "the present Queen of America" would direct you to the reigning female monarch. The failure occurs because the speaker (or the conversational context) attempts to apply a conditional instruction where the antecedent condition is unmet.

Furthermore, Strawson can appeal to the distinction between *semantic rules* and *pragmatic competence*. A competent speaker knows that definite descriptions carry existential presuppositions. When a speaker violates these presuppositions without a clear fictional or ironical framing, they are demonstrating a failure of pragmatic competence—a misunderstanding of how and when a referring expression may be legitimately deployed.

## V. Resolving the Tension: Russell vs. Strawson on Denoting and Non-Existence

We are now positioned to adjudicate between Bertrand Russell’s logical paraphrases and P.F. Strawson’s theory of denoting. 

| Dimension | Bertrand Russell (Logical Atomism / Descriptivism) | P.F. Strawson (Ordinary Language / Pragmatics) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Semantic Unit** | The Proposition (expressed by the sentence as a whole via logical form). | The Statement (made by a speaker using an expression in a context). |
| **Status of "The King of France is wise"** | Propositional; strictly **False** (due to the falsity of the uniqueness/existence conjunct). | Spurious / Void; **Neither True nor False** (presupposition failure). |
| **Definition of Meaning** | Truth-conditions governed by logical syntax and Russellian denoting concepts. | General directions for use in making statements and referring to particulars. |
| **Treatment of Non-Existence** | Absorbed into extensional logic via existential quantification. | Relegated to pragmatic preconditions and utterance-failure. |

### The Strengths and Limits of Russell’s Paraphrase

Russell’s genius lay in his ability to eliminate metaphysical puzzles by exposing logical form. By translating "The $F$ is $G$" into:
$$\exists x (Fx \land \forall y (Fy \supset y = x) \land Gx)$$
Russell neatly sidesteps the Meinongian jungle of subsistence. We do not need to posit a shadowy non-existent "Queen of America" for the sentence to have a truth-value; the sentence simply asserts that a unique entity satisfying the description exists and has the property $G$. Since it doesn't, the sentence is false. 

However, Russell’s approach comes at a heavy cost. It forces ordinary language into the Procrustean bed of first-order predicate logic. As Strawson rightly noted, when speakers utter sentences with empty descriptions, they do not feel as though they are making complex existential claims that happen to be false. If I say, "The man who is standing over there in the corner with a top hat is my uncle," and there is no man in the corner, you do not respond by saying, "That is false, because there is not exactly one man standing in the corner with a top hat." You respond by saying, "There is no man standing in the corner." The utterance lacks a presupposed subject; it does not make a false assertion about a phantom.

### The Triumphs and Vulnerabilities of Strawson’s Framework

Strawson captures this pre-theoretically correct intuition. He honors the pragmatic reality that reference is an action performed by speakers, not an automatic algebraic mapping between words and things. By distinguishing between sentence-meaning (directions for use) and utterance-reference, Strawson protects ordinary discourse from the flattening effects of logical atomism.

Yet, as our examination of vagueness and empty designators reveals, Strawson’s theory struggles when the boundaries of linguistic direction and contextual reality blur. If meaning is merely "directions for use," and those directions can be endlessly generated for nonexistent, vague, or institutionally impossible objects (like the Queen of America, the present King of France, or the round square), Strawson’s theory threatens to become too permissive. It shields utterances from truth-value evaluation by declaring them "void," but it struggles to provide a rigorous account of how semantic rules constrain our engagement with empty terms.

### Synthesis: The Complementarity of Logic and Pragmatics

Ultimately, the debate between Russell and Strawson is not a zero-sum game where one must be entirely discarded in favor of the other. Rather, they operate at two different, mutually illuminating levels of semantic inquiry:

1. **Russellian logic** provides the *truth-conditional skeleton* of language. It maps the structural commitments of propositions and ensures that our semantics can account for the logical behavior of empty designators without inflating our ontology.
2. **Strawsonian pragmatics** provides the *flesh and blood* of utterance. It explains how speakers navigate real-world contexts, establish reference, and rely on shared presuppositions to communicate effectively.

When confronted with expressions like "the present Queen of America," Strawson is correct that the speaker has failed to make a truth-evaluable statement due to a presupposition failure. But Russell is correct that the logical structure of the sentence exposes an existential commitment that cannot be wished away by appealing solely to speaker intentions. 

---

## Conclusion

P.F. Strawson’s theory of expressional meaning represents a monumental shift toward acknowledging the pragmatic dimensions of language. By defining meaning as a set of general directions for correct use, Strawson rescued the philosophy of language from the overly austere constraints of logical atomism, reminding us that expressions do not refer—speakers do.

However, when tested against expressions with non-existent or logically impossible referents—such as "the present Queen of America"—Strawson’s framework reveals inherent vulnerabilities to vagueness. When semantic directions point toward an ontological void, the distinction between a speaker's misuse and an expression's structural incoherence begins to blur. We are forced to ask whether linguistic directions are inherently unreliable or if speakers are simply prone to pragmatic misapplication.

The resolution of this tension does not lie in choosing between Russell’s logical paraphrases and Strawson’s theory of denoting, but in recognizing their interdependence. Language is both a system of logical constraints and an instrument of human action. While implicit directions for use guide our communicative acts, the failure of those directions in the face of non-existence demonstrates that reference remains uncomfortably tethered to the hard realities of the world. In the end, whether an expression points to a crown in Paris or a throne in Washington, the ghost in the machine of language reminds us that our words constantly reach out for a world that does not always answer.
