# The Axial Boundary and the Arbitrariness of Hick’s Pluralism

John Hick’s pluralistic hypothesis is one of the most ambitious and influential proposals in modern philosophy of religion. It promises to make sense of the remarkable diversity of the world’s religious traditions by locating them as “different human responses to the same transcendent reality.”  The hypothesis is famously structured by two commitments: a Kantian distinction between the Real as it is in itself and the Real as it is experienced through religious categories, and a soteriological criterion according to which a religion is authentic to the extent that it enables human beings to move from self-centeredness to Reality-centeredness. Hick further limits his hypothesis to what he calls “post-axial” religions, the world faiths that emerged from the so-called axial age, and he sets aside pre-axial and indigenous traditions such as Native American vision quests or African shamanic initiations. The question I wish to examine is whether this restriction is guilty of unreasonable arbitrariness.

In what follows, I argue that Hick’s restriction is indeed unreasonable by his own lights. The foundational principle on which his pluralism rests, namely that religious experience can be veridical because it is experience of the transcendent, cannot be coherently combined with an exclusive reliance on soteriological transformation as the criterion of religious validity. Once the foundational principle is taken seriously, there is no non-arbitrary reason to deny the status of authentic religious experience to pre-axial traditions.
