# The Ontology of Infinite Regress: Vlastos, Predication, and Identity in Plato’s *Parmenides*

## Introduction

In the first movement of Plato’s *Parmenides* (132a1–b2), the young Socrates is confronted by the veteran Eleatic visitor with a devastating dialectical challenge: the Third-Man Argument (TMA). This argument targets the foundational machinery of the middle-period theory of Forms, designed to demonstrate that the very semantic and ontological postulates intended to secure the stability of knowledge instead generate an endless, ungrounded proliferation of entities. 

For centuries, the TMA was treated as either a sophistical exercise or a peripheral curiosity. However, in his seminal 1954 essay "The Third Man Argument in Plato’s *Parmenides*," Gregory Vlastos transformed the debate. Vlastos argued that the TMA is logically valid, establishing that Plato’s ontology is genuinely inconsistent. According to Vlastos, this inconsistency stems from a direct collision between two core semantic commitments of the theory of Forms: **Self-Predication (SP)** and **Non-Identity (NI)**.

This essay evaluates Vlastos’s interpretation of the TMA, scrutinizing the mechanics of SP and NI, and assessing whether Plato’s ontology is indeed irredeemably crippled by an infinite regress. Furthermore, we will examine the subsequent philosophical counter-offensives—most notably those of Peter Geach, Wilfrid Sellars, and later revisionist scholars—who argue that Vlastos’s reading relies on a category mistake regarding the nature of Platonic predication and identity. 

By parsing the distinction between ordinary and focal predication, and by reassessing whether Forms are particulars or universals, we will determine whether Vlastos successfully exposed a fatal flaw in the middle-period ontology, or whether his analysis misconstrues Plato's metaphysical grammar.

---

## 1. The Anatomy of the Third-Man Argument and Vlastos’s Reconstruction

To appreciate the gravity of Vlastos’s critique, we must first reconstruct the TMA as it unfolds in the *Parmenides* and trace Vlastos's formalization of it. 

Parmenides initiates the argument by asking Socrates how he arrives at his belief in the existence of a single Form for each plurality of things. Socrates replies that when a number of things are large, they appear to have a single character (idea) when viewed together; hence, one Form of Largeness remains (132a1–4). Parmenides presses further:

> "And if you look at the others in the same way—at largeness itself and the large things that are such—won’t another large appear, over and above them all, by which all these appear large?" (132a6–8)

Socrates concedes this. Parmenides then draws the inevitable conclusion:

> "Then another Form of largeness will come to light, which has come to be alongside largeness itself and the things that share in it, and over all these yet another, by which all of them will be large. And none of your Forms will be any longer one, but infinite in number" (132b1–2).

Vlastos formalizes this dialectic into a rigorous logical deduction governed by four foundational assumptions that he attributes to Plato’s middle-period ontology:

1. **(A1) Self-Predication (SP):** If anything is a Form, it has the character it is a Form of. (e.g., Largeness itself is large).
2. **(A2) Non-Identity (NI):** If $a$ is large, $a$ is not identical to Largeness itself; the Form cannot be identical to the things that participate in it. (If $x$ is $F$, $x \neq F$-ness).
3. **(A3) Analogy (or "Direction"):** If a plurality of things are $F$, they are so because they share in a single Form, which is identical for all of them and is what they are.
4. **(A4) Non-Symmetry (or "Qualitative Similarity"):** Anything that is $F$ and is not the Form $F$-ness must share a common characteristic with all other things that are $F$.

From these premises, Vlastos constructs the regress. 
* *Step 1:* Suppose there is a plurality of large things ($A_1$). By (A3), they share in a Form, Largeness ($F_1$). 
* *Step 2:* By (SP), $F_1$ is itself large. 
* *Step 3:* By (NI), since $F_1$ is large, $F_1$ is distinct from the original plurality ($A_1$). 
* *Step 4:* Therefore, we now have a new plurality of large things: the original set $A_1$ plus $F_1$. Let us call this set $A_2$.
* *Step 5:* By (A3), this new plurality ($A_2$) must share in a *further* Form, $F_2$ (a "Third Man"). 
* *Step 6:* By (NI), $F_2$ is distinct from both $A_1$ and $F_1$. 

By iterating this process ($A_1 \to F_1 \to A_2 \to F_2 \to \dots \to A_n \to F_n$), we generate an infinite series of distinct Forms of Largeness ($F_1, F_2, F_3, \dots$). 

Vlastos’s most provocative claim is that this regress is **vicious**. It is not merely a benign infinite hierarchy (such as the infinite divisibility of a line or the series of numbers); rather, it destroys the ontological and semantic utility of the Forms. The primary function of a Form in Plato’s metaphysics is to provide a *unique*, definitive standard of intelligibility and semantic reference for a multiplicity of sensible particulars. If the invocation of a Form to explain a predicate necessarily generates an endless sequence of supplementary entities without ever grounding the predication, the explanatory power of the Theory of Forms collapses. Plato’s ontology fails because it is logically incoherent.

## 2. The Collision: Self-Predication versus Non-Identity

To evaluate Vlastos’s thesis, we must examine the two pivots upon which his entire logical machinery turns: **Self-Predication (SP)** and **Non-Identity (NI)**. Vlastos maintains that Plato is deeply committed to both, yet their simultaneous adoption is fatal.

### Self-Predication (SP)
Is Plato truly committed to Self-Predication? A cursory reading of the middle-period dialogues suggests an affirmative answer. In the *Phaedo* (100c), Plato speaks of the Beautiful itself, the Good itself, and the Just itself. In the *Symposium* (211a–b), the Beautiful is described as "itself by itself," yet it is also characterized in ways that imply it possesses the very attribute it instantiates. 

In the *Protagoras* (330c–e), Plato asserts that Justice is just and Holiness is holy. Vlastos interprets these locutions literally: they are identity statements or predications wherein the subject is a Form and the predicate is the character corresponding to that Form.

However, modern scholars have questioned whether "Justice is just" means what Vlastos thinks it means. In ordinary English, "The standard meter bar in Paris is meter" is category-confused, but "The Form of Justice is just" might be better understood as an expression of *focal meaning* or *definitional essence* rather than an accidental or even intrinsic property attribution. 

If SP is interpreted as "The Form of $F$ possesses the quality $F$ in the same way a sensible particular possesses it," then SP becomes philosophically toxic. It turns the transcendent, immutable Form into a gigantic instance of its own property—making Largeness itself a "large thing" alongside mountains and oceans.

### Non-Identity (NI)
Non-Identity is equally entrenched in Plato’s ontology. In the *Phaedo* (74a–c), Plato contrasts the equal sticks and stones with "Equality itself." He insists that Equality itself is *not* the equal sticks and stones; the sensible particulars that partake in a Form are radically distinct from the Form itself. If they were identical, the ontological chasm between the sensible realm of becoming and the intelligible realm of being would collapse. Particulars are deficient, relational, and subject to change; Forms are complete, autonomous, and eternal.

### The Inconsistency
Vlastos’s argument is that (SP) and (NI) are mutually exclusive once we apply them to a plurality. 
* If **SP** holds, then $F_1$ is large. 
* If **NI** holds, then because $F_1$ is large, $F_1$ *cannot be identical to* the original Form $F$ (or the original set of large things). 

Therefore, $F_1$ must be a distinct entity from $F$. 

Vlastos contends that Plato cannot abandon either premise without destroying his system. If he drops SP, Forms lose their semantic grounding—they no longer explain *why* particulars are $F$, because the Form itself would no longer be $F$ (abandoning the principle that a cause must possess the character it imparts). If he drops NI, he collapses the distinction between the sensible and intelligible worlds, leading to a grotesque collapsing of the transcendent framework. Thus, Vlastos concludes, Plato is trapped in a logical contradiction.

## 3. The Counter-Offensive: Sellars, Geach, and the "Focal Meaning" Defense

Vlastos’s interpretation quickly provoked a fierce backlash. The most sophisticated responses sought to show that Vlastos’s derivation of the TMA rests on a profound misunderstanding of Platonic predication, specifically projecting modern predicate logic onto an ancient metaphysical framework that operates under entirely different rules.

### Wilfrid Sellars and "Tiglath-Pileser"
In his classic 1955 paper "Vlastos and the ‘Third Man’," Wilfrid Sellars launched a direct assault on Vlastos’s formalization. Sellars argued that Vlastos equivocates on the meaning of the copula in the Self-Predication premise. 

According to Sellars, when Plato says "Justice is just," the "is" of self-predication is not the "is" of inherence (as in "Socrates is wise"), but rather an expression of *exemplification* or *paradigmatic constitution*. To say "Largeness is large" is not to say that the Form of Largeness has the property of largeness in the way a physical object is large; rather, it means that Largeness *constitutes* the standard of largeness. 

Sellars introduces a distinction between *trans-level* and *intra-level* predication. When a sensible particular is large, it participates in Largeness. But when the Form of Largeness is said to be large, it is a category mistake to treat this as an instance of the same relation that holds between the sensible particular and its property. Sellars uses the analogy of a paradigm: the standard meter in Paris *is* a meter, not because it possesses a hidden property of "meter-ness" tucked away inside its platinum-iridium alloy, but because it *defines* what a meter is. 

Therefore, when the TMA generates $F_1$ as "another large," it commits a fallacy of equivocation by treating the transcendent standard as if it were just another member of the sensible set.

### Peter Geach and the "Self-Predication Fallacy"
Peter Geach, in his critique of Vlastos, argued that Vlastos’s reconstruction relies on a principle that Plato explicitly rejects: the assumption that a general term has the same meaning when applied to a Form as when applied to a sensible particular. Geach points out that for Plato, sensible particulars are only *imperfectly* or *homonymously* $F$, whereas the Form is *paradigmatically* $F$. 

If "large" means one thing when applied to a mountain and another (though related) thing when applied to the Form of Largeness, then the set of "large things" ($A_1$) and the Form of Largeness ($F_1$) do not belong to the same logical universe. Consequently, one cannot group them together to form a new, expanded set ($A_2$) that demands a "Third Man." The regress is blocked at Step 4 because the set-theoretic aggregation required to generate the next step is illicit.

## 4. Evaluating Vlastos’s Reply and the Metaphysics of Participation

Vlastos was not idle in the face of these criticisms. In subsequent writings and in the revised editions of his work, he doubled down on his reading, arguing that critics like Sellars and Geach were engaging in "apologetic interpretation"—reading later, sophisticated linguistic distinctions back into a naive Plato who was genuinely trapped in the ontological nets of his own making.

To evaluate this meta-philosophical dispute, we must examine the concept of **Participation (*methexis*)**. Plato’s middle dialogues notoriously struggle to articulate *how* particulars participate in Forms. In the *Phaedo* and *Republic*, participation is often spoken of in spatial or quasi-physical terms (presence, sharing, imitation). 

If participation is conceived of in terms of *resemblance*—where a sensible large thing resembles the Form of Largeness—then the TMA becomes almost mathematically compelling. For if $A_1$ is large because it resembles Largeness ($F_1$), and $F_1$ is itself large (SP), then $A_1$ and $F_1$ must both resemble a *further* standard ($F_2$) in virtue of which they are *both* large. This is the **Similarity Version** of the TMA, which Parmenides himself hints at later in the dialogue (132d–133a).

Vlastos’s strongest point is that Plato’s middle-period ontology lacks the technical machinery to block this regress *unless* Plato fundamentally alters his theory of predication. And indeed, the historical trajectory of the Platonic corpus suggests that Plato *did* alter his theory. 

In late dialogues such as the *Sophist*, Plato abandons the naive separation (*chōris*) of the middle period and develops the theory of the *megista genē* (greatest kinds)—weaving together concepts of identity, difference, and community. The *Parmenides* itself is widely recognized by scholars not merely as a critique of external opponents, but as Plato’s own internal self-critique—an acknowledgment that the uncritical apparatus of the *Phaedo* and *Republic* is untenable.

## 5. Alternative Solutions: Asymmetry, Univocity, and the "Two-Worlds" Ontology

To determine whether Plato’s ontology is irredeemably inconsistent, we must assess several alternative interpretive frameworks that bypass Vlastos’s dilemma without dismissing the historical text as hopelessly confused.

### 1. The Asymmetry of Predication
One robust defense is to argue that Platonic predication is irreducibly *asymmetrical*. In modern formal semantics, "Socrates is wise" and "Wisdom is wise" use the copula to express instantiation and definition respectively. If Plato intuitively grasped this asymmetry—even if he lacked the formal apparatus of modern type theory—then Self-Predication is not a claim that the Form instantiates its own property as a member of a class, but rather that the Form *is* its property. 

To say "Justice is just" is to say "Justice is identical to what it is to be just." If this is the case, then $F_1$ (Justice) does not share a property with the just actions $A_1$; rather, $A_1$ *exhibits* Justice, while Justice *is* Justice. Thus, when we group $A_1$ and $F_1$ together, we are committing a category mistake—grouping an instance with an essence. The TMA fails because it treats essence and instance as homogeneous elements of a set.

### 2. The Univocity vs. Equivocity Debate
Vlastos’s argument depends heavily on the **Univocity Assumption**: the predicate '$F$' must mean the exact same thing whether applied to the Form or to the sensible particular. If "large" means "possessing spatial extension beyond a certain threshold" when applied to a horse, but means "the absolute paradigm of dimension" when applied to Largeness itself, then the inference from "Largeness is large" to "Largeness is a large thing among other large things" is blocked.

Did Plato hold to univocity? The evidence is mixed. In the *Phaedo* (74a–c), Plato contrasts sensible equals with the Equal itself, noting that sensible equals "strive to be like that other, but fall short," whereas the Equal itself is deficient in nothing. This "striving" and "falling short" strongly suggests an *analogical* or *focal* use of the predicate, not a univocal one. If the predicate is analogical, Vlastos’s premise (A4) (that anything that is $F$ and is not the Form must share a common characteristic with all other things that are $F$) collapses, and with it, the regress.

### 3. The "Self-Constitution" Interpretation
A more radical revisionist approach, championed by scholars like Allen and Code, suggests that Self-Predication is not a predication at all in the modern sense, but a statement of *self-constitution*. A Form is self-constituting: it is what it is through itself. 

To say "The Beautiful is beautiful" is not to attribute a property to a subject, but to state an identity: the Form is the ontological ground of its own character. If this reading is correct, Vlastos’s formulation of SP as an ordinary subject-predicate proposition is a category error. Vlastos treats Plato’s metaphysical axioms as if they were sentences in a first-order predicate calculus, ignoring the unique, non-relational status of Platonic Forms as uncaused first principles.

---

## 6. Conclusion: The Verdict on Vlastos and Plato

Gregory Vlastos’s interpretation of the Third-Man Argument remains one of the most brilliant and provocative interventions in twentieth-century scholarship on ancient philosophy. By forcing analytic rigor onto the poetic and often elusive prose of the middle dialogues, Vlastos exposed a profound structural tension in Plato’s metaphysics. 

Our evaluation yields a nuanced verdict. On one hand, **Vlastos was historically and dialectically right** to point out that if one reads Plato’s middle-period formulas with strict naive literalism—taking Self-Predication as property-possession and Non-Identity as generating a homogeneous set of instances—the Third-Man Argument is logically valid and devastating. Plato’s early formulations of participation and self-predication *do* generate an infinite regress if left unrefined.

On the other hand, **Vlastos was philosophically uncharitable** in his refusal to credit Plato with the intuitive distinctions (between definition and instance, univocity and analogy, essence and property) that his critics have subsequently reconstructed. The TMA is not merely a record of Plato’s logical blunder; it is the engine of his philosophical evolution. 

The *Parmenides* marks the turning point where Plato himself recognizes the inadequacy of the naive two-worlds ontology of the *Phaedo* and *Republic*. Far from demonstrating that Plato's entire metaphysical enterprise is a failure, the TMA serves as a rigorous diagnostic tool—an internal critique executed by Plato to clear away the conceptual debris of early formulations and pave the way for the sophisticated dialectic of the late dialogues. 

Thus, Vlastos did not write the obituary of Platonic ontology; rather, by sharpening the knife of logical analysis, he helped us understand the surgical precision with which Plato had to reconstruct his theory of Forms to save them from the abyss of the infinite regress.
