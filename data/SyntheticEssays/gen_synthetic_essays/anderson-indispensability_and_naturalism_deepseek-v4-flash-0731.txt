**Compromise Naturalism and the Indispensability Argument: Reconciling Truth, Practice, and Ontology**

The Quine-Putnam indispensability argument (QPIA) stands as one of the most formidable pillars of mathematical realism. In its canonical form, it rests on two premises: the holistic confirmation of scientific theories, and a thoroughgoing naturalism that denies any "first philosophy" superior to science. From these, it concludes that we ought to believe in the existence of the mathematical entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories. Penelope Maddy, in her influential critiques, has attacked this argument from the very ground it claims to occupy—the actual practice of science and mathematics. She argues that scientists and mathematicians do not treat mathematical entities as existents in the way Quine and Putnam suggest, and that a true naturalism, which defers to the methods of these disciplines, would not yield a Platonist ontology. Between the Scylla of Quine's monolithic holism and the Charybdis of Maddy's practice-based naturalism, there exists a viable middle path: *compromise naturalism*. This position, which carefully separates *epistemic realism* (the determinate truth of mathematical statements) from *ontological commitment* (the existence of mathematical objects), successfully defends the core of the indispensability argument against Maddy's objections. By decoupling truth from existence, it resolves the apparent incompatibility between confirmation holism and naturalism, sustains determinate truth-values for indispensable mathematics through a modal-structuralist framework, and reframes the normative force of the argument as a legitimate meta-scientific inference rather than an improper override of established methodology.

**I. The Quine-Putnam Argument and Maddy's Practice-Based Critique**

To understand the viability of compromise naturalism, one must first appreciate the precise nature of the conflict. The QPIA is a deductive argument rooted in two Quinean doctrines. The first is *confirmation holism*: our theories face the tribunal of experience not as individual sentences but as a corporate body. Evidence for the existence of electrons, for instance, simultaneously confirms the mathematical structures used to describe them, such as the calculus of differential equations. The second is *naturalism*: there is no external, philosophical vantage point from which to judge science. Science is the ultimate arbiter of what exists. Consequently, if mathematics is indispensable to physics, and physics is our best guide to reality, then the entities of mathematics—numbers, sets, functions—must exist. As Putnam famously declared, "Quine is right that we are committed to the existence of mathematical objects... because they are indispensable to our best theories."

Maddy’s critique, developed across works like *Realism in Mathematics* and *Naturalism in Mathematics*, targets the empirical premises of this argument. Her first line of attack is drawn from *scientific practice*. Scientists routinely employ idealizations—point masses, frictionless planes, perfectly rigid bodies—that they do not believe exist. More importantly, Maddy observes that scientists do not treat the mathematical components of their theories as existential claims in the same way they treat physical claims. The physicist who uses a Hilbert space to model a quantum system does not thereby assert the existence of a Platonic realm of infinite-dimensional vector spaces. The mathematics functions as a computational and representational tool, not as a catalog of entities. If we take scientific practice as our guide to ontology, as Quine's naturalism demands, we find that science is silent—or even hostile—to the existence of mathematical objects.

Her second line of attack comes from *mathematical practice*, specifically set theory. Mathematicians frequently work with axioms—such as large cardinal axioms—whose truth they do not assert. They explore the logical consequences of these axioms as a matter of mathematical curiosity, without committing to the existence of the sets they describe. If the indispensability argument were correct, then any mathematical assertion used in science would carry existential weight. Yet mathematicians routinely treat such assertions as conditional or hypothetical. Maddy concludes that the Quinean picture misrepresents the actual epistemic attitudes of practitioners. A true naturalism, which takes the methods of science and mathematics as the standard of rationality, would not yield a blanket commitment to mathematical entities.

The upshot of Maddy's critique is a dilemma: either we accept confirmation holism and reject naturalism (because holism forces an ontology that practice rejects), or we accept naturalism and reject the holism that underpins the QPIA. Compromise naturalism offers a way out by arguing that Maddy has conflated two distinct senses of "commitment"—epistemic commitment to the truth of a statement and ontological commitment to the existence of an entity. By disentangling these, we can retain the holistic confirmation of mathematical *truth* while adopting a naturalistic stance that refuses to posit a special realm of mathematical objects.

**II. Resolving the Holism-Naturalism Tension: The Epistemic/Metaphysical Distinction**

The alleged incompatibility between confirmation holism and naturalism is predicated on the assumption that holism forces a unified ontology. If we confirm the entire web of belief, including mathematics, then we must be committed to everything the web posits. But this inference conflates the *epistemology* of confirmation with the *metaphysics* of existence. Confirmation holism is a thesis about how evidence supports theories; it tells us that the justification for our mathematical beliefs is intertwined with the justification for our physical beliefs. It does not, by itself, dictate a specific metaphysical interpretation of the terms involved.

Compromise naturalism exploits this distinction. It agrees with Quine that the evidence for "2+2=4" is partly constituted by its indispensable role in physics. It agrees with Maddy that we should defer to scientific and mathematical practice when determining what exists. The resolution lies in recognizing that the *truth* of a mathematical statement can be confirmed holistically without thereby committing us to the *existence* of a mathematical object. To see this, consider the statement "The number of planets is eight." This statement is true. It is confirmed by astronomical evidence. Yet its truth does not require us to posit the existence of a "number" as a separate entity over and above the planets themselves. The truth of the statement is grounded in the physical arrangement of the solar system, not in a Platonic realm of numbers. Similarly, the truth of "There are infinitely many primes" is confirmed by its role in number theory, which in turn is indispensable to cryptography and physics. But we can accept this truth without asking what "prime" refers to as an object. We can be *realists* about the truth-value of the proposition while being *nominalists* about the entities it ostensibly quantifies over.

This separation dissolves the holism-naturalism tension. Holism applies to the *confirmation* of mathematical truths: they are justified because they are part of the best overall scientific framework. Naturalism applies to the *interpretation* of those truths: we should not posit entities beyond what scientific and mathematical practice licenses. Since practice does not license a realm of abstract objects, we refrain from positing them. The result is a position that is both holist (in epistemology) and naturalist (in metaphysics). We do not need to choose between Quine and Maddy; we can take the best of both. The holist is satisfied because mathematics is confirmed by its indispensability. The naturalist is satisfied because we do not violate the methodological scruples of scientists and mathematicians by injecting a Platonist ontology into their theories.

**III. Truth-Value Realism Without Ontological Commitment**

The key move in compromise naturalism is the explicit separation of *realism about truth* from *realism about objects*. Traditional Platonism holds that mathematical statements are true because they correspond to a mind-independent realm of abstract entities. This is a robust *object realism*. Compromise naturalism rejects this, but it does not fall into anti-realism. Instead, it adopts a form of *truth-value realism*: mathematical statements have determinate truth-values—they are either true or false—independently of our knowledge or conventions. This realism is sufficient to preserve the cognitive content of mathematics and its applicability to science.

How can a statement be true without there being an object to make it true? The answer lies in a deflationary or structuralist account of truth. Consider the statement "The successor of 0 is 1." In the language of Peano Arithmetic, this is true by definition. But what makes it true? A Platonist says "the number 1 exists." A compromise naturalist says "the statement is true because it correctly describes the structure of any ω-sequence." A structure is not an object; it is a pattern of relations. We can say that "2+2=4" is true because, in any system that satisfies the axioms of arithmetic, the sum of two and two is four. The truth of the statement is a property of the system's structure, not of a particular entity called "2". This is the essence of *structuralism*. Unlike Platonism, structuralism does not posit a realm of abstract objects. Unlike formalism, it does not reduce mathematics to mere symbol manipulation. It claims that mathematics is the science of structures, and that the truth of mathematical statements is determined by the properties of those structures.

This structuralist reading neatly answers Maddy's objection from scientific practice. When a physicist uses the mathematical structure of a Hilbert space, they are not committing to the existence of vectors as abstract objects. They are using the structure to represent the relations between physical states. The truth of the mathematical statements involved is grounded in the consistency and coherence of the structure, not in the existence of its elements. Maddy is right that scientists do not believe in numbers as entities; but they do believe in the *truth* of the mathematical statements they use. The compromise naturalist agrees with Maddy on the first point and with Quine on the second. The scientist believes that "the Schrödinger equation is true" (in the sense of correctly describing the evolution of the wave function), but they do not believe that the wave function is a physical object. By separating truth from objecthood, we honor both the practice of science and the indispensability of mathematics.

**IV. Alternative Realisms and Determinate Truth-Values**

A critical challenge for compromise naturalism is whether it can sustain *determinate truth-values* for indispensable mathematical entities that lack "ontological rights." If we refuse to grant existence to numbers, can we still say that "There are infinitely many primes" is determinately true? Platonism provides a straightforward answer: it is true because there really are infinitely many primes in the Platonic realm. But if we reject this realm, we must find another ground for determinacy.

Let us examine the alternatives. *Ante rem structuralism*, as defended by Stewart Shapiro, posits the existence of structures as abstract entities. This is essentially Platonism about structures, and it inherits all the epistemic and ontological problems of Platonism. It fails Maddy's naturalistic test because it posits entities that scientific and mathematical practice do not recognize. *In re structuralism*, which holds that structures are instantiated in physical systems, faces a different problem: it cannot account for the truth of statements about structures that are not instantiated in the physical world, such as the structure of the hyperreals. If the physical world does not contain an ω-sequence, then in re structuralism would render arithmetic false, which is absurd.

A more promising alternative is *modal structuralism*, developed by Geoffrey Hellman. Hellman argues that mathematical statements should be interpreted as modal claims: "Necessarily, if there is an ω-sequence, then..." For example, "There are infinitely many primes" becomes "Necessarily, if there is any system that satisfies the Peano axioms, then that system contains infinitely many primes." This interpretation does not require the actual existence of an ω-sequence. It only requires the *possibility* of such a system. The truth of the modal claim is determined by the logical coherence of the axioms and the rules of modal logic. This gives us determinate truth-values: either it is necessarily the case that any ω-sequence has infinitely many primes, or it is not. There is no vagueness or indeterminacy.

Does modal structuralism sustain the truth-values required by the indispensability argument? Yes. When a physicist uses a Hilbert space, they are implicitly relying on the modal claim that "necessarily, any system satisfying the axioms of Hilbert space has property P." The truth of this claim is what makes the physics work. We do not need to posit the existence of a Hilbert space as an abstract object; we only need the modal fact that certain structures are possible and have certain properties. This is a form of realism—it asserts that there are objective facts about what is mathematically necessary—but it is not a form of object realism. It does not grant ontological rights to numbers, sets, or spaces.

This modal-structuralist compromise satisfies the demands of both holism and naturalism. Holism is satisfied because the modal claims are confirmed alongside the physical theories that use them. The success of quantum mechanics confirms the truth of the modal claims about Hilbert spaces. Naturalism is satisfied because we are not positing any entities beyond those recognized by science. We are only positing modal facts about the structure of possible systems, which is a far more modest metaphysical commitment than Platonism. The physicist can accept the truth of the mathematics without believing in a realm of abstract objects. The mathematician can explore the consequences of axioms without committing to their existential import. Maddy's critique loses its force because we no longer claim that indispensability forces us to believe in the existence of mathematical entities; we only claim that it forces us to believe in the truth of mathematical statements, understood modally.

**V. Defending the Normative Intervention of Indispensability Theory**

The final and perhaps most contentious issue is the *normative* character of the indispensability argument. Maddy's naturalism is deeply anti-normative in a specific sense: she argues that philosophy has no authority to tell scientists or mathematicians what they *ought* to believe. If a scientist uses a mathematical theory without believing in the existence of its entities, that is their prerogative. The indispensability argument, by contrast, seems to issue a command: "You *must* believe in numbers because they are indispensable." Maddy charges that this improperly overrides the established methodology of science, which is perfectly comfortable using mathematics instrumentally.

Compromise naturalism defends the normative intervention by distinguishing between *methodological norms* and *rational norms*. Methodological norms govern how scientists conduct their research: what experiments to run, which equations to solve, how to interpret data. The indispensability argument does not issue such norms. It does not tell a physicist to change their equations or a mathematician to adopt a new axiom. It is silent on these matters.

However, the argument does issue a *rational norm*: if you accept a scientific theory, you are rationally committed to accepting the logical consequences of that theory. If a theory entails "There are infinitely many primes" (in the modal-structuralist sense), then you cannot consistently accept the theory and deny the truth of that statement. This is not an override of scientific methodology; it is a clarification of what that methodology entails. It is a meta-level inference about the implications of scientific success. It is akin to saying "If you accept the theory of evolution, you must accept that species share common ancestors." This is not telling biologists how to do their job; it is pointing out the logical consequences of their commitments.

Maddy's objection conflates these two types of norms. She assumes that any normative claim about science is an attempt to dictate its methods. But the indispensability argument is not a methodological prescription. It is an ontological or, in our compromise version, a *truth-ontological* inference. It says: "Given that you accept this scientific theory, you must accept the truth of the mathematics it uses." This is a demand of logical consistency, not a demand for a particular research program.

Furthermore, the compromise version softens the normative force in a way that makes it more palatable to scientific practice. If we adopt modal structuralism, the normative claim is not "You must believe in numbers" but rather "You must believe that the mathematical statements are true, where truth is understood modally." This is something that scientists already do. They already believe that the Schrödinger equation is true, that the laws of thermodynamics are true, that the axioms of group theory are true when applied to symmetries. They do not, however, believe in the existence of numbers as abstract objects. The compromise naturalist agrees with them. The normative intervention is thus reduced to a triviality: if you use mathematics, you must accept its truth. This is hardly an improper override of scientific methodology; it is a definition of what it means to use mathematics.

Maddy might respond that even this is too strong. A scientist might use a mathematical theory as a mere instrument, a heuristic device, without believing in its truth. For example, a physicist might use a non-convergent series as an approximation, knowing it is not strictly true. But this is a case of *using* mathematics that is *known to be false* in its literal sense. The indispensability argument concerns mathematics that is *indispensable* and *true*. If a theory uses a false mathematical statement, then the theory is false, and the scientist would not accept it as a final theory. The indispensability argument applies to the mathematics that is essential to the *successful* and *accepted* parts of science.
