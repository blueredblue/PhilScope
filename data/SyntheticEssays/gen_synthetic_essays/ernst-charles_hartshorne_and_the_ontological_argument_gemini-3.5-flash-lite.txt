# The Modality of Perfection: Hartshorne, Kant, and the Ontological Argument Reconsidered

## Introduction: The Metaphysical Fate of the Ontological Argument

The trajectory of rational theology in the modern epoch was long thought to have been definitively arrested by Immanuel Kant’s famous critique in the *Critique of Pure Reason*. By declaring that “existence is not a predicate,” Kant ostensibly severed the conceptual bridge that Anselm of Canterbury and René Descartes had sought to traverse—the bridge connecting the conceptual essence of an entity to its actual, extramental instantiation. For Kant, existence adds nothing to the concept of a thing; it merely posits the thing in its totality. To assert that a supreme being exists is not to amplify its conceptual content with a new determination, but merely to state that the concept has an object corresponding to it in the actual world. 

Yet, the history of philosophy is rarely characterized by permanent obituaries. In the twentieth century, the American process philosopher Charles Hartshorne undertook a radical rehabilitation of the ontological argument, drawing upon neglected elements of Anselm’s *Proslogion*—specifically the distinction between Anselm’s two formulations of the supreme being in Chapters 2 and 3—to construct a modal defense of divine necessity. Hartshorne’s revision does not merely contest Kant’s dictum; it seeks to outflank it by shifting the logical terrain from categorical assertion to modal necessity. By employing the conceptual machinery of quantified modal logic, Hartshorne argues that if a supreme, perfect being is even *possible*, then it is *necessary*, thereby rendering its actual existence logically inescapable.

This essay will evaluate Hartshorne’s revision of the ontological argument against Kant’s critique, interrogate the role of modal coextensiveness in resolving the classical concept of *actus purus*, and examine whether Hartshorne’s deployment of process metaphysics—specifically the distinction between abstract being and concrete becoming—successfully bridges the chasm between an *a priori* concept of perfection and the necessary existence of an ultimate reality. Ultimately, we must ask whether modal operationalism can rescue rational theology from the Kantian critique, or if it merely shifts the burden of proof from the concept of existence to the ungrounded assumption of absolute modal possibility.

## I. The Kantian Interdict and the Modal Counter-Strategy

To appreciate the audacity of Hartshorne’s intervention, one must first confront the formidable architecture of Kant’s critique. Kant’s assault on the Cartesian and Anselmian ontological arguments rests upon the foundational distinction between real predicates and logical predicates. A logical predicate can be any grammatical property assigned to a subject—even the copula “is.” A real predicate, however, is a determination that adds to the concept of the subject, augmenting its internal intelligibility and modifying its comprehension. 

Kant’s central insight is that existence is invariably a real predicate in grammar, but it can never be a real predicate in metaphysics. If I think of a hundred real dollars, my concept contains a specific numerical quantity, a metal alloy or paper composition, and a denomination. If I then assert that these hundred dollars *exist*, I have not added a single penny to the concept. The concept of the existent hundred dollars contains precisely the same conceptual determinations as the concept of the non-existent possible hundred dollars. To exist is merely to stand in a relation to the totality of experience; it is the positing of the object *in re*, distinct from its mere status *in intellectu*. Thus, Anselm’s leap from the definition of God as *aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari possit* (that than which nothing greater can be thought) to the actual existence of that entity commits a category mistake. It treats existence as an intensive magnitude or an internal perfection, whereas it is merely the existential positing of a subject with all its predicates.

Hartshorne’s response to Kant does not dispute the letter of the Kantian critique so much as it bypasses its spirit by elevating the ontological argument to the level of modal logic. Hartshorne observes that Kant’s critique applies primarily to *contingent* existence. For any ordinary object—a dollar, a mountain, a planet—its concept does not entail its existence, and therefore asserting its existence adds a new existential status that was lacking in the mere concept. But what if the entity in question is one whose very essence, by definition, includes modal status? What if the object is such that, if it is possible at all, it cannot fail to be actual?

Herein lies the hinge of Hartshorne’s reconstruction, which he formalizes by appealing to Anselm’s second formulation of the supreme being in *Proslogion* 3. Anselm argues that an entity which can be thought of as not existing is inferior to an entity which cannot be thought of as not existing—that is, an entity whose non-existence is logically impossible. Hartshorne formalizes this intuition using S5 modal logic. Let $G$ represent the divine nature: a being than which no greater can be conceived, possessing unsurpassable perfection. 

In modal terms, perfection entails necessity. If perfection is contingent, it is surpassed by a perfection that is necessary, for a being that exists in all possible worlds is inherently greater than one that exists in only some. Therefore, the formula for unsurpassable greatness ($G$) implies that if $G$ exists in any possible world, it exists in all possible worlds ($◇G \supset \square G$). 

Expressed within the S5 modal system—where necessity ($ \square $) and possibility ($ ◇ $) are mutually entailing across interchangeable worlds—the argument proceeds with relentless formal rigor:
1. $◇G \supset \square G$ (If it is possible that a necessary supreme being exists, then it is necessary that a necessary supreme being exists.)
2. $◇G$ (It is possible that a necessary supreme being exists.)
3. Therefore, $\square G$ (It is necessary that a necessary supreme being exists.)
4. Therefore, $G$ (Therefore, a necessary supreme being exists, since what is necessary is actual.)

Faced with this modal machinery, how does Kant’s critique fare? Hartshorne argues that Kant’s prohibition—"existence is not a predicate"—misses the target when applied to *necessary existence*. Necessary existence is not a mere positing or an ordinary attribute; it is a modal status entailed by the very meaning of unsurpassable perfection. To say that God exists necessarily is not to say that "God + existence" is greater than "God alone," but rather to say that the concept of God is the concept of a reality whose non-existence is intrinsically absurd, a self-contradiction. Kant’s critique assumes that all existence is accidental, contingent upon external conditions of intuition. But if there is a reality whose essence is its own ground of being—a *causa sui*—then modal coextensiveness renders the distinction between essence and existence obsolete for that unique entity.

## II. Actus Purus and Modal Coextensiveness: Resolving Classical Internal Contradictions

To evaluate whether Hartshorne’s modal version of the ontological argument succeeds, we must examine its metaphysical underpinnings, particularly its critique of classical theism’s concept of *actus purus* (pure actuality) and its reliance on "modal coextensiveness." 

Classical Thomistic and Aristotelian metaphysics posits God as *actus purus*—a being devoid of any potentiality, utterly immutable, impassible, and complete. In this classical framework, God’s essence *is* His existence (*ipsum esse subsistens*). However, process theologians like Hartshorne argue that classical theism falls into a profound internal contradiction when it attempts to reconcile *actus purus* with divine relationality, responsiveness, and supreme perfection. 

The contradiction runs as follows: If God is pure act, static and unchanging, then God cannot be affected by the world. An impassible being cannot experience suffering, joy, or responsiveness to the creatures it has made. Yet, classical theism simultaneously wishes to affirm that God is unsurpassably great, that God loves the world, and that human actions genuinely matter to God. But how can a being for whom nothing new can occur—who possesses no potentiality to receive novel determinations from creaturely interaction—be truly related to a dynamic, evolving creation? A being that cannot change, cannot acquire new relations, and cannot be acted upon is, from a relational standpoint, deficient. It is unresponsive, inert, and dead to the living pulse of reality.

Hartshorne resolves this contradiction by revising the very concept of perfection through the lens of dipolar theism and modal coextensiveness. For Hartshorne, perfection cannot be cast merely in terms of static immutability. Instead, unsurpassable greatness requires a synthesis of active and passive capacities: a concrete, evolving pole that interacts with the world, and an abstract, immutable pole that remains identical across all possible configurations of reality.

Modal coextensiveness is the logical linchpin of this revision. In classical logic, coextensiveness refers to two predicates or sets that apply to the exact same objects. In Hartshorne’s modal theology, modal coextensiveness denotes the structural identity between logical possibility and logical necessity when applied to the abstract divine essence. Hartshorne asserts that the abstract concept of God (the divine nature, or the generic property of being supreme) and the concrete instantiation of God must be understood through a dipolar matrix. 

In this matrix, God’s *abstract essence*—that God is love, that God is the supreme creator, that God possesses unsurpassable excellence—is invariant across all possible worlds. It is logically necessary. However, God’s *concrete actuality*—the specific contents of divine consciousness at any given moment, enriched by the free choices of creatures—is contingent, growing, and unsurpassably unsurpassable *in relation to what is actual*. 

By introducing modal coextensiveness, Hartshorne attempts to heal the breach in classical theism between necessity and relativity. In classical theism, to say God is necessary meant God had no internal relation to contingency; contingency was a secondary, defective mode of being. Hartshorne argues the exact opposite: necessity and contingency are not mutually exclusive opposites, but structural dimensions of a single, supremely perfect reality. God’s abstract necessity entails that *some* concrete state of divine experience will always exist, while the specific filling of that divine experience is coextensive with the totality of the contingent universe.

Does this modal coextensiveness successfully resolve the internal contradictions of *actus purus*? It certainly escapes the paralysis of the Aristotelian Prime Mover, who "thinks only on itself" and remains utterly indifferent to the cosmos. By framing perfection as *dipolar*—combining absolute abstract necessity with relative concrete contingency—Hartshorne makes room for divine love, responsiveness, and moral relationality. 

Yet, critics from both classical and analytic traditions argue that Hartshorne introduces a new set of contradictions. If God’s concrete state is constantly growing, changing, and being enriched by the world, then God is imperfect *prior* to receiving these creaturely inputs. A growing perfection is, by definition, a perfection that was previously incomplete. Does this not violate the Anselmian definition of "that than which nothing greater can be conceived"? If God can become greater tomorrow than God is today through the addition of creaturely experiences, then today’s God is surpassed by tomorrow’s God, rendering the divine reality finite, mutable, and temporally bound. Hartshorne’s defense—that God’s capacity to incorporate all realities makes God unsurpassable *at every moment*—relies on redefining unsurpassability not as static completion, but as unsurpassable receptivity. Whether this redefinition preserves the integrity of classical theism or abandons it for a panentheistic process cosmology remains the central fault line in the debate.

## III. The Central Assumption: The Possibility of Divine Existence

We must now confront the most vulnerable structural pillar of Hartshorne’s modal ontological argument: its central assumption, namely, that the existence of a necessary supreme being is *possible* ($◇G$).

In the S5 modal schema deployed by Hartshorne and later analytic defenders of the ontological argument (such as Alvin Plantinga), the deductive validity of the argument is ironclad. Once you grant the premise $◇G$ (It is possible that a necessary being exists), the axioms of S5 force the conclusion $\square G$ (It is necessary that a necessary being exists) and consequently $G$ (God exists). The entire weight of the argument, therefore, rests upon the epistemic and ontological justification of premise two. 

Why should anyone grant that a necessary supreme being is possible? 

To critics, this premise is a covert petitio principii—a begging of the question. To assert that a necessary being is possible is already to attribute to the concept of God an ontological status that transcends mere fiction. In standard modal logic, for a proposition to be possible ($◇P$), it must be free from internal contradiction. We can easily establish the possibility of a golden mountain because the concepts "mountain" and "made of gold" do not logically contradict one another, even though no such mountain exists in actuality. 

However, when applied to a *necessary* being, the modal status of possibility becomes exceptionally murky. If a necessary being exists, it exists in *all* possible worlds. If it does not exist, its non-existence is necessary; it cannot exist in *any* possible world (since necessity means truth across all worlds, and if a necessary proposition is false in one world, it is false in all). Therefore, when we ask whether a necessary supreme being is possible, we are asking: *Is the proposition "A necessary supreme being exists" coherent, and does it inhabit at least one possible world?*

If the divine nature is even *possible*, it is actual. But if it is *impossible* (i.e., if the concept of unsurpassable perfection contains a hidden contradiction), then it is impossible in *all* possible worlds, and God does not exist. Thus, the modal status of God is binary and extreme: God either exists in all possible worlds or in none. There is no middle ground of mere contingency.

Given this binarism, how does Hartshorne defend the premise $◇G$? Hartshorne argues that a concept is presumed possible unless it can be proven to be contradictory. The burden of proof rests upon the atheist to demonstrate that the concept of "a being than which no greater can be conceived" is intrinsically incoherent—that "perfection" is a self-contradictory notion, akin to a "square circle" or a "married bachelor." 

Yet, this defensive maneuver encounters fierce resistance from opponents of the argument. Atheists and agnostics argue that the concept of an unsurpassable perfection *is* fraught with internal contradictions. For instance, can a being possess all perfections simultaneously without logical friction? Can a being be both impassible (unchanging) and passionately responsive to the sufferings of the world (the exact tension Hartshorne tried to resolve through dipolarity)? Can a being possess infinite moral goodness while permitting gratuitous evil? If these traditional and process attributes generate genuine conceptual incoherence, then the concept of God is logically impossible, and $◇G$ is false.

Hartshorne’s response to this challenge requires us to examine his broader metaphysical framework: the distinction between abstract being and concrete becoming. It is here that Hartshorne attempts to provide the ontological cement that bridges the gap between the *a priori* concept of perfection and the necessary existence of a living reality.

## IV. Abstract Being, Concrete Becoming, and the Ontological Bridge

To understand how Hartshorne secures the bridge between *a priori* thought and extramental reality, we must analyze his utilization of the polarity between **abstract being** and **concrete becoming**. This distinction is the engine room of process philosophy, designed to overcome the static dualisms of classical metaphysics without collapsing into radical Heraclitean flux.

In Hartshorne’s ontology, every actual entity (and supremely, God) is a dipolar unity of abstract essence and concrete actuality. 
* **Abstract Being** refers to the structural identity, the generic character, or the "whatness" of the entity. For God, the abstract essence is the property of being *divine*—the unsurpassable synthesis of power, love, wisdom, and creativity. This abstract essence is eternal, immutable, and necessary. It does not change because it is a general characterization that abstracts from the specific temporal states in which it is embodied.
* **Concrete Becoming** refers to the particular, temporal, and relational filling of that abstract structure. At every moment, God experiences the concrete world; God absorbs the novel achievements, sufferings, and joys of every creaturely occasion. This concrete state is perpetually growing, cumulative, and contingent.

How does this polarity solve the problem of the ontological argument? In traditional philosophy, the ontological argument falters because it tries to bridge the gap between an abstract concept (existing only in the mind) and a concrete individual (existing in reality) through sheer conceptual fiat. Kant rightly observed that no amount of analyzing the concept of a hundred dollars will produce a single physical dollar in your wallet, because concepts are abstract and general, whereas existence is concrete and particular.

Hartshorne argues that his modal ontological argument bypasses the Kantian interdict precisely because the divine reality is *such* that its abstract essence logically entails its concrete instantiation. For ordinary objects, abstract essence and concrete existence are radically distinct. The abstract concept of "doghood" does not entail that any particular dog must exist; dogs are contingent, generated by biological processes, and liable to extinction. 

However, God is the unique exception to this rule—the *ens necessarium*. For God, the abstract essence is so structured that it cannot be exemplified in *any* possible world without possessing a corresponding concrete actuality. Why? Because a God who exists only as an abstract possibility or an empty conceptual schema is not unsurpassable. Unsurpassable perfection requires that the abstract essence of divinity be eternally wedded to an ever-growing concrete experience of reality. 

Thus, potentiality and actuality are harmonized in the divine nature. The potentiality for divine experience is eternal and necessary, while the actualized content of that experience is perpetually enriched by the creative advance of the universe. Potentiality does not merely bridge the gap between thought and reality; rather, divine potentiality *is* the structural matrix of reality itself. Because God’s abstract nature is to be the supreme recipient and synthesizer of all actualities, God’s possibility is inextricably bound to God’s necessity. To think of God as a mere possibility without actual instantiation is to misunderstand the very meaning of "perfection," which, unlike any finite concept, includes its own existential ground.

## V. Critical Evaluation: Does Hartshorne’s Argument Succeed?

We have traced the architecture of Hartshorne’s modal ontological argument through its engagement with Kant, its reconstruction of *actus purus* via modal coextensiveness, its reliance on the assumption of divine possibility, and its grounding in the polarity of abstract being and concrete becoming. We must now render a critical philosophical judgment: Does Hartshorne successfully rehabilitate the ontological argument, or does the argument ultimately founder on its own internal presuppositions?

### 1. The Triumph of Modal Rigor
On a purely formal level, Hartshorne’s modalization of Anselm’s argument is an analytical triumph. By shifting the debate from categorical existence ("God exists") to modal necessity ("If God is possible, God is necessary"), Hartshorne successfully shields the argument from the blunt instrument of Kant’s critique. Kant proved that existence is not a real predicate for contingent entities. But modal logic demonstrates that *necessary* existence is not a standard predicate at all; it is a structural entailment of modal status. If one grants the S5 axiom system—which is widely accepted in contemporary modal logic—the inference from $◇G$ to $\square G$ is deductively airtight. Critics of the ontological argument can no longer dismiss it simply by chanting Kant’s mantra; they must engage directly with the metaphysics of modality.

### 2. The Vulnerability of the Possibility Premise ($◇G$)
Nevertheless, the formal validity of the argument masks a deep material vulnerability. The entire argumentative burden rests upon premise two: the assertion that a necessary supreme being is possible. 

Here, Hartshorne’s argument faces a formidable dilemma. If the premise $◇G$ is established *a priori* by appealing to the intuitive clarity of the concept of perfection, critics can rightly charge that conceptual clarity does not guarantee ontological possibility. The history of philosophy is littered with concepts that seemed perfectly coherent to their inventors—such as absolute space, simultaneous action at a distance, or the caloric fluid—yet turned out to be conceptually incoherent or empirically empty. 

Conversely, if $◇G$ is to be defended by pointing to the concrete richness of the universe, process theology risks turning an *a priori* ontological argument into an *a posteriori* cosmological or teleological argument. If we must examine the structure of the world, the presence of creativity, and the organization of experience to infer that a supreme dipolar reality is possible, then the argument no longer rests on pure reason alone. It becomes dependent upon empirical observations about the nature of becoming, thereby forfeiting the pristine *a priori* status that made Anselm and Descartes’ arguments so intoxicating to rationalist philosophy.

### 3. The Theological Price of Dipolarity
Furthermore, we must evaluate whether Hartshorne’s dipolar resolution of *actus purus* preserves the God of religious devotion or substitutes a metaphysical abstraction. Classical theism posited God as immutable, impassible, and complete precisely because it sought to secure a transcendent anchor for human existence—a reality untouched by the tragedies, decay, and chaos of temporal flux. 

Hartshorne’s God, by contrast, is radically interdependent with the creation. God changes with every passing moment; God is enriched by our virtues and wounded by our sins. While this renders God deeply relational and morally compelling, it introduces profound theological anxieties. If God is perpetually changing and growing, is God truly the ultimate anchor of existence, or is God merely the supreme participant in a universal cosmic process? If God’s concrete actuality is dependent upon the free choices of finite creatures, then God’s perfection is vulnerable to creaturely failure. For many theologians, a God who depends upon the world for the filling of divine experience ceases to be the sovereign creator and becomes instead the cosmos's most sensitive inmate.

---

## Conclusion: The Enduring Legacy of the Modal Ontological Project

Charles Hartshorne’s revision of the ontological argument stands as one of the most audacious and sophisticated metaphysical achievements of the twentieth century. Confronted with the Kantian interdict against treating existence as a predicate, Hartshorne did not retreat into fideism or abandon rational theology. Instead, he weaponized modal logic, re-engineered Anselm’s neglected second formulation, and forged a dipolar metaphysics that sought to reconcile divine necessity with creaturely freedom.

Does the argument successfully prove the existence of God? In the strict sense of compelling universal assent, the answer must remain no. The modal ontological argument remains hostage to its foundational premise: the unproven, and perhaps unprovable, assumption that unsurpassable perfection is a logically coherent possibility. One person's conceptual coherence is another's internal contradiction.

Yet, to measure the value of Hartshorne’s argument solely by its success as an undeniable logical proof is to misunderstand its profound philosophical contribution. Hartshorne demonstrated that the ontological argument is not a naive linguistic trick, but a profound meditation on the architecture of reality. By forcing us to grapple with modal coextensiveness, the nature of necessity, and the intricate dance between abstract being and concrete becoming, Hartshorne rescued rational theology from static dogmatism and placed it in dynamic conversation with modern logic and process philosophy. Whether or not we are compelled to utter the final modal conclusion, Hartshorne’s revision ensures that the question of perfection remains eternally inescapable for human thought.
