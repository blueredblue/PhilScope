# Dignity, Successive Selves, and the Morality of Self-Affecting Risk

The morality of imprudent self-affecting conduct presents a distinctive problem for moral philosophy. It is easy to understand why smoking, dangerous drinking, or reckless driving might be morally objectionable when they expose other people to danger. It is more difficult to explain why such conduct should be wrong when its principal victim is the agent herself. A person who knowingly smokes may impose medical costs on others, distress on family members, or risks through second-hand smoke. Those consequences generate familiar interpersonal objections. But suppose that the conduct is private, that the smoker bears all the costs, and that she is competent, informed, and uncoerced. What, if anything, makes the conduct morally wrong rather than merely imprudent?

J. David Velleman’s Kantian account of the dignity of personhood and Derek Parfit’s reductionist account of personal identity offer two powerful but different answers. Velleman emphasizes the special status of persons as beings whose rational agency gives them dignity rather than a market price. On his view, certain actions are wrong because they treat one’s own person as something that may be exchanged or sacrificed for lesser benefits. Parfit, by contrast, challenges the assumption that personal identity is a deep, all-or-nothing relation extending straightforwardly through time. His model of successive selves suggests that the relation between a present person and a future person may be sufficiently weak that exposing one’s future self to serious harm resembles imposing a risk on another human being.

Both views illuminate important features of self-regarding conduct. Velleman explains why some forms of self-destruction can seem not merely foolish but degrading. Parfit explains why the fact that a future victim is “me” need not settle the moral question. Yet neither theory, in its strongest form, successfully establishes that ordinary imprudence such as smoking is morally wrong. Velleman’s argument risks converting the dignity of agency into a sweeping prohibition on self-sacrifice and self-endangerment. Parfit’s argument risks making the present self excessively constrained by the interests of temporally successive persons. Both can be made more plausible if they are interpreted as supplying stringent reasons rather than absolute prohibitions, and if they recognize consent, proportionality, personal authority, and the value of present agency. But those qualifications also reveal that neither framework, by itself, derives a complete moral condemnation of smoking.

## I. The special problem of self-regarding wrongs

A theory that condemns smoking as immoral must distinguish moral wrongness from imprudence. Smoking is normally bad for the smoker: it increases the probability of disease, disability, and premature death. But “bad for someone” does not automatically mean “wrong.” People are permitted to make many choices that predictably diminish their health. They may eat unhealthy food, decline exercise, engage in physically dangerous sports, work excessive hours, or spend money in ways that leave them less secure in old age. Even when a choice is irrational by the agent’s own long-term standards, it does not follow that the state, family, or morality may prohibit it.

This distinction is central to the issue of autonomy. An autonomous person is not merely a location at which benefits and burdens are distributed. She is also the author of a life, capable of deciding which risks, pleasures, projects, and sacrifices are worth undertaking. A moral theory that condemns every imprudent choice would fail to respect the agent as an agent. It would treat her as a dependent child whose welfare must be managed according to an external standard.

At the same time, autonomy cannot be the whole story. A person may voluntarily sell herself into slavery, deliberately mutilate herself for money, or kill herself to avoid a temporary inconvenience. In such cases, many people judge that something more than poor decision-making has occurred. The person has not merely chosen a low-value outcome; she has treated herself, or some aspect of herself, as disposable. The question is how to explain that judgment without implying that every sacrifice of health or longevity is immoral.

Velleman and Parfit approach this problem by challenging different assumptions. Velleman challenges the assumption that one may treat one’s own person as an ordinary object of exchange. Parfit challenges the assumption that the present and future self stand in the morally privileged relation implied by strict personal identity. Their theories therefore move in opposite directions. Velleman stresses the unity and special value of personhood, whereas Parfit stresses the separability of the temporal stages that make up a life.

## II. Velleman: dignity and the impermissibility of trading the person

The Kantian starting point is the distinction between price and dignity. Things that have a price may be exchanged for equivalent or greater benefits. Persons, however, possess dignity: a value that is not reducible to their usefulness, desirability, or exchange value. A person is not simply one more item that may be sacrificed whenever the expected benefits are sufficiently large. The reason is not that a person is infinitely valuable in the same quantitative sense that a very expensive object is valuable. Rather, persons are the sources of practical judgment and valuation. They are the beings who can set ends, assess reasons, and regard anything else as valuable.

Velleman’s reconstruction of the Kantian position focuses on this relationship between rational agency and value. The capacity to deliberate is not merely one possession among others. It is the capacity through which possessions, pleasures, achievements, and even life itself are assigned significance. If a person treats her own rational agency as a commodity that can be exchanged for advantages, she appears to place the condition of valuing on the same plane as the things valued. That is a category mistake. The agent cannot coherently regard herself as having an ordinary price because she is the standpoint from which prices and purposes are established.

This helps explain the moral objection to certain cases of self-sacrifice. Suppose a person kills herself to obtain money for her family, to escape a relatively minor embarrassment, or to secure some other benefit that could have been pursued by less extreme means. The objection is not simply that death is worse than the benefit. It is that the person has treated her own person as a means of production—as an asset to be liquidated for an external return. She has “traded” herself for something that, according to the Kantian picture, should be subordinate to the dignity of the person.

The mechanism of Velleman’s argument can be reconstructed in several steps.

First, rational agency is the ground of the values that make ordinary benefits worth pursuing. Food, money, pleasure, reputation, and health matter within a life directed by an agent.

Second, because agency is the source or condition of such valuation, it cannot be assigned a price in the same way as those benefits. To ask how much money would compensate for the destruction of one’s person is not simply to conduct a difficult calculation. It is to treat the source of valuation as though it were one of the objects whose value is calculated.

Third, an act that intentionally sacrifices the person for a lesser benefit expresses an attitude of commodification. The agent acts as though her person were an instrument that could be exchanged for an outcome. The act is therefore not merely imprudent. It fails to acknowledge the dignity of the person who is doing the choosing.

Fourth, this prohibition may extend beyond suicide. If it is wrong to trade one’s whole person for a benefit, it may also be wrong to sacrifice essential capacities, bodily integrity, or the conditions of agency for trivial gains. One might thereby treat the person as a resource to be consumed rather than as an end whose authority sets limits on permissible exchange.

This argument has genuine force. It explains why there is something morally troubling about the thought, “I will destroy myself because the immediate payoff is worth it.” It also avoids the implausible suggestion that a sufficiently large reward could justify anything. If dignity is reducible to welfare, then perhaps a person could permissibly sell herself into any condition so long as the expected benefits were high enough. The Kantian view preserves an important moral boundary around the person.

Nevertheless, the application to smoking is not straightforward. Smoking does not ordinarily involve an explicit decision to exchange one’s person for a benefit. The smoker typically seeks relaxation, pleasure, concentration, social belonging, or relief from anxiety. She does not normally reason: “I will surrender my future health and agency in exchange for this cigarette.” The practice is gradual, probabilistic, and often addictive. It involves a series of choices whose cumulative effects are serious, but each individual act need not be understood as a transaction in which the person is deliberately sold.

Velleman might reply that explicit intention is unnecessary. A person may treat herself as a commodity through patterns of action even if she does not formulate the principle consciously. If she repeatedly sacrifices health, longevity, and bodily integrity for momentary gratification, she may be living according to a principle that gives trivial benefits authority over the conditions of personhood. In that sense, smoking might express the view that one’s future agency may be traded for present pleasure.

But this extension creates difficulty. Nearly every valuable activity involves some sacrifice of future condition for present goods. A person postpones sleep to finish a book, accepts physical injury in athletic competition, takes a demanding job that damages health, or spends savings that would otherwise support her in old age. Even moderate consumption of alcohol or sugar may be defended by appeal to enjoyment. If any deliberate acceptance of health risks treats the person as a tradable resource, then Velleman’s theory becomes excessively restrictive.

The distinction between constitutive and incidental threats to personhood may help. To destroy or permanently incapacitate oneself for a trivial benefit is plausibly to treat the person as a means. By contrast, accepting a limited risk or temporary impairment for a worthwhile activity need not deny the dignity of agency. The issue is not whether the agent incurs any cost, but whether she treats the basic conditions of personhood as disposable and whether the benefit is proportionate to the sacrifice.

That distinction, however, must be supplied by the theory rather than simply read off from the concept of dignity. Kantian language can support a strong prohibition, but it does not by itself determine the threshold. If dignity means that a person may never intentionally diminish her health for pleasure, then the view is implausible. If it means that one may not reduce one’s entire person to an exchangeable instrument, then it may be true but too weak to establish that smoking is wrong. Smoking falls between these poles: it is not normally an act of total self-liquidation, but it may be an entrenched practice that subordinates long-term agency to short-term gratification.

## III. Parfit: reductionism and the moral status of future selves

Parfit’s theory begins from a different challenge. We ordinarily assume that personal identity is a deep relation: either the future individual is literally the same person as the present individual or she is not. Parfit argues that this assumption exaggerates the importance of identity. What matters in survival may be psychological continuity and connectedness—relations such as memory, intention, character, and the continuation of projects—rather than the existence of an additional metaphysical fact of identity.

On this “reductionist” view, a person is not a separately existing entity that remains wholly present through time. There are successive stages or selves connected by varying degrees of psychological relation. In ordinary cases the relation is strong enough for practical purposes: I have reason to care about my future because my memories, plans, values, and consciousness will continue. But the relation may weaken over time or in cases of radical psychological change. The future person who suffers from a disease caused by present conduct may be psychologically continuous with the present person, but she is not related to the present person in a way that automatically gives the present person an unlimited right to impose burdens on her.

Parfit’s model transforms the question of self-regarding harm. If the future self is not metaphysically identical with the present self in the morally decisive sense, then a present person’s decision to smoke may resemble a decision to impose a health risk on another person. The future smoker may be a later person with her own experiences, interests, and standpoint. She will suffer the pain of cancer or respiratory disease when the present smoker no longer exists in the same psychological condition. The present self benefits from smoking, while a later self bears much of the cost.

The argument can again be reconstructed in stages.

First, the moral importance of a relationship should depend on what that relationship consists in, not simply on the label “same person.” Psychological continuity and connectedness matter because they ground anticipation, memory, concern, and the transmission of projects. But they come in degrees.

Second, the relation between present and future selves can therefore be weaker than the ordinary concept of identity suggests. The present self may have less psychological connection to a distant future self than to a close friend or family member.

Third, if it would be wrong to impose a serious, uncompensated risk on another person for a trivial present benefit, then the weakening of psychological connectedness removes the obvious reason for treating the same act as morally innocent when the victim is one’s future self.

Fourth, the present person’s special authority over her future should not be assumed. If future persons are distinct subjects of welfare, then the present self may have obligations to them analogous to obligations owed to other people. A person who smokes is not merely making herself unhealthy; she is affecting the conditions under which a later person will live.

Parfit’s argument is particularly powerful against a simplistic appeal to prudential unity. One might say, “The future smoker is me, so I am free to choose for her.” Parfit replies that the word “me” may conceal the real moral structure. The future individual will experience the harm; the present individual receives the pleasure. The relation between them may be sufficiently attenuated that the present self cannot invoke identity as a blanket justification.

This account also clarifies why time does not automatically erase responsibility. If I knowingly place a harmful burden on another person ten years from now, the delay does not make the act harmless. Likewise, the fact that the victim of present smoking will exist only later does not eliminate the possibility of harm. The present act can be a cause of a future loss even if the future subject is not currently present.

Yet Parfit’s analogy between future selves and other people has limits. The present and future selves are not strangers. The current person has an unusually intimate relation to the future individual: she makes the future person possible, controls many of the conditions of her life, and may possess detailed knowledge of her values and plans. More importantly, the future self is not merely a passive recipient of burdens. She is the anticipated continuation of the present agent’s projects. Decisions made now are part of the process by which a single practical life is constructed, even if no deep metaphysical identity unifies its stages.

The danger is that Parfit’s theory may give each temporal stage a veto over the others. If the present self is one person and the future self another, then the present self may appear morally prohibited from choosing anything that creates a substantial future risk. But ordinary agency requires the ability to make decisions whose costs and benefits are distributed across time. Saving for retirement, studying for a future career, undergoing painful medical treatment, and accepting present danger for future security all involve one stage of a life acting for another. The asymmetry is not simply exploitation; it is also cooperation across time.

Parfit’s own framework can recognize this point. Psychological connectedness may justify special concern even if it does not justify absolute identity. A future self strongly connected to the present may be treated as a participant in a continuing project. Moreover, moral permissions can allow risk imposition when the person exposed to risk has authorized it or when the risk is proportionate to a benefit. The theory does not have to treat every temporal transfer as a case of wrongful harm.

Still, if Parfit’s argument is interpreted as saying that any serious risk to a future self is morally equivalent to a comparable risk imposed on another person, smoking appears difficult to defend. The smoker knows that a later individual will bear the health costs. The later individual cannot withdraw from the risk once the addiction or disease has developed. In that respect, smoking resembles imposing a risk on a nonconsenting person for the sake of a relatively minor present advantage.

## IV. The objection from excessive restrictiveness

The central objection to both theories is that they seem to entail too many moral prohibitions. If Velleman is right that one may not trade one’s person for benefits, then why should one be allowed to consume alcohol, eat unhealthy food, engage in dangerous sports, or take a job that predictably shortens one’s life? If Parfit is right that a future self is morally comparable to another person, why may the present self impose any significant risk on her future successor?

This objection is not merely a complaint about inconvenience. It concerns the rightful role of the present self. A human life is temporally extended, and the present self must make choices under conditions of uncertainty. It cannot preserve every future option, avoid every risk, or guarantee that its successors will approve of its decisions. A theory that requires the present self to maximize the welfare of future selves may erase the practical authority of the person who is currently deliberating.

The objection can be stated in three forms.

First, there is a problem of threshold. Health risks are continuous. Smoking carries a high risk of serious harm, but so do many less stigmatized activities. If morality condemns smoking because it risks future health, it needs a principled way to distinguish smoking from ordinary indulgence. The distinction cannot simply be “large risks are wrong,” because many permissible activities involve large risks. Nor can it be “unnecessary risks are wrong,” because much of ordinary life is not strictly necessary.

Second, there is a problem of aggregation. A single cigarette may create a small risk, but repeated smoking creates a substantial one. If each act is evaluated independently, the moral wrongness may seem too weak to explain the serious judgment against the practice. If the pattern is evaluated as a whole, however, many other lifestyle choices may become condemnable. The same concern applies to drinking, overeating, sleep deprivation, and sedentary living.

Third, there is a problem of authority. The future self may have interests, but the present self has a legitimate claim to decide how a life is lived. A future person who would prefer to be healthy cannot automatically veto every present pleasure. The present self may value spontaneity, social connection, relief from stress, or immediate enjoyment more than the future self would. To deny this authority is to turn morality into a form of temporal paternalism.

Velleman’s answer might be that authority itself is bounded by dignity. The present self is not entitled to dispose of the person merely because she currently occupies the decision-making position. The future self is also a person, and the present self cannot use her as a means. This response blocks the thought that temporal priority gives unlimited control.

But it risks an unstable conclusion. If the future self’s dignity restricts the present self whenever a serious health cost is foreseeable, then the present self is left with little room for self-direction. The Kantian principle of treating persons as ends would apply not only to interpersonal conduct but to every relation among the temporal parts of one life. Unless the theory recognizes a broad permission to accept reasonable risks, it undermines the autonomy it seeks to protect.

Parfit’s answer might be that the interests of the future self have moral weight because she is a distinct subject of experience. Present authority cannot be assumed merely from the fact that the future person descends from the present one. Yet the same argument can be turned against Parfit. If each future self has an independent claim against the present self, then the present person may be required to live for the benefit of people who do not yet exist as psychologically continuous agents. The moral demand becomes especially severe because the present self has no symmetrical power to demand sacrifices from the past.

The objection therefore points to a temporal version of distributive justice. The present self receives benefits and imposes burdens, but she is also the person who must make a decision under uncertainty. A sound theory must explain both why future harms matter and why present agency retains legitimate priority in some contexts.

## V. Can smoking be distinguished from ordinary risk?

The theories can avoid excessive restrictiveness only by introducing several qualifications.

The first is intentional structure. Velleman’s strongest case concerns conduct that deliberately annihilates or degrades the person for a benefit that is clearly subordinate to personhood. Smoking usually does not have that structure. The smoker seeks a benefit that may be psychologically significant even if it is not objectively weighty. The act is often habitual, addictive, or a response to stress. That does not make smoking permissible, but it weakens the claim that the smoker is expressing a principle of self-commodification.

Parfit’s framework also benefits from attention to intention. The moral status of a risk depends partly on what the agent is doing with it. A person who accepts a risk as an unavoidable side effect of pursuing an important project differs from a person who imposes the same risk solely for a trivial benefit. Smoking is often pursued for pleasure or relief, but those goods should not be dismissed as morally irrelevant. The question is whether the benefit is proportionate to the expected harm and whether less damaging means of obtaining it are reasonably available.

The second qualification is consent. The future self cannot provide contemporaneous consent, but the present self may be understood as authorizing risks across the course of a life. People ordinarily make long-term commitments on behalf of their future selves. They choose careers, relationships, medical treatments, financial plans, and forms of recreation without waiting for the future person’s explicit approval. This authorization is not unlimited, particularly where addiction impairs later freedom, but it gives the present self a legitimate role.

Smoking complicates consent because nicotine dependence can undermine future autonomy. The initial choice may be voluntary, while later choices are increasingly constrained. A person may thereby create a condition in which her future self is less able to revise the decision. This feature strengthens the moral objection: smoking is not merely a risk but potentially a self-reinforcing practice that narrows future agency. Yet even here, the objection is strongest against the mechanisms of addiction and loss of control, not against every isolated act of smoking.

The third qualification is proportionality. A moral theory can allow some self-affecting risks while condemning risks that are extreme, irreversible, or imposed for trivial reasons. Proportionality would distinguish moderate danger undertaken for meaningful benefits from severe danger undertaken for negligible ones. It would also consider the availability of alternatives. If a person can obtain the same benefit at minimal cost by another means, knowingly choosing a highly damaging method may be more objectionable.

Smoking generally performs poorly under this test. Its long-term risks are serious, its benefits are relatively limited, and less harmful means of relaxation or stimulation often exist. Nevertheless, proportionality supports a judgment of serious imprudence more readily than a judgment of strict moral wrongness. A person may reasonably regard immediate relief from anxiety as valuable, especially where social, economic, or psychological circumstances make alternatives inaccessible. The judgment should therefore remain sensitive to addiction and background conditions.

The fourth qualification is the distinction between harm and wrong. Parfit can show that smoking harms a future individual in the sense that it makes her worse off than she would otherwise have been. But showing that an act causes harm does not by itself show that it is wrong. Parents may impose painful vaccinations on children for their benefit; surgeons may cause injury to cure disease; present persons may impose burdens on future persons as part of mutually beneficial arrangements. Harm is morally significant, but its wrongness depends on consent, justification, proportionality, and alternatives.

Similarly, Velleman can show that some conduct expresses disregard for dignity without showing that every instance of self-endangerment is wrongful. Expression matters, but it must be interpreted carefully. The meaning of smoking may be self-neglect, addiction, social conformity, or deliberate defiance—not necessarily the commodification of personhood.

## VI. The rightful role of the present self

The deepest issue is whether the present self has a rightful role that neither dignity nor successive-selves theory should erase. The present self is not merely a temporary administrator for future beneficiaries. She is a subject with present interests, projects, relationships, and experiences. A life is not valuable only because it remains available for future use. Present enjoyment, relief, and meaning are part of what makes survival worth having.

This point does not entail that present desires always prevail. The present self may be shortsighted, addicted, depressed, or manipulated. A person’s current preference may fail to express her considered judgment. Nor does the present self have the right to impose burdens on other people. But in the self-affecting case, the present agent possesses a special kind of authority because the decision concerns the shape of her own life.

That authority can be understood without invoking a metaphysically deep personal identity. Even if the present and future selves are distinct stages, the present self is responsible for organizing the relationship among them. Psychological continuity provides a basis for treating the life as a project, while reductionism explains why the project contains genuine conflict among temporal interests. The appropriate conclusion is not that the present self is sovereign or that the future self has an absolute veto. It is that each stage has standing, and the present stage has the role of deliberating about their competing claims.

A model of temporal agency might therefore include three principles. First, future selves are not morally invisible. Their suffering and loss count, even when the present self receives the immediate benefit. Second, present agency is not morally irrelevant. The person currently deciding may permissibly accept risks and sacrifices as part of a life she has reason to value. Third, serious restrictions are justified when the present choice substantially destroys future agency, imposes extreme and irreversible burdens, or is made under conditions that compromise informed and voluntary judgment.

This model yields a differentiated assessment of smoking. Smoking is morally criticizable because it involves foreseeable harm to a future self, often for modest benefits, and because addiction can impair later autonomy. Parfit helps explain why the future victim’s status cannot be dismissed by saying, “She is just me.” Velleman helps explain why the practice may represent a failure to respect the value of one’s own agency when it becomes a settled willingness to sacrifice health and independence for trivial gratification.

But the theories do not establish that every act of smoking is morally wrong in the same way that assault or deception is wrong. Their arguments establish, at most, that smoking is presumptively imprudent and potentially morally objectionable under certain conditions: when the risks are substantial, the benefits slight, the choice uninformed or compulsive, and the practice destructive of future agency. They do not justify a general prohibition on all self-affecting health risks. A competent adult may retain the right to make choices that are risky, eccentric, or contrary to long-term welfare.

## VII. Evaluation

Velleman’s chief strength is its account of why self-destruction can be morally degrading even when it is voluntary. It recognizes that the person is not merely a bearer of welfare and that rational agency has a status that cannot be captured by cost-benefit calculation. The theory is particularly persuasive in cases where an agent treats her life or bodily integrity as disposable for a trivial or external reward. It preserves a norm of self-respect that liberal autonomy theories can sometimes neglect.

Its weakness is indeterminacy and overextension. The idea that one may not trade one’s person for benefits does not readily classify gradual, probabilistic, and addictive practices. If interpreted broadly, it condemns too much; if interpreted narrowly, it does not reach smoking. The theory must distinguish the destruction of personhood from the ordinary acceptance of bodily costs, but the principle of dignity alone does not provide a precise boundary.

Parfit’s strength is its exposure of the moral significance of temporal distance and psychological discontinuity. It prevents the present self from using personal identity as a rhetorical device to evade responsibility for future suffering. The future self is a subject who can be harmed, and the present self’s special relationship to her does not make her interests disappear. This is a powerful correction to the idea that all self-regarding choices are morally exempt.

Its weakness is the threat to temporal autonomy. If the future self is treated too much like another person, present choices become vulnerable to an indefinite series of claims from later stages of the life. The result is not simply moral seriousness but possible paralysis. The present self must be allowed to undertake risks, pursue present goods, and shape the life whose future stages Parfit asks us to respect. Reductionism shows that future selves matter; it does not show that they possess unrestricted authority over the present.

Neither position, then, fully establishes that smoking is morally wrong. Velleman provides a principled condemnation of treating oneself as a mere means, but ordinary smoking is not necessarily such a transaction. Parfit provides a principled account of why smoking can harm a future self in a morally relevant sense, but harm alone does not establish wrongness, and the analogy with interpersonal harm must be limited by authorization and proportionality.

The most defensible conclusion is intermediate. Smoking is not morally innocent simply because it is self-affecting, but neither is it automatically a violation of dignity or a wrongful assault on another person. It is a practice that can be morally defective when it reflects disregard for future agency, involves severe and avoidable risks, or is sustained by manipulation and addiction. Yet moral condemnation should not become a license for coercive paternalism or a denial of the agent’s authority over the form of her own life.

## Conclusion

Velleman and Parfit illuminate two sides of the moral problem of imprudence. Velleman emphasizes the dignity of the person as the source of value and argues that one cannot permissibly trade that person for lesser benefits. Parfit emphasizes the separability of successive selves and argues that a future self may be sufficiently distinct that imposing serious risk on her resembles imposing risk on another person. In different ways, both theories challenge the comforting thought that self-regarding action is automatically morally permissible.

Yet both face the same fundamental danger: they can make the present self too subordinate to a moralized conception of future personhood. Velleman may condemn ordinary self-sacrifice as commodification; Parfit may treat ordinary temporal decision-making as harmful imposition. Without qualifications, both theories generate overly restrictive conclusions about everyday health risks and threaten the autonomy they seek to defend.

A more plausible view preserves their insights while limiting their reach. Future selves count, and their suffering cannot be dismissed. Personhood has dignity, and one should not treat one’s life as a disposable instrument. But the present self also has rightful authority to pursue present goods, accept proportionate risks, and organize a life across time. Smoking can therefore be judged seriously imprudent and, under particular conditions, morally objectionable. What neither Velleman nor Parfit conclusively demonstrates is that every imprudent self-affecting act is morally wrong. The proper lesson is not that autonomy defeats all duties to the future, nor that the future automatically defeats present freedom. It is that self-respect and temporal responsibility must be balanced within a conception of agency that treats the life as both a continuing project and a succession of genuinely valuable selves.
