# The Architecture of the In-Between: Beyond Sartrean Objectification Toward Mutual Transcendence in Interpersonal Communication

## Introduction: The Ontological Predicament of the Interpersonal

The trajectory of modern existential and phenomenological thought is haunted by an originary fracture: the entry of the Other threatens the integrity of the solitary ego. In *Being and Nothingness*, Jean-Paul Sartre diagnosed this encounter not as a communion of harmonious consciousnesses, but as an inescapable ontological conflict. For Sartre, the look (*le regard*) of the Other petrifies my subjectivity, turning me from a center of pure transcendence into a fixed, spatialized object. In response, I attempt to recapture my lost freedom by turning the Other into an object in turn. Mutual objectification becomes the foundational matrix of social reality; communication, under this grim horizon, is reduced to a dialectic of shame, domination, and failed synthesis. 

Yet, to rest within the Sartrean cul-de-sac is to render genuine interpersonal communication an ontological impossibility. If the Other is perpetually either my executioner or my slave, the ethical demand for authentic relation collapses into an endless power struggle. This essay evaluates the claim that person-to-person communication requires moving beyond Sartre’s model of mutual objectification toward a paradigm of *mutual transcendence*. By synthesizing the theological-existential insights of Paul Tillich—specifically his concept of indeterminacy within the subject-subject relationship—with Luce Irigaray’s radical ontology of sexual difference and "the two," I will construct a framework for an ethics of dialogue that avoids both totalizing assimilation and radical, unbridgeable alienation. 

This journey demands an interrogation of the dialectic between the static and the dynamic. How does the self balance the necessary, anticipatory objectification of the Other-as-object with the ethical demand to recognize the Other-as-subject? I will argue that genuine conversation requires a paradoxical movement: we must provisionally prepare for interaction through conceptual approximations—a structural necessity of the finite ego—only to radically relinquish these static forms in the crucibles of actual dialogue. Ultimately, I will resolve that authentic interpersonal communication successfully replaces the foundational Sartrean drive toward domination with a dynamic, risky anticipation of mutual transformation.

---

## Part I: The Sartrean Impasse – The Look, Objectification, and the Death of Communication

To move beyond Sartre, we must first inhabit the terrifying clarity of his diagnosis. In Sartre's phenomenological schema, consciousness is defined by its *pour-soi* (for-itself)—a nihilating nothingness that disrupts the dense, static plenum of the *en-soi* (in-itself). My primordial relation to the world is one of mastery and transcendence; I organize objects around my projects. However, the sudden appearance of the Other introduces a cataclysmic rupture: the "Look." 

When I am caught in the gaze of the Other, I realize that I am seen. My world, which I had constituted from my own subjective vantage point, is suddenly stolen away, captured, and integrated into a foreign perspective. As Sartre famously illustrates through the example of the voyeur peering through a keyhole, the sudden sound of footsteps in the hall instantly shatters his unreflective absorption. He is no longer the free master of his acts; he is a *voyeur*, fixed, judged, and categorized by an external consciousness. The Other’s look petrifies my fluidity into a frozen essence. The *pour-soi* is alienated into an *en-soi-pour-autrui* (an in-itself-for-another).

Because this alienation threatens my freedom, the interpersonal encounter becomes a zero-sum game of ontological warfare. I can neither ignore the Other nor submit to their gaze without committing ontological suicide. Consequently, I must retaliate by looking back. By turning my gaze upon the Other, I attempt to capture their freedom, petrifying *their* subjectivity into an object for *me*. As Sartre asserts, "Conflict is the original meaning of being-for-others." 

Within this framework, communication is fraught with insurmountable paradoxes. Language, too, becomes an instrument of capture. To speak is to name, to classify, and therefore to pin the living butterfly of the Other's subjectivity behind the glass of conceptual categories. Love and desire, for Sartre, are doomed failures: love seeks to capture the Other’s freedom voluntarily, demanding that the Other freely choose to become an object for me—a contradiction in terms that inevitably collapses into sadism or masochism. 

Sartre leaves us stranded in an interpersonal dystopia where the bridge between subjects is perpetually burned by the flames of mutual objectification. Communication is merely the management of this mutual siege. To attain an ethics of genuine encounter, therefore, we must dismantle this ontology of war and replace it with a spatiality of the between that permits the preservation of subjectivity without defaulting to totalizing violence.

## Part II: Paul Tillich and the Indeterminacy of the Subject-Subject Relation

If Sartre locks us in a prison of reciprocal gazes, the Protestant theologian and philosopher Paul Tillich offers a way out by re-envisioning the ontological structure of the human encounter through the lens of participation, correlation, and the paradoxical nature of the "self-world" relation. 

For Tillich, human existence is characterized by an inherent estrangement, yet humanity is fundamentally constituted for communion. In his systematic ontology, the individual self (*principium individuationis*) is never a monad; it actualizes its being only through participation in a structured objective reality and, crucially, through the encounter with other subjects. However, true encounter requires a delicate balance between individual centering and universal participation. 

Central to Tillich’s interpersonal philosophy is the concept of *indeterminacy* within the subject-subject relationship. Unlike objects in the natural world, which are fully determinable, measurable, and exhaustible by scientific or technical categorization, a true subject resists complete determination. There is an inexhaustible depth to human existence—what existentialists call the abyss of freedom and what Tillich frames through the ontological polarities of *individualization* and *participation*. 

When we approach the Other as a subject rather than a mechanism, we encounter a radical indeterminacy. The Other cannot be fully conceptualized, predicted, or encompassed by any systematic matrix of personality types, social roles, or historical conditioning. To treat the Other as a fully determinate object is to commit an ontological reductionism; it is to treat a living abyss as a finished monument. 

Yet, Tillich recognizes a profound tension here: human finitude makes complete, unmediated communion impossible. We are bound by the limitations of language, culture, psychology, and sensory apparatus. We *must* use conceptual tools—what we might call structural approximations—to navigate the social world. The ethical demand, for Tillich, lies in the recognition that these approximations are always provisional and ultimately inadequate to the living reality of the Other. 

In the subject-subject relationship, indeterminacy functions as a theological and ethical safeguard against totalitarianism—both political and interpersonal. When I acknowledge the indeterminacy of the Other, I acknowledge that the Other’s center of meaning transcends my comprehension. The Other is not merely a mirror of my ego, nor a tool for my projects, nor a static obstacle to my freedom. The Other is a bearer of unconditioned depth. 

Thus, Tillich moves us past Sartre by suggesting that the gaze of the Other is not necessarily an act of ontological execution. It can be an invitation to participate in a shared ground of being. The encounter does not demand the annihilation of one freedom by another; rather, it opens a space where both subjects are continually re-centered by the presence of an irreducible mystery.

## Part III: Luce Irigaray and the Ethics of "The Two"

While Tillich provides an ontological architecture of depth and indeterminacy, the contemporary French philosopher and psychoanalyst Luce Irigaray diagnoses the historical failure of Western metaphysics to sustain a genuine subject-subject relation, offering instead a revolutionary geometry of interpersonal encounter grounded in sexual difference and the concept of "the two."

Irigaray’s philosophical project is largely a critique of the phallogocentric tradition, which, she argues, has operated under the spell of a singular, totalizing economy of the Same. In this economy, the Other is never encountered in their radical alterity; rather, the Other is reduced to a mirror, a reflection, a negative, or a subset of the overarching Subject (historically constructed as the universal male ego). This mirrors Sartre’s critique of objectification, but Irigaray roots it in a deeper cultural and linguistic amnesia: the inability of Western thought to think *two* distinct, irreducible subjects who coexist without one subsuming, dominating, or homogenizing the other.

In works such as *An Ethics of Sexual Difference* and *To Be Two*, Irigaray insists that person-to-person communication requires an absolute paradigm shift: we must move from a monistic or dialectical model (where two terms are synthesized into a higher unity, typically erasing difference) to a pluralistic ontology of *the two*. The number two, for Irigaray, is not merely a quantitative marker; it is an ontological threshold. It signifies that there are two irreducibly distinct bodily, linguistic, and subjective universes that can never be merged, fused, or totalized.

Crucially, Irigaray anchors this indeterminacy and alterity in *carnal reality*. For Irigaray, communication is never merely a disembodied exchange of abstract ideas (as Cartesian rationalism would have it); it is an encounter between two sensible, living bodies. The skin, the breath, the voice, and the gaze are the mediums through which the self touches the edge of the Other's world. 

To achieve a true relation of "the two," we must abandon the maternal fusion of pre-Oedipal infancy and the patriarchal mastery of the autonomous ego. We must cultivate a "culture of the two" where each subject maintains their own subjective locus while remaining radically open to the touch, speech, and gaze of the Other. This requires what Irigaray calls a "sensible transcendental"—a way of experiencing the divine or the universal not in some abstract, otherworldly realm, but precisely in the tactile, linguistic space *between* two distinct bodies.

In the context of interpersonal communication, Irigaray’s model of "the two" demands that we cease treating dialogue as a battlefield where one voice attempts to colonize or silence the other. Conversation ceases to be an act of translation where the Other is made to speak *my* language, and becomes a hospitable dwelling place where two distinct idioms resonate alongside one another. The Other remains an irreducible mystery—a subject who can never be fully mapped or mastered—yet this mystery does not alienate us; it invites us into an endless, ethical choreography of mutual respect.

## Part IV: The Dialectic of Preparation – Static Approximations vs. Dynamic Dialogue

We have established the theoretical imperative: to move from Sartrean mutual objectification to a Tillichian-Irigarayan model of mutual transcendence and indeterminacy. But how does this transpire in the messy, concrete actuality of daily interaction? This brings us to a crucial tension within the phenomenology of communication: the dialectic between *static approximations* and *dynamic relinquishment*.

### The Necessity of Anticipatory Objectification
Finite human consciousness cannot step into an encounter utterly naked, unmediated, and devoid of structure. To function in the social world, we rely on sedimented habits, linguistic conventions, psychological archetypes, and prior knowledge about the interlocutor. When I approach an Other, I invariably mobilize conceptual frameworks: I categorize them by gender, profession, ethnicity, past behavior, and social role. 

From a strict Sartrean or radical ethical standpoint, this preliminary framing looks suspiciously like the very objectification we are trying to escape. By boxing the Other into a conceptual category (e.g., "my colleague," "an authority figure," "a distressed friend"), I am performing a provisional reduction of their infinite indeterminacy. I am constructing a *static approximation* of the Other before the dialogue even begins.

However, a phenomenological critique of communication must recognize that this preliminary objectification is not only inevitable; it is ethically and practically *necessary*. Without structural anticipations, we would be paralyzed by cognitive overload, vulnerable to absolute chaos, and incapable of formulating a coherent utterance. Language itself is a system of sedimented generalities—signs that treat singular experiences as repeatable categories. To speak to someone is to deploy a generalized lexicon that inherently risks flattening their absolute uniqueness.

Thus, the self must perform a necessary, initial movement of bounding and categorizing the Other-as-object. We enter the communicative space with maps, scripts, expectations, and projections. The error does not lie in having these approximations; the error lies in *mistaking the map for the territory*—in allowing the static approximation to foreclose the living reality of the dynamic encounter.

### The Crucible of Relinquishment
The threshold of genuine dialogue occurs precisely at the moment of *relinquishment*. When the actual conversation begins—when the living voice of the Other breaks through my anticipatory framework—I am summoned to undergo a conversion of attention. I must voluntarily surrender my static approximations, letting my conceptual armor fall away in the face of the Other's living indeterminacy.

This relinquishment is painful and terrifying. It requires relinquishing the safety of control. As long as I maintain my pre-packaged categorization of the Other, I remain the master of the interaction; I can anticipate their moves, manage their reactions, and protect my ego from surprise or vulnerability. To drop these approximations is to step into the abyss of the unknown—to risk being wounded, transformed, or fundamentally misunderstood.

This is precisely where Tillich’s indeterminacy and Irigaray’s "two" intersect with dialogical practice. In genuine conversation, the words spoken are not merely the transmission of coded data from one pre-determined subject to another; they are an ontological event. The speaker and the listener abandon their fixed positions in the conceptual landscape and meet in the *in-between* (*Zwischenmenschliche*). 

Martin Buber’s distinction between the I-It and I-Thou relations illuminates this transition. The I-It relation is the realm of static approximations, utilitarian exchange, and objectification (the Sartrean baseline). The I-Thou relation is the sudden, grace-like emergence of direct, mutual presence. Yet, Buber reminds us that we cannot live permanently in the I-Thou; the human condition forces us back into the I-It. The ethical task is not to eliminate the I-It entirely, but to allow the I-It to be continually pierced, transfigured, and renewed by the flash of the I-Thou.

Therefore, the dialectic of communication operates as a rhythm: 
1. **Preparation:** We construct static approximations to navigate the approach toward the Other.
2. **Encounter:** We enter the communicative space, armed with our maps.
3. **Relinquishment:** Upon the impact of the Other's living look and voice, we let go of our static categories, exposing ourselves to their radical indeterminacy.
4. **Reconstitution:** We emerge from the dialogue transformed, our conceptual frameworks altered by the encounter.

## Part V: Resolving the Tension – Domination vs. Anticipation of Mutual Change

We are now equipped to resolve the central tension of our inquiry: Does genuine conversation successfully replace the drive toward domination with a dynamic anticipation of mutual change?

Sartre would view the very phrase "anticipation of mutual change" as a utopian illusion, a bourgeois softening of the harsh ontological truth that human relations are fundamentally agonistic. For Sartre, if two consciousnesses meet, one must absorb or be absorbed; one must dominate or submit. To hope for mutual transformation is to ignore the foundational opacity and friction of the *pour-soi*.

Yet, when we integrate Tillich’s ontology of depth and Irigaray’s carnal ethics of the two, we discover that the Sartrean impasse is not an inescapable decree of nature, but the pathology of an unredeemed, defensive ego. The drive toward domination arises precisely from fear—the terror of being objectified, engulfed, or annihilated by the gaze of the Other. Domination is preemptive defense. If I turn you into an object, I neutralize your threat to my sovereignty.

Genuine interpersonal communication offers an alternative to this paranoid economy by shifting the telos of the encounter from *self-preservation* to *mutual transcendence*. Transcendence, in this context, does not mean escaping the material world into a realm of pure spirit; it means the ecstatic movement of the self *beyond* its current boundaries toward the Other, resulting in a transformed state of being for both participants.

To enter into an anticipation of mutual change is to adopt an entirely different ontological posture. Instead of asking, "How can I master this situation, categorize this person, and protect my autonomy?" the dialogical subject asks, "What new reality will be born between us that neither of us could have generated alone?"

### The Anatomy of Mutual Transcendence
When two subjects meet in the spirit of Irigaray’s "two" and Tillich’s indeterminacy, several structural shifts occur:

1. **The Suspension of Teleological Finality:** In dominating communication, I enter the conversation with a pre-determined goal: to persuade, to win, to impress, or to extract information. In mutual transcendence, the outcome of the conversation is radically open. We do not know where the dialogue will lead us. We allow the conversation to possess its own momentum, trusting the emergent properties of the in-between.
2. **Vulnerability as Strength:** Domination relies on invulnerability—the impenetrable armor of the *en-soi*. Mutual transcendence requires what Emmanuel Levinas terms "passivity"—an exposedness to the wound of the Other’s alterity. To change mutually, I must allow my presuppositions to be dismantled. I must risk being proven wrong, being deeply moved, and having my worldview permanently reconfigured.
3. **The Generative Third:** Communication ceases to be a zero-sum transfer of goods and becomes a generative third term. As Irigaray suggests, the space *between* two bodies and two languages is a living matrix. Something is created in genuine dialogue that belongs to neither speaker individually, yet enriches both. 
4. **The Transmutation of the Look:** The Sartrean look, which petrifies and enslaves, is redeemed. The gaze of the Other is no longer experienced as a panoptic surveillance that strips away my freedom; it is experienced as an *invitation*—a summons to wake up from the dogmatic slumber of my solipsism and step into a wider, richer landscape of shared being.

### Overcoming the Drive Toward Domination
Is this transition from domination to mutual anticipation merely a moralistic exhortation—a polite plea to "be nice" to one another? Absolutely not. It is an ontological necessity for authentic human flourishing. 

The drive toward domination ultimately exhausts the self. To constantly maintain the posture of the master requires an expenditure of perpetual vigilance and violence, leading to profound existential loneliness. The tyrant is the ultimate prisoner of the *en-soi*. 

Conversely, the movement toward mutual transcendence opens the self to an inexhaustible source of vitality. By surrendering the futile quest to totalize the Other, we gain access to the inexhaustible depth of human indeterminacy. We discover that we are most fully ourselves not when we stand in solitary, defiant isolation against the world, but precisely when we are undone, re-created, and expanded by the living presence of an Other who remains forever beyond our grasp.

---

## Conclusion: The Horizon of the In-Between

The claim that person-to-person communication requires moving beyond Jean-Paul Sartre’s ontological conflict of mutual objectification toward a model of mutual transcendence is not merely a theoretical preference; it is the definitive condition for the possibility of authentic human life. 

Sartre’s diagnostic brilliance exposed the dark underside of social reality: the persistent temptation to reduce the living, breathing subject to a rigid, manageable object of utility or threat. This Sartrean baseline of mutual objectification remains a constant gravitational pull in human interaction, fueled by fear, egoism, and the demands of finite survival. We see its footprints in every manipulative conversation, every totalizing ideology, and every defensive retreat into the fortress of the self.

Yet, as Paul Tillich and Luce Irigaray demonstrate, human existence is not exhausted by this agonistic zero-sum game. Through Tillich’s concept of the subject’s radical *indeterminacy*, we are reminded that the Other possesses an unconditioned depth that resists all our attempts at final categorization. Through Irigaray’s revolutionary ontology of *the two*, we learn to celebrate an irreducible carnal duality where two distinct subjective universes can meet, touch, and resonate without one consuming the other.

Navigating this terrain demands a sophisticated dialectic. We acknowledge the structural necessity of static approximations—the conceptual maps and cultural scripts we must provisionally deploy to navigate the complex social world. But we recognize that these approximations are merely scaffolding. The ethical heart of communication beats in the courageous moment of *relinquishment*, where we drop our conceptual armor upon entering the actual crucible of dialogue.

Ultimately, genuine conversation succeeds in replacing the primitive Sartrean drive toward domination with a dynamic, risky anticipation of mutual change. In this space of mutual transcendence, the interpersonal encounter ceases to be a battleground of competing egos and becomes a generative, living architecture of the in-between. We are no longer executioners or prisoners of the gaze; we are fellow travelers in an endless, unfolding horizon of shared becoming.
