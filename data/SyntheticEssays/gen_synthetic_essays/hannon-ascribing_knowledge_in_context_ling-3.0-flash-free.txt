**The Contextualist Turn and the Limits of Reconciliation: Evaluating the Contextualist Solution to Skepticism**

The problem of skepticism stands as one of the most formidable challenges in Western epistemology. From Descartes’ evil demon to contemporary brain-in-a-vat scenarios, the skeptical hypothesis posits that our sensory experience is systematically misleading, and that we lack the justification required to rule out radical alternative hypotheses about the nature of our reality. If the skeptic is correct, we do not know that we have hands, that the external world exists, or that the past is real. The contextualist solution emerged in the late twentieth century as a sophisticated attempt to navigate this impasse. Proponents such as Keith DeRose and David Lewis argued that the ascription of knowledge is context-sensitive: the semantic content of the word "knows" shifts depending on the conversational context, allowing for the truth of "I know I have hands" in everyday settings while permitting its falsity in the philosophical seminar. This essay evaluates the contextualist solution to skepticism by examining whether ascriptions of knowledge are genuinely context-sensitive, assessing whether contextualism successfully reconciles ordinary empirical knowledge with the skeptical hypothesis, and scrutinizing three critical objections: the mere consideration objection, the failure to account for the mechanism of context and its shifts, and the charge that contextualism begs the question against the skeptic.

**The Contextualist Thesis and the Reconciliation of Knowledge and Skepticism**

At its core, contextualism asserts that ascriptions of knowledge are context-sensitive in a manner analogous to gradable adjectives like "tall" or "flat." Just as the proposition that "John is tall" is true relative to a context in which the average height is five feet, but false relative to a context in which the average height is six feet, the proposition that "S knows that P" is true relative to a context in which the epistemic standards are low, but false relative to a context in which the standards are high. DeRose’s influential formulation of semantic contextualism posits that the meaning of "knows" is partially determined by the conversational stakes. In low-stakes contexts—such as a casual conversation about the weather or everyday empirical observations—the standard for knowledge is low enough that the skeptical hypothesis is not relevantly considered, and thus, "S knows that P" is true. In high-stakes contexts—such as a philosophical inquiry into the foundations of empirical knowledge—the standard is raised to exclude skeptical possibilities, rendering ordinary knowledge attributions false.

This framework offers an elegant reconciliation of ordinary empirical knowledge with the skeptical hypothesis. It preserves the intuition that we do, in fact, know ordinary facts about the world—such as "I know I have hands" or "I know the coffee is hot"—without falling into the radical skepticism that denies all empirical knowledge. Simultaneously, it respects the logical force of the skeptical argument: if the standards are raised to exclude the possibility of a deceiving God or a brain in a vat, then we do not know those ordinary facts. Contextualism thus provides a dynamic, pragmatic account of our epistemic practices. It explains why the same utterance can be true in one setting and false in another, not because the speaker’s evidence has changed, but because the standards governing the word "knows" have shifted. In this way, contextualism captures the phenomenology of our epistemic lives: we feel we know in the mundane, but we feel the pressure of doubt in the philosophical.

However, the success of this reconciliation is contingent upon the acceptance of the contextualist’s core claim: that ascriptions of knowledge are fundamentally context-sensitive. Critics have argued that the contextualist’s reliance on context-sensitivity is merely a semantic sleight of hand that fails to address the underlying epistemological problem. Nevertheless, the primary philosophical objections to contextualism lie not in its general architecture, but in the specific mechanisms it invokes to explain how context interacts with knowledge ascriptions.

**The Mere Consideration Objection and the Dissolution of Ordinary Knowledge**

A powerful objection to contextualism centers on the mere consideration of the skeptical hypothesis. The objection argues that the mere *entertainment* of the skeptical hypothesis introduces an unusually restrictive standard that dissolves ordinary knowledge, thereby rendering the contextualist solution ineffective in private deliberation. If epistemic standards are context-sensitive, then it seems that whenever a person thinks about the skeptical hypothesis, their context shifts, and their ordinary knowledge evaporates.

Consider a person walking down the street, casually thinking to themselves, "I know I have hands." According to contextualism, this knowledge attribution is true because the conversational context is low-stakes, and the standard for knowledge is correspondingly low. But what happens when the same person pauses, reflects on Descartes’ evil demon, and entertains the possibility that they are a disembodied brain being fed illusory experiences? If the mere consideration of the skeptical hypothesis raises the epistemic standard, then the person no longer "knows" they have hands, even in their own private thoughts. This seems absurd. We do not cease to know ordinary facts simply because we have entertained a skeptical scenario in our minds. If contextualism entails that private thought is subject to the same context-sensitive standards as public assertion, then it fails to account for the stability of ordinary empirical knowledge in the face of skeptical reflection.

Contextualists have attempted to deflect this objection by drawing a rigid distinction between conversational context and cognitive context. They argue that standards shift only in public conversations, where the stakes are pragmatically raised by the audience or the topic, not in private deliberation. In private thought, the standard for "knows" remains low and invariant. However, this distinction is deeply problematic and reveals the fragility of the contextualist framework. If the mere consideration of a skeptical hypothesis in private thought does not raise the standard, then the contextualist must explain why the physical presence of a skeptic in a conversation does. Why is the "conversational context" privileged over the "deliberative context"? The skeptic does not need to be physically present to raise the stakes of the epistemic evaluation; the mere possibility of deception is sufficient to undermine the certainty of empirical knowledge. By insisting that standards only shift in public discourse, the contextualist appears to be making an ad hoc stipulation designed solely to preserve the truth of ordinary knowledge attributions, rather than providing a principled account of how epistemic standards operate.

Furthermore, if the mere consideration of the skeptical hypothesis does raise the standard, then ordinary knowledge is indeed dissolved by skeptical reflection. We would never be able to engage in practical reasoning or act on our empirical beliefs without first resolving the skeptical problem. But we do not do this; we act on our knowledge of the everyday world without first proving that we are not brains in vats. If contextualism requires that we constantly adjust our epistemic standards based on our private reflections, it becomes an impractical and psychologically implausible theory. The mere consideration objection thus threatens to either make contextualism collapse into invariantism (where standards never shift, and skepticism wins) or to make it absurd (where private knowledge fluctuates with every skeptical thought). In either case, the contextualist fails to provide a stable reconciliation of ordinary empirical knowledge with the skeptical hypothesis.

**The Failure to Account for the Mechanism of Context and the Recognition of Shifts**

Beyond the mere consideration objection lies a deeper, more structural failure within contextualism: the inability to account for how context determines epistemic standards and how shifts in context are recognized. Contextualism posits that context sets the standard for "knows," but it remains notoriously vague about the nature of this context and the mechanism by which it operates.

What constitutes a context? Is it the physical environment, the topic of conversation, the identity of the interlocutors, or the pragmatic stakes of the utterance? Contextualists typically point to pragmatic factors, such as the practical stakes of the conversation, as the trigger for shifting epistemic standards. Yet, this reliance on pragmatic factors creates a significant gap in the theory. Epistemic standards are, by their nature, normative and cognitive; they concern what counts as sufficient evidence or justification. Pragmatic stakes concern practical interests, such as the consequences of being wrong. The leap from "this conversation has high practical stakes" to "the standard for knowledge is now high" requires a bridge principle that contextualism never adequately provides. Why should practical interests dictate epistemic standards? A skeptic might argue that the practical stakes are irrelevant to the truth of the matter; the brain in a vat scenario is possible regardless of whether one is trying to impress a date or pass an exam. By making epistemic standards a function of pragmatic context, contextualism risks making knowledge a mere function of conversational convenience rather than a reflection of epistemic justification.

Moreover, contextualism fails to account for how shifts in context are recognized. When a person moves from a low-stakes conversation to a high-stakes philosophical discussion, how do they recognize that the epistemic standard has shifted? To recognize the shift, one must already be operating under some epistemic standard. But what standard is used to recognize the shift? If the standard for recognizing the shift is itself context-sensitive, we are caught in a vicious regress. The meta-contextual problem reveals that contextualism lacks a stable anchor. It tells us that "knows" means different things in different contexts, but it does not tell us how we navigate between these contexts or how we determine which context we are currently in. Without a clear mechanism for context determination and recognition, the contextualist solution remains a semantic description rather than an epistemological explanation. We are left with a theory that says knowledge is relative to context, but cannot tell us how we know what context we are in, thereby leaving the very concept of knowledge it seeks to analyze in a state of indeterminacy.

**The Circularity Objection: Begging the Question Against the Skeptic**

Perhaps the most devastating criticism of contextualism is that it begs the question against the skeptic by assuming what it claims to know. The skeptic’s challenge is fundamentally about justification: given the skeptical hypothesis, do we have sufficient justification to rule out radical alternative possibilities? The contextualist’s response is to lower the standard of justification required for knowledge in ordinary contexts. However, in doing so, the contextualist presupposes the very epistemic framework that the skeptic is challenging.

The skeptic argues that the existence of a skeptical possibility (such as the brain in a vat) undermines the justification for our ordinary empirical beliefs. The contextualist replies that in ordinary contexts, the skeptical possibility does not defeat knowledge because the standard for knowledge is low. But this reply assumes that the low standard is epistemically justified. The skeptic will immediately retort: "Why should the low standard be accepted? If the skeptical hypothesis is live, then it is relevant to the justification of our beliefs, regardless of what conversational standard we adopt." The contextualist is essentially defining knowledge in a way that excludes the skeptical hypothesis by stipulation, rather than providing an independent justification for why the skeptical hypothesis is irrelevant. By stipulating that "knows" only requires a low standard in ordinary contexts, the contextualist is assuming that the ordinary context provides a low standard, which is precisely the claim the skeptic disputes.

This circularity becomes apparent when we examine the contextualist’s reliance on ordinary language. The contextualist argues that because ordinary language uses "knows" in contexts with low standards, we can legitimately claim ordinary knowledge. But the skeptic questions the reliability of ordinary language itself. If we are brains in vats, our linguistic practices and conversational contexts are merely simulations, and the standards they generate are illusory. The contextualist cannot appeal to the facts of ordinary language to defeat the skeptic without first assuming that ordinary language is a reliable guide to truth—a presupposition the skeptic explicitly denies. The contextualist is using the concept of "knows" (with its lowered standards) to explain the concept of "knows" (with its lowered standards). The framework is closed, and it fails to provide any independent epistemic foothold against the skeptical hypothesis. It is, in essence, a semantic strategy that avoids the epistemological problem rather than solving it. By redefining knowledge to fit the context, the contextualist begs the question against the skeptic, who is precisely concerned with whether the redefined knowledge corresponds to any genuine epistemic achievement.

**Conclusion**

The contextualist solution to skepticism represents a significant and influential contribution to epistemological discourse. By positing that ascriptions of knowledge are context-sensitive, contextualism successfully captures the dynamic, pragmatic nature of our epistemic practices. It offers a compelling account of why we feel we know ordinary facts in everyday life, yet feel the force of doubt in philosophical inquiry. However, a thorough evaluation reveals that contextualism ultimately fails to provide a robust philosophical solution to skepticism. The objection that the mere consideration of a skeptical hypothesis introduces an unusually restrictive standard that dissolves ordinary knowledge exposes the theory’s inability to account for private deliberation without rendering it ad hoc or absurd. The failure of contextualism to account for how context determines epistemic standards or how shifts in context are recognized leaves the theory structurally unstable, unable to explain the bridge between pragmatic circumstances and normative epistemic evaluation. Finally, the criticism that contextualism begs the question against the skeptic by assuming what it claims to know reveals that the theory is ultimately circular, offering a semantic redefinition rather than an epistemological justification. While contextualism may serve as a useful descriptive tool for understanding the phenomenology of knowledge ascription, it fails as a normative solution to the skeptical challenge. It dissolves the linguistic problem of skepticism by shifting the meaning of "knows," but it leaves the underlying epistemological problem—the justification of our beliefs against radical doubt—entirely unresolved.
