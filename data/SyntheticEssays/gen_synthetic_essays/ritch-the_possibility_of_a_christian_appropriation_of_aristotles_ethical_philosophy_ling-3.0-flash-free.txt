**The Divine and the Human: Martha Nussbaum, Aristotle’s Book X, and the Foundations of Christian Ethics**

The *Nicomachean Ethics* has long served as the cornerstone of Western moral philosophy, yet its final book remains one of the most fiercely contested textual battlegrounds. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether Aristotle’s ethical framework culminates in an immanent, human-centered flourishing, or whether it gestures toward a transcendent hierarchy that elevates the contemplative life above the practical virtues. Martha Nussbaum, in her seminal work *The Fragility of Goodness*, offers a robust defense of the former: an anthropocentric reading that unifies the practical moral virtues of Books I–IX with the contemplative ideal of Book X. However, a rigorous evaluation of Nussbaum’s interpretation reveals a profound tension. While her framework—specifically her arguments regarding species-relative goods, the incommensurability of a plurality of ethical values, and principles as provisional summaries of good decisions—successfully captures Aristotle’s immanent teleology, it ultimately fails to account for the transcendent overreach of Book X. This overreach introduces a hierarchy that anticipates New Testament theological doctrines, creating an irreconcilable tension between human vulnerability and the pursuit of the divine life. Consequently, while Aristotelian ethics provides an indispensable rational scaffolding, it cannot independently serve as a sufficient foundation for Christian doctrine without the supernatural elevation that only theological revelation can supply.

Nussbaum’s anthropocentric interpretation is built upon a careful defense of Aristotle’s practical ethics against scholars who argue that Book X renders the ethical life of Books I–IX merely preparatory and ultimately subordinate. Her first major argument concerns the nature of species-relative goods. Nussbaum argues that Aristotle defines the human good strictly by the human function (*ergon*), which is rational activity in accordance with virtue. By tethering eudaimonia to the specific capacities and limitations of human nature, Nussbaum demonstrates that Aristotle’s ethical framework is inherently anthropocentric. For Nussbaum, the practical virtues—courage, temperance, justice—are not mere stepping stones to a higher, alien form of life; rather, they are constitutive elements of the human good itself. To abandon the practical life for contemplation is to abandon the very essence of what it means to be human. Thus, the practical virtues are not demoted by Book X; they remain the primary locus of human flourishing.

Second, Nussbaum defends the incommensurability of a plurality of ethical values within eudaimonia. Critics of the *Nicomachean Ethics* often point to the tension between the practical and the contemplative as evidence of a fractured ethical vision. Nussbaum, however, argues that Aristotle’s concept of eudaimonia is a composite good, comprising a plurality of incommensurable but harmonized activities. Just as a flourishing human life requires both honor and pleasure, or justice and friendship, so too does it require both the active exercise of practical virtue and the serene activity of contemplation. Nussbaum insists that Aristotle does not establish a rigid hierarchy where contemplation wholly displaces practical action. Instead, the plurality of values remains in dynamic tension, reflecting the complexity of human life. This reading preserves the integrity of Books I–IX, ensuring that the ethical life is not reduced to a mere prelude for a divine activity.

Third, Nussbaum’s interpretation hinges on her treatment of principles as provisional summaries of good decisions. Against the notion that Aristotle’s ethics is a rigid deontology or a strict rule-based system, Nussbaum highlights the role of *phronesis* (practical wisdom). Principles in Aristotle’s ethics are not universal, exceptionless laws; they are heuristic summaries derived from the deliberations of the *phronimos* (the person of practical wisdom). This renders Aristotle’s moral framework deeply contextual and flexible. By grounding ethics in the provisional and situational nature of practical reasoning, Nussbaum ensures that the practical virtues retain their autonomy. They are not subordinated to a rigid, abstract contemplative ideal, but are instead the living, breathing expression of rational activity adapted to the contingencies of human existence. Through these three pillars—species-relative goods, the plurality of values, and the provisional nature of principles—Nussbaum constructs a cohesive anthropocentric framework that captures Aristotle’s final position on eudaimonia as an integrated, human-centered flourishing.

Yet, despite the persuasive power of Nussbaum’s anthropocentric synthesis, Book X of the *Nicomachean Ethics* introduces a radical shift that her framework struggles to contain. In the famous passages of 1177b–1179a, Aristotle argues that the best activity is contemplation (*theoria*) because it is the most continuous, most self-sufficient, and most divine. He explicitly states that the intellect (*nous*) is "more divine than the rest of us," and that in contemplation, the intellect approaches the divine. This is not merely a claim that contemplation is a superior human activity; it is a claim that the contemplative life elevates the human being into a transcendent hierarchy. The practical virtues, by contrast, are bound to the mutable, vulnerable, and political realm of human affairs. Book X thus introduces a verticality that Nussbaum’s horizontal, anthropocentric framework attempts to flatten.

This transcendent hierarchy in Book X does more than simply rank activities; it anticipates the doctrinal structures of New Testament theology. The idea that human flourishing culminates in an activity that elevates the soul to the divine, rendering the agent partaker in the nature of God, resonates profoundly with the Christian concept of *theosis* (deification) and the beatific vision. In the New Testament, the ultimate end of human life is not merely a life of moral virtue and civic engagement, but the vision of God and the transformation of the believer into the likeness of Christ (2 Peter 1:4). Aristotle’s contemplation, while not explicitly Trinitarian or Christological, shares this eschatological trajectory: it is an activity that transcends the human condition, pointing toward an eternal, unchanging reality that lies beyond the temporal flux of practical virtue. By emphasizing the divine nature of the intellect in contemplation, Aristotle opens a door that Nussbaum’s anthropocentric framework keeps firmly closed, a door through which the theology of the New Testament eagerly passes.

The tension between human vulnerability and the pursuit of the divine life is the crux of the philosophical difficulty that arises from this reading. Throughout Books I–IX, Aristotle emphasizes the fragility of human life. Eudaimonia requires external goods—health, wealth, friends, and political stability—because the practical virtues must be enacted in a vulnerable, suffering world. The virtuous person is not impervious to fortune; they are, as Nussbaum herself emphasizes, fragile. The practical virtues are precisely the tools by which humans navigate and mitigate this vulnerability. Yet, the contemplative life of Book X demands a radical detachment from this vulnerability. Contemplation is described as an activity of pure, unencumbered intellect, requiring the stilling of the passions and the withdrawal from the turbulent affairs of the polis. To pursue the divine life, one must, to a significant degree, transcend the very vulnerability that defines the human condition.

This creates an apparent contradiction: Can an ethical system grounded in human vulnerability—the very condition that necessitates practical virtue—simultaneously demand the transcendence of that vulnerability in contemplation? If practical virtue is the highest expression of the human good, how can the contemplative ideal, which requires a withdrawal from the practical, be the ultimate fulfillment? The resolution of this contradiction lies in recognizing that Aristotle’s *theoria* is not merely a cognitive exercise, but an ontological ascent. The practical virtues purify the soul, rendering it capable of receiving the divine light of contemplation. Yet, the contemplative life itself requires a kind of symbolic death to the vulnerable, practical self. It is an activity that anticipates a state of being where vulnerability is no longer the defining feature of the agent.

This is precisely the point at which Aristotelian ethics intersects with, yet ultimately diverges from, Christian theology. The Christian doctrine of the resurrection and the beatific vision similarly resolves the tension between vulnerability and the divine, but it does so through a mechanism that Aristotelian rationalism cannot provide. In Christianity, human vulnerability is not transcended by intellectual detachment, but by divine grace and the Incarnation. God enters into human vulnerability in the person of Christ, suffering and dying, thereby redeeming the vulnerable condition rather than requiring its abandonment. The Christian pursuit of the divine life is not a withdrawal from the world into the stillness of the intellect, but a participation in the suffering and resurrection of Christ. Thus, while Aristotle’s Book X anticipates the Christian desire for the divine, it resolves the vulnerability contradiction through an immanent, rationalist ascent that ultimately leaves human suffering behind. Christianity, by contrast, resolves it through a transcendent, theological descent that redeems suffering from within.

Consequently, the question arises: Can Aristotelian ethics successfully provide a rational foundation for Christian doctrine? The answer is both yes and no, depending on how one defines "foundation." Nussbaum’s anthropocentric framework demonstrates that Aristotle’s ethics provides an exceptionally robust rational foundation for *natural* morality. The principles of practical wisdom, the recognition of species-relative goods, and the incommensurable plurality of values offer a comprehensive, reason-based account of how to live a good human life. This natural morality aligns with the Christian concept of natural law, which Aquinas famously integrated into Thomistic theology. On this level, Aristotelian ethics is indispensable; it provides the rational scaffolding upon which Christian moral theology can be built.

However, Aristotelian ethics cannot provide a sufficient foundation for the *supernatural* doctrines of Christianity. Nussbaum’s framework, by strictly limiting the human good to anthropocentric flourishing, cannot accommodate the doctrine of grace, the necessity of divine revelation, or the beatific vision. If eudaimonia is exhausted by the integration of practical virtue and contemplation within human nature, there is no rational space for the idea that human nature is fallen, in need of redemption, and ultimately destined for a union with God that exceeds its natural capacities. The transcendent hierarchy of Book X gestures toward this supernatural end, but it does so through a rationalist logic that ultimately falls short of the theological truth. The "divine life" in Aristotle is an activity of the intellect that approximates the divine; in Christianity, the divine life is a relational union with a personal God achieved through grace.

Therefore, the tension between Nussbaum’s anthropocentric interpretation and the transcendent hierarchy of Book X cannot be fully resolved within the bounds of secular philosophy. Nussbaum is correct that Aristotle’s final position on eudaimonia is deeply concerned with the integrated life of the virtuous human being, and her framework accurately captures the immanent teleology of Books I–IX. Yet, she is incorrect in assuming that this immanent framework fully contains the implications of Book X. By reading the *Nicomachean Ethics* strictly through an anthropocentric lens, Nussbaum domesticated the divine, preventing Book X from speaking its full, unsettling theological language. Book X does introduce a transcendent hierarchy that anticipates New Testament doctrines, not because Aristotle was a closet Christian, but because the rational pursuit of the highest human activity inevitably pushes against the boundaries of human nature and points toward a transcendence that reason alone cannot secure.

In the final analysis, Aristotelian ethics provides a necessary but insufficient foundation for Christian doctrine. It supplies the rational architecture: the structure of virtue, the teleology of human action, and the recognition of a highest good. But it cannot supply the supernatural content. The contradiction between human vulnerability and the pursuit of the divine life is not resolved by Aristotelian rationalism, but by the Christian theological claim that God bridges the gap through the Incarnation and the grace of the Holy Spirit. Nussbaum’s framework is a brilliant defense of the immanent Aristotle, but it is a defense that ultimately keeps the philosopher firmly within the walls of the polis, missing the transcendent light that Book X casts toward the divine, a light that only the theological doctrines of the New Testament can fully illuminate.
