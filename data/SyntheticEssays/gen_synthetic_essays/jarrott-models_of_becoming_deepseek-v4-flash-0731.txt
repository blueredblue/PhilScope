**The Edge of Becoming: Memory, Indistinguishability, and the Metaphysics of Temporal Flow**

The experience of time’s passage—the sense that moments slip from a not-yet into a now and then recede into a fixed past—is among the most intimate and stubborn features of human consciousness. For the A-theorist, this subjective flow is not an illusion but a direct apprehension of a mind-independent metaphysical reality: the objective becoming of the world. The most intuitive articulation of this is the Growing Block theory, which posits that the past and present exist, while the future does not, and that the present is the dynamic frontier of existence. Yet the plausibility of this ontology rests on a fragile assumption: that the unique phenomenology of temporal flow uniquely tracks this particular structure. If alternative models of temporal becoming—specifically the Diminishing Block theory, where the past is continuously erased, and the Backwards Block theory, where the block grows into the past—could generate subjective experiences of flow that are phenomenally indistinguishable from those produced by the Growing Block, then the evidential weight of common-sense experience would be severely compromised. In this essay, I will argue that while the Diminishing Block can, in principle, produce an indistinguishable subjective experience, the Backwards Block is rendered incoherent by Michael Dummett’s critique of backward causation. More importantly, I will contend that even the psychological indistinguishability of the Diminishing and Growing Blocks does not subvert the evidential weight of common-sense experience for the Growing Block ontology. This is because the *content* of experience—specifically the veridical structure of memory—provides a decisive inferential argument in favor of the Growing Block over both the Diminishing Block and traditional Presentism. The raw phenomenology of flow is indeed underdetermined by the direction of becoming, but the epistemic status of memory, combined with the incoherence of backward causation, rescues the Growing Block as the best explanation of our temporal experience.

**I. The Geometry of Becoming: Three Models and the Phenomenology of Flow**

To evaluate the claim of indistinguishability, one must first delineate the precise metaphysical structures at play. The **Growing Block (GB)** theory, championed by C.D. Broad and later by Michael Tooley, posits a four-dimensional manifold in which the past and present exist, but the future is nothing. The present is the "edge" of this block—the leading frontier of existence. As time passes, new events are added to the block, and the edge moves forward. The subjective experience of flow arises from the constant accretion of new facts: the world is perpetually becoming more.

The **Diminishing Block (DB)** theory, sometimes called the "Shrinking Block," is the mirror image. Here, the present and future exist, but the past does not. The present is again the edge of existence, but instead of being the leading frontier of growth, it is the *trailing* edge of a block that extends into the future. As time passes, events are progressively erased from the past. The world is perpetually becoming less—a constant process of ontological annihilation. The present is the point where existence terminates, and the future is the vast, existing realm from which we are continually retreating.

The **Backwards Block (BB)** theory is a more exotic variant. In this model, the future and present exist, and the block is growing *backwards* into the past. The present is the frontier, but the direction of becoming is reversed: instead of new events being added to the future side of the present, new events are being added to the past side. The past is continuously being created, while the future is a fixed, static plenum. This implies that present events are causally responsible for bringing about past events—a form of backward causation.

All three models share a crucial feature: they are A-theories that posit an objective, dynamic edge of temporal existence. The question is whether the subjective experience of this edge—the felt "passage" of time—can be made identical across these three distinct geometries. The phenomenology of flow is typically characterized by a few key features: the sense of a moving "now," the directedness of time (from future to past), and the asymmetry of memory (we remember the past, not the future). The GB model explains these features straightforwardly. The present is the edge of becoming; the past is the accumulated, fixed record; the future is a void awaiting creation. Our memories track the existing past, and our anticipations track a non-existent future.

Can the DB model replicate this? On the surface, it seems impossible. If the past is being erased, then our memories—which purport to represent past events—would be systematically false. However, a careful analysis reveals that the *phenomenal* character of experience could be identical. Consider a subject, S, at time t. In the GB model, S has memories of events at t-1, t-2, etc., because those events exist. In the DB model, S at time t also has memories of events at t-1, t-2, etc., but those events no longer exist. The *content* of the memories—the qualitative feel of remembering a specific event, the feeling of temporal distance, the clarity of recent versus distant memories—could be exactly the same.
