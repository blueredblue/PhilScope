**Title: The Unraveling of Intentionality: Hursthouse’s Arational Actions and the Limits of Davidson’s Belief-Desire Theory**

**Introduction**

The philosophy of action has long been dominated by the conviction that to act intentionally is to be moved by reasons. In the standard Humean picture, an intentional action is the product of a belief and a desire, linked by a principle of rationality. Donald Davidson’s belief-desire theory of intentional action refines this picture, arguing that the causal explanation of an action requires that the agent’s mental states—specifically, their beliefs and desires—constitute the primary reasons for the action. On Davidson’s account, if an action is not caused by a belief-desire pair, it is not intentional in the full sense; it is either a mere reflex, a bodily movement without a rational source, or an accidental byproduct of an agent's psychology. However, this robust framework faces a profound challenge from the phenomenology of emotional outbursts. Rosalind Hursthouse’s theory of arational actions, rooted in Aristotelian virtue ethics, posits that some actions are driven by occurant emotions—passions that overwhelm the agent without the mediation of a belief-desire pair. This essay evaluates whether Hursthouse’s theory successfully undermines Davidson’s belief-desire theory, assesses the distinction between pre-named and post-named epistemic states during emotional outbursts, and resolves the resulting normative dilemma regarding moral culpability. Ultimately, it will be argued that Hursthouse’s account does not collapse into unintentional reflexes, but rather necessitates a revised framework of intentionality that accommodates the causal and normative weight of pre-named, arational emotional actions.

**Davidson’s Belief-Desire Theory and the Architecture of Intentionality**

Donald Davidson’s account of intentional action rests on the premise that the mental states that cause actions must themselves be rational, or at least connected by a rational principle. For Davidson, an action is intentional if and only if it is caused by a belief and a desire, and the belief-desire pair is connected by a principle of rationality. This principle dictates that the agent’s belief and desire must be consistent with one another and with the agent’s broader system of beliefs and desires. In Davidson’s framework, the causal chain runs as follows: the agent has a desire (e.g., to quench their thirst) and a belief (e.g., that this glass contains water), and the action (drinking) is the rational effect of these two mental states converging on the world.

Crucially, Davidson’s theory is deeply committed to the idea that intentional actions require a named, conceptualized motivation. The agent must be able to provide a first-person explanation of their action in terms of their reasons. If an action cannot be explained by a belief-desire pair, it falls outside the scope of intentionality. Davidson explicitly draws the boundary between intentional action and mere bodily movement here: a reflexive twitch or a startle response lacks the rational architecture of a belief-desire pair and is therefore not an intentional action in the philosophically rigorous sense. For Davidson, the causal story of an intentional action must ultimately be a rational story. The action must be the output of a cognitive process that weighs reasons, even if this weighing happens quickly and unreflectively.

However, Davidson’s framework struggles to account for the sudden, overwhelming nature of emotional outbursts. When an agent is seized by a sudden fit of rage or a paralyzing wave of fear, the phenomenology suggests that the action is not the product of a calm weighing of reasons. Instead, the emotion seems to bypass the deliberative apparatus entirely. It is precisely this phenomenological gap that Hursthouse’s theory seeks to exploit, arguing that Davidson’s model is insufficient to capture the full spectrum of human action.

**Hursthouse’s Theory of Arational Actions and the Challenge to Davidson**

Rosalind Hursthouse, drawing on Aristotelian moral psychology, introduces the concept of arational actions to challenge the sufficiency of the belief-desire model. Hursthouse argues that some actions are not driven by a belief-desire pair but are instead caused directly by an occurant emotion. An occurant emotion, such as a sudden flash of anger or a visceral surge of lust, is not itself a belief or a desire. Rather, it is a passionate state that happens to the agent, an affective reaction that bypasses the slow, deliberative processes of reason.

Hursthouse’s crucial move is to classify these arational actions as intentional in a broader, Aristotelian sense, while simultaneously demonstrating that they do not fit Davidson’s narrow, Humean criteria. For Hursthouse, an action can be intentional without being the product of a belief-desire pair. When an agent acts out of a sudden, overwhelming emotion, the action is directed toward an object and is performed with a certain kind of awareness, even if the agent does not consciously deliberate about their reasons beforehand. The emotion itself provides the motivational force. This directly undermines Davidson’s belief-desire theory because it demonstrates that the causal chain of intentional action does not strictly require a belief-desire pair; it can be initiated by a non-propositional, affective state.

The success of Hursthouse’s challenge hinges on the distinction between what I will term pre-named and post-named epistemic states. During an emotional outburst, the agent’s epistemic state is often pre-named: the emotion is a raw, unreflective surge that has not yet been conceptualized or labeled. The agent acts before they can name the emotion or articulate the belief-desire pair that would justify the action under Davidsonian standards. In contrast, a post-named epistemic state occurs after the fact, when the agent reflects on their outburst and constructs a narrative: "I acted that way because I believed he was disrespecting me, and I desired to defend my honor." The post-named state provides the rationalization, but the pre-named state is where the action actually originates.

**Pre-Named Actions and the Breakdown of Standard Causal Accounts**

The distinction between pre-named and post-named epistemic states is pivotal in understanding how pre-named actions challenge standard causal accounts. A standard causal account, such as Davidson’s, requires that the action be caused by a specific mental state—namely, a belief-desire pair. When an action is pre-named, it lacks both a named emotion (in the conceptual sense) and a corresponding belief-desire pair at the moment of execution. The agent does not yet possess the propositional attitude ("I believe that...") or the evaluative desire ("I want...") that would link the action to a reason. Instead, the action is caused by the occurant emotion itself.

This poses a severe problem for the standard causal account. If the action is caused by an emotion that is not a belief-desire pair, then the standard causal chain is broken. The action is not the effect of a rational belief-desire combination; it is the effect of an affective state that is, by definition, arational. Hursthouse argues that this does not render the action unintentional or mere reflex. Rather, it reveals that intentionality is not exhausted by propositional attitudes. The pre-named action retains an intentional structure—it is directed at an object (the target of the anger) and is performed with a certain kind of agency—but this structure is not mediated by the belief-desire pair that Davidson requires.

Consider an agent who, in a sudden burst of pre-named rage, strikes another person. At the exact moment of the strike, the agent does not possess the belief "this person has wronged me" and the desire "I want to punish them" in a way that constitutes a rational principle. The anger is an occurant emotion that surges through the agent, causing the movement of the arm. If we strictly adhere to Davidson’s framework, this action is not intentional; it is a bodily movement caused by an emotional state that lacks rational articulation. But phenomenologically and practically, it feels and functions as an intentional action. Hursthouse’s theory captures this by arguing that the occurant emotion itself is the rational source of the action, albeit a source that operates below the threshold of explicit belief-desire reasoning.

**The Normative Dilemma: Culpability and the Capacity for Moderation**

The existence of arational, pre-named actions creates a profound normative dilemma regarding moral culpability. If an action is performed without a corresponding belief-desire pair—if it is driven purely by an occurant emotion that bypasses rational deliberation—then it seems that the agent cannot be held directly morally responsible for the action. On Davidson’s account, moral responsibility requires that the agent’s action be grounded in their beliefs and desires. If the belief-desire pair is absent, the action lacks a rational source, and the agent is, in a sense, a victim of their own passional nature rather than its author.

This creates an uncomfortable lacuna in moral philosophy: if agents can perform harmful actions during emotional outbursts without belief-desire pairs, and if such actions are not intentional in the Davidsonian sense, then the agent appears to be morally blameless. Yet, intuitively, we do not absolve agents of all responsibility simply because they were overwhelmed by emotion. We still hold them accountable, at least in part, for their outbursts.

The resolution of this dilemma lies in examining the capacity for intentional preventative emotion moderation. While the pre-named action itself lacks a belief-desire pair and is therefore not directly intentional, the agent possesses the capacity—and the prior history of exercising that capacity—to moderate their emotions before they erupt. This capacity for moderation is itself an intentional, rational activity. Through habituation, therapy, and deliberate practice, an agent can cultivate the disposition to prevent emotional outbursts or to channel them constructively. 

The capacity for preventative moderation bridges the gap between the arational action and the agent’s moral culpability. If an agent fails to exercise this capacity—if they neglect their emotional training and allow a pre-named emotion to dictate their actions—they can be held indirectly accountable for the outburst. The culpability is not for the specific, pre-named action itself (which lacks the rational architecture of a belief-desire pair), but for the prior failure to moderate the disposition that produced the action. This aligns with Aristotelian virtue ethics, where character is formed through repeated action, and where failing to cultivate virtue renders an agent responsible for the vices that eventually manifest in uncontrolled outbursts. Thus, while Hursthouse’s arational actions escape direct Davidsonian intentionality, they remain tethered to the agent’s moral character through the intentional work of preventative moderation.

**Resolving the Tension: Revised Intentionality or Collapse into Reflexes?**

The underlying tension in this debate is whether actions driven purely by occurant emotion require a revised framework of intentionality, or whether they simply collapse into unintentional reflexes. If Hursthouse’s arational actions truly lack any rational structure—any direction toward an object, any connection to the agent’s evaluative stance—then they would indeed collapse into mere reflexes, indistinguishable from a knee-jerk or a spasm. However, Hursthouse’s theory does not collapse into this reduction, and a revised framework of intentionality is required.

A reflex, in the standard philosophical sense, is a physiological response that lacks intentional content. It does not have an object in the world that it is directed toward in a meaningful, evaluative way. When an agent flinches at a sudden loud noise, the flinch is a reflex; it is not anger at the noise, nor is it a desire to escape the noise in a way that involves propositional belief. In contrast, an occurant emotion like anger or fear is inherently object-directed. Even in its pre-named, arational state, the emotion is *about* someone or something. The agent is not merely having a physiological reaction; they are being emotionally affected by a perceived slight, a threat, or a loss. This object-directedness constitutes a form of intentionality that is distinct from, but not reducible to, the belief-desire pair.

Therefore, the correct resolution is that actions driven purely by occurant emotion require a revised framework of intentionality. We must expand our understanding of what constitutes a "reason" for action to include non-propositional, affective reasons. In this revised framework, an occurant emotion is not a belief-desire pair, but it is a motivational state that provides a reason for action in virtue of its object and its felt quality. The pre-named action is intentional because it is caused by an emotion that is directed at the world, even if that emotion has not yet been conceptualized into a belief-desire pair. The post-named state then retroactively provides the belief-desire pair that satisfies Davidson’s criteria for rational explanation, but the pre-named state reveals that the causal origin of the action was affective, not cognitive.

This revised framework does not destroy Davidson’s account; rather, it extends it. Davidson’s belief-desire theory remains the gold standard for fully articulated, deliberative intentional action. But Hursthouse’s arational actions reveal that this model is insufficient for capturing the full range of human agency. We need a dual-aspect theory of intentionality: one that recognizes the rational, belief-desire dimension of action, and another that recognizes the affective, occurant dimension. The pre-named action is neither a fully rational Davidsonian action nor an unintentional reflex; it occupies a middle ground, an affective intentionality where the emotion itself serves as the bridge between the agent and the world.

**Conclusion**

Rosalind Hursthouse’s theory of arational actions successfully undermines Donald Davidson’s belief-desire theory by exposing the limitations of the Humean model in accounting for the phenomenology of emotional outbursts. Through the distinction between pre-named and post-named epistemic states, Hursthouse demonstrates that pre-named actions challenge standard causal accounts by lacking both a named emotion and a corresponding belief-desire pair at the moment of execution. These actions are caused directly by occurant emotions, breaking the rational causal chain that Davidson requires for intentionality. The resulting normative dilemma regarding moral culpability is resolved not by absolving the agent, but by recognizing the capacity for intentional preventative emotion moderation as the bridge to indirect accountability. By examining the agent's failure to cultivate emotional regulation, we can hold them responsible for the arational outbursts that stem from their unmoderated character. Ultimately, actions driven purely by occurant emotion do not collapse into unintentional reflexes; they demand a revised framework of intentionality that accommodates the object-directed, motivational force of emotion. In doing so, Hursthouse enriches our understanding of human action, revealing that intentionality is not merely the product of belief and desire, but also the visceral, pre-reflective force of the passions themselves.
