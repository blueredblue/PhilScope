**The Boundaries of Benevolence: Kantian Justice, Individual Rights, and the State’s Duty of Beneficence**

The question of whether the state possesses an independent moral duty of beneficence stands at the intersection of political philosophy and moral theory, demanding a rigorous examination of the relationship between the collective entity of the state and the moral agency of the individual. When evaluated through the lens of Immanuel Kant’s moral philosophy, the tension between the state’s obligation to secure the conditions of freedom and the inviolability of individual autonomy becomes particularly acute. Allen D. Rosen’s interpretation of Kantian justice argues that the state does indeed bear an independent moral duty of beneficence, grounding this obligation in the state’s role as the necessary guarantor of the external conditions under which freedom can be realized. Conversely, an alternative framework rooted in a strict defense of individual rights contends that the state’s moral duties are strictly limited to the preservation of rights, and that any coercive welfare measure transgresses the bounds of the Categorical Imperative. This essay evaluates the tension between Rosen’s Kantian interpretation and the rights-based alternative framework, arguing that the state does not possess an independent moral duty of beneficence that can justify coercive taxation for the needy. Instead, the Categorical Imperative, operating through the principles of freedom—specifically equality and liberty—constrains the legitimate actions of the state to the framework of right, while the distinction between the united will and popular majority consent reveals why positive state welfare measures inherently violate individual freedom under Kant’s Metaphysics of Morals.

To understand the philosophical stakes of this debate, one must first grasp the foundational role of the state in Kant’s political philosophy. For Kant, the state is not merely a police power that enforces contracts and punishes transgressors; it is the institutional embodiment of the moral law in the public sphere. The state, or the *Rechtsstaat*, exists to secure the conditions under which rational agents can pursue their ends without interference, thereby actualizing the autonomy that is the core of moral worth. Allen D. Rosen, in his seminal work on Kantian justice, extends this reading to argue that the state’s duty of beneficence is not merely a matter of charity, but a structural requirement of justice itself. Rosen posits that because the state creates the legal and social conditions necessary for the exercise of freedom, it bears a correlative duty to ensure that these conditions do not systematically exclude or impoverish its citizens. In Rosen’s view, the state’s duty of beneficence arises from its unique position as the bearer of the united will, and it is this duty that licenses coercive taxation to support the needy.

Rosen’s interpretation hinges on a specific reading of the Categorical Imperative as it applies to the state’s legislative and coercive functions. According to Rosen, the state must legislate according to maxims that could be universalized, and it must treat humanity in every citizen as an end in itself, never merely as a means. From this, Rosen derives the argument that the state’s duty of beneficence is a duty of justice, not of virtue. Justice, for Kant, involves the external, coercive conditions under which the freedom of one rational being is compatible with the freedom of all. Rosen argues that extreme poverty and deprivation constitute a denial of the conditions necessary for freedom. If a citizen cannot meet their basic needs, their capacity to act autonomously is severely compromised. Therefore, the state’s duty to provide for the needy is not a discretionary act of charity, but a strict duty of justice required to maintain the equal freedom of all citizens. Under this framework, coercive taxation to support the needy is not a violation of individual rights; rather, it is the necessary mechanism by which the state fulfills its independent moral duty of beneficence and secures the universal condition of right.

However, the alternative framework defending individual rights offers a starkly different interpretation of the Categorical Imperative and the principles of freedom. This framework, often aligned with a strict libertarian or rigorous Kantian reading, argues that the state’s moral duties are limited to the protection of negative rights—specifically, the rights to life, liberty, and property. From this perspective, the Categorical Imperative’s second formulation, which demands that we treat humanity never merely as a means, provides a powerful shield against coercive state beneficence. If the state coerces a taxpayer to fund welfare programs for the needy, it instrumentalizes the taxpayer, using their labor and property as a mere means to fulfill the state’s independent beneficence. The taxpayer’s autonomy is violated because their will is overridden by the collective will of the state, which dictates how their resources must be allocated.

The principles of equality and liberty, as articulated in Kant’s moral philosophy, further constrain the legitimacy of such state action. Liberty, in the Kantian sense, is fundamentally negative liberty—the freedom of the individual from external interference by others, including the state. Positive liberty, or the freedom to achieve certain states of being (such as economic security), is not a right that can be coerced into existence without violating the negative liberty of others. Equality, similarly, must be understood as the equal sovereignty of each citizen over their own person and faculties, not as an equal distribution of outcomes or resources. The alternative framework argues that coercive taxation for the needy violates the principle of equality because it imposes an unequal burden on the taxpayer, forcing them to sacrifice their own autonomy and property to serve the ends of another. In this view, the state’s legitimate actions are strictly constrained to the enforcement of the framework of right, which guarantees non-interference, rather than extending into the realm of positive beneficence, which requires interference.

The crux of the dilemma regarding coercive taxation to support the needy lies in the distinction between the state’s duty to secure the framework of right and its duty to exercise beneficence. Kant sharply distinguishes between duties of right (*jure*) and duties of virtue (*sua sponte*). Duties of right are strict, universally binding, and enforceable; they concern the external actions of individuals that are compatible with the freedom of all according to a universal law. Duties of virtue, on the other hand, concern the disposition of the will to promote the happiness of others, and they are not enforceable. If the state possesses an independent moral duty of beneficence, as Rosen argues, then it must be a duty of right, because only duties of right can be coercively enforced. But can beneficence truly be a duty of right? The alternative framework argues that it cannot. Beneficence, by its very nature, must be voluntary to be moral; a coerced act of beneficence is a contradiction in terms, as it strips the agent of the moral choice to be generous. Therefore, when the state coerces a citizen to be beneficent through taxation, it commits a moral wrong by violating the citizen’s autonomy.

To resolve this tension, one must examine the role of the united will versus popular majority consent in authorizing public laws. Kant’s political philosophy, particularly in his *Doctrine of Right*, grapples with the question of how laws can be legitimate if they are imposed on those who do not consent to them individually. Kant rejects the social contract theory that grounds law in the actual consent of each individual, recognizing that such a consent is practically impossible for a vast polity. Instead, Kant invokes the concept of the united will. The united will is not the sum of individual private wills, nor is it the will of the majority; rather, it is the will of all as it ought to be, legislating from the perspective of a single, unified rational agent. The united will is the source of legitimate law because it legislates universally, respecting the freedom of every citizen as an end in itself.

Popular majority consent, by contrast, is merely the aggregation of private, subjective wills. A law passed by a popular majority does not necessarily conform to the Categorical Imperative, because the majority may vote to violate the rights or autonomy of the minority. If the state relies on popular majority consent to authorize coercive taxation for the needy, it risks legitimizing the instrumentalization of the minority for the perceived benefit of the majority. Under Rosen’s interpretation, the state’s duty of beneficence is grounded in the united will, not in majority rule. Rosen argues that because the state embodies the united will, its laws are legitimate even if they coerce individuals, provided they are universalizable and respect the freedom of all. Thus, for Rosen, coercive taxation for the needy is justified because it is an expression of the united will legislating for the conditions of freedom.

Yet, the alternative framework argues that this conflation of the united will with state beneficence is fundamentally flawed. The united will, in Kant’s philosophy, is the will that legislates for the freedom of all, which primarily entails the non-interference of each citizen’s autonomy. The united will cannot legitimately will that the state engages in positive beneficence at the expense of individual autonomy, because doing so would violate the very freedom that the united will is meant to secure. If the majority of citizens vote to tax the minority to support the needy, this does not constitute the united will; it constitutes a tyranny of the majority that violates the principle of equality. The minority taxpayer is treated as a mere means to the end of the majority’s conception of the good, rather than as an end in themselves. Therefore, positive state welfare measures authorized by popular majority consent lack moral legitimacy, and the state cannot claim an independent duty of beneficence that overrides the individual rights of its citizens.

This brings us to the central dilemma: do positive state welfare measures inherently violate individual freedom under Kant’s Metaphysics of Morals? The answer, from the perspective of the alternative framework defending individual rights, is an unequivocal yes. Kant’s Metaphysics of Morals establishes that the moral worth of an action lies in its conformity to the Categorical Imperative, which demands that we act only on maxims that we can will to be universal laws. The maxim of coercive taxation to support the needy cannot be universalized without contradiction. If universalized, it would destroy the very institution of private property and voluntary exchange upon which a free society depends, reducing the state to a mechanism of forced redistribution that negates the autonomy of the producer. Furthermore, the maxim violates the Formula of Humanity by treating the taxpayer merely as a means to the end of the state’s beneficence.

Rosen might counter that the maxim of taxation for the needy can be universalized if understood as a necessary condition for the equal freedom of all. In this reading, the maxim is not "take from the rich to give to the poor," but rather "legislate an external framework in which all citizens have the material conditions necessary to exercise their autonomy." However, this counter-argument relies on a specific empirical claim about what conditions are necessary for autonomy, which is inherently contestable and risks expanding the scope of state coercion indefinitely. The alternative framework argues that Kant’s Metaphysics of Morals provides a strict boundary: the state must guarantee the framework of right (non-interference), but it cannot legitimately coerce citizens to fulfill positive duties of virtue, such as beneficence. Positive welfare measures inherently violate individual freedom because they transform the state from a guarantor of negative liberty into an active agent of positive intervention, thereby coercing citizens to act as benefactors against their will.

The alternative framework further argues that the state’s role as the bearer of the united will does not grant it the authority to exercise beneficence coercively. The united will legislates for the freedom of all, and the only way to ensure this freedom is to strictly limit the state’s coercive powers to the enforcement of rights. If the state expands its role to include an independent duty of beneficence, it necessarily encroaches upon the sovereignty of the individual, turning them into a mere instrument of the state’s moral agenda. This not only violates the principle of liberty but also undermines the principle of equality, as it imposes a disproportionate burden on those whose property is seized, while treating them as subordinate to the state’s paternalistic aims. In Kantian terms, the state cannot legitimately claim a right to the labor or property of its citizens beyond what is necessary to secure the framework of right, because to do so would be to treat them as property of the state, contrary to the Formula of Humanity.

Moreover, the alternative framework highlights the epistemological and moral impossibility of the state fulfilling an independent duty of beneficence without violating individual rights. Beneficence requires a sensitive, context-dependent judgment about what others need and how best to help them. When the state attempts to fulfill this duty coercively, it replaces the voluntary, empathetic response of the individual with a mechanistic, bureaucratic allocation of resources. This bureaucratic coercion strips the act of its moral character, transforming what might be a genuine duty of virtue into a mere exercise of state power. The alternative framework contends that the moral dignity of beneficence lies in its voluntary nature; when coerced by the state, it ceases to be beneficence in the moral sense and becomes merely a transfer of wealth sanctioned by law. Therefore, the state’s independent duty of beneficence is not only unjustifiable from a rights-based perspective, but it also corrupts the very moral virtue it purports to promote.

In conclusion, the evaluation of whether the state possesses an independent moral duty of beneficence reveals a profound tension within Kantian political philosophy. While Allen D. Rosen’s interpretation offers a compelling argument that the state, as the guarantor of the conditions of freedom and the bearer of the united will, has a duty of beneficence that justifies coercive taxation, the alternative framework defending individual rights provides a more rigorous adherence to the Categorical Imperative and the principles of freedom. The CI, through its demands of universalizability and the treatment of humanity as an end in itself, strictly constrains the state’s legitimate actions to the framework of right. Coercive taxation to support the needy instrumentalizes the taxpayer, violates the principle of liberty by interfering with their autonomous use of their property, and breaches the principle of equality by imposing an unequal burden on the individual. The distinction between the united will and popular majority consent further underscores that welfare measures authorized by majority rule lack moral legitimacy, as they fail to respect the autonomy of the minority. Ultimately, positive state welfare measures inherently violate individual freedom under Kant’s Metaphysics of Morals, because the state cannot legitimately coerce citizens to exercise the virtue of beneficence. The state’s moral duty is not one of independent beneficence, but of securing the conditions under which individuals are free to exercise their own beneficence voluntarily.
