**The Weight of What Might Have Been: Resolving the Dialectic of Future-Like-Ours**

Don Marquis’s “Why Abortion is Immoral” remains one of the most influential and debated contributions to the philosophy of bioethics. In his landmark essay, Marquis proposes the “future-like-ours” (FLO) account, arguing that the wrongness of killing rests not on the sanctity of life itself, nor on the possession of a soul, but on the catastrophic deprivation of the victim’s future. According to Marquis, killing is wrong because it deprives the victim of “a future like ours”—a future containing the value of all the experiences, activities, projects, and enjoyments that constitute a typical human life. While Marquis’s argument elegantly sidesteps the complexities of personhood and consciousness by focusing on the value of the future itself, it has drawn substantial philosophical fire. Peter McInerney, Robert Lovering, and Walter Sinnott-Armstrong have each raised formidable objections concerning personal identity, continuous consciousness, and moral rights, respectively. By scrutinizing these critiques, we are forced to confront a fundamental metaphysical and normative question: Does the normative weight of killing rest on the privation of actualized futures, or the deprivation of unrealized possibilities? By analyzing thought experiments involving the isolated hermit and the paraplegic victim, we can resolve the dialectical tension between actual future ownership and potential future possibilities, ultimately determining whether abortion constitutes an immoral loss.

Marquis’s FLO account operates by transposing the wrongness of killing from the present state of the victim to the future state of the victim. He argues that what makes killing an irrevocable wrong is that it robs the individual of the value of their entire future. Crucially, Marquis does not require that the victim actually possess a fully realized, actualized future at the moment of death; rather, the wrongness lies in the loss of the *unrealized possibilities* contained within that future. For Marquis, the value of a future is intrinsic to the potential experiences it contains, regardless of whether those experiences have been actualized yet. This framework was designed to capture the intuitive wrongness of killing adults and children alike, bypassing the counterintuitive implication that killing a teenager is less wrong than killing an infant because the teenager has actualized more of their life. However, by focusing on the deprivation of unrealized possibilities, Marquis inadvertently opens the door to the objection that the moral weight of killing depends entirely on the actualized subject who owns that future.

Peter McInerney’s objection targets the metaphysical foundations of the FLO account by raising the problem of personal identity. McInerney argues that for a deprivation to be a wrong *against a specific person*, there must be a continuous psychological identity linking the present victim to the future whose future is being stolen. If personal identity is constituted by psychological continuity, then the entity that exists at the time of death must be the same entity that will exist in the future to enjoy that future. McInerney utilizes the thought experiment of the isolated hermit to illustrate this point. Imagine a person who is born and immediately isolated on a deserted island, never interacting with another human being and never developing the psychological continuity that constitutes their personal identity as a social, rational agent. Does this hermit have a future-like-ours? If the hermit is killed, is it wrong because it deprives the hermit of *their* future?

McInerney’s point is subtle but devastating. If the hermit lacks the psychological continuity that grounds their identity as a person, then the "future" that is lost is not strictly *theirs*. The deprivation is tragic, but it is not a wrong against a specific, continuous self. The normative weight of killing, McInerney contends, rests on the privation of an *actualized* future that belongs to an actualized person. The unrealized possibilities of the hermit do not constitute a moral loss against the hermit if the hermit, as a continuous psychological entity, does not exist. Applied to the fetus, McInerney’s objection suggests that the fetus, lacking the continuous psychological identity of the adult whose future it shares, does not possess a future-like-ours that is properly its own. The fetus may have a future that is *like* ours, but it does not have *our* future. Therefore, the deprivation of the fetus's unrealized possibilities does not constitute a wrong against the fetus as a person, because the fetus lacks the actualized future ownership that grounds the moral claim.

Robert Lovering’s objection shifts the focus from the metaphysical identity of the subject to the phenomenological requirements of a future-like-ours. Lovering argues that the value of a future is intrinsically tied to continuous consciousness. A future-like-ours is valuable precisely because it is a future that *we* will experience, filled with the qualia of pleasure, the satisfaction of projects, and the richness of conscious life. Lovering introduces the thought experiment of the paraplegic victim to test the limits of this requirement. Consider a person who has been a paraplegic since birth, lacking continuous consciousness of the physical activities and sensory experiences that constitute a typical human future—such as walking, running, or feeling the ground beneath their feet. If this paraplegic is killed, have they been deprived of a future-like-ours? 

Lovering argues that the paraplegic *does* have a FLO, because they possess continuous consciousness of the experiences they *do* have—their relationships, their intellectual pursuits, their emotional life. The wrongness of killing the paraplegic rests on the privation of their actualized consciousness and the unrealized possibilities remaining within their continuous stream of experience. However, Lovering draws a crucial distinction: the normative weight of killing rests on the actualized continuity of consciousness that anchors the unrealized possibilities. A fetus, lacking continuous consciousness altogether, does not possess the experiential substrate required for a future-like-ours. To deprive a fetus of its unrealized possibilities is not to deprive a conscious subject of a future; it is merely to prevent the emergence of a future that could never have been experienced by the entity as it currently exists. The wrongness of killing, therefore, is not about the destruction of potential experiences in the abstract, but the interruption of an actualized conscious life that has a stake in its own continuation. Without continuous consciousness, there is no subject to be wronged by the deprivation of unrealized possibilities.

Walter Sinnott-Armstrong’s objection targets the normative and ethical dimension of the FLO account, focusing on the concept of moral rights. Even if we grant that a fetus has a future-like-ours and that killing deprives it of that future, Sinnott-Armstrong argues, this does not make killing the fetus morally wrong unless the fetus possesses the moral right to that future. Rights, Sinnott-Armstrong contends, are not merely descriptive of value; they are normative claims that ground moral duties. The wrongness of killing is fundamentally a violation of the victim's right to their future. Sinnott-Armstrong revisits the isolated hermit thought experiment to dismantle the FLO account’s reliance on unrealized possibilities. If a hermit is completely isolated, does the hermit have a moral right to their future? If the hermit is killed, the world loses the value of the hermit's unrealized possibilities, but is the hermit's death a moral wrong *against* the hermit?

Sinnott-Armstrong argues that moral rights require a subject capable of bearing them, and that the moral community is what confers and recognizes these rights. The isolated hermit, despite lacking social ties, is still a moral agent or a rights-bearer, and thus their killing is a wrong against them. However, the fetus lacks the actualized moral agency and the status of a full rights-bearer. For Sinnott-Armstrong, the deprivation of unrealized possibilities is not inherently immoral; it becomes immoral only when it violates the moral rights of a subject. Abortion, in this view, deprives the fetus of unrealized possibilities, but because the fetus lacks the moral rights that would make this deprivation a wrong *against* the fetus, abortion does not constitute an immoral loss. The normative weight of killing rests not on the abstract value of the future, but on the violation of the specific moral rights of the actualized subject.

The dialectical tension between actual future ownership and potential future possibilities crystallizes when we synthesize these three objections. On one side of the tension stands the Marquisian view that the normative weight of killing rests on the deprivation of unrealized possibilities—the sheer value of the future that is lost. On the other side stands the synthesis of McInerney, Lovering, and Sinnott-Armstrong, who argue that the normative weight of killing rests on the privation of an *actualized* future owned by a continuous, conscious, rights-bearing subject. The isolated hermit and the paraplegic victim serve as the crucial tests for resolving this tension. 

Consider the isolated hermit. If the normative weight of killing rested solely on the deprivation of unrealized possibilities, the hermit’s death would be a profound moral tragedy, for the world is deprived of the value of the hermit's unrealized future. However, McInerney and Sinnott-Armstrong remind us that the hermit's moral claim to that future depends on the hermit's actualized psychological continuity and moral status. The hermit is wronged because *they* are the actualized subject whose future is being stolen. The unrealized possibilities only acquire normative weight when they are tethered to an actualized owner. Without the actualized owner, the unrealized possibilities are merely potential values in the abstract, not moral losses against a person.

Now consider the paraplegic victim. Lovering’s analysis shows that the paraplegic possesses continuous consciousness, which serves as the actualized anchor for their unrealized possibilities. The paraplegic is deprived of the actualized consciousness that will experience the future, and thus the deprivation of their unrealized possibilities is a wrong against them. The paraplegic's case demonstrates that actualized consciousness is the necessary condition for the moral weight of unrealized possibilities. The fetus, lacking continuous consciousness, is akin to the hermit lacking psychological continuity: it possesses unrealized possibilities, but it lacks the actualized subject that gives those possibilities moral weight. 

The resolution of this dialectical tension requires us to reject the notion that the normative weight of killing rests exclusively on the privation of actualized futures *or* the deprivation of unrealized possibilities in isolation. Rather, the normative weight of killing rests on the *intersection* of the two: the deprivation of unrealized possibilities *as they are owned and anchored by an actualized subject*. The wrongness of killing is not merely the destruction of a future-like-ours in the abstract; it is the violation of a continuous, conscious, rights-bearing subject's claim to their own future. The actualized subject is the necessary vessel that gives moral weight to the unrealized possibilities. Without the actualized subject, the unrealized possibilities are merely potentialities devoid of moral claim.

When we apply this resolution to the ethics of abortion, the dialectic compels a specific conclusion. Does abortion constitute an immoral loss? Under a strict Marquisian framework that privileges the deprivation of unrealized possibilities, abortion is immoral because it deprives the fetus of a future-like-ours. However, when we integrate the objections of McInerney, Lovering, and Sinnott-Armstrong, this conclusion collapses. The fetus lacks the actualized psychological continuity (McInerney), continuous consciousness (Lovering), and moral rights (Sinnott-Armstrong) that would allow it to *own* the unrealized possibilities of its future. The fetus is a bearer of potential, but not an actualized subject with a claim to a future. Therefore, the normative weight of the loss is absent. Abortion terminates a potential future-like-ours, but it does not wrong a person by depriving them of their future. The loss is tragic in a metaphysical sense—the world loses a possible human life—but it is not an immoral loss against a moral subject.

Some may argue that this resolution is a form of speciesism or a failure of compassion, suggesting that the potential for a future-like-ours is sufficient for moral status. Yet, this objection fails to grapple with the profound differences between actualized and potential realities. If the normative weight of killing rested purely on potential, then the isolated hermit and the irreversibly comatose patient would possess identical moral weight to the conscious adult, rendering the distinctions drawn by McInerney and Lovering meaningless. The hermit and the paraplegic are protected not merely because they *could* have a future, but because they *actually* possess the continuous consciousness and identity that makes that future *theirs*. The fetus, by contrast, lacks this actualized ownership. 

In conclusion, Don Marquis’s future-like-ours account offers a powerful framework for understanding the wrongness of killing, but it is fundamentally incomplete without an account of the subject who owns the future. The objections of McInerney, Lovering, and Sinnott-Armstrong collectively demonstrate that the normative weight of killing cannot rest solely on the deprivation of unrealized possibilities. Personal identity requires actualized psychological continuity; the value of a future requires continuous consciousness; and moral rights require an actualized rights-bearing subject. By analyzing the isolated hermit and the paraplegic victim, we see that the moral loss of killing is inextricably tied to the actualized owner of the future. The dialectical tension between actual future ownership and potential future possibilities resolves in favor of the actualized subject: unrealized possibilities only acquire moral weight when they are claimed by a continuous, conscious, and rights-bearing person. Consequently, abortion does not constitute an immoral loss, for it deprives a potential person of unrealized possibilities, but it does not violate the rights or wrong an actualized subject. The moral weight of killing rests not on the future that might have been, but on the person who owns it.
