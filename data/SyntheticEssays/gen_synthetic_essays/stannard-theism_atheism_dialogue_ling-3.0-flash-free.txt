**The Dialectical Failure of Theism and Atheism: Epistemological Rules, Ethical Maxims, and the Impossibility of Objective Truth**

The debate between theism and atheism is often presented as a contest of objective truth claims, where one side asserts the existence of a divine being and the other denies it. Yet a closer examination of the philosophical underpinnings of this debate reveals a more troubling dynamic: the argument is frequently not a pursuit of ontological reality, but a dialectical failure driven by the imposition of epistemological rules. The rules of evidence, parsimony, and burden of proof, rather than pointing toward objective truth, function as procedural gatekeepers that render the debate circular and ultimately unresolvable. By evaluating Scott A. Shalkowski’s critiques of the presumption of atheism, analyzing Michael Scriven’s defense of atheism through the lens of everyday reasoning and sense-certainty, and incorporating G.W.F. Hegel’s account of the confrontation between Faith and Enlightenment in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, it becomes evident that the critics in this debate commit profound communicative failures by disguising ethical assumptions as epistemic principles. Consequently, the tension between transcending contextual origins and acknowledging the genesis of epistemic frameworks can only be resolved by accepting that all epistemic rules ultimately emerge from post-contextual ethical maxims, and that the philosophy of religion can never escape its contextual origins in the pursuit of objective truth.

The presumption of atheism, most famously articulated by J.J.C. Mackie and defended by Michael Scriven, posits that atheism is the default rational position, and that the burden of proof rests entirely upon the theist. Scott A. Shalkowski mounts a rigorous critique of this presumption, arguing that it relies upon a triad of epistemological rules—parsimony, verifiability, and the burden of proof—that do not establish objective truth but rather dictate the terms of a rigged debate. Shalkowski’s first critique targets the principle of parsimony, or Ockham’s razor, which dictates that we should not multiply entities beyond necessity. In the context of theism versus atheism, parsimony is wielded as a rule to favor the naturalistic worldview over the supernatural. However, Shalkowski argues that parsimony is a heuristic, a pragmatic rule of thumb for scientific inquiry, not an ontological truth. When applied to the ultimate question of existence, parsimony becomes a dogmatic rule that arbitrarily excludes the divine by definitional fiat. The theist is not merely adding an entity to an already complete ontology; they are proposing a fundamentally different framework of reality. To apply parsimony as an epistemic rule is to assume the very metaphysical framework—naturalism—that is under dispute, thereby transforming a contested metaphysical claim into an unquestionable procedural norm.

Shalkowski’s second critique addresses the criterion of verifiability, which demands that meaningful statements be empirically testable. The atheistic presumption often relies on the logical positivist notion that claims about God are cognitively meaningless because they cannot be verified by sensory experience. Yet Shalkowski demonstrates that verifiability is itself an epistemological rule born of a specific historical and scientific context, not an objective standard of truth. By elevating verifiability to a supreme epistemic principle, the critic effectively disqualifies any discourse that operates outside the bounds of empirical science, including metaphysics, ethics, and theology. This is a dialectical failure because it does not refute the theistic claim; it merely alters the rules of the game so that the theistic claim cannot even be recognized as a legitimate participant. The debate ceases to be about what is true and becomes about what fits within a narrow, procedurally defined epistemic framework.

Finally, Shalkowski critiques the shifting of the burden of proof. The presumption of atheism demands that the theist provide positive evidence for God, while the atheist is exempt from providing positive evidence for the non-existence of God. Shalkowski argues that this procedural rule is an ethical maxim masquerading as an epistemic principle. It assumes that the default state of human inquiry is skepticism toward the supernatural, which is a culturally and historically contingent ethical stance, not an objective epistemic rule. By framing the burden of proof as an epistemic imperative, the atheist critic disguises their ethical preference for a naturalistic worldview as a neutral rule of rational discourse. Thus, Shalkowski’s critiques reveal that the presumption of atheism does not rest on objective truth; it rests on a set of epistemological rules that function as dialectical traps, ensuring that the debate is decided by procedural maneuvering rather than by the truth of the matter.

Michael Scriven’s defense of atheism provides a fascinating case study in how unexamined presuppositions about everyday reasoning and sense-certainty underpin the atheist’s epistemic stance. Scriven argues that atheism is the rational default because, in the absence of evidence, one should withhold belief in God, just as one withholds belief in the tooth fairy. His defense relies heavily on the epistemology of everyday reasoning—the common-sense assumption that the world operates according to regular, natural laws and that we should trust our ordinary experience. Scriven’s atheism is grounded in the presupposition that the natural world is all that exists, a presupposition he treats as the baseline of rational thought. However, this baseline is not a neutral epistemic starting point; it is a presupposition rooted in the immediate certainty of everyday life.

To understand the depth of Scriven’s presupposition, we must invoke Hegel’s concept of sense-certainty (*Sinnesgewißheit*), which he explores at the very beginning of the *Phenomenology of Spirit*. Sense-certainty is the immediate, unreflective certainty of the "this-here," the pure givenness of the present moment. In everyday reasoning, the atheist operates from a form of secularized sense-certainty: the immediate, unquestioned certainty that the material world is all there is, that the laws of nature are uniform, and that empirical observation is the ultimate arbiter of truth. Scriven’s defense of atheism relies on this unreflective trust in the everyday. He assumes that because the natural world behaves predictably and consistently, there is no need to posit a transcendent ground. But this reliance on everyday reasoning is precisely what Hegel critiques in sense-certainty: it is the certainty of the immediate that fails when subjected to the demands of universality and mediation. The everyday atheist, like the figure of sense-certainty, clings to the immediate "this" without recognizing that their certainty is already mediated by a vast, unexamined framework of naturalistic presuppositions. Scriven’s atheism, therefore, is not a product of objective epistemic rules but an extension of the unreflective, practical certainty of everyday life, elevated to the status of a metaphysical principle without acknowledgment of its contingent origins.

It is here that Hegel’s account of the confrontation between Faith and Enlightenment in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* becomes indispensable for examining whether the critic commits communicative failures by disguising ethical assumptions as epistemic principles. In Hegel’s narrative, the Enlightenment (*Aufklärung*) represents the principle of the understanding (*Verstand*), which seeks to grasp the absolute in the finite, to render everything transparent to reason through rules and categories. Faith (*Glaube*), by contrast, represents the standpoint of feeling and the immediate certainty of the divine, which clings to the transcendent and the unconditioned. The Enlightenment claims to have overcome the immediacy of Faith by substituting it with the universality of rational rules. In the theism/atheism debate, the atheist is the Enlightenment: they demand that the theistic claim be subjected to the universal rules of reason—parsimony, verifiability, and burden of proof. The theist is Faith: they cling to the immediate certainty of revelation and transcendence.

The communicative failure occurs because the Enlightenment (the atheist critic) believes it is operating from a neutral, objective epistemic standpoint, while in reality, it is enforcing an ethical maxim. For Hegel, the Enlightenment’s demand for self-certainty is fundamentally an ethical stance; it is the desire of the subject to be the absolute ground of its own law, to achieve autonomy through the mastery of reason. When the atheist critic wields parsimony or verifiability as objective epistemic rules, they are not engaging in neutral truth-seeking; they are asserting the ethical maxim of rational autonomy and naturalism. They disguise this ethical assumption as an epistemic principle, thereby committing a profound communicative failure. The theist, operating from the ethical maxim of theocentric dependence and the immediate certainty of faith, is dismissed as "irrational" or "unscientific." But this dismissal is not an epistemic judgment; it is an ethical one. The critic cannot recognize the theist’s standpoint because the Enlightenment’s self-certainty requires the negation of all that is immediate and transcendent. The debate becomes a dialectical failure because the atheist refuses to acknowledge that their epistemic rules are themselves the expression of an ethical commitment to the autonomy of reason, and the theist refuses to acknowledge that their faith is an expression of an ethical commitment to theocentric dependence. Neither side can reach objective truth because both are trapped in the mutual negation of their ethical stances, each disguised as a neutral epistemic rule.

This analysis leads us to the central tension posed by the prompt: can the rules governing the philosophy of religion ever transcend their contextual origins, or do all epistemic frameworks ultimately emerge from post-contextual ethical maxims? The dialectical failure of the theism/atheism debate suggests that the former is impossible. Epistemic rules such as parsimony, verifiability, and burden of proof cannot transcend their contextual origins because they are not objective truths floating in a vacuum; they are historically and culturally situated maxims that have been universalized and detached from their immediate ethical context. Parsimony emerged from a context of intellectual economy and the practical need to navigate a complex world without infinite proliferation of hypotheses. Verifiability emerged from the context of the scientific revolution, where the authority of empirical observation was an ethical imperative against dogmatic authority. The burden of proof emerged from the context of legal and democratic discourse, where the ethical maxim of protecting the innocent required the prosecution to prove its case. These rules were originally ethical maxims for living in a particular historical world; they were then abstracted into epistemic principles and falsely treated as objective truths.

Therefore, all epistemic frameworks ultimately emerge from what we might call post-contextual ethical maxims. A post-contextual ethical maxim is one that has been lifted out of its immediate, historical context and presented as a universal, timeless principle, yet it still bears the structural imprint of its genesis. The maxim of parsimony is a post-contextual ethical maxim: it originated in the ethical desire for intellectual simplicity and practical efficiency, but it is now presented as an objective epistemic rule. When the atheist critic invokes parsimony to defeat the theist, they are not appealing to objective truth; they are appealing to a post-contextual ethical maxim that has been severed from its original ethical context. The same applies to the maxim of verifiability, which originated in the ethical desire to avoid dogmatism and subject claims to communal scrutiny, but is now treated as an objective criterion of meaning.

The resolution of the tension, then, is not to find a neutral epistemic framework that transcends context, but to recognize that the philosophy of religion is an arena where post-contextual ethical maxims clash. The theism/atheism debate is not a failure because it fails to reach objective truth; it is a failure because the participants mistakenly believe they are operating from objective truth at all. The dialectical failure is driven by the illusion of epistemic neutrality, a product of the Enlightenment’s desire to present its ethical maxims as objective rules. To resolve the debate, one must perform an act of philosophical self-reflection akin to Hegel’s *Aufhebung* (sublation): the rules of the debate must be both negated and preserved. We must negate the pretension that parsimony or verifiability are objective truths, while preserving their utility as procedural tools within their proper context. 

Ultimately, the rules governing the philosophy of religion cannot transcend their contextual origins because they are not rules of logic or mathematics; they are rules of life, born from the ethical struggles of communities attempting to make sense of their world. The atheist critic who demands verifiability is not asking an objective question about the nature of reality; they are asking the theist to conform to the ethical maxims of a naturalistic worldview. The theist who appeals to revelation is not making an irrational leap; they are appealing to an ethical maxim of dependence on the transcendent. The debate between theism and atheism, therefore, is not a dialectic between truth and falsehood, but a dialectic between competing ethical maxims that have been disguised as epistemic principles. The communicative failure is inevitable as long as both sides refuse to acknowledge the ethical genesis of their own epistemic rules. The path forward is not to win the debate by imposing one set of epistemological rules over another, but to recognize that all epistemic frameworks are ultimately expressions of our ethical commitments to the world. Objective truth remains elusive in this domain not because of our cognitive limitations, but because the very rules we use to seek it are the artifacts of our ethical striving.
