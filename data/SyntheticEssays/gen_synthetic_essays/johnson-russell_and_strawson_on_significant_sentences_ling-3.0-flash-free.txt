The problem of denoting phrases—specifically those that purport to refer to objects that do not exist—has long stood as a central puzzle in the philosophy of language. When a speaker utters a sentence such as “The present King of France is bald,” they appear to employ a grammatically well-formed, syntactically meaningful sentence that nonetheless fails to latch onto any extant object in the world. The question that arises is how such a sentence can possess significance, and what its truth value might be. In the early twentieth century, this problem catalyzed a profound dialectical tension between Bertrand Russell’s logical analysis of denoting phrases and P. F. Strawson’s pragmatic distinction between the use of a sentence and the sentence itself. Russell, in his landmark 1905 essay “On Denoting,” sought to resolve the paradoxes of empty reference by reducing denoting phrases to quantificational logical forms, thereby preserving the law of excluded middle and assigning a definitive truth value to sentences containing empty terms. Strawson, in his influential 1950 rebuttal “On Referring,” challenged Russell’s conflation of significance and truth value, arguing that the sentence itself may lack a truth value while remaining perfectly significant through its use in assertion. This essay evaluates the dialectical tension between these two frameworks and assesses whether a unified framework combining Russell’s logical analysis with Strawson’s theory of reference can successfully resolve the intertwined problems of truth values and significance in sentences containing empty referring terms. It will be argued that while a unified framework provides a richer, multi-layered account of reference that successfully illuminates the conditions of significance, it ultimately fails to definitively resolve the problem of truth values because the tension between semantic truth conditions and pragmatic truth-value attribution is structurally irreconcilable.

To understand the dialectical tension, one must first fully grasp the architecture of Russell’s theory of denoting phrases. Russell’s central insight is that denoting phrases—such as “the present King of France”—do not function as referential terms that stand for objects in the world, whether existent or non-existent. Instead, they are incomplete symbols whose meaning is entirely derived from their logical context within a sentence. For Russell, the meaning of a denoting phrase is exhausted by the logical conditions under which it can be eliminated. In “On Denoting,” Russell demonstrates that the sentence “The present King of France is bald” can be analyzed into a complex logical proposition that asserts three things: (1) there exists at least one present King of France, (2) there is at most one present King of France (uniqueness), and (3) that individual is bald. Formally, this is represented as $\exists x (Kx \land \forall y (Ky \to y=x) \land Bx)$. 

Russell’s analysis yields a decisive resolution to the problem of truth values. Because the sentence is logically equivalent to the conjunction of existence, uniqueness, and the predicate, the falsity of the existence clause renders the entire conjunction false. Consequently, “The present King of France is bald” is simply false. Furthermore, the negation of the sentence, “The present King of France is not bald,” is logically equivalent to $\exists x (Kx \land \forall y (Ky \to y=x) \land \neg Bx)$. Since the existence clause is false, the negation of the predicate over an empty domain yields a vacuous truth, making the negation true. Russell thus preserves classical logic and the law of excluded middle: the sentence is false, its negation is true, and neither is meaningless. Significance, for Russell, is secured not by the reference of the denoting phrase to an object, but by the logical structure of the sentence as a whole. The sentence is meaningful because it makes a determinate claim about the world, and it is false because that claim is not satisfied by the facts.

Strawson’s critique, articulated in “On Referencing,” strikes directly at the heart of Russell’s logical edifice. Strawson concedes that Russell’s analysis captures something about the logical form of sentences, but he argues that Russell fundamentally mistakes the nature of significance and truth value. Strawson’s primary target is what he calls Russell’s “referential theory of meaning,” the assumption that the primary bearer of truth value is the sentence itself, and that the meaning of a sentence is exhausted by its truth conditions. Strawson argues that we must distinguish between the *sentence* and the *use of a sentence*. The sentence “The present King of France is bald” is an abstract string of words; it possesses a certain sense or significance because we understand the grammatical and semantic rules governing its constituent parts. However, when a speaker actually produces this sentence in a conversational context, they are performing an act of assertion. It is the act of assertion—the *use* of the sentence—that is the primary bearer of truth value.

Strawson’s crucial move is to decouple significance from truth value. A sentence can be perfectly significant and meaningful without having a truth value at all. When a speaker utters “The present King of France is bald” in a context where there is no King of France, the speaker is not asserting a false proposition; rather, the act of assertion is defective because the necessary presupposition—that there is a present King of France—has failed. The sentence itself, stripped of its use, remains significant; it carries meaning and can be evaluated for its logical properties. But the act of asserting it in a context where the presupposition fails results in a truth-value gap: the utterance is neither true nor false. Strawson further refines the theory of reference by distinguishing between the *referential* and *attributive* uses of a denoting phrase. In a referential use, the speaker intends to pick out a specific individual via the description; if no such individual exists, the referential act fails. In an attributive use, the speaker is merely describing whoever fits the description, and the truth of the sentence depends on whether anyone fits it. This distinction highlights that the meaning and truth conditions of a sentence are not purely logical but are deeply embedded in the speaker’s intentions and the context of utterance.

The dialectical tension between Russell and Strawson thus crystallizes around the ontological status of the sentence and the nature of truth. Russell insists on a semantic primacy: the sentence is the unit of meaning and truth, and truth is an objective relation between the sentence and the world, independent of the speaker. Strawson insists on a pragmatic primacy: the act of assertion is the unit of meaning and truth, and truth is a property of what the speaker says, not of the abstract sentence in isolation. For Russell, the problem of empty terms is a logical problem, solvable by analyzing the sentence into its constituent quantifiers and predicates. For Strawson, the problem of empty terms is a pragmatic problem, arising from the mismatch between the presuppositions of the speaker’s assertion and the reality of the world. Russell eliminates the problem of empty terms by rendering them false; Strawson dissolves the problem by rendering them truth-valueless. The tension is not merely a difference of opinion, but a fundamental clash between a logic of truth and a logic of assertion.

Given this irreconcilable tension, the question arises whether a unified framework can be constructed that synthesizes Russell’s logical rigor with Strawson’s pragmatic insights. Such a framework would need to account for the logical structure of denoting phrases (Russell) while also acknowledging the contextual determination of truth values in actual speech acts (Strawson). A promising attempt at this synthesis involves treating denoting phrases as possessing a dual-layered significance: a logical-semantic layer and a pragmatic-contextual layer. On this view, the logical form of a sentence containing a denoting phrase establishes its truth conditions in the abstract, as Russell argued. The sentence “The present King of France is bald” has the logical form $\exists x (Kx \land \forall y (Ky \to y=x) \land Bx)$, which provides the semantic conditions under which the sentence would be true or false. However, the pragmatic layer, following Strawson, dictates that the actual assignment of a truth value to an utterance of this sentence depends on the context of assertion. In a context where the speaker intends to refer to an existing individual, the utterance is evaluated against the logical truth conditions. In a context where the speaker merely entertains a hypothetical description, the utterance may be used in a way that suspends the demand for a truth value, much like a mathematical supposition.

This unified framework can indeed be seen as resolving the problem of *significance*. By combining Russell’s analysis with Strawson’s pragmatic theory, we can explain how empty referring terms are meaningful without invoking Meinongian objects or resorting to the notion of incomplete symbols that lack a referent altogether. The denoting phrase “the present King of France” is significant because it contributes to the logical form of the sentence, providing the conditions under which the sentence would be true, and because it serves a referential or attributive function in the speaker’s communicative act. The unified framework thus preserves the logical structure that Russell deemed necessary for meaning, while incorporating Strawson’s insight that meaning is inseparable from the use of language in context. Significance is no longer a purely logical affair, nor a purely pragmatic one, but an interplay between the logical syntax of denoting phrases and the semantic-pragmatic function of assertion.

However, when we turn to the problem of *truth values*, the unified framework encounters profound difficulties that suggest it fails to achieve a definitive resolution. The core of the problem is whether a sentence containing an empty referring term can simultaneously possess determinate logical truth conditions (Russell) and lack a truth value in pragmatic use (Strawson). The unified framework attempts to accommodate both by making truth values context-dependent. In contexts where the referent exists, the sentence is true or false according to Russell’s logical analysis. In contexts where the referent is missing, the sentence lacks a truth value according to Strawson’s pragmatic analysis. Yet, this resolution merely relocates the tension rather than dissolving it. By making truth values relative to the context of assertion, the unified framework undermines the objectivity of truth that Russell’s logical analysis was designed to secure. If the truth value of a sentence depends on the speaker’s pragmatic intentions, then logic becomes hostage to the contingencies of conversation. The Law of Excluded Middle, which Russell fought so hard to preserve, becomes a context-sensitive heuristic rather than a universal logical law.

Furthermore, the unified framework struggles with the paradoxes that arise from the interaction of the logical and pragmatic layers. Consider the sentence “The present King of France is not bald.” Under Russell’s pure logical analysis, this sentence is true because the existential quantifier fails, making the negation vacuously true. Under Strawson’s pragmatic analysis, the sentence lacks a truth value because the presupposition fails. The unified framework must choose between these two outcomes, or it must adopt a disquotational or contextualist stance that says the sentence is true *in logic* but lacks a truth value *in use*. But this split creates a new problem: if the sentence is logically true, what does it mean to say it lacks a truth value? A truth-value gap is itself a truth value assignment (or lack thereof), which implies that the sentence has a specific semantic status. The unified framework cannot coherently maintain that a sentence is both logically true (Russell) and pragmatically truth-valueless (Strawson) without falling into contradiction. The very attempt to unify the two theories exposes the incompatibility of their foundational commitments: Russell’s commitment to bivalence and the sentence as the primary truth-bearer, and Strawson’s commitment to the context-dependence of truth and the assertion as the primary truth-bearer.

The failure of the unified framework to resolve the problem of truth values does not, however, render the synthesis philosophically worthless. The dialectical tension between Russell and Strawson reveals that the problem of empty referring terms is not a single, monolithic problem, but a composite of two distinct problems: the problem of logical truth conditions and the problem of pragmatic significance. Russell’s logical analysis provides the necessary formal apparatus for understanding the semantic structure of language and the conditions under which sentences about non-existent objects can be evaluated. Strawson’s pragmatic distinction provides the necessary corrective to the assumption that logical truth conditions exhaust the function of language in communication. A unified framework that acknowledges this duality—treating logical truth conditions as the abstract, idealized structure of meaning, and pragmatic truth-value attribution as the concrete, contextual act of communication—offers the most comprehensive account of language we currently possess. It successfully resolves the problem of significance by showing that empty terms are meaningful both logically and pragmatically. It partially resolves the problem of truth values by contextualizing them, but it ultimately fails to resolve them definitively because the very concepts of “logical truth” and “pragmatic truth value” operate on incompatible planes.

In conclusion, the dialectical tension between Russell’s theory of denoting phrases and Strawson’s distinction between the use of a sentence and the sentence itself represents a foundational fault line in analytic philosophy. Russell’s logical analysis demonstrates that sentences containing empty referring terms are not meaningless but are instead false, thereby preserving the integrity of classical logic. Strawson’s pragmatic critique demonstrates that sentences can be significant yet lack truth values, thereby preserving the richness of language as a tool for communication rather than a mere instrument of logical calculus. A unified framework that attempts to combine these two approaches can successfully illuminate the layered nature of significance, showing how denoting phrases function both as logical operators and as vehicles for pragmatic reference. However, this unified framework cannot successfully resolve the problem of truth values and significance in sentences containing empty referring terms, because the resolution requires a compromise between the bivalent, objective truth conditions of logic and the context-sensitive, pragmatic truth-value gaps of assertion. The tension between Russell and Strawson is not a historical artifact to be overcome, but an enduring philosophical problem that reflects the irreducible duality of language as a logical structure and a communicative practice.
