## The Function of Ambiguity: A Critical Assessment of Kemp’s Proposition-Function Modification of Davidson’s Paratactic Theory

Donald Davidson’s paratactic theory of indirect discourse stands as one of the most provocative and influential proposals in the philosophy of language. By treating the sentence “Galileo said that the earth moves” as a conjunction of two distinct speech acts—an utterance of “Galileo said that” and an utterance of “The earth moves”—Davidson sought to dissolve the apparent intensionality of propositional attitude reports. The demonstrative “that” refers to the subsequent utterance token, and the main clause asserts a relation of “samesaying” between the original speaker and that token. This theory elegantly avoids the need for propositions as abstract entities, but it founders on a notorious ambiguity problem: the demonstrative’s referent is unclear, and the “samesaying” relation is notoriously vague. Gary Kemp has proposed a significant modification, arguing that the demonstrative refers not to an utterance token, but to a *proposition-function*—a function from contexts to propositions. This essay evaluates Kemp’s modification, arguing that while it successfully addresses the formal ambiguity of indexical shifting, it fails to resolve the deeper ambiguity of deliberate or context-dependent meaning. Furthermore, I will contend that Kemp’s account, and the paratactic framework as a whole, rests on a fundamentally flawed syntactic premise: the treatment of “that” as a demonstrative rather than a complementizer. Cross-linguistic evidence from Hungarian, a language with a rigid distinction between demonstratives and complementizers, provides a decisive empirical refutation of this premise, thereby undermining the theoretical viability of the entire paratactic project.

### 1. Davidson’s Paratactic Theory and the Genesis of the Ambiguity Problem

Davidson’s proposal, first articulated in “On Saying That,” is a radical departure from Fregean and Russellian accounts of indirect discourse. The central insight is that the logical form of “Galileo said that the earth moves” is not a two-place relation between Galileo and a proposition, but rather a paratactic conjunction of two independent sentences. The first sentence, “Galileo said that,” contains a demonstrative pronoun “that,” which points to the second sentence, “The earth moves.” The overall truth conditions are determined by whether Galileo’s original utterance stands in a relation of “samesaying” to the utterance of the second sentence. Davidson’s motivation is to avoid the “third realm” of propositions, which he finds ontologically suspect, and to provide a compositional semantics grounded in the concrete tokens of speech.

However, this theory immediately confronts a cluster of ambiguities. The most pressing is the *reference* of the demonstrative. Does “that” refer to the *token* utterance produced by the reporter, or to the *type* of utterance? If it refers to the token, then the theory must account for the fact that the token is a physical event occurring at a specific time and place, and that its content is dependent on the context of its production. If it refers to the type, then the theory reintroduces a form of abstraction that Davidson sought to avoid. A more serious ambiguity arises with indexicals. Consider the report, “John said that I am tall.” If “that” refers to the reporter’s utterance token, then the indexical “I” correctly refers to the reporter. But what if John originally said, “I am tall”? In that case, the original utterance had a different context, and the “I” referred to John. The paratactic theory must posit a relation of “samesaying” that somehow abstracts away from the specific context of the original utterance, but it is unclear how a relation between two concrete utterance tokens can achieve this without invoking the very propositions it seeks to eliminate.

This is the core of the ambiguity problem: the theory is ambiguous about *what* the demonstrative points to (token or type) and *what* the “samesaying” relation is supposed to preserve (the truth conditions, the content, the mode of presentation, or the illocutionary force). Davidson’s own response is to invoke the notion of “samesaying” as a primitive, but this merely relocates the problem. The relation must be defined in a way that handles indexicals, quantifiers, and ambiguity, yet any such definition seems to require a robust notion of propositional content, which the theory was designed to avoid. Consequently, the paratactic theory appears to be caught in a dilemma: either it is too vague to provide determinate truth conditions, or it implicitly relies on the very propositions it rejects.

### 2. Kemp’s Proposition-Function Modification

Gary Kemp’s modification, developed in his work on Davidson and meaning, attempts to resolve the indexical and token/type ambiguity by replacing the referent of the demonstrative. Kemp argues that “that” does not refer to an utterance token, but to a *proposition-function*. A proposition-function is a function from contexts (or indices) to propositions. In the report “John said that I am tall,” the demonstrative “that” refers to a function \( f \) such that for any context \( c \), \( f(c) \) is the proposition that the speaker of \( c \) is tall. When the reporter utters the sentence, the context of the report supplies the value of the function, yielding the proposition that the reporter is tall. This elegantly handles the indexical problem: the function abstracts away from the specific context of the original utterance, while the report’s context determines the actual proposition.

Kemp’s modification also offers a potential solution to the token/type ambiguity. The proposition-function is not a concrete token nor a mere type; it is a structured abstract entity that maps possible contexts to propositions. This allows the theory to preserve the compositionality of the report without committing to the existence of a single, determinate proposition as the object of the attitude. The “samesaying” relation can now be defined as a relation between the original speaker and the proposition-function, rather than between two utterance tokens. Specifically, the relation holds if the original speaker’s utterance expresses a proposition that is the value of the function at the relevant context.

This modification is a significant improvement over Davidson’s original theory. It provides a principled account of indexical shifting, which was a major source of ambiguity. Furthermore, it offers a way to handle *de dicto* and *de re* readings. For a *de dicto* reading, the proposition-function might map contexts to propositions that include a specific mode of presentation; for a *de re* reading, it might map to a proposition about the individual directly. By allowing the function to be sensitive to the context of the report, Kemp can account for the fact that the same utterance can be reported in different ways.

### 3. The Persistent Problem of Deliberate Ambiguity

Despite these advances, Kemp’s proposition-function modification fails to resolve the most intractable form of ambiguity: deliberate or context-dependent ambiguity in the original utterance. Consider the case where John utters the sentence, “The bank is high.” This sentence is lexically ambiguous, meaning either the financial institution is elevated or the riverbank is elevated. Suppose John deliberately utters this sentence to convey both meanings simultaneously, perhaps as a pun or a double entendre. A reporter, Mary, later says, “John said that the bank is high.” Under Kemp’s account, the demonstrative “that” refers to a proposition-function. But which function? There are at least two functions: one that maps contexts to the proposition about the financial institution, and another that maps contexts to the proposition about the riverbank. Mary’s report must select one of these functions to be the referent of “that.” If she selects the first, the report is true only if John samesaid the proposition about the financial institution, which is only half of what he said. If she selects the second, the report is similarly incomplete. If she selects neither, then the demonstrative fails to have a determinate referent, and the report is semantically underdetermined.

Kemp might respond that the proposition-function could be a function that maps contexts to a *set* of propositions, or perhaps a disjunction. However, a function, by definition, is single-valued. A function that maps a context to a set of propositions is not a proposition-function in the intended sense; it is a set-function. Moreover, even if we allowed such a function, the report “John said that the bank is high” would then assert that John samesaid the set of both propositions, which is a different claim from saying he said either one. The report would be true only if John intended to assert both, which is a specific and unusual speech act. Deliberate ambiguity, however, does not always involve asserting both meanings; it often involves leaving the meaning indeterminate, allowing the hearer to choose. The reporter, in such a case, is faced with a dilemma: either she reports one of the determinate meanings (thereby misreporting John’s speech act), or she reports the ambiguity itself (which requires a non-functional referent).

This failure is not merely a technical quibble. It reveals a fundamental flaw in the proposition-function approach. The ambiguity of the original utterance is not a property of the context in which it is reported; it is a property of the utterance’s content. Kemp’s modification shifts the locus of ambiguity from the utterance to the function, but the function must be specified in a way that determines a unique proposition for each context. If the original utterance is ambiguous, there is no unique proposition to serve as the value of the function. The function, therefore, cannot be defined without presupposing a disambiguation of the original utterance. But the whole point of indirect discourse is often to report what was said *without* disambiguating it. A theory that forces the reporter to disambiguate in order to pick a referent for the demonstrative is a theory that cannot adequately handle the phenomenon of deliberate ambiguity.

Furthermore, Kemp’s account struggles with *context-dependent* ambiguity beyond lexical homonymy. Consider a case where John utters a sentence that is only meaningful relative to a specific set of background assumptions or conversational implicatures. For example, John says, “It’s cold in here,” intending to convey that someone should close the window. Mary reports, “John said that it’s cold in here.” The proposition-function would map the context of the report to the proposition that it is cold in here. But John’s utterance also carried an implicature that the window should be closed. Does the report capture this? Kemp’s account, like Davidson’s, is a theory of *semantic* content, not pragmatic implicature. However, the ambiguity problem in indirect discourse often concerns the boundary between what is said and what is implicated. If the reporter intends to report the implicature, she would need a different proposition-function. Kemp’s theory provides no principled way to determine which function is intended, leaving the report ambiguous in a way that is not resolved by the context of the report.

### 4. The Syntactic Objection: Demonstrative vs. Complementizer

The philosophical and semantic problems with Kemp’s modification are compounded by a more fundamental syntactic objection. Both Davidson’s original theory and Kemp’s modification rely on the claim that the word “that” in indirect discourse is a demonstrative pronoun. This is a crucial syntactic assumption. In the paratactic theory, “that” functions as a deictic term that points to the following sentence. However, in standard generative grammar, the “that” in “John said that the earth moves” is not a demonstrative; it is a *complementizer*—a functional head that introduces a subordinate clause. The complementizer “that” does not refer to anything; it serves a purely syntactic function, marking the clause as a complement of the main verb.

The evidence for this distinction is overwhelming. First, the complementizer “that” cannot be stressed, whereas the demonstrative “that” can be contrastively stressed. One can say, “I want *that* book,” but not “John said *that* the earth moves” with stress on “that” to refer to the following clause. Second, the complementizer cannot be used anaphorically. The demonstrative “that” can refer back to a previously mentioned utterance, as in “John said that, and then left.” But the complementizer cannot be used in this way; it is not a pronoun. Third, the complementizer is often optional, whereas the demonstrative is not. One can say “John said the earth moves” without “that,” but one cannot say “John said the earth moves” while intending “the earth moves” to be a separate sentence. Finally, the complementizer participates in syntactic phenomena such as *that*-trace effects, where the presence of “that” blocks extraction of the subject. For example, “Who did John say that left?” is ungrammatical for many speakers, whereas “Who did John say left?” is acceptable. This is a purely syntactic constraint that has no analogue for demonstratives.

The paratactic theory, by treating “that” as a demonstrative, must posit a radical restructuring of the syntax of English. It would have to claim that the surface string “John said that the earth moves” is actually a conjunction of two separate sentences, with an implicit demonstrative pronoun. This is not merely a notational variant; it is a claim about the psychological reality of the grammar. If the complementizer “that” is a distinct syntactic category from the demonstrative “that,” then the paratactic theory is simply false as a description of the logical form of English sentences. Kemp’s modification does not address this syntactic issue. He simply substitutes a proposition-function for an utterance token as the referent of the demonstrative, but he retains the claim that “that” is a demonstrative. If “that” is actually a complementizer, then the entire paratactic framework collapses, regardless of what the demonstrative is supposed to refer to.

### 5. Cross-Linguistic Evidence from Hungarian

The syntactic objection is not merely a theoretical preference; it is empirically confirmed by cross-linguistic data. Hungarian provides a particularly illuminating case. In Hungarian, indirect discourse is introduced by the complementizer *hogy* (meaning “that” as a conjunction). The demonstrative pronoun *az* (that) is a distinct lexical item, used for deictic reference. Crucially, Hungarian does not use the demonstrative to introduce a subordinate clause. For example, the English sentence “John said that the earth moves” is rendered in Hungarian as:

*János azt mondta, hogy a föld mozog.*
(John that-acc said, that the earth moves.)

Here, *azt* is the accusative form of the demonstrative *az*. It is a cataphoric pronoun that refers to the upcoming *hogy*-clause. However, the complementizer *hogy* is a separate particle. The syntax of the sentence is clearly subordinating: the *hogy*-clause is a subordinate clause embedded under the main verb *mondta* (said). It is not a paratactic conjunction of two independent sentences.
