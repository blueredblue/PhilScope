The question of what constitutes a rule has long haunted philosophy, tempting the mind toward an endless search for an essential core that defines its very nature. The claim that rules are family-resemblance concepts—that they resist analytic definition and can only be understood through the practice of learning by example—strikes at the heart of our rational self-image. We naturally assume that to understand a rule, we must possess a clear, explicit definition of it, a set of necessary and sufficient conditions that delineate its boundaries. Yet, Ludwig Wittgenstein’s later philosophy dismantles this assumption, arguing that the meaning of a rule is not an abstract, static essence but is inextricably bound to its application in action. Through the lens of Wittgenstein’s account of rule-following, illuminated by the musical practices of Schubert’s staccato notes and Beethoven’s tempo, we can see that the meaning of a rule is constituted entirely by its performance, and that the tension between seeking an essence and recognizing that instruction, imitation, and the capacity to "go on" exhaust the content of rule-following can be resolved by accepting that the rule is nothing other than the living tissue of its practice.

To evaluate the claim that rules are family-resemblance concepts, we must first understand Wittgenstein’s critique of essentialism. In the *Philosophical Investigations*, Wittgenstein famously rejects the idea that concepts possess a fixed essence that unifies all their instances. He illustrates this with the concept of "game," noting that board games, card games, Olympic games, and children’s games share only overlapping and criss-crossing similarities—what he calls "family resemblances." There is no single feature common to all games, nor is there a boundary that sharply defines what a game is; rather, the concept extends and blurs at its edges. Rules, Wittgenstein suggests, operate similarly. We might be tempted to define a rule analytically as a normative proposition that dictates a specific course of action, but such a definition fails upon inspection. Legal rules, grammatical rules, game rules, and musical rules all exhibit family resemblances. A traffic law and a rule of counterpoint share the superficial trait of prescribing behavior, yet they differ radically in their domains and enforcement mechanisms. 

The failure of analytic definition stems from a deeper grammatical illusion. When we ask for the essence of a rule, we are misled by the grammar of the word "rule." The noun "rule" suggests an object, an entity that exists independently of its use, waiting to be discovered. But Wittgenstein urges us to look at the use of words rather than their etymology or surface grammar. A rule is not a hidden object behind the action; it is the action itself, embedded in a form of life. Consequently, rules resist analytic definition because definition requires abstracting a rule away from its context of use, which kills the very thing it purports to define. To extract the "rule of staccato" from the living practice of a musician is to produce a hollow formula, like saying "staccato means to play notes detached." But this formula does not tell a novice *how* to play detached; it merely gestures toward the practice. The meaning of the rule is not contained in the formula; it is realized in the execution.

This brings us to the heart of Wittgenstein’s argument: the meaning of a rule is tied directly to its application in action. Nowhere is this more vividly illustrated than in the domain of music, where rules are not merely theoretical prescriptions but are enacted in real time by the performer. Consider the staccato markings found in the compositions of Franz Schubert. In a musical score, a staccato dot above or below a note is a rule: it instructs the performer to shorten the note. But what does "shorten" mean? Is it half the value? A third? An eighth? Analytic definition fails here because the rule cannot be reduced to a mathematical fraction. The meaning of Schubert’s staccato is not contained in the dot itself, but in the *action* of performing it. Schubert’s staccato is often singing, light, and conversational, requiring the pianist or string player to execute a specific gesture that lifts the bow or depresses the key with a particular weight and release. The rule of Schubertian staccato is understood only through the practice of learning by example, by listening to a master and imitating the articulation until the physical gesture becomes second nature. The rule is not the dot; the rule is the sound and the motion produced by the dot.

Similarly, Beethoven’s tempo markings present a profound challenge to the idea that rules can be defined analytically. When Beethoven writes "Allegro" or "Poco moto," he is issuing a rule about the speed of the music. Yet, tempo is notoriously resistant to precise definition. "Allegro" is not a metronome marking; it is an affective and kinetic instruction. The tempo of a Beethoven sonata is not a fixed mathematical value but a dynamic, breathing entity that shifts with the phrase structure, the emotional arc, and the acoustic space of the concert hall. To understand the rule of Beethoven’s tempo is not to consult a dictionary definition of "fast," but to *do* the fast. The conductor and the performer internalize the rule through the physical act of keeping time—the beating of the baton, the swaying of the body, the pulse in the fingers. The meaning of the tempo rule is exhausted by its application in action. When a musician follows Beethoven’s tempo, they are not applying an abstract definition to a physical action; they are enacting the rule. The rule *is* the action.

Wittgenstein’s point is that to follow a rule is to do something, and the meaning of the rule is constituted by the doing. This view dissolves the illusion that there is a hidden essence of the rule, a secret definition that we grasp intellectually before we translate it into physical behavior. In the act of playing Schubert’s staccato or conducting Beethoven’s Allegro, the rule and the application are one. The rule is not a blueprint that precedes the action; it is the action itself, unfolding in time. This is why rules are family-resemblance concepts: just as games are unified not by a common essence but by overlapping practices, rules are unified by the overlapping ways they are applied in the living practices of individuals and communities. The meaning of a rule is the network of its uses, and to isolate the rule from its use is to drain it of its meaning entirely.

This perspective forces us to confront a deep tension at the heart of rule-following: the tension between the rational desire to seek an essence and the empirical reality that instruction, imitation, and the capacity to "go on" exhaust the content of rule-following. On one hand, human beings are driven by the philosophical urge to define, to pin down concepts with precision, to capture the "essence" of a rule so that it can be transmitted with certainty. On the other hand, Wittgenstein’s famous rule-following paradox demonstrates that this certainty is impossible. If I am given a rule and a single instance of its application, I am never logically compelled to apply the rule in exactly the same way the next time. There is always the possibility of a different, yet equally valid, interpretation. The "sceptic" might argue that I can never truly know if I am following the rule correctly, because any justification for my action would require another rule, which would itself require justification, leading to an infinite regress.

Wittgenstein resolves this scepticism not by providing an essentialist foundation for rules, but by shifting our perspective from the solitary mind to the social, practical context of rule-following. We learn rules not by grasping an essence, but by being instructed, by imitating, and by developing the capacity to "go on"—to continue a pattern of action consistently. When a child learns the rule of staccato, they do not receive a definition; they receive an example, a demonstration, and a correction. The teacher plays the note, the child imitates, and the teacher says, "Not like that, like this." Through this iterative process, the child learns how to go on. They develop a "feel" for the rule, an embodied sensibility that guides their future actions without requiring an explicit analytic definition.

The tension between seeking an essence and recognizing the sufficiency of instruction and imitation is resolved when we realize that the "exhaustion" of rule-following by practice is not a tragic limitation of human reason, but the very condition of its possibility. If the content of rule-following were exhausted by instruction, imitation, and the capacity to "go on," then these practices do not merely point toward a hidden essence; they constitute the rule entirely. The capacity to "go on" is not a separate faculty that we use to apply an essence to a new situation; it is the very fabric of the rule itself. When we say we know how to "go on," we mean that we have been formed by a practice such that our future actions will naturally accord with the rule. The rule is the habit, the trained response, the shared understanding of a community.

In music, this is profoundly clear. A novice violinist does not learn the rule of Beethoven’s tempo by reading a philosophical treatise on the essence of tempo. They learn it by imitating the conductor, by feeling the pulse in the orchestra, by practicing the physical motions required to execute the beat. The "rule" of the tempo is exhausted by the instruction (the conductor’s beat), the imitation (the orchestra following the beat), and the capacity to "go on" (maintaining the pulse through the entire movement). If we were to ask the violinist, "What is the essence of the tempo rule?" they might be confused. They know the rule not as an abstract concept, but as a living, breathing action that sustains the music. The meaning of the rule is the action, and the action is the rule. The tension dissolves because the question itself is a grammatical confusion; we mistakenly assume that because we can speak of a rule, there must be a "thing" called a rule that exists independently of its use.

This leads to a deeper philosophical implication: the meaning of a rule is not a private, mental object that the individual holds in their mind before acting. The rule is not a "mental rule" that is then translated into physical action. Rather, the rule is the action, the entire network of dispositions, habits, and social practices that constitute the application of the rule. When Wittgenstein says that the rule is what you do with it, he means that the rule does not exist prior to its application. The application is the rule coming into being. This is why the family resemblance of rules is so pervasive: rules are not natural kinds with fixed essences; they are tools in the toolbox of human life, shaped by the needs and practices of the communities that wield them. The staccato of Schubert and the tempo of Beethoven are not governed by an essence that exists independently of their performance; they are constituted by the performance itself. The rule is the sound, the gesture, the breath, and the shared understanding of the musicians.

To say that instruction, imitation, and the capacity to "go on" exhaust the content of rule-following is not to reduce rules to mere mechanical habits, devoid of meaning or rationality. Rather, it is to recognize that rationality itself is a form of life. The capacity to "go on" is not blind repetition; it is the creative, responsive continuation of a practice that is sensitive to context, audience, and expressive intent. When a pianist plays Schubert’s staccato, they are not mechanically shortening notes; they are responding to the musical phrase, shaping the articulation to fit the emotional arc of the song. The rule provides the boundaries of the practice, but the application is an art, an intelligent responsiveness to the particular demands of the moment. The meaning of the rule is not a fixed essence that the artist consults; the meaning is generated in the act of intelligent application. 

Thus, the claim that rules are family-resemblance concepts that resist analytic definition and can only be understood through learning by example is not merely a valid philosophical position; it is a profound insight into the nature of human rationality and culture. The attempt to define rules analytically is a category mistake, a confusion of grammar that treats dynamic practices as static objects. By looking at the living application of rules in music, we see that the meaning of a rule is its use, and its use is an action. The tension between seeking an essence and recognizing the sufficiency of practice is resolved when we abandon the notion that rules are hidden essences waiting to be discovered, and accept that rules are the very activities that constitute our shared forms of life. Instruction, imitation, and the capacity to "go on" do not merely approximate the essence of a rule; they are the rule itself, breathing and alive in the hands of those who follow it.
