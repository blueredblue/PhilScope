# The Will's Inclination Toward the Universal Good: Aquinas, Volitionalism, and the Acquisition of Christian Faith

## I. Introduction: The Problem of Faith's Origin

The acquisition of Christian faith presents a philosophical puzzle of considerable depth. Unlike knowledge, which seems to require sufficient evidence to compel the intellect's assent, faith involves the acceptance of propositions whose evidential basis is, at least from the perspective of unaided reason, insufficient to produce certitude in the manner of scientific demonstration. How, then, does one come to hold such propositions as true? This question has occupied philosophers and theologians for centuries, and it is at the heart of evaluating the claim that Thomas Aquinas advances what might be termed an "indirect descriptive volitionalism" regarding the origin and nature of faith.

The term "indirect descriptive volitionalism" encodes several important commitments. "Volitionalism" signals that the will—the rational appetite or desire for the good—plays a constitutive and primary role in the act of faith, rather than being merely subordinate to intellectual processes. "Descriptive" indicates that Aquinas is not prescribing a method by which one ought to acquire faith, but rather analyzing how faith actually operates within the human person. "Indirect" specifies the causal mechanism: the will does not directly dictate the content of belief, but rather moves the intellect to assent to propositions whose evidence it finds insufficient to compel belief on its own terms. This essay evaluates Aquinas's account along these dimensions, examining how the will moves the intellect, the metaphysical identity between being and goodness invoked to defend the account against charges of epistemic unjustifiedness, and the philosophical tension generated by situating faith within the will's orientation toward the universal good rather than within direct intellectual demonstration. Ultimately, this essay argues that the resolution of this tension lies in the theological and moral transformation of the human agent's heart and desires—a transformation that reconfigures both the intellect's capacity to perceive evidence and the will's orientation toward the ultimate end of human existence.

## II. The Epistemological Problem: Insufficient Evidence and the Demand for Assent

Before examining Aquinas's solution, it is essential to articulate the problem he addresses. The epistemological challenge of faith can be stated simply: the propositions of Christian faith—the Trinity, the Incarnation, the Resurrection—are not demonstrable by natural reason in the way that the conclusions of geometry or natural science are demonstrable. They exceed the evidential resources available to the unaided human intellect. Yet the Christian tradition insists that faith is not irrational, not arbitrary, and not blind. Faith, as the Hebrews 11:1 tradition has it, is "the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." The question, then, is how assent to such propositions can be rational, justified, or even intelligible if the intellect alone cannot generate the motivation to believe.

Aquinas addresses this problem by drawing a fundamental distinction between the speculative intellect, which operates by necessity when presented with adequate evidence, and the practical will, which operates by choice in the absence of such necessity. In natural knowing, the intellect assents to what it perceives as true because the evidence compels it; the intellect cannot withhold assent from what it clearly perceives. But in matters of faith, the evidence is not of this compulsive character. The intellect sees partial reasons, hints, and invitations, but not the full demonstration that would render assent necessary. In this gap between insufficient evidence and the act of belief, Aquinas locates the operation of the will.

The will, for Aquinas, is the rational appetite—the desire for the good as apprehended by reason. Unlike the intellect, which is directed toward truth, the will is directed toward goodness. Every act of the will is an act of desiring or choosing something perceived as good. When the intellect encounters propositions whose evidence is insufficient to compel belief, it is the will that steps in, not to fabricate reasons where none exist, but to move the intellect toward assent on the basis of a prior inclination toward the good that those propositions promise. The will, in other words, does not believe for no reason; it believes because it has been drawn toward the good that faith proposes, and this drawing moves the intellect to assent.

## III. Indirect Descriptive Volitionalism: The Mechanism of Will-to-Intellect Movement

The claim that Aquinas advances an "indirect descriptive volitionalism" requires careful unpacking. The indirectness of the account lies in the causal structure of faith's genesis. The will does not directly produce belief as though it were a mechanical act of fiat. Rather, the will moves the intellect through a complex mediation involving grace, habituation, and the presentation of reasons. Aquinas distinguishes between the primary and secondary movers in the act of faith. God, as the primary mover, moves the human will through grace. The human will, moved by grace, then moves the intellect to assent. The intellect, in turn, assents not because the evidence is overwhelming but because it has been moved by the will's inclination toward the good that faith represents.

This is indirect in the sense that the will does not supply the evidential content of faith. The intellect still performs its proper function of considering the propositions of faith, weighing the reasons for and against them, and determining whether they are worthy of assent. But the intellect's assessment of the reasons is itself shaped and moved by the will's prior orientation. The will, desiring the universal good—God Himself—sees the propositions of faith as good and worthy of acceptance, and this perception of goodness moves the intellect to assent even when the intellectual evidence alone would not suffice.

The descriptiveness of the account is equally important. Aquinas is not offering a normative theory of how one ought to come to faith, but rather analyzing the actual structure of the act of faith as it occurs in the believer. He describes faith as an act of the intellect moved by the will, and this description is grounded in his broader metaphysical framework concerning the relationship between intellect and will, between reason and desire. The will does not replace the intellect in faith; it completes and elevates it. The intellect still judges, still weighs, still considers—but it does so within a framework of desire that directs its attention and shapes its assessment of what counts as sufficient reason.

This account has significant implications. It suggests that the acquisition of faith is not a purely cognitive achievement but involves the whole human person, including the affective and appetitive dimensions. One does not simply weigh the evidence and, if it tips sufficiently, arrive at faith. Rather, one is drawn toward faith by a prior inclination—whether natural or supernaturally grace-enhanced—toward the good that faith promises. The will's movement toward this good is the condition under which the intellect finds the evidence sufficient.

## IV. The Metaphysical Identity of Being and Goodness: A Defense Against Epistemic Unjustifiedness

The philosophical objection most frequently leveled against volitionalist accounts of faith is that they render belief epistemically unjustified. If the will moves the intellect to assent to propositions whose evidence is insufficient, is this not a form of wishful thinking? Is it not irrational to believe on the basis of desire rather than on the basis of evidence? These charges assume that rationality requires the intellect to operate independently of the will, that belief should be determined solely by the strength of the evidence, and that any movement of the will into the domain of belief introduces arbitrariness or bias.

Aquinas's defense against these charges rests on a profound metaphysical claim: the identity between being and goodness, captured in the scholastic maxim "bonum et ens convertuntur." In Aquinas's metaphysics, the transcendentals—being, unity, truth, and goodness—are not merely analogous properties that beings happen to possess; they are convertible, meaning that to be is to be good, and to be good is to be. The ultimate being, God, is ipsum esse subsistens—subsistent being itself—and this being is also the summum bonum, the universal good. The goodness of God is not a quality that God happens to have alongside His existence; it is identical with His being, which is His act of existing.

This metaphysical identity has profound implications for the epistemology of faith. If being and goodness are convertible, then the intellect's apprehension of being and the will's apprehension of goodness are not two separate cognitive operations but two aspects of a single engagement with reality. The intellect, in knowing that something exists, implicitly grasps its goodness; the will, in desiring something as good, is oriented toward its being. The will's movement toward the universal good is therefore not an arbitrary preference but a rational orientation toward the ultimate reality of things. The will is not overriding the intellect; it is completing the intellect's natural orientation toward truth by directing it toward the ultimate truth, which is the ultimate being and the ultimate goodness.

Furthermore, the identity between being and goodness provides a metaphysical ground for the claim that faith is not epistemically unjustified. The will does not move the intellect to believe in propositions that are disconnected from reality; rather, it moves the intellect to assent to propositions whose referents—the divine persons, the redemptive acts of Christ, the ultimate destiny of the human person—are real beings, real instances of being, and therefore real instances of goodness. The will's inclination toward the universal good is an inclination toward being itself, and the propositions of faith are true descriptions of being. Thus, the will's movement toward faith is not a movement away from truth but a movement toward the deepest truth, which is the being of God.

The objection that faith lacks certainty can also be addressed through this metaphysical framework. Aquinas distinguishes between different species of certitude. Scientific knowledge provides the highest species of certitude, grounded in necessary demonstrations. Faith provides a lower species of certitude, grounded not in the intrinsic evidence of the propositions believed but in the authority of the one who reveals them—God. But this lower species of certitude is not the absence of certitude; it is a genuine species of certainty that is appropriate to the object of faith. Because the one who reveals is the ultimate being, the ground of all truth and goodness, faith's certitude is anchored in being itself. It is not the certainty of the intellect that has exhausted its evidence, but the certainty of the will that has been moved by the ultimate goodness to assent to what the ultimate being has revealed.

## V. The Philosophical Tension: Will, Intellect, and the Direction of Faith

Despite the metaphysical defenses available to Aquinas, a genuine philosophical tension remains at the heart of his account of faith. The tension arises from the proposal that faith operates through the will's inclination toward the universal good rather than through direct intellectual demonstration. If faith is primarily a matter of the will, does this not undermine the intellectual character of faith? Does it not reduce faith to a kind of emotional or volitional commitment that is independent of rational assessment? And if the will moves the intellect, is the intellect still performing its proper epistemic function, or has it been subordinated to a non-epistemic faculty?

This tension is not merely academic. It reflects a deep philosophical divide between traditions that prioritize the intellect in the acquisition of knowledge and traditions that recognize the role of desire, love, and volition in shaping what we take to be true. In the Platonic tradition, knowledge is a kind of vision of the forms, and the intellect's relation to truth is direct and immediate. In the Aristotelian tradition, by contrast, the intellect is more closely tied to the senses and to practical deliberation, and the will plays a constitutive role in the pursuit of knowledge. Aquinas synthesizes these traditions, but the synthesis generates its own tensions.

The core of the tension can be formulated as follows: if the will moves the intellect to assent to propositions whose evidence is insufficient, then the intellect's assent is not fully determined by the evidence. But if assent is not fully determined by the evidence, then it seems that the intellect is not doing its proper epistemic work. The intellect appears to be in a kind of epistemically anomalous state—neither fully knowing (as in science) nor merely opining (as in conjecture), but occupying a third, intermediate state that is sustained by volitional rather than intellectual forces.

Aquinas's response to this tension involves a nuanced account of the different kinds of intellectual acts and the different ways in which the intellect can be moved. The intellect can be moved in two ways: intrinsically, by the evidence of the things known, and extrinsically, by something outside itself, such as the will or a divine illumination. In natural knowledge, the intellect is moved intrinsically. In faith, the intellect is moved extrinsically by the will, but this extrinsic movement does not bypass the intellect's proper function; it elevates and directs it. The intellect in faith still judges, still weighs, still considers—but its judgment is informed and moved by a desire that has been shaped by grace and oriented toward the ultimate good.

This extrinsic movement of the will is not arbitrary because it is grounded in the metaphysical order of reality itself. The will is moved toward faith because faith is directed toward the ultimate being, which is the ultimate good. The will's inclination toward the universal good is not a capricious preference but a rational response to the nature of reality as it is apprehended by the intellect, even if only partially and indirectly. The will sees, in a manner of speaking, what the intellect has not yet fully grasped, and it moves the intellect forward toward a fuller apprehension.

## VI. Resolution Through Transformation: The Theological and Moral Implications

The resolution of the philosophical tension generated by Aquinas's account of faith lies in recognizing that the will's inclination toward the universal good is not a static orientation but a dynamic process that transforms the human agent. Faith is not merely an initial act of will that produces intellectual assent; it is a transformative habit that reconfigures the entire human person—intellect, will, and affections—in relation to God. This transformation is both theological, in that it is wrought by grace, and moral, in that it involves the cultivation of virtues and the ordering of desires.

Theological transformation occurs through the infusion of grace, which elevates the human faculties beyond their natural capacities. Grace does not replace nature but perfects it. In the case of faith, grace heals the intellect's limitation and strengthens the will's inclination toward the good. The infused habit of faith enables the intellect to assent to supernatural truths that would otherwise be inaccessible, and it enables the will to desire these truths as goods proportionate to the human person's ultimate end. Grace is not an external coercion of the intellect but an internal elevation that makes the intellect capable of receiving truths that exceed its natural light.

Moral transformation, by contrast, involves the cultivation of the infused theological virtues—faith, hope, and charity—and their interaction with the acquired moral virtues. Charity, the highest of the theological virtues, is the love of God for God's own sake. It transforms the will's inclination from a merely natural desire for the good into a supernatural love that is ordered toward the beatific vision. As charity grows, it transforms not only the will but also the intellect, enabling the intellect to see the world and its truths in the light of divine love. This is the famous Thomistic claim that charity illuminates the intellect: the love of God disposes the intellect to perceive truths that would otherwise remain hidden.

The transformation of the human agent's heart and desires is thus the key to resolving the tension between will and intellect in faith. The tension arises only if one assumes that the will and the intellect operate as independent faculties whose operations can be assessed separately. But in Aquinas's framework, the human person is a unified substantial form, and the faculties of intellect and will are integral to this unity. The will's movement toward the universal good is not an external imposition on the intellect but an expression of the human person's deepest orientation toward being and truth. When the will is transformed by grace and charity, the intellect is transformed as well, not because the will overrides the intellect but because the will disposes the intellect toward a more adequate apprehension of reality.

This resolution has important implications for the moral life of the believer. If faith is sustained by the will's inclination toward the universal good, then the moral transformation of the agent is not merely a consequence of faith but a condition of its ongoing exercise. Faith without charity is dead, as the epistle of James insists, and this is not merely a theological slogan but a philosophical insight about the relationship between volition and intellect. The person whose will is disordered, whose desires are misdirected, whose heart is divided, cannot sustain the act of faith because the will's inclination toward the universal good is impeded by disordered affections and sinful habits. Conversely, the person whose will is ordered by virtue, whose desires are purified by grace, finds the intellect increasingly capable of assenting to the truths of faith because the intellect is being drawn forward by the will's transformed orientation.

The moral implications extend to the practice of the virtues. The cardinal virtues—prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance—dispose the human agent to perceive and pursue the good rightly. Prudence, in particular, is the virtue that governs the practical intellect, enabling it to discern the appropriate means to the ends desired by the will. In the context of faith, prudence disposes the intellect to weigh the reasons for belief appropriately, not in isolation from the will's orientation but in conjunction with it. The virtues, therefore, are not external to the epistemology of faith but integral to it. They shape the conditions under which the will moves the intellect and the intellect moves the will in a virtuous cycle of mutual reinforcement.

## VII. Conclusion: Faith as the Synthesis of Will and Intellect in the Pursuit of Being

The evaluation of Aquinas's indirect descriptive volitionalism regarding the acquisition of Christian faith reveals a philosophical account of remarkable sophistication and depth. By locating the origin of faith in the will's inclination toward the universal good, Aquinas provides a framework for understanding how assent to propositions whose evidence is insufficient to compel belief can nevertheless be rational, justified, and grounded in the nature of reality itself. The metaphysical identity between being and goodness, far from being an ad hoc defense, is the cornerstone of this account: it establishes that the will's movement toward faith is not an arbitrary preference but a rational orientation toward the ultimate reality of things, which is both being and goodness in their ultimate identity.

The philosophical tension generated by situating faith within the will's operation rather than within direct intellectual demonstration is genuine but resolvable. The resolution lies in recognizing that the will and the intellect are not independent faculties whose operations can be assessed in isolation but are integral dimensions of the unified human person. The will's movement toward the universal good elevates and directs the intellect, and the intellect's apprehension of truth is informed and deepened by the will's orientation toward goodness. This synthesis is not achieved by human effort alone but is wrought by grace, which transforms the human agent's heart and desires, enabling the intellect and will to operate in harmony toward the ultimate end of human existence.

The theological and moral implications of this account are far-reaching. They suggest that the acquisition and sustenance of faith are not merely intellectual achievements but involve the whole human person in a process of transformation that touches the deepest dimensions of desire, virtue, and grace. Faith is not a leap in the dark but a movement of the whole person toward the light of being, a movement that is initiated by the will's desire for the universal good and sustained by the intellect's growing apprehension of the truth that is God. In this sense, Aquinas's account of faith is not merely a theory of belief but a vision of the human person's ultimate vocation: to know and love the God who is both being and goodness, and in that knowledge and love, to find the fulfillment of all desire and the resolution of all intellectual seeking.
