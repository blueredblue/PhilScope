### The Rigid Designator and the Phantom of Description: A Critical Evaluation of Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt Argument

The question of how a proper name—such as “Aristotle” or “Gödel”—manages to pick out a specific object in the world has been a foundational problem in the philosophy of language. For much of the twentieth century, the dominant answer was provided by the descriptivist tradition, championed by Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, and P.F. Strawson. Descriptivism holds that a name is semantically equivalent to a cluster of definite descriptions—that the meaning of “Aristotle” is “the teacher of Alexander the Great” or “the author of the *Nicomachean Ethics*.” The referent of the name is simply whatever object uniquely satisfies those descriptions. This view was challenged with unprecedented force by Saul Kripke in his 1970 lectures, *Naming and Necessity*, where he deployed a series of devastating thought experiments. The most famous of these is the Gödel-Schmidt counterexample, designed to show that descriptions are not only insufficient but entirely unnecessary for successful reference. However, the debate did not end with Kripke. John Searle, a prominent defender of a modified descriptivism, mounted a sophisticated defense, arguing that the causal-historical chains Kripke proposes are themselves saturated with descriptive intentionality. This essay will argue that Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment successfully undermines the classical descriptivism of Frege, Russell, and Strawson, and that Searle’s defense—while philosophically ingenious—ultimately fails because it conflates the *fixing* of reference with the *transmission* of reference. By analyzing the dialectical tension between attributive and referential uses of names, it becomes clear that causal-historical chains, not associated definite descriptions, are the primary engines of reference. Descriptions play a heuristic role in initial baptism, but they do not constitute the semantic content of names, nor do they ground the causal chains that transmit reference across generations of speakers.

#### Section I: The Descriptivist Orthodoxy and Its Core Intuition

To understand the force of Kripke’s attack, one must first appreciate the internal logic of descriptivism. For Frege, the sense (*Sinn*) of an expression is its mode of presentation—the way an object is given to us. The sense of a proper name is a definite description that uniquely identifies the referent (*Bedeutung*). Frege’s classic example of “the morning star” and “the evening star” demonstrates that two expressions can have the same referent but different senses, which explains why identity statements can be informative. Consequently, a name like “Aristotle” has a sense that consists of descriptions like “the student of Plato” or “the founder of the Lyceum.” If we know the sense, we can determine the referent. Russell radicalized this view, arguing that ordinary proper names are not genuine logical constants but are in fact abbreviated definite descriptions. “Aristotle” is shorthand for “the teacher of Alexander the Great who wrote the *Metaphysics*.” In Russell’s theory of descriptions, the sentence “Aristotle was wise” is analyzed as a conjunction of existential claims: “There exists an x such that x taught Alexander, x wrote the *Metaphysics*, and x is unique in these respects, and x is wise.” If no such unique individual exists, the sentence is false, and the name fails to refer. P.F. Strawson softened this view, shifting the focus from the semantics of sentences to the pragmatics of speech acts. For Strawson, referring is something a speaker *does* with a name, and this act presupposes that the audience can supply the relevant descriptive background to identify the referent. Despite their differences, all three theorists share a fundamental commitment: the reference of a name is determined by the satisfaction of associated definite descriptions. The name is a tool for picking out an object by describing its properties.

This descriptivist framework has an intuitive appeal. When we encounter an unfamiliar name, we ask, “Who is that?” The answer typically involves a description: “He is the author of *Naming and Necessity*.” It seems natural to assume that this description is what anchors the name to its bearer. Furthermore, descriptivism elegantly explains how communication is possible—if a speaker and hearer share the same description, they can successfully coordinate reference. However, this very intuition is what Kripke systematically dismantles.

#### Section II: Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt Counterexample and the Semantic Undermining

Kripke’s *Naming and Necessity* presents a three-pronged attack on descriptivism: the modal argument, the epistemic argument, and the semantic argument. The modal argument demonstrates that names are rigid designators—they pick out the same object in all possible worlds. “Aristotle” refers to the actual historical individual, even in a counterfactual world where he never taught Alexander. A definite description, by contrast, is non-rigid; it picks out different objects in different worlds depending on who satisfies it. Thus, if “Aristotle” meant “the teacher of Alexander,” then in a world where Aristotle never taught Alexander, “Aristotle” would refer to someone else, which is absurd. The epistemic argument shows that identity statements involving names are often necessary a posteriori, whereas identity statements involving descriptions are contingent. We can know a priori that “the teacher of Alexander is the teacher of Alexander,” but we cannot know a priori that “Aristotle is the teacher of Alexander.” If the name were synonymous with the description, the latter would be analytic and a priori, which it is not.

The semantic argument, however, is the most directly devastating, and it is here that the Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment resides. Kripke asks us to imagine a counterfactual history. Suppose that the man we call “Gödel” did not actually discover the incompleteness of arithmetic. Instead, a man named Schmidt—a mathematician living in Vienna—proved the incompleteness theorem. Gödel, a cunning and unscrupulous individual, murdered Schmidt and stole his manuscript, publishing it under his own name. Consequently, the definite description “the man who discovered the incompleteness of arithmetic” uniquely refers to Schmidt, not Gödel. Now, consider a speaker who knows nothing of this history and uses the name “Gödel” to refer to the man who discovered the incompleteness theorem. According to classical descriptivism, this speaker’s utterance of “Gödel” should refer to Schmidt, because Schmidt is the unique individual satisfying the description. Yet, intuitively, the speaker intends to refer to Gödel—the man they have heard about, the man they believe discovered the theorem, even though their belief is false. If the speaker were to discover the truth, they would not say, “Ah, so I was actually referring to Schmidt all along.” Rather, they would say, “I was mistaken in my belief about Gödel.” The name “Gödel” continues to refer to the historical Gödel, regardless of which descriptions are true of him.

This thought experiment is not merely a quirk of counterfactual history; it strikes at the heart of the descriptivist thesis. It demonstrates that a name can refer to an object even when the speaker’s associated descriptions are false of that object and true of someone else. The description does not determine the referent; rather, the referent is determined by a causal-historical chain that traces back to an initial baptism. In the actual world, someone pointed to Gödel and said, “Let this man be called ‘Gödel’.” Subsequent speakers acquired the name through a chain of communication—they heard it from someone who heard it from someone else, ultimately reaching the initial baptism. This chain, not any description, is what fixes the reference. Kripke’s argument is so powerful because it shows that descriptivism conflates the *epistemic* role of descriptions (how we identify a referent in our own minds) with the *semantic* role of names (what determines the referent in the language). Even if descriptions are useful for initial identification, they are not constitutive of the name’s meaning.

#### Section III: Searle’s Cluster Theory and the Defense via Intentional Content

Recognizing the vulnerability of classical descriptivism, John Searle offered a sophisticated defense in his paper “Proper Names” (1958) and later responses to Kripke. Searle’s first move is to abandon the idea that a name is equivalent to a *single* definite description. Instead, he proposes a cluster theory: a proper name is associated with a cluster, or family, of descriptions. The referent is the object that satisfies a *sufficiently large* number of these descriptions, or a weighted majority of them. This avoids the immediate problem of a single false description—if “Gödel” is associated with “the discoverer of incompleteness,” “the author of the incompleteness proof,” “a famed logician,” and “a professor at Princeton,” then even if the first description is false, enough others might still pick out Gödel. Searle argues that this cluster is the sense of the name, and it is this sense that determines reference.

However, Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt scenario can be modified to defeat even the cluster theory. We can imagine a scenario where *all* the descriptions we associate with Gödel are false—he was not a logician, he did not discover incompleteness, he was not a professor, and so on. If the cluster theory holds, the name would fail to refer. But intuitively, the name still refers to the historical Gödel. Kripke’s point is that no finite set of descriptions is guaranteed to be true of the referent, because the referent is a contingent individual who could have had entirely different properties.

Searle’s more robust defense, however, focuses on the nature of the causal chain itself. Kripke argues that reference is transmitted through a chain of communication where each speaker intends to refer to the same object as the person from whom they heard the name. Searle seizes on this point. He argues that this “intention to refer to the same object” is itself a mental state with intentional content. Intentionality, in Searle’s philosophy, is the directedness of the mind toward objects and states of affairs. The intention to refer to the same object as one’s predecessor is not a purely causal, mechanical process; it is a contentful mental act. The content of this intention can be expressed as a description: “the object to which my teacher was referring when she said ‘Gödel’” or “the object that stands at the origin of this particular chain of communication.” This is a *metalinguistic* or *indexical* description. It does not describe the intrinsic properties of the referent (e.g., being a logician), but rather its relational properties (e.g., being the source of a particular name).

Searle’s claim is that these metalinguistic descriptions are essential to the causal chain. Without them, the chain would be a mere sequence of physical events—sound waves, neural firings—with no semantic significance. What makes the chain a *chain of reference* rather than a chain of noise is that each speaker has the intention to refer, and this intention has descriptive content. Therefore, the causal-historical theory, when properly analyzed, collapses back into a form of descriptivism. The descriptions are not about the referent’s properties, but about its position in the causal network. Furthermore, Searle appeals to the community: the reference of a name is ultimately determined by the collective intentionality of a linguistic community. The community has a shared set of descriptions about Gödel, and even if individual speakers are ignorant or mistaken, the community’s background assumptions anchor the name. This communal description—a “conceptual scheme” of sorts—provides the intentional content that grounds the causal chain.

#### Section IV: The Dialectical Tension: Attributive vs. Referential Uses

To evaluate Searle’s defense, we must turn to the dialectical tension between attributive and referential uses of definite descriptions, a distinction introduced by Keith Donnellan. In an *attributive* use, a speaker uses a description to refer to whoever or whatever satisfies that description. For example, if I say, “Smith’s murderer is insane,” and I have no idea who the murderer is, I use the description attributively to refer to the unknown person who fits the description. In a *referential* use, a speaker uses a description to refer to a specific individual they have in mind, even if that individual does not actually satisfy the description. If I point at a man in a suit at a party and say, “Smith’s murderer is insane,” but the man in the suit is actually innocent, I am using the description referentially to pick out that specific man.

Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment exploits the attributive use. The speaker in the scenario uses “Gödel” solely because they associate it with the description “the discoverer of the incompleteness theorem.” In this attributive sense, the description would indeed refer to Schmidt. However, Kripke’s point is that proper names are not typically used attributively in this way. When we use a proper name, we are almost always using it *referentially*—we intend to pick out a specific historical individual, regardless of whether our associated descriptions are accurate. The referential use of a name is grounded in a causal connection to that individual, not in a description.

This creates a dialectical tension. If Searle insists that the causal chain is reducible to metalinguistic descriptions, then he must explain how these descriptions themselves acquire their referential force. Consider the metalinguistic description “the object my teacher referred to as ‘Gödel’.” This description is used *attributively*—it refers to whatever object satisfies the condition of being the referent of my teacher’s utterance. But what determines the referent of my teacher’s utterance? It is not a description, but another causal chain leading further back. At some point, we reach the initial baptism, where a speaker pointed at Gödel and named him. This initial baptism is a purely causal, demonstrative act. It does not rely on a description of Gödel’s properties; it relies on the speaker’s perceptual contact with the object. The causal chain, therefore, terminates in a non-descriptive, demonstrative act. Searle’s metalinguistic descriptions merely push the problem back one step. They do not eliminate the need for a causal anchor; they presuppose it.

Moreover, the distinction between attributive and referential uses reveals that Kripke’s argument is not about whether descriptions *can* be used to refer, but whether they are *necessary* for reference. In the Gödel-Schmidt scenario, we can imagine a speaker who has no descriptive content associated with the name “Gödel” at all—they have simply heard the name and intend to refer to the person it names. Even with zero descriptive content, the name refers successfully via the causal chain. This shows that descriptions are not merely insufficient; they are entirely dispensable. Searle’s attempt to inject intentionality into the causal chain fails because the *intention to refer* is not equivalent to a *description of the referent*. An intention can be directed at an object without the agent being able to articulate any individuating description of it. I can intend to refer to “that person over there” without knowing anything about them. This is a de re intention, not a de dicto one.

#### Section V: Why Causal Chains Do Not Depend on Descriptive Intentionality

Searle’s strongest claim is that causal chains of reference rely essentially on descriptive intentionality. He argues that without the intentional content of speakers—their beliefs, desires, and intentions—a sequence of utterances would be meaningless noise. This is true, but it does not vindicate descriptivism. The intentionality required for reference is not *descriptive* intentionality; it is *causal* intentionality. When I intend to refer to the person my teacher called “Gödel,” my intention is not to refer to someone who satisfies a certain description, but to refer to the *source* of my name. This is an indexical, demonstrative intention. It is analogous to pointing my finger and saying “that.” The content of my intention is not a set of properties, but a causal link.

To see this, consider a case where the causal chain is disrupted but the metalinguistic description remains. Suppose I hear the name “Gödel” from a teacher who is actually a robot programmed to speak gibberish, and the name was randomly generated with no connection to any historical individual. In this case, my metalinguistic description “the person my teacher called ‘Gödel’” would fail to refer, because there is no person behind the utterance. The description is vacuously satisfied by nothing. This demonstrates that the description does not *create* the reference; it merely *points* to the causal chain. If the chain is broken, the description is empty. Therefore, the causal chain is conceptually prior to the description. The description is a way of *talking about* the chain, but it is not the ground of the chain’s referential power.

Furthermore, Searle’s appeal to communal descriptions suffers from the same circularity. The community’s shared description of Gödel—say, “the man who discovered incompleteness”—is itself a set of beliefs about a causal individual. If those beliefs are false, the community does not change its referent; it changes its beliefs. The name “Gödel” remains anchored to the historical individual via the causal chain of actual communication. The community’s descriptions are *epistemic* devices—they help speakers identify the referent in their own minds—but they are not *semantic* devices. Kripke’s distinction between fixing the reference and giving the meaning is crucial here. In the initial baptism, a description might be used to fix the reference (e.g., “I name the man who wrote this manuscript ‘Gödel’”). But once the name is introduced, it becomes a rigid designator, independent of that description. Subsequent speakers do not need to know the description; they only need to be part of the causal chain. The chain transmits reference like a baton in a relay race. The baton is the name, and the runners are the speakers. The baton does not change its identity based on the runners’ beliefs about it.

#### Conclusion: The Triumph of the Causal-Historical Theory

In conclusion, Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment delivers a fatal blow to the descriptivist theory of proper names as articulated by Frege, Russell, and Strawson. The scenario demonstrates that a name can refer to an object even when every associated definite description is false of that object and true of another. This is because names are rigid designators whose reference is fixed by an initial baptism and transmitted through causal-historical chains, not by the satisfaction of descriptions. Searle’s defense, which attempts to rescue descriptivism through a cluster theory and the intentional content of causal chains, is philosophically sophisticated but ultimately unsuccessful. The cluster theory fails because no finite set of descriptions is guaranteed to be true of the referent. The intentionality defense fails because it conflates the intention to refer (which is a de re, causal attitude) with a description of the referent (which is a de dicto, contentful attitude). The metalinguistic descriptions Searle proposes—such as “the person my teacher called ‘Gödel’”—are parasitic on the causal chain. They presuppose the chain’s existence rather than explaining it. If the chain is broken, the descriptions are empty; if the chain is intact, the descriptions are redundant.

The dialectical tension between attributive and referential uses of names further clarifies the issue. Descriptions can be used attributively to pick out whoever fits them, but proper names are primarily used referentially to pick out specific historical individuals. When we use a name referentially, we rely on the causal chain to secure the referent, not on our beliefs about the referent’s properties. The causal-historical theory, therefore, provides a more accurate account of how names achieve reference. It acknowledges that descriptions play a heuristic role in initial baptism and in helping speakers identify referents in their own minds, but it insists that these descriptions are not the semantic content of the name. The name is a rigid designator, and its reference is determined by the chain of communication that links the current speaker back to the original act of naming. Kripke’s Gödel-Schmidt thought experiment is not merely a clever counterexample; it is a paradigm shift that reorients the philosophy of language from a descriptive to a causal framework. In the end, the phantom of description is exorcised, and the causal chain stands as the true foundation of reference.
