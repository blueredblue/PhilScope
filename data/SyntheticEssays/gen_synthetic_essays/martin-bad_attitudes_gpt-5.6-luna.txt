# Propositional Attitudes, Computational Explanation, and the Architecture of Mind

## Introduction

A central question in the philosophy of cognitive science concerns the explanatory status of propositional attitudes: beliefs, desires, intentions, expectations, judgments, and other mental states individuated by their semantic contents. We ordinarily explain behavior by saying that an agent believed that it would rain, desired to remain dry, and therefore carried an umbrella. Such explanations appear to invoke law-like relations among symbolic contents. Beliefs interact with desires, practical reasoning transforms one representation into another, and the resulting cognitive states produce action. The apparent success of this explanatory practice has motivated forms of folk psychological realism: the view that propositional attitudes are not merely convenient ways of describing behavior, but real states that enter into genuine psychological regularities.

Yet computational cognitive science has generated a powerful challenge to this picture. Artificial neural networks do not typically represent information as discrete, sentence-like symbols with determinate truth conditions. They process patterns through high-dimensional activation spaces, distributed representations, nonlinear transformations, and gradual adjustment of connection weights through gradient descent. Their behavior may be explained not by appealing to beliefs and desires as semantically articulated entities, but by describing the dynamics of activation and learning within a network. This raises a difficult question: do propositional attitudes genuinely serve as causal-explanatory entities, or are they only abstract descriptions that obscure the neurobiological mechanisms responsible for cognition?

Jerry Fodor’s language of thought hypothesis offers the most influential defense of a robustly computational and representational account of propositional attitudes. On this view, cognition involves the manipulation of symbolic structures in an internal language. Mental representations have syntactic form and semantic content, and thought processes are computational operations over those representations. Fodor’s theory promises to explain the productivity, systematicity, compositionality, and inferential character of human thought. At the same time, artificial neural networks challenge the assumption that cognitive computation must consist in operations over sentence-like structures. They suggest that intelligence can arise from distributed, subsymbolic mechanisms without explicit rules over propositions.

The opposition between these approaches is not simple. It is not enough to ask whether the mind contains literal sentences or whether neural networks contain beliefs in a commonsense sense. The deeper issue concerns explanatory levels. Propositional attitudes may be too abstract to identify the mechanisms that produce cognition, yet sufficiently structured to explain patterns of reasoning, language use, and action. Conversely, neural networks may offer mechanistic accounts of learning and representation while struggling to capture the semantic and inferential regularities that propositional attitudes describe naturally.

This essay evaluates the claim that propositional attitudes fail to serve as adequate causal-explanatory entities in computational cognitive science. It argues that the claim is persuasive if it is directed against the idea that folk psychological categories, by themselves, constitute a complete mechanistic account of cognition. But it is too strong if it implies that propositional attitudes are dispensable at every explanatory level. Fodor’s language of thought correctly identifies the importance of structured representation and compositional processing, but its classical commitment to discrete symbolic architecture does not exhaust the possibilities of computation. Artificial neural networks demonstrate that causal cognition can be realized through distributed and dynamical mechanisms. Nevertheless, these mechanisms do not automatically eliminate the need for content-level explanation. An adequate cognitive science should therefore be pluralistic: it should distinguish between abstract intentional explanation, computational architecture, and neurobiological implementation, while investigating how they constrain one another. An eliminative program is not required, although a revisionary program may be.

## 1. The Explanatory Role of Propositional Attitudes

Folk psychology explains behavior by attributing intentional states to agents. To say that Maria believes that the train is late is to ascribe a state with a particular content. To say that she wants to arrive on time is to identify another state whose content concerns a desired condition. The conjunction of these states, together with background assumptions, explains why she checks a timetable, calls a taxi, or hurries to the station.

Such explanations are distinctive because they appeal not merely to internal states but to relations among contents. Beliefs tend to combine with other beliefs in systematic ways. If someone believes that all birds have wings and believes that robins are birds, they may infer that robins have wings. If someone desires a state of affairs and believes that a certain action will bring it about, the desire and belief may rationalize the action. The explanatory force appears to derive from the logical and semantic relations among the contents of attitudes.

Folk psychology therefore seems to posit a domain of law-like generalizations. These laws are rarely exceptionless in the manner of fundamental physical laws, but they are not arbitrary. A person who believes that the refrigerator contains food and desires to eat will, under ordinary circumstances, tend to open the refrigerator. The generalization depends on the content of the states and on their relations to one another. It would lose its point if beliefs and desires were treated as contentless internal conditions.

This feature supports folk psychological realism. If propositional attitudes were merely shorthand for observable behavior, then the explanatory role of content would be mysterious. The same behavior may arise from different beliefs and desires, while the same belief may produce different behavior in different circumstances. Intentional explanations appear to unify diverse behavioral manifestations by identifying a common internal reason. The belief that a glass contains water may explain drinking, filling a cup, or offering the glass to someone else, depending on the agent’s desires and surrounding beliefs.

However, the explanatory success of folk psychology does not establish that propositional attitudes are basic causal mechanisms. A map explains a traveler’s route by representing the traveler’s goals and beliefs, but the map is not itself a neural mechanism. Similarly, a psychological explanation may identify a pattern at a level of abstraction without revealing how the pattern is physically implemented. The question is whether such abstraction is legitimate and scientifically useful, or whether it obscures the real causal organization of cognition.

A useful distinction here is between causal relevance and causal sufficiency. A belief may be causally relevant to behavior without being a complete specification of the mechanism by which it produces behavior. For example, saying that a person’s belief caused an action may be analogous to saying that a computer program caused a calculation. The program is not identical to a particular voltage fluctuation, yet it can explain the organization of the calculation. The issue is not whether the program or belief is “really” causal in the same way as a physical force, but whether its structure corresponds to stable patterns in the underlying system.

## 2. Fodor’s Language of Thought

Jerry Fodor’s language of thought hypothesis, developed most fully in *The Language of Thought* and later defended in work on modularity and the mind, provides a sophisticated attempt to connect propositional attitudes with computational mechanisms. Its central idea is that thinking involves the manipulation of mental representations in an internal symbolic system, often called Mentalese. These representations possess combinatorial syntax and semantic content. A belief that snow is white is not merely an undifferentiated neural condition; it is realized by a structured representation whose parts correspond, in some manner, to snow, whiteness, and predication.

Fodor’s theory draws on the computational theory of mind. A computational process operates over representations according to formal rules. The causal properties of the process depend primarily on the syntactic or formal properties of the representations, while the semantic properties explain why the computation is cognitively meaningful. For example, a system might transform the representation “John is at home” and the representation “If John is at home, then John is safe” into “John is safe.” The causal transition is explained by the formal structure of the representations and the rule governing their combination.

This framework appears to offer a bridge between folk psychology and mechanistic explanation. Folk psychology attributes beliefs and desires; computational theory identifies those attitudes with computationally manipulable representations. A belief is a stored representation, a desire may be represented in a different functional role, and reasoning consists in rule-governed transformations. The result is a theory in which propositional attitudes are not merely descriptions of behavior. They correspond to internal symbolic structures that enter into causal processes.

Fodor’s position is motivated by several properties of human cognition. First is productivity. Human beings can entertain indefinitely many thoughts, including thoughts they have never previously encountered. A finite stock of primitive concepts can generate an unlimited number of structured propositions. Second is systematicity. If someone can understand “John loves Mary,” they will generally be able to understand “Mary loves John,” even though the two propositions differ in content. Third is compositionality. The meaning of a complex thought depends systematically on the meanings of its constituents and their mode of combination. Fourth is inferential coherence. Human thought involves operations that preserve or transform structure in ways that seem naturally described in symbolic terms.

A language-like representational system explains these features elegantly. The same constituent can occur in multiple combinations, and the same computational operations can apply across distinct contents. Fodor therefore argues that any adequate theory of cognition must account for the causal role of syntactically structured representations. The mere existence of neural activity is not enough. A theory must explain why cognition displays the systematic and productive character of thought.

The language of thought hypothesis also supports a realist interpretation of propositional attitudes. If a belief is realized by an internal representation with causal consequences, then its content is not an ontological fiction. The belief that the door is open may be physically realized in a particular symbolic structure, and that structure may participate in reasoning, planning, and action. Propositional attitudes are thus not external interpretive labels but functional states of a computational system.

Nevertheless, the theory faces several difficulties. It does not follow from the fact that cognition is computational that its representations must resemble sentences. Computation can operate over vectors, probability distributions, dynamical states, or learned features. Nor does formal syntax by itself explain how a symbol acquires semantic content. A symbol system can manipulate tokens according to rules while remaining semantically ungrounded. The language of thought hypothesis therefore needs an account of how internal symbols are connected to perception, action, and the world.

Moreover, the theory risks making the causal role of content derivative. If the causal transitions are determined by syntax, then semantic content may be causally idle except insofar as it is correlated with formal structure. This creates a tension. Fodor wants mental representations to be both semantically meaningful and causally efficacious, but computational formalism appears to explain transitions through shape, position, and rule-defined relations rather than meaning itself. The theory must show how semantic properties are realized in, and causally integrated with, syntactic organization.

## 3. Connectionism and the Neuro-Computational Alternative

Artificial neural networks challenge the language of thought model by offering a different conception of representation and computation. A neural network consists, in simplified form, of units connected by weighted pathways. Inputs produce patterns of activation; these patterns are transformed through layers of nonlinear processing; outputs are generated through the network’s learned functions. During training, the system adjusts connection weights, often through gradient descent, to reduce an error or loss function.

The network need not contain a discrete symbol corresponding to “cat,” “red,” or “the door is open.” Instead, information may be encoded in a distributed pattern across many units. A concept is represented not by a single token but by a region, direction, trajectory, or configuration in a high-dimensional activation space. Similar inputs may produce nearby patterns; abstract regularities may correspond to geometric relations among activation states. Computation consists in the transformation of these patterns rather than in the manipulation of sentence-like structures.

This architecture has important explanatory advantages. It models learning as a gradual adjustment of internal parameters rather than as the acquisition of explicit rules. It can capture sensitivity to statistical regularities, context effects, graded similarity, robustness to noisy input, and graceful degradation after damage. These features resemble aspects of biological cognition that are difficult to explain in a classical symbolic architecture. The network can learn to classify objects without being given a list of explicit rules defining those objects. It can generalize from examples by constructing internal representations that preserve task-relevant regularities.

The causal story in a neural network is also more directly tied to implementation. The activation of one layer affects the activation of another; weight matrices transform input vectors; nonlinearities alter the geometry of representational space; error signals modify parameters during learning. These are concrete mechanisms. If the network outputs a prediction, one can investigate which units, features, layers, and pathways contributed to that output. The explanation does not require positing that the system explicitly believed a proposition and then applied a rule to it.

Gradient descent is especially significant because it challenges the view that cognition must be organized around pre-specified symbolic rules. The network discovers internal representations through optimization. What it learns is not necessarily a set of explicit propositions but a function mapping inputs to outputs. The structure of the function may be distributed across many parameters. A network can therefore exhibit behavior that looks inferential without storing inference rules in a sentence-like form.

Connectionism also complicates the idea of discrete content. In a high-dimensional space, representations are often context-sensitive and graded. The representation of a word, object, or situation may shift according to surrounding information. Similarity is represented geometrically rather than through a shared symbolic constituent. This may better reflect the fluidity and context dependence of human cognition. Biological neural systems are not obviously composed of symbolic tokens manipulated by centralized rules; they are recurrent, noisy, plastic, and massively parallel.

For these reasons, neural networks appear to support the claim that propositional attitudes are not adequate causal-explanatory entities. A network’s behavior can be explained through activation dynamics and learned transformations without assigning it beliefs and desires. Indeed, the network may produce coherent and flexible behavior despite lacking explicit representations with determinate propositional content. If such systems are cognitive, then propositional attitudes cannot be necessary for every form of intelligence.

Still, the conclusion must be qualified. The fact that a neural network can be described in mechanistic terms does not show that intentional descriptions are useless. Consider a trained language model. Its internal states may be distributed and high-dimensional, but one may still ask whether it represents that a certain event occurred, whether it expects a continuation, or whether it has inferred a relation. These questions may be answered imperfectly, but they are not automatically illegitimate. A network can implement a representation without storing a discrete symbol that transparently expresses its content.

The relevant issue is therefore not whether neural networks contain propositions in a classical format. It is whether their internal dynamics instantiate content-bearing states whose causal roles can be characterized intentionally. A vector representation may encode a proposition in a distributed, approximate, or context-dependent manner. The causal explanation may need to combine geometric description with semantic interpretation.

## 4. The Dialectical Tension Between the Two Frameworks

The debate between symbolic and connectionist approaches is often presented as a conflict between two incompatible conceptions of mind. The symbolic camp emphasizes explicit structure, compositionality, rule-governed inference, and semantic interpretation. The connectionist camp emphasizes distributed representation, learning, graded similarity, context sensitivity, and dynamical interaction. Each approach identifies genuine weaknesses in the other.

The symbolic approach explains why cognition is systematic. If a system represents “the dog chased the cat” through constituents and syntactic relations, it can recombine those constituents in novel ways. It can distinguish “the dog chased the cat” from “the cat chased the dog” because the representations encode relational structure. Symbolic architectures also provide interpretable explanations. One can state which premises were represented, which rule was applied, and which conclusion followed.

Connectionist systems, by contrast, have historically struggled with systematicity and compositionality. A network may learn to associate familiar combinations without reliably generalizing to novel ones. It may represent “John loves Mary” and “Mary loves John” as unrelated patterns rather than as structured variations of a common relational schema. Although modern architectures can display considerable compositional behavior, the mechanism by which they achieve it is often opaque. Their success may depend on statistical regularities in the training distribution rather than on a general capacity for recombination.

The connectionist response is that symbolic theorists often assume the very structures they claim to explain. Human cognition need not contain explicit sentence-like representations in order to behave systematically. A network may encode relational structure through learned geometry, attention patterns, tensor-like interactions, recurrence, or specialized subnetworks. Systematicity may be an emergent property of the network’s organization rather than a direct consequence of symbolic tokens.

Furthermore, symbolic accounts can be too rigid. Human cognition is not always rule-governed in an explicit or exceptionless manner. Categorization is graded; memory is reconstructive; reasoning is influenced by context and similarity; perception and action operate continuously. Neural networks capture these features naturally. They also offer a plausible account of how cognitive capacities are learned from experience. A classical language of thought must explain not only how symbols are manipulated but how the symbol system is acquired and connected to perception and action.

This dialectic indicates that neither framework should be identified with an entire theory of cognition. Classical symbol systems excel at describing abstract structure and explicit reasoning. Neural networks excel at modeling learning, perception, similarity, and distributed constraint satisfaction. Human cognition may involve both. A person can recognize a face through distributed perceptual processing, retrieve an associated memory through pattern completion, and then use explicit linguistic or conceptual representations in deliberative reasoning. The boundaries between these processes may be partial and functionally organized rather than absolute.

The most productive question is not “Are thoughts symbolic or neural?” but “What kinds of representation and computation are required for different cognitive capacities, and how are they integrated in biological systems?” Propositional attitudes may not be the fundamental units of all cognition, but they may characterize some forms of cognition at an appropriate level. Similarly, high-dimensional activation dynamics may explain mechanisms without replacing every content-level description.

## 5. Are Propositional Attitudes Too Abstract?

The charge that propositional attitudes are “merely abstract” can mean several different things. It may mean that they are not located at a unique neural site. It may mean that they supervene on complex patterns of neural activity rather than being simple physical states. Or it may mean that intentional descriptions fail to identify the mechanisms responsible for behavior. These claims should be separated.

The first claim is unobjectionable. A belief is unlikely to be realized by a single neuron or a simple local state. It may involve recurrent activity across distributed systems, long-term synaptic changes, perceptual capacities, memory structures, linguistic resources, and action dispositions. But complexity does not disqualify a state from being causally real. Many biological properties, such as homeostasis or immune memory, are realized through distributed mechanisms. Their causal reality consists in the organized pattern, not in a simple physical component.

The second claim is also compatible with realism. Propositional attitudes may be multiply realizable. Different neural structures, organisms, or artificial systems could implement functionally similar beliefs. This is one reason functional descriptions are useful: they identify patterns that remain stable across physical realizations. The belief that water is available might be implemented differently in a human, a robot, and an animal, yet the content can explain action in each case if the relevant functional relations are preserved.

The strongest claim is that intentional descriptions conceal the mechanisms and therefore cannot count as causal explanations. Here the criticism has force. Saying that a person believed that danger was present does not tell us how perception generated the state, how memory influenced it, how competing representations were resolved, or how motor systems were recruited. A folk psychological explanation may be too coarse for questions about neural implementation, learning, or intervention. If the aim is to predict the effects of manipulating a particular circuit, a belief-desire description may be insufficient.

Yet explanatory insufficiency is not the same as explanatory invalidity. A description can be causally informative without being complete. In physics, thermodynamic descriptions do not become false because statistical mechanics provides a more detailed account. In computer science, an algorithmic explanation does not become useless because the algorithm is implemented by transistors. Higher-level explanations remain valuable when they track stable organizational properties and support counterfactual reasoning.

The crucial issue is whether propositional attitudes form a causally relevant level rather than merely a verbal gloss. One test is intervention. If changing a subject’s belief while holding other relevant factors fixed would systematically alter behavior, then the belief has a kind of causal significance. In practice, such interventions are difficult because beliefs are embedded in networks of other states. Nonetheless, psychological research routinely manipulates expectations, goals, interpretations, and judgments in ways that affect action. These effects suggest that content-level states are not empty labels.

A second test is explanatory compression. A good intentional explanation can unify many behavioral outcomes under one state description. It may predict how an agent will respond across novel contexts, especially when the agent’s goals and background assumptions are known. If a description using propositional attitudes supports such generalization better than an exhaustive account of individual neural states, it has legitimate explanatory value.

A third test is mechanistic linkage. Propositional attitudes are stronger as scientific entities when theories specify how they are realized. A belief should not be treated as an unanalyzed inner sentence; it should be connected to memory dynamics, perceptual processing, decision systems, and learning mechanisms. The more a cognitive science can map content-level functions onto neural and computational organizations, the less plausible it becomes to regard attitudes as merely folk inventions.

## 6. Does Connectionism Eliminate Content?

The success of neural networks might encourage an eliminativist conclusion. If intelligent behavior can emerge from networks without explicit propositions, perhaps beliefs and desires should be abandoned. Eliminative materialists such as Paul and Patricia Churchland have argued that folk psychology may resemble an outdated theory whose posits will eventually be replaced by neuroscience. On this view, “belief” may be like “phlogiston”: a useful term within an old framework but not a component of the mature science.

The analogy is provocative, but it is not decisive. Phlogiston theory failed because its entities did not support a successful and progressively refined causal science. Folk psychology, by contrast, supports robust prediction, explanation, and social coordination. Its concepts are embedded in practices of interpretation that remain indispensable in many contexts. The fact that a more detailed theory exists does not by itself establish that the older vocabulary should be eliminated.

Moreover, neural networks themselves do not eliminate representation; they transform its form. A network’s activation patterns are interpreted as encoding features, categories, predictions, or relations. Even when no single unit corresponds to a concept, the system may possess distributed representations with functional content. Eliminating propositional attitudes in favor of “activation vectors” may simply replace one representational vocabulary with another. The question then becomes how the vector states acquire content and how their content relates to behavior.

One might respond that neural network representations are not genuinely semantic because their interpretation is imposed by an external observer. A vector has no intrinsic meaning; it merely participates in causal transitions. But this objection threatens symbolic theories as well. A token in a language of thought also has no meaning solely in virtue of its physical shape. Its semantic role depends on its relations to perception, action, other representations, and the world. Symbolic and distributed representations therefore face a common problem of grounding.

Connectionism may offer a more natural route to grounding because representations develop through interaction with sensory input, motor output, reward, and environmental regularities. A network trained to predict or control its environment acquires internal states that reflect task-relevant structure. Yet learning correlations is not automatically equivalent to possessing determinate propositional content. A network might predict that an event will occur without representing that event in a language-like form. The semantics may be probabilistic, context-sensitive, and action-oriented rather than propositional in the classical sense.

This suggests a distinction between eliminative and revisionary approaches. An eliminative approach denies that propositional attitudes have a legitimate place in cognitive science. A revisionary approach retains intentional vocabulary but revises its ontology. Beliefs may be understood as stable, action-relevant predictive dispositions implemented by distributed neural dynamics. Desires may be understood as value-weighted control states. Expectations may be modeled as probability distributions rather than categorical propositions. Such a theory does not preserve folk psychology unchanged, but neither does it discard content altogether.

The revisionary approach is especially plausible because ordinary attitudes are often graded and uncertain. People can weakly believe, suspect, imagine, or tacitly expect. Neural and computational models can represent uncertainty through probability distributions or activation patterns. A propositional attitude need not be a discrete all-or-nothing sentence in the head. It can be an abstract characterization of how a system is disposed to update, infer, and act under varying conditions.

## 7. The Adequacy of Fodor’s Computational Realism

Fodor’s theory remains valuable because it identifies a genuine explanatory problem for purely associationist accounts. If cognition were only a sequence of distributed activations shaped by statistical learning, it would be difficult to explain the stability of conceptual relations, inferential transitions, and novel recombination. Fodor is right that cognitive science must explain more than input-output regularities. It must account for the internal organization that makes behavior productive and systematic.

However, the classical language of thought may not be the only way to meet this demand. Fodor’s framework tends to treat syntax as discrete and combinatorial, while biological cognition may rely on softer forms of structure. Representations can preserve relational information through continuous geometry. A network can encode that two objects are similar, that one event tends to precede another, or that a relation reverses when its arguments are swapped. These relations need not be represented by symbols with explicit constituent boundaries.

In addition, contemporary computational models often combine symbolic and neural resources. Neural systems can learn representations that support algorithmic operations; attention mechanisms can selectively bind features; recurrent dynamics can maintain structured information over time; external memory can provide discrete storage; differentiable architectures can approximate operations traditionally described as symbolic. Such systems suggest that the distinction between symbolic and subsymbolic computation is not absolute. Symbol-like behavior can emerge from neural mechanisms, and neural mechanisms can be organized to support structured operations.

Fodor’s account also leaves underdeveloped the problem of learning. A language of thought may explain how already available representations are manipulated, but it does not by itself explain how primitive concepts, syntactic categories, or semantic connections are acquired. If the language of thought is innate, the theory risks nativist excess; if it is learned, the learning problem reappears at a different level. Neural networks provide a model of gradual acquisition, though their own learning mechanisms do not fully explain human conceptual development.

Finally, Fodor’s strict separation between computational cognition and neurobiology can be limiting. A cognitive architecture should not merely specify abstract symbol manipulation while treating implementation as irrelevant. The particular features of neural systems—recurrent connectivity, timing, plasticity, noise, embodiment, and neuromodulation—may shape what forms of cognition are possible. Computational explanation must therefore be constrained by biological organization rather than floating free of it.

## 8. Toward a Layered and Pluralistic Cognitive Science

The most defensible position is a layered account of explanation. At one level, intentional psychology characterizes agents in terms of beliefs, desires, goals, and reasons. At another, computational theory specifies the transformations that implement inference, learning, prediction, and control. At a third, neuroscience identifies the biological mechanisms that realize those computations. These levels are not isolated, but neither are they reducible without remainder to one another.

The familiar distinction between Marr’s computational, algorithmic, and implementation levels is useful here. The computational level asks what problem the system solves and why a particular computation is appropriate. The algorithmic level asks how information is represented and transformed. The implementation level asks how neural structures physically realize those operations. Propositional attitudes are most naturally located at the computational or intentional level, though they may correspond to algorithmic structures in some systems. Neural activation spaces and gradient descent belong more directly to the algorithmic and implementation levels.

A complete science should connect these levels. An intentional explanation that cannot be linked to computation risks becoming vague. A neural explanation that cannot be related to the system’s goals and representational functions risks becoming an inventory of mechanisms without cognitive interpretation. The explanatory task is not to choose one level and abolish the others, but to show how the levels constrain each other.

This layered model also clarifies what it means for a propositional attitude to cause behavior. A belief is not a mysterious nonphysical force. It is a functional state realized by physical processes. Its causal role is mediated by the computational organization of the system. In a symbolic architecture, the mediation may involve discrete representations and rules. In a neural network, it may involve a distributed activation pattern that shifts the geometry of subsequent processing. The same intentional description can therefore be implemented in different ways, provided the relevant functional relations are preserved.

Nevertheless, not every folk psychological category will survive unchanged. Some attitudes may be too coarse, culturally variable, or theoretically unstable. Others may require refinement. For example, “belief” might decompose into confidence-weighted predictive representations, memory traces, linguistic commitments, and action policies. “Desire” might decompose into value signals, motivational states, and control objectives. A mature cognitive science may retain the vocabulary while changing its theoretical interpretation.

The success of this project depends on developing methods for relating semantic content to neural representations. Interpretability research, representational similarity analysis, causal probing, dynamical systems theory, and computational modeling all contribute to this effort. The goal should not be to find a single neuron for each proposition. Rather, it should be to identify stable patterns of neural organization that support content-sensitive inference and action. A representation may be distributed yet causally manipulable; high-dimensional yet semantically structured.

## 9. Is an Eliminative Program Required?

The strongest eliminativist argument is that propositional attitudes are too indeterminate to support precise causal laws. “Belief” covers many different neural and computational states. Two people may both believe that the bus is late while differing in confidence, perceptual evidence, memory, linguistic understanding, and action readiness. The same person’s belief may be realized differently across contexts. If the category does not pick out a uniform mechanism, it may seem unsuitable for scientific explanation.

But scientific kinds need not be perfectly homogeneous. Biological and psychological categories often identify clusters of mechanisms organized around functional roles. A memory is not one uniform physical entity, yet memory remains a scientifically useful concept. A disease can involve multiple pathways while still supporting causal generalizations. The relevant question is whether the category tracks enough structure to support prediction and intervention.

Folk psychological categories may be more heterogeneous than mature scientific concepts, but this supports refinement rather than immediate elimination. Science often begins with coarse classifications and develops more precise subcategories. The appropriate successor to ordinary belief may be a family of computationally characterized states, including explicit symbolic commitments, probabilistic expectations, perceptual predictions, and action-oriented policies. This process can preserve what is explanatory in folk psychology while abandoning what is theoretically confused.

Elimination would be justified if intentional descriptions systematically failed to predict, explain, or connect with mechanisms. Yet human practices of reasoning, communication, planning, and social interpretation rely heavily on content-sensitive generalizations. Even neuroscience must often describe experiments in terms of what subjects perceive, expect, remember, or decide. A neural account that prohibited all such descriptions would be less explanatory, not more.

There is also a pragmatic issue. Cognitive science studies systems whose behavior is organized around environmental significance. A representation of food matters because it guides eating; a representation of danger matters because it coordinates avoidance; a representation of another agent’s intention matters because it guides social interaction. To describe these states solely as vectors or activation trajectories may preserve formal information while losing the normative and relational dimensions that make the behavior intelligible.

This does not mean that content is fundamental in the metaphysical sense. Propositional attitudes may depend on neural dynamics and lack independent causal powers. But dependence does not entail explanatory redundancy. A higher-level property can be wholly realized by lower-level processes while remaining indispensable for characterizing organization. Eliminative materialism is therefore not required to ground cognitive science in non-propositional dynamics.
