# Mythical Time, Trauma, and Historical Agency: Merleau-Ponty between Freud, Badiou, and Gély

Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s notion of mythical time presents a serious challenge to any philosophy that understands history as a succession of objectively identifiable moments. In mythical time, the past is not simply over, nor is it preserved as an inert collection of causes. It remains operative within the present as an invisible thickness, a sediment that can be reactivated, transformed, or displaced. Historical meaning therefore has a retroactive structure: an event may acquire its decisive significance only later, through the subsequent configuration of a field in which it is remembered, repeated, interpreted, or institutionally embodied.

This structure invites comparison with Sigmund Freud’s theory of trauma, especially the Freudian concept of *Nachträglichkeit*, or deferred action. For Freud, an event does not necessarily become traumatic at the moment it occurs. A later event may confer traumatic meaning upon an earlier experience, which then returns as something that had not been fully present to consciousness in the first place. The past is thus constituted retrospectively as a source of present symptoms. Merleau-Ponty’s mythical time resembles this temporal logic, but it is not reducible to psychoanalytic trauma. In Freud, deferred action often concerns the belated organization of psychic conflict; in Merleau-Ponty, retroactivity belongs to the ontological structure of perception, history, institution, and embodiment as such.

The question is whether this temporal framework can support Alain Badiou’s account of subject-formation. Badiou maintains that the subject does not pre-exist the event. A subject emerges through fidelity to an event that interrupts the established order of a situation. Yet Merleau-Ponty’s emphasis on sedimentation, embodiment, and historical inheritance seems to complicate the sharp distinction between a pre-event situation and a post-event subject. Raphaël Gély’s critique is important in this connection because it resists the idea that one can simply posit a subject as already formed prior to the event and then attribute agency to it. Gély’s phenomenological concern is that subjectivity is always generated within relations of sense, embodiment, and intersubjective institution. The subject is neither a sovereign origin nor an empty effect of an event.

Mauro Carbone’s interpretation of Merleau-Ponty’s “wild Being” and the chiasm of the touching and the touched offers a way to mediate between these alternatives. Wild Being names a dimension of ontological productivity that precedes the distinction between subject and object without being a primitive substance behind them. The touching-touched chiasm shows that agency is possible only because the agent is also exposed, affected, and constituted by what it encounters. Through this chiasmatic structure, the invisible sediment of history can remain active without determining the future mechanically, while the right of rectification can remain continuous without presupposing an absolutely self-grounding subject.

The resulting ontology is non-serial. Time is not a line composed of externally related moments, but a field of overlapping and reversible dimensions in which the past is carried forward, the present reconfigures what has been, and the future is already latent in the possibilities of the present. Such an ontology can sustain a coherent account of historical agency, but only if agency is understood neither as absolute novelty nor as the execution of a pre-existing identity. Agency is the chiasmatic power of instituting a new sense within a field that both enables and resists transformation.

## I. Mythical time and the retroactivity of the event

Merleau-Ponty’s concept of mythical time must be distinguished from the ordinary idea of myth as an untrue story. Mythical time refers instead to a temporal mode in which different moments are not cleanly separated from one another. A myth does not belong exclusively to the past. It organizes a present, directs a future, and continues to function as a living structure of meaning. Its events are not merely remembered; they are repeatedly reenacted, interpreted, and embodied. In this sense, mythical time is less a chronology than a pattern of coexistence.

Merleau-Ponty’s reflections on history, perception, and institution consistently undermine the notion that temporal experience consists of a series of punctual “nows.” The present is never a pure instant. It includes retentions of what has just passed, anticipations of what is about to occur, and sedimented meanings inherited from a larger cultural and bodily past. A gesture, for example, is intelligible only against a background of acquired practices. A political institution persists through habits and symbols that exceed the intentions of any individual participant. A historical event continues to act through its interpretations, commemorations, exclusions, and material consequences.

Mythical time names this thickness of temporality. The past exists not as a finished object but as an operative dimension of the present. At the same time, the meaning of the past is not fixed once and for all. A later development can reveal that an earlier event had a significance that was not previously available. The event is therefore both anterior and posterior to its meaning. It happens at one point in chronological time, but its historical identity unfolds through later acts of interpretation and institution.

This is why Merleau-Ponty’s temporal ontology is retroactive. Retroactivity does not mean that the present magically changes the empirical facts of the past. Rather, it means that what counts as the past, what it means, and what possibilities it contains are determined through its relation to subsequent configurations. The significance of the French Revolution, the meaning of a political defeat, or the historical character of a personal wound can change as new struggles make visible dimensions that were previously concealed. The event is not first fully constituted and then later interpreted. Its sense is progressively instituted.

This point can be clarified through Merleau-Ponty’s notion of institution. An institution is not merely an established rule or organization. It is an event that opens a field of future possibilities and continues to organize experience after its occurrence. Language, art, political practices, and personal relationships are instituted in this way. Once instituted, they are neither wholly determinate nor infinitely malleable. They provide a style, a direction, and a reservoir of possible developments. Yet their meaning is fulfilled only through later responses. Institution is thus a temporal structure in which the past establishes a future that can nevertheless exceed the past’s initial intention.

The mythical event has a similar structure. It does not belong solely to an origin. It is an origin that remains active by being repeated and transformed. The origin is not a first cause external to its effects. It is present in the effects as an open demand. A founding event becomes historical only because it continues to solicit reinterpretation. It can be betrayed, revived, universalized, narrowed, or redirected. Its historical life is therefore neither simple persistence nor simple disappearance.
