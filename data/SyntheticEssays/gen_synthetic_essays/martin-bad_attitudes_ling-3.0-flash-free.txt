**The Symbolic Shadow: Propositional Attitudes, Fodor’s Language of Thought, and the Non-Propositional Imperative of Neuro-Computational Cognitive Science**

The claim that propositional attitudes fail to serve as adequate causal-explanatory entities in computational cognitive science strikes at the very foundation of how we understand the mind. Propositional attitudes—mental states such as beliefs, desires, and intentions—are traditionally characterized by their relation to propositions (e.g., believing *that* it will rain, desiring *that* one stays dry). For decades, the dominant paradigm in cognitive science, largely championed by Jerry Fodor, has treated these attitudes as the fundamental building blocks of cognition, instantiated within a "language of thought" (LoT). However, the rise of artificial neural networks (ANNs) and the neuro-computational perspective has introduced a profound dialectical tension. This tension pits the folk psychological realism of symbolic, law-governed mental contents against the non-propositional dynamics of high-dimensional activation spaces and gradient descent learning. 

This essay evaluates the claim that propositional attitudes are inadequate causal-explanatory entities by analyzing Fodor’s LoT hypothesis alongside the alternative framework provided by ANNs. It assesses whether propositional attitudes operate merely at an abstract level of generality that obscures underlying neurobiological mechanisms, and it argues that an eliminative program is indeed required to ground cognitive science in non-propositional dynamics. To achieve this, the essay must first reconstruct the symbolic architecture of Fodor’s realism, then examine the causal-explanatory deficit inherent in propositional abstraction, and finally, articulate how the neuro-computational perspective resolves this deficit through geometric and gradient-based dynamics.

### Fodor’s Language of Thought and the Realism of Folk Psychology

Jerry Fodor’s *The Language of Thought* (1975) posited that human cognition operates on a mental code—a language of thought—composed of discrete, combinatorial symbols. In this framework, the mind is essentially a computer, and the mind’s software is the syntax of this mentalese. Propositional attitudes are the primary interface between this internal symbolic system and the external world. When we say that a person believes *that* Paris is the capital of France, or desires *that* they are wealthy, we are attributing a specific syntactic relation between a mental symbol and a proposition. 

Fodor’s framework is the epitome of folk psychological realism. This realism asserts that the vocabulary of everyday mental states—beliefs, desires, intentions—refers to real, causally efficacious entities within the cognitive architecture. Propositional attitudes are not merely predictive tools; they are the very mechanisms by which the mind operates. In Fodor’s view, cognitive processes are governed by law-like relations between symbolic mental contents. The mind infers, deduces, and composes symbols according to strict syntactic rules, much like a logical calculus. The causal power of a belief lies in its functional role: a belief that *p* causes behavior in conjunction with a desire that *q* because the LoT architecture mechanically links these symbolic representations. 

This folk psychological realism provides a compelling macro-level explanatory framework. It accounts for the rationality and systematicity of human behavior. If John believes it will rain and desires to stay dry, it is law-like that he will take an umbrella. The propositional attitude of belief serves as the causal antecedent, and the action serves as the consequent. For Fodor, this is not merely a description of behavior but an explanation of the cognitive machinery that produces it. The language of thought supplies the syntax, and propositional attitudes supply the semantics; together, they form a closed causal system capable of explaining the richness of human thought. 

However, the adequacy of this causal-explanatory framework is severely challenged when we scrutinize the nature of the "symbols" and the "laws" that govern them. The law-like relations posited by folk psychological realism are highly abstract. They describe *that* a belief causes an action, but they remain entirely silent on *how* the belief exerts this causal force. This brings us to the central tension between symbolic realism and the neuro-computational alternative.

### The Causal-Explanatory Deficit of Propositional Attitudes

The primary failure of propositional attitudes as causal-explanatory entities lies in their abstraction. To say that a person is in a state of belief is to assign them a position within a formal, symbolic system. But this assignment functions as a placeholder rather than a mechanism. In computational cognitive science, a causal-explanatory entity must map onto the physical and computational processes that actually generate behavior. Propositional attitudes, by their very nature, operate at a level of generality that obscures the underlying neurobiological mechanisms. 

Consider the difference between a folk psychological explanation and a neurobiological one. Folk psychology explains a child’s fear of dogs by stating that the child believes dogs are dangerous and desires to avoid harm. This explanation is intuitively satisfying at the macro-level, but it is causally vacuous at the micro-level. It does not specify the neural circuits, the synaptic weights, or the activation dynamics that constitute the belief or the desire. The propositional attitude "belief that dogs are dangerous" is an abstract functional role that could be realized by a myriad of different neurobiological configurations. 

This is the problem of multiple realizability, which Fodor himself championed. While multiple realizability is a strength of the LoT hypothesis—allowing cognitive states to be substrate-independent—it simultaneously undermines the explanatory power of propositional attitudes. If a belief can be realized by vastly different neural substrates, then the belief itself cannot be the specific causal mechanism producing a behavior. The belief is a macro-level abstraction that sits atop a complex, heterogeneous neurobiological base. By treating the abstraction as the explanation, cognitive science commits a category mistake, mistaking the map for the territory. 

Furthermore, Fodor’s LoT posits law-like relations between symbolic mental contents, but these laws are purely syntactic and lack causal grounding. In physics, laws describe the necessary relations between physical entities (e.g., gravity between masses). In Fodor’s cognitive architecture, the "laws" are rules of syntactic combination (e.g., modus ponens). But syntactic rules do not cause physical actions; only physical processes do. When Fodor claims that a belief-desire pair causes an action via the LoT, he is positing a homunculus who follows syntactic rules. This does not explain the causal transition from the physical brain to the physical behavior; it merely redescribes it in the language of formal logic. Propositional attitudes, therefore, fail as causal-explanatory entities because they are epistemically useful fictions that halt inquiry into the actual causal mechanisms of the brain.

### The Neuro-Computational Alternative: High-Dimensional Spaces and Gradient Descent

The alternative framework provided by artificial neural networks (ANNs) offers a radically different ontology of mind, one that directly addresses the causal-explanatory deficits of propositional attitudes. Rather than relying on discrete symbols and syntactic rules, ANNs model representation through high-dimensional activation spaces and learning through gradient descent. 

In an ANN, there are no explicit symbols or propositions. There are no "belief" nodes or "desire" nodes. Instead, representations are distributed across vast networks of artificial neurons, encoded as vectors in high-dimensional spaces. A concept, such as "dog," is not a discrete symbol but a specific region or trajectory within this activation space. The semantic properties of a concept are captured by its geometric relationship to other concepts; similar concepts occupy nearby regions in the vector space. This is a fundamentally non-propositional form of representation. It is continuous, distributed, and geometric, rather than discrete, symbolic, and combinatorial.

The causal dynamics of ANNs are driven by gradient descent learning. When an ANN is trained, it adjusts its internal weights to minimize a loss function. This process is inherently causal and mechanistic: an input activates a layer of neurons, an error signal is computed at the output, and the weights are adjusted proportionally to the gradient of the error. There is no "belief-desire" inference engine orchestrating this process; the behavior emerges from the local, causal interactions of thousands of individual weight adjustments. 

This neuro-computational perspective provides a genuinely causal-explanatory framework. When an ANN classifies an image as a dog, we can trace the causal pathway: specific pixels activated input neurons, which propagated weighted signals through hidden layers, shifting the activation vector through the high-dimensional space until it crossed the decision boundary of a specific output node. The explanation is mechanistic, specifying not just the functional role of the representation, but the exact physical (or computational) process by which the representation was realized and acted upon. 

The dialectical tension between folk psychological realism and the neuro-computational perspective is thus stark. Folk psychological realism posits law-like relations between symbolic mental contents—discrete, compositional, and syntactically governed. The neuro-computational perspective models representation through high-dimensional activation spaces and gradient descent learning—continuous, distributed, and geometrically governed. The former describes the *phenomenology* of the mind from the agent’s first-person perspective; the latter describes the *mechanism* of the mind from a third-person, neurobiological perspective. 

### Do Propositional Attitudes Obscure Underlying Neurobiological Mechanisms?

The question of whether propositional attitudes operate merely at an abstract level of generality that obscures underlying neurobiological mechanisms must be answered in the affirmative. To say that propositional attitudes obscure neurobiological mechanisms is not merely to say that they are less detailed; it is to say that they actively misdirect scientific inquiry by providing a false sense of causal closure. 

Propositional attitudes function as explanatory termini. When a cognitive scientist explains a behavior by citing a belief or a desire, the explanation often stops there. The folk psychological vocabulary acts as a causal black box. Because the language of propositional attitudes is so powerful and intuitive, it encourages scientists to treat the macro-level abstraction as the fundamental reality, rather than as an emergent property of underlying neurobiological processes. 

This obscuration is particularly evident in the "symbol grounding problem." In Fodor’s LoT, symbols derive their meaning from their relations to other symbols (syntactic definition). But this merely pushes the question of meaning back a step: what gives the symbols their intrinsic, causal connection to the world? Propositional attitudes cannot ground themselves in the physical world because they are defined purely by their relations within the symbolic system. They are causally isolated from the neurobiological substrate that implements them. 

By contrast, ANNs do not suffer from the symbol grounding problem because they have no symbols. Their representations are grounded directly in the sensory inputs and the error signals of the training data. The "meaning" of a vector in an ANN's activation space is constituted by its causal role in the gradient descent process and its geometric proximity to other vectors. There is no gap between the representation and the mechanism; the representation *is* the mechanism. 

Thus, propositional attitudes operate at an abstract level of generality that not only fails to explain the neurobiological mechanisms of cognition but actively obscures them. They provide a convenient, macro-level shorthand for predicting behavior, but they are epistemically and ontologically inadequate as causal-explanatory entities. They are, in effect, the "temperature" of the mind—a useful macro-state that hides the complex, non-linear micro-dynamics of neural computation.

### The Necessity of an Eliminative Program

Given the failure of propositional attitudes as causal-explanatory entities and their tendency to obscure neurobiological mechanisms, the question arises: is an eliminative program required to ground cognitive science in non-propositional dynamics? The answer is a resounding yes, though the nature of this elimination must be carefully delineated.

Eliminative materialism, most famously associated with the Churchlands, argues that folk psychology is a radically false theory that will eventually be replaced by neuroscientific vocabulary. While strict eliminativism—claiming that beliefs and desires do not exist at all—is philosophically contentious, an eliminative program targeting the *causal-explanatory status* of propositional attitudes is necessary for the maturation of cognitive science. 

To ground cognitive science in non-propositional dynamics, we must eliminate propositional attitudes as fundamental causal entities. This does not mean we must stop using the words "belief" and "desire" in everyday conversation or even in high-level psychological theorizing. Folk psychology remains an incredibly effective predictive tool. We will still say, "She took an umbrella because she believed it would rain." However, we must eliminate the assumption that the "belief" is a causal-explanatory entity in its own right. Instead, the "belief" must be reconceptualized as a high-dimensional activation pattern or an attractor state within a neural network, and the "taking of the umbrella" must be explained by the gradient descent dynamics that led to that specific motor output.

The eliminativist move is required because as long as cognitive science relies on propositional dynamics as its primary explanatory framework, it remains tethered to an abstract, non-mechanistic ontology. Cognitive science cannot achieve a true understanding of the mind if its fundamental explanatory entities are abstract symbols whose causal powers are entirely derived from the syntactic rules of a formal system, rather than from the physical laws governing neural tissue. 

The transition from propositional to non-propositional dynamics requires a shift from a syntactic to a geometric understanding of cognition. In the propositional framework, cognition is a matter of symbol manipulation. In the neuro-computational framework, cognition is a matter of navigating a high-dimensional loss landscape. An ANN does not "believe" a proposition; it occupies a region of an activation space that minimizes prediction error. The "belief" is an emergent property of the network's position in this space, not a fundamental causal entity. 

By eliminating propositional attitudes as fundamental causal-explanatory entities, cognitive science can finally engage directly with the neurobiological mechanisms that produce behavior. It can investigate the specific geometries of activation spaces, the precise dynamics of gradient descent, and the topological structures of neural manifolds. This is not to say that the folk psychological perspective is useless; it is to say that its explanatory privileges must be revoked. Folk psychology can survive as a pragmatic, macro-level user manual for predicting behavior, but it must be demoted from a fundamental theory of the mind to a phenomenological shadow cast by the underlying non-propositional dynamics.

### Conclusion

The claim that propositional attitudes fail to serve as adequate causal-explanatory entities in computational cognitive science is robustly supported by the dialectical tension between folk psychological realism and the neuro-computational perspective. Fodor’s language of thought hypothesis, while providing a powerful macro-structural framework for understanding the systematicity of cognition, ultimately relies on abstract symbolic entities that lack mechanistic causal grounding. Propositional attitudes operate as law-like relations between mental contents, but these laws are syntactic and formal, failing to bridge the gap between the physical brain and physical behavior. 

The alternative framework provided by artificial neural networks reveals that representation and cognition can be modeled through high-dimensional activation spaces and gradient descent learning. In this framework, there is no need for discrete symbols, propositional contents, or syntactic rules. Instead, cognition emerges from the continuous, geometric dynamics of neural activation and error minimization. This non-propositional perspective provides a genuinely causal-explanatory account that does not obscure underlying neurobiological mechanisms.

Ultimately, propositional attitudes operate at an abstract level of generality that obscures the mechanistic realities of the brain. They function as convenient abstractions that halt causal inquiry, much like "temperature" obscures the kinetic energy of molecules. To ground cognitive science in non-propositional dynamics, an eliminative program is not merely desirable but necessary. We must eliminate propositional attitudes as fundamental causal-explanatory entities, relegating them to the status of emergent, macro-level abstractions. Only by stripping away the symbolic shadow of folk psychology can cognitive science fully illuminate the non-propositional, geometric, and gradient-based machinery that constitutes the mind.
