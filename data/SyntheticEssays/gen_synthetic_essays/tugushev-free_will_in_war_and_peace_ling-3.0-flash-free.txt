Beyond the Whole and the Void: Evaluating Tugushev’s Critique of Tolstoy’s Determinism and the Problem of Spontaneous Nothingness

The question of free will versus determinism has long haunted the philosophical tradition, manifesting in literary and metaphysical forms that continue to challenge our understanding of human agency. Nowhere is this tension more vividly explored than in Leo Tolstoy’s epic novel *War and Peace*, where the author famously deploys a *reductio ad absurdum* argument to dismantle the conventional notion of free will. In Tolstoy’s formulation, free will is an illusion because any attempt to posit an uncaused, autonomous human action collapses under the weight of an unbroken causal chain. Every movement, thought, and decision is the inevitable product of a preceding series of causes, stretching back infinitely into the past and outward into the totality of the cosmos. To escape this determinism, Tolstoy argues, one must accept the metaphysical presupposition of the "totality of being"—the idea that the individual is merely a transient node within an unyielding, deterministic whole. 

In recent philosophical scholarship, Nikolay Tugushev has offered a compelling critique of Tolstoy’s deterministic framework, arguing that rejecting the metaphysical presupposition of the totality of being successfully circumvents the deterministic conclusion. Tugushev contends that by dismantling the monistic, totalizing conception of reality that Tolstoy relies upon, the individual is liberated from the crushing weight of cosmic determinism. However, this liberation raises a profound and perilous question: does rejecting the totality without reducing free will to a spontaneous, self-caused force arising out of nothingness? If the individual is not merely a cog in the deterministic machine of the whole, does the individual become an arbitrary, uncaused cause—a *causa sui* that generates itself ex nihilo? This essay evaluates Tugushev’s critique by examining whether his rejection of Tolstoy’s totality successfully navigates the treacherous middle ground between deterministic submersion and the absurdity of spontaneous nothingness.

**Tolstoy’s Reductio ad Absurdum and the Metaphysical Presupposition of the Whole**

To understand the necessity of Tugushev’s intervention, one must first grasp the formidable architecture of Tolstoy’s argument in *War and Peace*. Tolstoy’s *reductio ad absurdum* against free will operates by exposing the logical impossibility of an uncaused cause. When a person believes they are exercising free will—choosing to raise their arm, deciding to speak a particular word—they are mistaken. What they experience as a free choice is, in reality, the inevitable culmination of a vast, invisible network of prior causes. The person’s biological makeup, their upbringing, their current mood, the linguistic conventions they have internalized, and the historical circumstances that have shaped their very neurons all converge to produce the specific action at the specific moment. To claim that the individual could have acted otherwise is to claim that the sum total of these preceding causes could have been different, which is itself a contradiction. 

Tolstoy extends this logic from the individual to the macrocosm of human history. In *War and Peace*, he famously argues that the movements of armies, the rise and fall of empires, and the trajectory of nations are not the result of the "free will" of kings, generals, or leaders. Just as an individual is determined by prior biological and psychological causes, a historical actor is determined by the totality of social, economic, and geographical forces. The leader believes they are directing events, but they are merely the most visible expression of an underlying, deterministic current. Tolstoy writes that the illusion of free will arises from the human inability to perceive the infinite continuum of causes. Because the chain is so vast and intricate, the individual experiences their action as an originating point, when in fact it is merely a link in an unbroken sequence.

The metaphysical presupposition underlying this argument is what Tugushev identifies as the "totality of being." For Tolstoy, the totality is an ontological given—a seamless, deterministic fabric where every event is intrinsically connected to every other event. The part (the individual) cannot escape the whole (the totality), and the whole is entirely deterministic. This presupposition is what allows Tolstoy to move from the observation of causal chains to the absolute conclusion of determinism. If the totality of being is a closed, deterministic system, then free will is logically impossible. The *reductio ad absurdum* succeeds because it forces the proponent of free will into an impossible position: they must either admit that their will is uncaused (which violates the principle of causality) or admit that it is caused (which negates its freedom). Tolstoy’s conclusion is that free will is a fiction, a psychological artifact of our ignorance of the totality.

**Tugushev’s Critique: Dismantling the Totality**

Nikolay Tugushev’s critique of Tolstoy’s argument begins precisely at this metaphysical presupposition. Tugushev argues that Tolstoy illicitly assumes the ontological status of the "totality of being," treating it not as a descriptive model of reality, but as an absolute, monistic metaphysical truth. By accepting the totality as an unassailable given, Tolstoy effectively forecloses any possibility of agency before the argument even begins. Tugushev’s intervention is to reject this presupposition, arguing that the totality is a methodological abstraction rather than an ontological necessity. 

If the totality is merely a conceptual tool we use to understand the interconnectedness of phenomena, then it does not possess the deterministic power that Tolstoy attributes to it. By rejecting the totality as a metaphysical absolute, Tugushev argues that the *reductio ad absurdum* loses its force. The deterministic conclusion—that free will is an illusion because all events are part of an unbreakable causal chain—no longer follows necessarily. Without the presupposition of the totality as a closed, deterministic system, the individual is no longer ontologically subordinated to the whole. The individual escapes the trap of being merely a passive, determined node in a cosmic machine. 

Tugushev’s critique thus opens a space for agency. By denying that the totality dictates the absolute determination of the part, he suggests that the individual possesses a degree of independence from the causal continuum. The individual is not entirely swallowed by the whole; there is an ontological gap, a space of indeterminacy, where the self can act. Tugushev’s rejection of the totality is therefore a necessary condition for the rehabilitation of free will. It dismantles the monistic determinism that Tolstoy uses to reduce human beings to mere effectuators of prior causes. In doing so, Tugushev successfully circumvents Tolstoy’s deterministic conclusion, restoring a semblance of ontological dignity to the human subject.

**The Specter of Spontaneity: Freedom as Self-Caused Nothingness**

Yet, Tugushev’s critique, while effectively dismantling Tolstoy’s determinism, exposes a profound and dangerous vulnerability in the concept of free will. If the individual is not determined by the totality, what grounds their actions? The logical trajectory of rejecting the totality seems to lead inexorably toward the opposite extreme: the spontaneous, self-caused force arising out of nothingness. 

If an action is not determined by the totality of prior causes—which includes the agent’s own biological history, psychological constitution, and environmental context—then what is causing the action? The only remaining option, within a framework that denies external determination, is that the action is self-caused. The agent becomes a *causa sui*, a self-moving origin that generates its own decisions out of the void of the unconditioned. But a force that arises out of nothingness is inherently arbitrary. If the will is not shaped by the totality of its past—by its memories, its desires, its physiological states, and its social embeddedness—then it is a pure, unmoored spontaneity. Such a will is not free in any meaningful sense; it is merely random. 

This problem echoes the existentialist critique of radical freedom, most notably in Jean-Paul Sartre’s assertion that human beings are "condemned to be free." Sartre argued that if there is no God or predetermined human nature (analogous to Tolstoy’s totality), then the individual must create their values and actions from nothingness (*le néant*). Yet Sartre himself recognized the anguish of this position, acknowledging that a freedom that is entirely self-grounded is a burden that threatens to dissolve into caprice and absurdity. An action that springs spontaneously from nothingness lacks the rational continuity and moral weight that characterize genuine agency. If my decision to raise my arm is not caused by anything—not by my desire, my belief, my history, nor the totality of the cosmos—but is simply a spontaneous self-creation, then the concept of responsibility collapses. Why hold someone accountable for an action that arises from the void, untethered to any prior state of the self? 

Tugushev’s critique, by severing the link between the individual and the totality, risks reducing free will to this very absurdity. By rejecting the deterministic presupposition of the whole, Tugushev inadvertently leaves the individual stranded in a nihilistic space where the will is nothing but an uncaused, self-caused spark. The deterministic conclusion is avoided, but at the cost of reducing free will to a mere arbitrary eruption, a metaphysical accident that lacks any rational or moral foundation.

**Situated Agency: Navigating Between the Whole and the Void**

To avoid the twin traps of deterministic totality and spontaneous nothingness, one must reconceptualize the nature of agency in a way that transcends the binary Tolstoy and Tugushev have left behind. The solution does not lie in reverting to Tolstoy’s totalizing determinism, nor in embracing the absurdity of uncaused spontaneity, but in articulating a model of situated agency. 

Situated agency posits that the individual is neither entirely determined by the totality nor entirely self-caused out of nothingness. Instead, the agent is a dynamic, emergent field that interacts with its conditions without being wholly subsumed by them. The individual is shaped by the totality—their biology, history, and social context—but they are not reducible to it. The agent possesses a degree of autonomy that allows them to reflect upon, resist, and reconfigure the conditions that determine them. This is not spontaneity arising from nothingness; it is a structured, rational spontaneity that draws its content from the agent’s situated existence while transcending the mechanical determination of the whole. 

Consider the act of a political dissident choosing to speak out against an oppressive regime. The dissident’s action is not determined by the totality in a fatalistic sense; the regime does not force their hand, and their decision is not merely the inevitable output of a deterministic causal chain. Yet, the dissident’s action is not a spontaneous self-creation out of nothingness either. It is grounded in the dissident’s deeply held values, their historical consciousness, their empathy for the oppressed, and their rational assessment of the situation. The dissident’s freedom is not the absence of causality, but the capacity to integrate causal influences into a novel, self-authored response. The self is both the product of the totality and the author of its actions.

This model aligns with the Aristotelian distinction between necessary and voluntary action, and it resonates with the Kantian ideal of autonomy as self-legislation. For Kant, freedom is not the absence of causality, but the capacity of the rational will to impose the moral law upon itself. The individual is determined by their rational nature, but this determination is not an external, mechanical constraint; it is a self-grounding principle. Similarly, situated agency allows us to reject Tolstoy’s totality without lapsing into the arbitrary spontaneity of Tugushev’s void. Freedom becomes the capacity for self-determination within the given conditions of existence, rather than an uncaused, isolated act of the will.

**Evaluating Tugushev’s Success**

Does Tugushev’s critique successfully circumvent the deterministic conclusion without reducing free will to a spontaneous, self-caused force arising out of nothingness? The answer is nuanced: Tugushev’s critique is a necessary but insufficient condition for a robust account of free will. 

Tugushev succeeds admirably in the first half of his task. By identifying and rejecting Tolstoy’s metaphysical presupposition of the totality of being, he successfully dismantles the deterministic *reductio ad absurdum*. He demonstrates that the totalizing monism upon which Tolstoy’s argument depends is an unjustified dogma, not an ontological necessity. By stripping away this presupposition, Tugushev opens the conceptual space for human agency, freeing the individual from the ontological subordination to the whole. In this regard, his critique is a vital corrective to the suffocating determinism that Tolstoy’s novel so powerfully articulates.

However, Tugushev fails in the second half of his task if he leaves the concept of free will merely as the negation of determinism. By rejecting the totality without providing a positive ontology of agency, Tugushev’s critique leaves the door open to the reduction of free will to spontaneous nothingness. If the individual is not determined by the whole, and if no alternative framework of situated, rational self-determination is provided, the logical default is an arbitrary, self-caused will. This is not freedom; it is the philosophical equivalent of a random number generator masquerading as a conscious agent. To truly circumvent determinism without collapsing into absurdity, Tugushev must supplement his critique with an account of how the individual can be both shaped by and transcendent of the totality. 

The danger of Tugushev’s approach lies in its implicit reliance on a binary logic: either the individual is determined by the totality, or the individual is an uncaused cause. This binary is a false dichotomy. By accepting it, Tugushev risks reproducing the very absurdity he seeks to escape. A more sophisticated philosophical move would be to reconceive the relationship between the part and the whole not as one of strict determination or absolute independence, but as one of dynamic, reciprocal interaction. The individual is constituted by the totality, but the totality is also constituted by the individuals within it. Agency emerges from this reciprocal constitution, not from the void of absolute spontaneity nor from the prison of total determination.

**Conclusion**

Tolstoy’s *reductio ad absurdum* in *War and Peace* remains one of the most devastating critiques of naive free will ever committed to literature. By positing the metaphysical presupposition of the totality of being, Tolstoy constructs a deterministic fortress that seems to leave no room for human agency. Nikolay Tugushev’s critique successfully breaches this fortress by rejecting the totality as a metaphysical absolute, thereby circumventing the deterministic conclusion and restoring a space for the individual. 

Yet, the liberation Tugushev offers is precarious. By severing the individual from the totality, his critique risks transforming free will from an illusion of determinism into an absurdity of spontaneous nothingness. A will that creates itself ex nihilo is not free; it is arbitrary, lacking the rational and historical continuity that grounds moral responsibility. Therefore, Tugushev’s critique, while logically potent against Tolstoy’s totalizing determinism, is incomplete. To successfully navigate the space between the whole and the void, Tugushev must move beyond mere negation and articulate a positive vision of agency. 

The ultimate vindication of free will lies not in the rejection of the totality alone, but in the affirmation of situated self-determination. Human freedom is neither the mechanical unfolding of a deterministic whole nor the uncaused eruption of a spontaneous void; it is the dynamic, rational capacity of the situated individual to engage with, reflect upon, and transform the conditions that constitute them. Only by embracing this middle path can we avoid the twin absurdities of Tolstoy’s determinism and the nothingness of pure spontaneity, arriving at a conception of free will that is both metaphysically robust and existentially meaningful.
