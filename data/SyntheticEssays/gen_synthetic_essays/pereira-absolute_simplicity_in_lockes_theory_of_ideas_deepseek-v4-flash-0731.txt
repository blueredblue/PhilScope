## The Indivisible Atom of Thought: A Defense of Absolute Simplicity in Locke’s Theory of Ideas

In the foundational architecture of John Locke’s empiricism, the mind is a *tabula rasa*, a blank slate upon which experience inscribes its characters. The fundamental units of this inscription are “simple ideas”—the irreducible atoms of thought that constitute the entire furniture of human understanding. Locke declares them to be “not compounded” and asserts that the mind “can neither make nor destroy them.” Yet, beneath this deceptively clear pronouncement lies a profound metaphysical ambiguity. Are these simple ideas absolutely indivisible—metaphysical monads with no internal parts whatsoever—or are they merely relatively simple, functioning as simple units for human perception while secretly harboring a complex substructure? This question, far from being a pedantic scholastic quibble, strikes at the very heart of Locke’s epistemology. It determines the coherence of his distinction between simple and complex ideas, the consistency of his account of mental passivity, and the very nature of mental composition. The dialectical tension between Nicholas Jolley’s defense of relative simplicity and D. J. O’Connor’s spatial compounding objection exposes a critical fault line in Lockean scholarship. I will argue that despite the intuitive appeal of relative simplicity, the thesis of absolute simplicity is not only defensible but philosophically necessary for a coherent Lockean system. Adopting absolute simplicity resolves the spatial objection by redefining the ontological status of simple ideas, preserves the strict passivity of the mind against Vere Chappell’s charge of inconsistency, and necessitates a commitment to strong compositionalism over weak compositionalism, thereby providing the only viable framework for understanding how the passive reception of atoms can generate the active construction of complex knowledge.

### I. The Ambiguous Foundation: Locke’s Definition of Simplicity

To evaluate the thesis of absolute simplicity, one must first excavate the precise nature of Locke’s claim. In *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (II.II.1), Locke defines simple ideas as those that “contain not any thing in them, but one uniform appearance, or conception in the mind, and is not distinguishable into different ideas.” He elaborates that these ideas are “the materials of all our knowledge,” and that the understanding, when presented with them, is “merely passive.” The mind can repeat, compare, and unite these ideas, but it cannot “invent or frame one new simple idea” nor “destroy those that are there.”

This definition is pregnant with ambiguity. The phrase “not distinguishable into different ideas” could be read epistemologically: a simple idea is one whose internal complexity, if any, is beneath the threshold of conscious discrimination. This is the interpretation championed by Jolley. Alternatively, it could be read metaphysically: a simple idea is an entity that possesses no parts whatsoever, a qualitative atom that is logically prior to any act of division. This is the absolute interpretation.

The distinction is crucial. If simplicity is relative, it is a function of the perceiver’s cognitive architecture. A simple idea is simply the smallest unit of perception available to a given mind. If simplicity is absolute, it is a property of the idea itself, independent of any observer. The former is contingent and empirical; the latter is necessary and logical. Locke’s language often leans toward the absolute. When he says the mind “cannot make or destroy” these ideas, he is making a strong metaphysical claim about the limits of mental agency. The mind’s power is entirely combinatorial—it can shuffle and rearrange pre-existing atoms, but it cannot synthesize new ones from sub-atomic material. This powerlessness implies that simple ideas are not products of mental synthesis but are given wholly and completely. If they were relatively simple—composed of sub-perceptual parts—the mind would, in some sense, be involved in their construction, even if unconsciously. Thus, the text itself leans toward a strong reading, but the explicit philosophical defense of this position requires navigating the treacherous waters of O’Connor’s objection.

### II. O’Connor’s Spatial Compounding Objection and the Failure of Relative Simplicity

The most formidable challenge to absolute simplicity arises from the apparent spatiality of many simple ideas. D. J. O’Connor argues that Locke’s account of simple ideas of sensation—such as colors, tastes, and sounds—cannot be absolutely simple because they are invariably presented as having spatial or temporal extension. For instance, the simple idea of “red” is never experienced as a bare, dimensionless quale; it is experienced as a red *patch*, a red *surface*, or a red *object*. A patch of red has spatial parts—a left half and a right half, an upper region and a lower region. If it has parts, it is complex. Consequently, O’Connor contends that Locke’s simple ideas are, in fact, complex aggregates of more basic phenomenal elements, and that Locke’s distinction between simple and complex is a psychological artifact rather than a metaphysical reality.

Nicholas Jolley offers a sophisticated defense against this objection by appealing to relative simplicity. Jolley argues that a simple idea is simple because the mind cannot *perceive* its parts. Just as a “minimum visible” in optics is the smallest perceptible point of light—which, despite being theoretically divisible, is experienced as a unified whole—so too are Lockean simple ideas functionally simple. The redness of a patch is simple *for us* because we cannot introspectively divide it into component parts. The mind receives it as a unified gestalt, and its potential divisibility is irrelevant to its status as a simple idea. Jolley’s defense is essentially phenomenal: simplicity is a property of conscious experience, not of the underlying metaphysical structure.

While Jolley’s response is elegant, it ultimately fails to salvage the coherence of Locke’s system for several reasons. First, relative simplicity renders the simple/complex distinction arbitrary and contingent. If simplicity is defined by the limits of human perception, then it becomes an empirical question whether a given idea is simple. A being with superior perceptual faculties—say, a creature with microscopic vision—would find our “simple” ideas of color to be complex, composed of myriad sub-patches. This would make the foundational distinction of Locke’s epistemology relative to the species, undermining its universal and necessary character. Locke’s entire project is to provide a universal account of human understanding, grounded in a stable set of epistemic primitives. Relative simplicity cannot provide such stability.

Second, and more critically, relative simplicity introduces a hidden form of mental activity that contradicts Locke’s explicit commitment to the passivity of the mind in receiving simple ideas. If a simple idea is composed of sub-perceptual parts, then the mind must perform some operation to unify these parts into a single, coherent phenomenal experience. Even if this operation is unconscious or pre-attentive, it is still an act of synthesis. The mind would be actively constructing the simple idea from its constituent elements, which is precisely what Locke denies. The mind is supposed to be a passive recipient of simple ideas, a mirror reflecting the world. If it must actively synthesize sub-parts to form the simple idea, then it is not passive at all. This problem is compounded when we consider that Locke explicitly states that the mind can only combine ideas that are already simple. If simple ideas are themselves the products of combination, the regress is infinite or foundationaless.

Third, Jolley’s relative simplicity fails to account for the logical indivisibility that Locke requires. Locke’s simple ideas are the ultimate terms of analysis. When we decompose a complex idea like “gold,” we arrive at simple ideas of yellowness, weight, fusibility, and so forth. These are the end-points of analysis. If these end-points are themselves merely relatively simple—containing hidden, unperceived parts—then analysis has not reached its logical terminus. There is always a further level of decomposition available, even if we cannot perceive it. This undermines the explanatory power of Locke’s theory. Simple ideas are supposed to provide a foundation for knowledge; they are the self-evident, unanalyzable data upon which all reasoning is built. If they are secretly complex, they cannot serve this foundational role.

Thus, while Jolley’s relative simplicity offers a psychological escape from O’Connor’s spatial objection, it does so at the cost of metaphysical coherence. It makes the distinction between simple and complex a matter of degree rather than kind, introduces an illicit mental activity into the passive reception of sensation, and fails to provide a logical foundation for analysis. To salvage Locke’s project, we must embrace absolute simplicity.

### III. The Case for Absolute Simplicity: Qualities, Not Quantities

The key to defending absolute simplicity against O’Connor’s spatial objection lies in a careful distinction between the *idea* itself and the *object* or *mode of presentation* of that idea. O’Connor’s objection rests on a category error: he conflates the simple idea with the complex perception of an extended object. When I look at a red wall, I do not have a single simple idea of “red” that is extended. Rather, I have a complex idea of a wall, which is a substance, combined with the simple idea of redness, the simple idea of extension, and the simple idea of solidity. The simple idea of redness is not a red patch; it is the *quality* of redness. A quality, unlike a quantity, has no parts.

Consider the simple idea of “heat.” We do not experience heat as a spatial expanse; we experience it as a quale—a certain felt intensity. We can, of course, attribute heat to a spatial object, but the heat itself is not composed of spatial parts. Similarly, the simple idea of “white” is not a white surface; it is the whiteness. The surface is a complex idea formed by the mind combining the simple idea of whiteness with the simple idea of extension. The simple idea of extension itself is not an extended *thing*; it is the idea of a certain relation or structure, which, when combined with other ideas, yields the perception of an extended object. Locke explicitly distinguishes between the idea of extension and the idea of a body. The former is a simple idea; the latter is a complex one.

Therefore, O’Connor’s objection that simple ideas have spatial parts is misguided. It assumes that simple ideas are themselves spatial entities. But Locke’s simple ideas are not entities in the world; they are the qualitative contents of consciousness. A quality is not divisible in the way a quantity is. The redness of a rose cannot be split into two half-rednesses. It can only be instantiated in different locations, but the quality itself remains a unified, indivisible essence. This is precisely what it means to be absolutely simple.

This re-interpretation resolves the spatial compounding objection. The simple idea of red is not a red patch; it is the pure qualitative content of redness. The patch is a complex idea, constructed by the mind’s active combination of the simple idea of redness with the simple idea of extension. The mind is passive in receiving the redness, but it is active in constructing the patch. This is consistent with Locke’s account. He states that the mind can “repeat, compare, and unite” simple ideas to form complex ones. The perception of a red patch is a complex idea of a substance (the wall) modified by a quality (redness) and a quantity (extension). The simple ideas themselves are the indivisible atoms of this construction.

Furthermore, absolute simplicity is required to explain the *necessity* of Locke’s distinction. If simple ideas are absolutely simple, then the distinction between simple and complex is a hard, logical boundary. A simple idea is a fundamental quality; a complex idea is a combination of such qualities. There is no middle ground. This provides a clear, principled criterion for classification. If simple ideas were relatively simple, the boundary would be fuzzy, dependent on the vagaries of perception. Absolute simplicity restores the logical rigor that Locke’s epistemology demands. It allows us to say, with certainty, that the idea of “yellow” is simple, while the idea of “gold” (yellow + weight + fusibility + etc.) is complex.

### IV. Resolving the Passivity Problem: Against Vere Chappell

Vere Chappell has identified a significant tension in Locke’s account of mental operations. Locke claims that the mind is entirely passive in receiving simple ideas, yet he also claims that the mind is active in forming complex ideas. Chappell argues that this creates an inconsistency. If the mind is active in forming complex ideas, then it must possess some power of synthesis or combination. But if it possesses this power, why is it not also active in forming simple ideas? Why can it not create new simple ideas by combining sub-perceptual elements? Chappell suggests that Locke’s account of passivity is either incoherent or requires a sharp, unjustified division between passive reception and active construction.

The thesis of absolute simplicity provides a clean resolution to this problem. The mind is passive in receiving simple ideas because simple ideas are absolutely indivisible. They have no parts to combine. The mind cannot be active in their formation because there is nothing for it to do. It cannot break them down (they have no parts), and it cannot build them up (there are no smaller elements to combine). They are given whole, as unified qualitative atoms. The mind’s passivity is thus not a psychological limitation but a logical necessity. It is impossible to be active in the creation of something that is absolutely simple, because activity requires the manipulation of parts, and absolute simplicity precludes parts.

Conversely, the mind is active in forming complex ideas because complex ideas are, by definition, compositions of simple ideas. The activity of the mind consists in the power to repeat, compare, and unite these atomic elements. This activity is not a violation of passivity, because it operates *on* the passive receptions. The mind does not create the simple ideas; it merely arranges them. It is like a builder who receives bricks (simple ideas) and constructs a house (complex idea). The builder does not create the bricks; he is passive with respect to their existence. But he is active in arranging them into a structure.

If we adopted relative simplicity, Chappell’s objection would be devastating. If a simple idea were composed of sub-perceptual parts, then the mind’s reception of that idea would require an act of synthesis to unify those parts. This synthesis would be a form of mental activity, contradicting the claim that the mind is passive in receiving simple ideas. The mind would be actively constructing the very atoms of thought, which is precisely what Locke denies. Therefore, to preserve the coherence of the passive/active distinction, we must insist on absolute simplicity. The mind is passive because it receives indivisible atoms; it is active only when it combines those atoms into complex structures. This division of labor is only possible if the atoms are absolutely simple.

### V. Strong vs. Weak Compositionalism: The Necessary Consequence

The adoption of absolute simplicity has profound implications for how we understand the nature of complex ideas. Locke distinguishes between two modes of mental operation: the passive reception of simple ideas and the active composition of complex ideas. The question is: what kind of composition is involved? There are two main options. Weak compositionalism holds that a complex idea is merely an aggregate or a heap of simple ideas. The complex idea of “gold” is just the sum of its simple components (yellowness + weight + fusibility + etc.), with no additional structure or unity imposed by the mind. Strong compositionalism holds that a complex idea is a structured unity, where the mind actively imposes a specific organization or relation upon the simple components. The complex idea of “gold” is not just a list of properties but a unified concept of a substance that has these properties in a specific, interrelated way.

Absolute simplicity necessitates a commitment to strong compositionalism. If simple ideas are absolutely indivisible, then they possess no internal structure. They are like individual letters. To form a word (a complex idea), the mind cannot rely on any pre-existing structure within the letters themselves. The letters are just isolated atoms. The mind must actively impose an order and a relation to create a meaningful word. Similarly, to form a complex idea like “triangle,” the mind must not only collect the simple ideas of three lines and three angles but must also actively arrange them into a closed, three-sided figure. The structure of the triangle is not contained in the simple ideas themselves; it is imposed by the mind’s act of combination.

Weak compositionalism, on the other hand, is incompatible with absolute simplicity. If a complex idea were merely an aggregate, it would lack the unity and coherence that Locke attributes to it. Consider the complex idea of a “man.” It is not just a random collection of simple ideas (e.g., shape, color, motion, reason). It is a unified concept of a rational animal. The unity of this concept requires an active act of synthesis, binding the simple ideas together into a coherent whole. If the simple ideas were relatively simple (containing hidden structure), one might argue that the unity of the complex idea is somehow pre-figured in the structure of the simples. But with absolute simplicity, there is no pre-figured structure. The unity must come entirely from the mind’s active imposition.

This commitment to strong compositionalism is not a weakness but a strength of the absolute simplicity thesis. It aligns perfectly with Locke’s own account of the understanding’s activity. Locke states that the mind has the power to “combine several simple ideas into one compound one, and thus all complex ideas are made.” This act of combination is not a passive aggregation; it is an active, deliberate construction. The mind chooses which simple ideas to combine and how to relate them. This is the essence of strong compositionalism. The mind is not a mere container that passively holds a collection of ideas; it is an active agent that structures its contents into meaningful concepts.

Furthermore, strong compositionalism is required to explain the possibility of abstract and general ideas. To form the general idea of “triangle,” the mind must abstract from particular instances and combine the essential features (three sides, three angles) into a unified concept. This abstraction and combination require active mental effort. It cannot be achieved by mere aggregation. The mind must actively select the relevant simple ideas and impose a structural relation upon them. This is only possible if the simple ideas are absolutely simple, because the mind’s active power is the sole source of structure. If the simples were relatively complex, the mind’s active role would be diminished, and the distinction between the given and the constructed would collapse.

Thus, absolute simplicity does not merely resolve a local problem; it necessitates a comprehensive theory of mental composition. It mandates that complex ideas are not just sums but structured wholes, actively forged by the understanding. This strong compositionalism is the only way to account for the unity, coherence, and generality of human thought. It is the necessary corollary of an atomistic epistemology.

### Conclusion: The Indispensable Atom

The debate over the nature of Lockean simple ideas is not a marginal footnote in the history of philosophy; it is the fulcrum upon which the entire weight of his empiricist system turns. D. J. O’Connor’s spatial compounding objection challenges the very possibility of indivisible units of thought, while Nicholas Jolley’s defense of relative simplicity offers a tempting psychological escape. Yet, as I have argued, relative simplicity is a philosophically bankrupt solution. It renders the simple/complex distinction contingent, introduces an illicit mental activity into the passive reception of sensation, and fails to provide a logical foundation for analysis.

The thesis of absolute simplicity, by contrast, emerges as the only coherent interpretation. By distinguishing between the simple idea as a qualitative atom and the complex perception of extended objects, we can dissolve O’Connor’s objection. The simple idea of redness is not a red patch; it is the indivisible quality of redness. This interpretation preserves the strict passivity of the mind against Vere Chappell’s charge of inconsistency. The mind is passive because it receives indivisible atoms; it is active only when it combines them. Finally, absolute simplicity necessitates a commitment to strong compositionalism. Complex ideas are not mere aggregates but structured unities, actively forged by the understanding. This strong compositionalism is the only way to account for the unity and generality of human concepts.

In the final analysis, absolute simplicity is not merely a defensible reading of Locke; it is the indispensable foundation of his entire philosophical project. Without it, the mind is not a passive recipient of the world’s data but an active constructor of its own perceptual primitives, collapsing the distinction between the given and the made. With it, Locke provides a robust, coherent account of how a passive, receptive mind can build the vast and intricate edifice of human knowledge from the simplest, most indivisible atoms of thought. The simple idea, as an absolute unit, is the bedrock of empiricism, and it is upon this bedrock that the entire structure of the understanding must stand or fall.
