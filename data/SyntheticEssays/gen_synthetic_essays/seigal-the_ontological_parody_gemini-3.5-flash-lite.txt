### The Modal Escape Hatch: Charles Hartshorne, the Logic of Perfection, and the Structural Vulnerability of Ontological Parody

#### Introduction: The Ontological Enterprise and the Specter of Parody

The history of natural theology is littered with the wrecks of ontological arguments, yet the enterprise stubbornly refuses to stay interred. At the core of St. Anselm’s *Proslogion* 2 and 3 lies an audacious conceptual maneuver: the extraction of actual, extramental reality from the mere definition of a *quo nihil majus cogitari possit* (that than which nothing greater can be conceived). Anselm’s original formulation has long faced the epistemological and metaphysical barrier of the parody objection—most famously voiced by his contemporary, the monk Gaunilo of Marmoutiers, through the conceit of the *Insula Integerrima*, or the Lost Island. Gaunilo’s critique exposed a foundational structural vulnerability in Anselm's logic: if the definition of an entity as "the greatest conceivable" entails its existence, then one can populate the metaphysical landscape with an infinite archipelago of maximally excellent, yet absurd, entities simply by prefixing "greatest conceivable" to any arbitrary noun.

Immanuel Kant subsequently drove what many philosophers believed was the final nail into the ontological coffin with his famous dictum that "existence is not a real predicate." Kant argued that existence adds nothing to the concept of a subject; it merely posits the subject *in relation to the intellect*. 

Yet, mid-twentieth-century analytic metaphysics witnessed a dramatic resurgence of the ontological argument, spearheaded principally by Charles Hartshorne and later formalized by Alvin Plantinga. Recognizing the fatal ambiguities in Anselm's conflation of ordinary existence with perfection, Hartshorne sought to rehabilitate the argument by anchoring it within a rigorous modal logic—specifically, Brouwerian ($B$) and S5 modal systems—and synthesizing it with the neoclassical metaphysics of Alfred North Whitehead. Hartshorne claimed that by shifting the logical burden from *contingent* existence to *necessary* existence, and by deploying a "logic of perfection" bolstered by Becker’s Postulate, he could successfully insulate the ontological argument from traditional parody objections. 

This essay evaluates Hartshorne’s claim. Does moving from contingent to necessary existence insulate the ontological argument from the generation of mutually exclusive entities and unwanted ontological commitments? Through a granular analysis of Hartshorne’s modal axioms, his deployment of Becker’s Postulate, and a critical confrontation with Gaunilo’s island and Michael Martin’s "Absolute Evil One," I will argue that Hartshorne’s modal framework fails to achieve immunity. Despite its formal sophistication, Hartshorne's argument remains structurally vulnerable to parody because it equivocates on the coherence of maximal instantiation, ultimately collapsing into a framework where logical possibility is illegitimately smuggled into metaphysical necessity through ad-hoc definitional fiat.

#### Section I: The Architectonic of Hartshorne’s Modal Ontology

To understand Hartshorne’s defense against parody, one must first grasp the mechanics of his formulation. Hartshorne famously accused Anselm of confusing two distinct versions of the ontological argument in the *Proslogion*. Chapter 2 of the *Proslogion*, according to Hartshorne, represents a weaker, logically vulnerable argument: it treats existence as a flat predicate and attempts to move from the idea of God to God’s actual existence. Chapter 3, however, contains the true seed of a valid metaphysical proof. Here, Anselm recognizes that existence is not a homogeneous property, but that *necessary* existence is categorically superior to *contingent* existence. 

Hartshorne formalizes this insight within the framework of modal logic. His argument relies on the concept of a "perfection"—defined as a property that is intrinsically unsurpassable, such that if it is exemplified at all, it is exemplified in every possible world (or alternatively, that its non-existence is logically impossible). Let $G$ stand for "divinity" or the property of being "divine," defined as the conjunction of all perfections. 

Hartshorne’s modal argument proceeds via the following steps, utilizing the axioms of modal logic (specifically S5, characterized by the axiom $5: \Diamond P \rightarrow \square \Diamond P$):

1. **Premise 1 (Definition):** God is, by definition, a being with maximal greatness (or supreme perfection). Maximal greatness entails possessing unsurpassable excellence in every possible world.
2. **Premise 2 (Possibility):** It is possible that a maximally great being exists ($\Diamond G$).
3. **Axiom (S5):** If something is possible, its possibility is necessary ($\Diamond P \rightarrow \square \Diamond P$). Therefore, if a maximally great being possibly exists, it necessarily exists ($\square G$).
4. **Conclusion:** Therefore, a maximally great being exists ($\square G \rightarrow G$).

The brilliance of this maneuver lies in its evasion of Kant’s critique. Kant was correct that ordinary, contingent existence is not a real predicate; adding "exists" to the concept of a hundred possible dollars does not add a single cent to their conceptual content. However, *necessary* existence is not a contingent property added to a subject; rather, it is a structural modality of the subject’s essence. If a being’s essence is such that its non-existence is logically absurd—if it is a necessary being—then to conceive of it as possible is analytically equivalent to conceiving of it as actual in all logical space.

Yet, the linchpin of Hartshorne’s entire edifice is **Premise 2**: the assertion that a maximally great being is *logically possible*. Hartshorne recognizes that an atheist can readily block the argument by denying Premise 2, claiming that the concept of God is internally incoherent, thereby rendering $\Diamond G$ false. To preempt this move, Hartshorne introduces his "Logic of Perfection," which relies heavily on modal axioms and, crucially, **Becker’s Postulate** (formalized by the logician Oskar Becker), to demonstrate that perfection is either logically necessary or logically impossible—there is no middle ground of mere contingency.

---

#### Section II: Becker’s Postulate and the Logic of Perfection

Hartshorne’s deployment of Becker’s Postulate is the engine that drives his ontological proof past the objections of classical empiricism and positivism. Becker’s Postulate is a modal thesis which states that *contingent necessity* or *contingent impossibility* of perfection is impossible; in modal terms:

$$\Diamond \neg G \rightarrow \square \neg G$$
*(If it is possible that God does not exist, then it is necessary that God does not exist.)*

Or, viewed through its contrapositive equivalent:

$$\neg \square \neg G \rightarrow \neg \Diamond \neg G$$
Which simplifies, in S5 systems, to:

$$\Diamond G \rightarrow \square G$$
*(If God is possible, God is necessary.)*

Why does Hartshorne believe Becker’s Postulate holds true for perfections? His logic of perfection rests on the premise that perfections are, by definition, unsurpassable properties. A property is a perfection if and only if its instantiation is intrinsically superior to its non-instantiation, and its absence is a lack or limitation. 

Hartshorne argues that a perfection cannot be a matter of contingent fact. If a property like supreme power, supreme knowledge, or supreme goodness were only contingently exemplified—that is, if it existed in this world but failed to exist in some other possible world—then its possession would depend on external conditions, accidents, or limitations. But a perfection, by definition, is self-existent and independent of external causal matrices. Therefore, if a perfection exists in *any* possible world, its essence is such that nothing could prevent its existence in *every* possible world. Conversely, if it is deficient or impossible in any single possible world, its very concept involves an internal contradiction, rendering it impossible in *all* possible worlds.

Thus, the modal status of a perfection collapses the tripartite traditional modal categories (contingent, impossible, necessary) into a stark binary: **a perfection is either logically impossible or logically necessary.** 

With this machinery in place, Hartshorne issues a sweeping challenge to the skeptic: To deny God's existence is not merely to claim that God happens to be absent from the actual world; it is to claim that God is a *logical impossibility*. The atheist must prove that the concept of a maximally great being contains an intrinsic contradiction (such as the paradox of the stone or an incoherent combination of attributes like omnipotence and moral perfection). If the atheist cannot demonstrate this internal incoherence, then the concept remains logically possible ($\Diamond G$). And once $\Diamond G$ is granted, Becker’s Postulate and the S5 axiom $\Diamond P \rightarrow \square \P$ inexorably drag the skeptic across the logical finish line: $\Diamond G \rightarrow \square G \rightarrow G$.

It is precisely at this juncture—the transition from the absence of demonstrated incoherence to metaphysical actuality—that the specter of parody rises with renewed, modal fury.

#### Section III: The Parody Objection Modernized — Gaunilo’s Island and the Logic of Unwanted Ontological Commitments

To evaluate whether Hartshorne’s modal framework successfully insulates the ontological argument from parody, we must first examine how traditional parodies function and how modalized parodies operate. 

In *Pro Insipiente* ("On Behalf of the Fool"), Gaunilo of Marmoutiers famously challenged Anselm by substituting "God" with the "Lost Island":

> "If a man should try to prove to me by such reasoning that this island truly exists and that its existence should no longer be doubted, I should either think that he was joking, or I should not know which of us I ought to judge the more foolish..."

Gaunilo’s objection is an argument by structural analogy (reductio ad absurdum). He claims that if Anselm’s logic is valid, one can define *any* finite, contingent object—an island, a mountain, a palace—as possessing "maximal excellence" within its category (e.g., the island than which no greater can be conceived) and thereby prove its actual existence through pure conceptual analysis.

Anselm’s standard historical reply was that his argument applies *exclusively* to that than which a greater cannot be conceived, and an island is intrinsically limited—it is a physical object bounded by space, subject to geologic decay, and composed of contingent matter. Therefore, an island cannot possess unsurpassable greatness, because one can always conceive of a non-physical, eternal, or morally perfect reality that is infinitely greater than any lump of rock and sand.

Hartshorne deploys a similar defense against Gaunilo: physical islands are inherently contingent, parochial, and subject to internal structural limits. They do not instantiate *perfections* in Hartshorne’s sense of the term. A perfection, for Hartshorne, is a property that can be maximized across all possible worlds without internal incoherence. An island, however, has defining characteristics (e.g., being surrounded by water, having a specific spatial extension) that are logically incompatible with unsurpassable greatness. To speak of a "greatest possible island" is to commit a category mistake; greatness cannot be maximized within the category of finite, material topography.

#### Section IV: Michael Martin and the Absolute Evil One

Recognizing that Hartshorne’s defense successfully sidesteps parochial parodies like Gaunilo’s island by restricting the argument to *authentic perfections* (properties whose maximal instantiation is coherent), contemporary critics—most notably philosopher Michael Martin—formulated far more sophisticated parodies designed to detonate the logic of perfection from within.

In his critique of modal ontological arguments, Michael Martin introduces the concept of the **Absolute Evil One** (or malignant perfection). Let us construct the argument for the Absolute Evil One (let $E$ stand for this entity) using Hartshorne’s exact logical scaffolding:

1. **Definition:** The Absolute Evil One is a being that possesses maximal malignity (surpassable malevolence, absolute power harnessed for absolute destruction, supreme cruelty, and radical ontological independence) in every possible world.
2. **Becker’s Postulate for Evil:** Malign perfection, just like moral perfection, cannot be merely contingent. If absolute evil exists in any possible world, its nature is such that it is uncaused, unlimited, and necessary. Conversely, if it is impossible in any world, it is impossible in all worlds.
3. **Possibility Premise:** It is logically possible that the Absolute Evil One exists ($\Diamond E$).
4. **Modal Collapse (S5):** By the S5 axiom ($\Diamond P \rightarrow \square P$), if the Absolute Evil One is possible, it is necessary ($\square E$).
5. **Conclusion:** Therefore, the Absolute Evil One necessarily exists ($\square E \rightarrow E$).

The force of Martin’s parody is devastating to Hartshorne’s system. Why should we accept that the concept of a maximally evil being is logically impossible? Atheists and agnostics routinely argue that the concept of a maximally *good* God is incoherent due to the problem of evil, hiddenness, and logical contradictions in divine attributes (e.g., how can an immutable being respond to petitionary prayer?). Yet, if theism is deemed coherent enough to pass the threshold of logical possibility ($\Diamond G$), the symmetry of modal logic dictates that the concept of a maximally *evil* being ($\Diamond E$) is equally coherent. 

If both $\Diamond G$ and $\Diamond E$ are granted epistemic admission as "not yet proven logically impossible," we arrive at a catastrophic metaphysical collision: modal logic forces the simultaneous, necessary existence of both the supreme good and the absolute evil. Since God (as traditionally defined by Hartshorne) is essentially good, omnibenevolent, and opposed to absolute evil, the simultaneous necessary existence of God and the Absolute Evil One generates a direct logical contradiction in modal space ($\square(G \land \neg E)$ collides with $\square E$).

How does Hartshorne respond to this? Hartshorne’s defense rests entirely on his doctrine of the **Asymmetry of Perfection**. 

---

#### Section V: The Asymmetry of Perfection and the Defeat of Parody

Hartshorne anticipated that critics would attempt to construct symmetrical parodies like the Absolute Evil One, and his response constitutes the most sophisticated line of defense in his neoclassical metaphysics. Hartshorne argues that *perfection* is not a symmetrical logical operator; goodness and evil do not occupy equivalent metaphysical strata.

According to Hartshorne’s logic of perfection:
1. **Goodness is intrinsically positive, while evil is intrinsically parasitic or privative.** Drawing on classical Augustinian and neo-Platonic metaphysics, Hartshorne maintains that evil has no positive ontological reality of its own; it is always a corruption, lack, or misdirection of a good. A maximally evil being would require immense power, intelligence, and organizational capacity—all of which are *perfections* in themselves. Therefore, absolute evil is conceptually parasitic upon absolute goodness. One cannot have a maximally powerful, maximally intelligent agent of pure destruction without attributing foundational perfections to it, which collapses the "evil" back into a corrupted manifestation of the divine.
2. **Perfection entails unsurpassability.** A property is a perfection only if its maximum degree is logically coherent and unsurpassable. Hartshorne argues that while "power," "knowledge," and "love" can be maximized without internal incoherence (forming the concept of God), "malevolence" or "cruelty" cannot be maximized. Why? Because absolute malevolence aims at the destruction of all value, including the very consciousness and agency required to experience or execute malevolence. A maximally evil being would logically destroy its own subjects, its own capacity for relational awareness, and its own arena of operation. Malevolence is inherently self-defeating and entropic, whereas divine love and creative power are self-amplifying and generative. Therefore, "maximal evil" is a conceptual oxymoron—a square circle—whereas "maximal goodness" is logically coherent.

Thus, Hartshorne attempts to block Martin’s Absolute Evil One by denying the parity of the possibility premise ($\Diamond E$). While $\Diamond G$ is coherent because goodness is self-consistent and ontologically primary, $\Diamond E$ is strictly *impossible* ($\neg \Diamond E$) because absolute evil is self-destructive and conceptually parasitic.

#### Section VI: Critical Evaluation — Does Hartshorne’s Defense Hold?

We have now arrived at the philosophical crucible. Does Hartshorne’s asymmetry thesis successfully insulate his modal ontological argument from parody, or does it smuggle in unjustified metaphysical assumptions under the guise of formal logic?

I argue that Hartshorne’s defense fails upon rigorous examination, for three interconnected reasons: **the equivocation on the coherence of maximization, the question-begging nature of the asymmetry thesis, and the vulnerability to non-moral parodies that bypass the privation theory of evil.**

##### 1. The Equivocation on Coherence
Hartshorne dismisses the Absolute Evil One by declaring maximal malevolence to be self-defeating and conceptually incoherent. But this maneuver cuts both ways and threatens to hoist the modal ontological argument by its own petard. 

If the standard for logical possibility ($\Diamond P$) requires demonstrating that a concept is free from internal contradiction, then the traditional concept of God faces an extraordinarily high, arguably insurmountable, bar of coherence. Philosophers have argued for centuries that the traditional attributes of God—divine omniscience combined with libertarian free will, immutability combined with divine responsiveness to human prayer, and omnipotence combined with moral perfection—are logically incompatible. If omnipotence entails the ability to do anything, can God create a stone too heavy for God to lift? If God possesses middle knowledge, how does this reconcile with creaturely freedom?

If these traditional divine attributes harbor even a single latent logical contradiction, then $\Diamond G$ is false. And if $\Diamond G$ is false, then by Becker’s Postulate ($\Diamond G \rightarrow \square G$), God’s non-existence is necessary ($\square \neg G$). 

Hartshorne cannot simply assert that divine perfection is coherent while evil perfection is incoherent without engaging in massive philosophical labor. By shifting the burden entirely onto the coherence of the concept, the modal ontological argument ceases to be an *argument for God's existence* and becomes an epistemological promissory note: the argument only works *if* one already assumes that the complex, multifaceted concept of a maximally great being is logically coherent across all possible worlds. But proving the absolute, unassailable logical coherence of infinite goodness, infinite power, and infinite knowledge is a task as difficult as proving God's existence in the first place. The ontological argument thus begs the question: it assumes the very modal reality it purports to establish.

##### 2. The Failure of the Privation Theory against Modal Parodies
Hartshorne’s reliance on the traditional privation theory of evil (that evil is merely the absence of good) to dismantle Michael Martin’s Absolute Evil One is philosophically outdated and metaphysically suspect. 

In contemporary analytic philosophy and modal metaphysics, one does not need to posit "malevolence" as a primitive, metaphysically independent substance to construct a structural parody. Consider a modal parody that bypasses moral categories entirely and targets **maximal indifference** or **maximal chaos**—what we might call the **Absolute Entropic Principle** ($AE$).

Let $AE$ be defined as:
* An entity that possesses maximal entropic dispersion, absolute structural dissolution, and supreme informational noise in every possible world.
* $AE$ is not "evil" (which would trigger Hartshorne’s privation critique); rather, $AE$ is the foundational, irreducible principle of cosmic disintegration. 
* Is maximal entropy coherent? Absolutely. Physics describes our actual universe as tending inexorably toward thermodynamic equilibrium (maximum entropy). To conceive of a maximally entropic ground of reality is entirely coherent—indeed, many materialist cosmologies view chaos and void as the primordial baseline of existence, out of which order is a rare, highly improbable fluctuation.

If we apply Hartshorne’s modal framework to $AE$:
1. **Possibility:** It is logically possible that a foundational, maximally entropic principle governs modal space ($\Diamond AE$).
2. **Becker’s Postulate:** A foundational ontological principle cannot be merely contingent; if entropy is fundamental, it is necessary ($\square AE$).
3. **S5 Collapse:** Therefore, $AE$ necessarily exists.

Hartshorne’s defense regarding the "asymmetry of goodness" completely collapses here. Entropy, chaos, and structural dissolution are not "privations" of goodness in any meaningful theological sense; they are positive physical and metaphysical realities. If Hartshorne claims that unsurpassable greatness must be *normatively positive* (i.e., that greatness entails value), the critic can simply ask: *By what independent, non-question-begging standard of modal logic is "value" ontologically privileged over "chaos" or "neutral void"?* 

To declare that only positive value can be necessary is to import an unverified axiological realism into the formal machinery of modal logic. Modal logic is blind to moral or axiological value; it operates on truth-values, accessibility relations, and quantifiers. Smuggling a preference for "perfection" into the definition of modality turns the ontological argument from a demonstration of objective reality into an exercise in semantic stipulativeness.

##### 3. Structural Parity and the Arbitrariness of the Possibility Premise
The most devastating critique of Hartshorne’s modal ontological argument is that its formal structure is so permissive that it can be weaponized to prove the necessary existence of *any* maximal property, provided the creator of the parody has sufficient imagination. 

Let us construct a neutral modal parody: **The Maximally Arbitrary Entity (The Orthogonal Maximum, or OM)**.
* Let $OM$ be defined as an entity that instantiates maximal relational complexity and absolute formal symmetry in every possible world.
* Its definition contains no moral predicates, no privations, and no internal contradictions. It is simply a formal metaphysical anchor.
* By Becker’s Postulate and S5, if $OM$ is possible, $OM$ is necessary.

Why should we accept $\Diamond G$ as intuitively plausible while rejecting $\Diamond OM$ or $\Diamond AE$? Hartshorne’s answer is that perfections are uniquely tied to our immediate spiritual, aesthetic, and rational intuitions—we *sense* that unsurpassable greatness is a coherent and compelling ideal. 

However, appealing to intuitive plausibility is a fatal retreat for a deductive metaphysical argument. Intuitions are culturally conditioned, historically contingent, and deeply subjective. What appears intuitively coherent to a neoclassical process philosopher steeped in panentheism will appear utterly bizarre or logically opaque to a nominalist, a logical positivist, or a Buddhist philosopher. If the validity of the ontological argument rests on whether one "intuits" the modal possibility of a maximally great being, then the argument ceases to be a compelling proof for the skeptic and becomes merely an echo chamber for the already convinced.

---

#### Section VII: Conclusion — The Impossibility of Modal Insulation

Charles Hartshorne performed a heroic service for natural theology by rescuing the ontological argument from Anselm’s naive, flat-footed realism and anchoring it in the sophisticated machinery of modal logic, S5 axioms, and Becker’s Postulate. By shifting the debate from contingent existence to necessary existence and demanding that atheists disprove the logical possibility of perfection, Hartshorne shifted the burden of proof in fascinating ways.

Yet, our evaluation demonstrates that Hartshorne’s modal version of the ontological argument **does not successfully resolve traditional parody objections.** 

The attempt to insulate the argument fails at its most vulnerable joints:
1. **The Possibility Premise ($\Diamond G$) remains an unsubstantiated dogma.** To establish that a maximally great being is possible, Hartshorne must prove the absolute internal coherence of an infinitely complex cluster of attributes (omnipotence, omniscience, moral perfection, necessary existence). Until that proof is delivered—an impossible demand given perennial theological paradoxes—$\Diamond G$ remains an assumption rather than a demonstrated fact.
2. **The Asymmetry of Perfection is an ad-hoc rescue maneuver.** Hartshorne’s dismissal of parodies like Michael Martin’s Absolute Evil One relies on the unproven theological assumption that goodness is ontologically primary and evil is privative. When confronted with non-moral parodies based on neutral ontological principles like maximal entropy or formal symmetry, the privation theory collapses entirely.
3. **Structural Parity triumphs over modal engineering.** Modal logic is a formal system; it does not care what content is poured into its variables. If the S5 axiom and Becker’s Postulate can be used to hoist a maximally great God out of the swamp of mere possibility into necessary actuality, the exact same logical levers can be utilized to hoist a legion of competing, mutually exclusive maxima into necessary existence. 

Ultimately, Hartshorne’s modal ontological argument remains what Immanuel Kant suspected all such arguments are: an attempt to lift oneself by the hair out of the quagmire of pure conceptual possibility into the solid ground of actual reality. By defining perfection in such a way that its possibility entails its necessity, Hartshorne has not discovered a window into the structure of objective reality; he has merely drawn a magnificent circle around his own definitions and declared that nothing outside it can possibly exist. The parody objections—from Gaunilo’s island to Martin’s Absolute Evil One—reveal that the door to this modal sanctuary swings both ways, allowing any conceptual chimera to enter once the locks of logical possibility are picked.
