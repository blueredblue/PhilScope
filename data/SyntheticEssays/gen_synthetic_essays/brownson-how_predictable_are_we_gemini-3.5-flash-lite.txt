# The Limits of the Stance: Why Dennett’s Intentional Strategy Fails in the Face of Human Suboptimality

## Introduction

In his extensive work on the philosophy of mind, Daniel Dennett has consistently argued that understanding the mental lives of complex entities does not require uncovering the occult machinery of Cartesian souls or even committing to the strict neurophysiological reductionism of eliminative materialism. Instead, Dennett offers an instrumentalist and pragmatically grounded framework: the Intentional Stance. According to this view, when we wish to predict and explain the behavior of an entity—whether a thermostat, a chess-playing computer, a pigeon, or a human being—we frequently find it most efficient to treat that entity as a rational agent possessing beliefs, desires, and other intentional states. 

Crucially, Dennett’s deployment of the Intentional Stance is anchored in an overarching evolutionary presupposition. He maintains that natural selection is a maximizer of design efficiency, ruthlessly weeding out suboptimal engineering and sculpting cognitive systems that approximate ideal rationality. Consequently, to adopt the Intentional Stance toward an organism is to presume that the organism *ought* to believe what it *ought* to believe given its environment and goals, and that it *ought* to act accordingly to maximize its evolutionary fitness.

However, this reliance on an idealized, adaptationist model of evolution creates a deep theoretical vulnerability. By building the assumption of optimal rationality into the foundational mechanics of the Intentional Stance, Dennett renders his predictive strategy ill-equipped to account for the pervasive suboptimality, irrationality, and architectural idiosyncrasies of actual human beings. 

This essay argues that Dennett’s predictive strategy fails because it rests upon indefensible adaptationist assumptions drawn from evolutionary biology—assumptions thoroughly critiqued by biologists such as Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin, who demonstrated that biological systems are rife with spandrels, constraints, and non-adaptive evolutionary spillage. Furthermore, this theoretical gap is exposed by philosophers like Stephen Stich, whose empirical work on cognitive psychology reveals that human beings routinely make systematic errors, harbor fallacious beliefs, and violate the axioms of instrumental rationality. 

Finally, I will argue that to rescue behavioral prediction from the Procrustean bed of Dennett’s optimization models, we must abandon the pure, normatively driven Intentional Stance in favor of an empathetic predictive strategy—one that asks, “*What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?*” This shift from abstract, idealized rationality to situated, empathetic simulation provides a far more coherent, psychologically realistic framework for navigating the messy reality of human action.

## Part I: The Mechanics of the Intentional Stance and Its Evolutionary Anchor

To evaluate Dennett’s framework, we must first examine its structure and its functional dependencies. Dennett outlines three primary strategies or "stances" for predicting and explaining physical systems:

1. **The Physical Stance:** Used for predicting the behavior of physical objects based on their mass, chemical composition, and physical laws. Here, we calculate the trajectory of a falling rock or the thermal dissipation of a radiator.
2. **The Design Stance:** Used when we understand a system's function by assuming it is designed to perform a specific task. We do not need to know the exact physical constitution of a digital watch or an internal combustion engine; we need only know its design and assume it will function according to that design.
3. **The Intentional Stance:** Reserved for complex systems whose behavior is so intricate that the design and physical stances become computationally intractable. In this stance, we treat the object as a rational agent. We attribute beliefs (what the system takes to be true about the world) and desires (what the system values or seeks to achieve), and we deduce its subsequent behavior via the principle of instrumental rationality: the agent will do what it has reason to do to further its desires given its beliefs.

Dennett insists that the Intentional Stance is not merely a useful heuristic for humans who happen to lack the computational power to trace millions of neural firings. Rather, it is constitutive of intentionality itself. For Dennett, there is no "real" inner essence of belief residing behind the behavioral facade; being a believer just *is* being an entity whose behavior is reliably and robustly predictable via the Intentional Stance.

However, the efficacy of the Intentional Stance depends on a normative baseline. If we are to attribute beliefs and desires to a system, we must have a principled way of deciding *which* beliefs and desires to attribute. Dennett solves this through the principle of **rationality**. We assume that the agent is rational—that its belief-fixing mechanisms are generally truth-tracking, that its inferential machinery is logically coherent, and that its actions are optimized to achieve its goals. If an agent confronts a predator, we attribute to it the belief that the predator is dangerous, the desire to survive, and the action-generating belief-desire pair that leads directly to flight.

Where does this baseline of rationality come from? Dennett explicitly grounds it in evolutionary biology. Mother Nature, operating via natural selection, is the ultimate designer. Evolution ruthlessly eliminates organisms that harbor systematically false beliefs or act in ways counter to their reproductive self-interest. Therefore, natural selection acts as a universal optimizing agent, ensuring that biological creatures approximate idealized, rational agents. As Dennett writes, "Evolution is the process of design that builds intentional systems." 

It is precisely this evolutionary anchor—this foundational commitment to adaptationism—that ties the success of the Intentional Stance to the premise that human minds are optimized, rational instruments shaped to fit their ecological niches with maximal efficiency.

## Part II: The Biological Critique: Gould, Lewontin, and the Myth of Universal Optimization

Dennett’s reliance on adaptationism as the engine of cognitive rationality puts him at direct odds with major developments in evolutionary biology, most notably the critique of strict adaptationism articulated by Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin in their seminal 1979 paper, “The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm.”

Gould and Lewontin targeted the adaptationist program—the mindset, prevalent among evolutionary biologists and frequently imported by evolutionary psychologists and philosophers like Dennett, which views every morphological, physiological, and behavioral trait as an optimally designed adaptation sculpted by natural selection for a specific current utility. They likened this approach to Dr. Pangloss in Voltaire’s *Candide*, who maintained that this is the best of all possible worlds and that everything is made for the best. 

In architecture, the term "spandrel" refers to the tapered, triangular spaces formed between the tops of adjacent arches supporting a dome. These spaces are necessary structural byproducts of placing a circular dome upon rounded arches meeting at right angles. Once the spandrels exist, architects may paint them with elaborate frescoes, as Michelangelo did in the Sistine Chapel. A naive observer might look at the frescoes and conclude that the structural geometry of the cathedral was designed specifically to display those paintings. But this gets the causal arrow backward: the paintings are a secondary exploitation of a structural byproduct, not the reason the space exists.

Gould and Lewontin argued that biological organisms are replete with spandrels—traits that are structural byproducts of other evolutionary developments, historical constraints, genetic linkages, or developmental architecture, rather than direct adaptations for fitness. Furthermore, evolution is constrained by physics, chemistry, phylogenetic history, and sheer developmental developmental noise. It does not produce optimal engineering; it produces tinkering, compromises, and kludges that are "good enough" to pass the threshold of reproductive viability.

When we map this biological critique onto Dennett’s Intentional Stance, the foundational assumptions of his predictive strategy begin to fracture. Dennett requires that human cognitive architecture approximate an ideal rational optimization engine because he assumes natural selection optimizes cognition in the same way an engineer optimizes an aircraft wing. But if the human mind is a patchwork of evolutionary spandrels—if our cognitive architecture is laden with historical baggage, pleiotropic genetic side effects, structural constraints, and non-adaptive behavioral byproducts—then the assumption of idealized rationality collapses.

A human being is not an optimized calculating machine; she is an evolutionary kludge. We possess visual systems prone to optical illusions, memory systems that reconstruct narratives rather than recording facts, and emotional processing loops that frequently hijack our deliberative capacities. If we apply Dennett’s Intentional Stance to an organism whose cognitive architecture is riddled with spandrels and structural inefficiencies, the attribution of optimal rationality yields wildly inaccurate predictions. When an agent acts irrationally, self-destructively, or illogically, the Dennettians are forced into increasingly convoluted epicycles of belief-desire attribution—attributing bizarre, hidden, or irrational-sounding desires just to preserve the *a priori* commitment to underlying rationality.

## Part III: The Psychological Reality: Stephen Stich and Human Suboptimality

While Gould and Lewontin undermined Dennett’s evolutionary premises from the side of biology, philosopher Stephen Stich launched a devastating critique from the side of cognitive psychology and philosophy of science, directly challenging the assumption that human beings are naturally rational maximizers.

In works such as *The Fragmentation of Reason* (1990) and numerous foundational essays in cognitive science, Stich argued against what he termed "Aprioristic Psychologism" and the pervasive assumption among analytic philosophers that human cognitive processes naturally approximate normative standards of rationality (such as expected utility theory, deductive logic, and Bayesian probability updating). 

Stich pointed to the massive empirical literature in cognitive psychology—pioneered by researchers like Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman—which demonstrated that human reasoning is systematically plagued by cognitive biases and heuristics. When faced with probabilistic reasoning, framing effects, base-rate fallacies, confirmation biases, and loss aversion, human subjects do not behave like Dennett’s idealized intentional systems. Instead, they make predictable, systematic errors that violate the axioms of rational choice.

For example, consider the conjunction fallacy exposed by Tversky and Kahneman in the famous "Linda problem." Subjects are given a description of Linda as a young, single, outspoken, and deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice. They are then asked which is more probable: that Linda is a bank teller, or that Linda is a bank teller *and* active in the feminist movement. A vast majority of subjects judge the second option to be more probable, violating a foundational law of probability theory (the probability of a conjunction cannot exceed the probability of its conjuncts).

From a Dennettian perspective, how are we to interpret this behavior using the Intentional Stance? If we adhere to Dennett's requirement of rationality, we are forced to attribute to Linda’s evaluator a deeply irrational set of beliefs or a bizarre, non-standard conceptual scheme in which set inclusion rules do not apply. But this trivializes the Intentional Stance, turning it into an unfalsifiable dogma. If *any* behavior—no matter how logically incoherent or statistically illiterate—can be rationalized by inventing sufficiently strange beliefs and desires, then the Intentional Stance loses its predictive and explanatory bite. It ceases to be an empirical strategy and becomes an exercise in hermeneutic charity run amok.

Stich argues that there is no good reason to suppose that natural selection favors cognitive systems that track normative standards of truth and logical validity. Evolution cares about *fitness*, not truth. A false belief that prompts swift flight from a harmless rustle in the bushes is evolutionarily superior to a true, painstakingly verified belief that arrives one second too late to prevent predation. Our cognitive mechanisms are cobbled together to solve specific adaptive problems in ancestral environments, often relying on fast-and-frugal heuristics that trade accuracy for speed and computational tractability.

Consequently, Stich contends that the normative models of rationality championed by Dennett and classical decision theorists have no privileged status in cognitive science. Human beings are fundamentally suboptimal reasoning engines. When we attempt to predict human behavior by assuming that people possess coherent belief systems and process information in accordance with optimal rationality, our predictive models fail precisely where human beings diverge from the idealized norm—which is almost everywhere outside of highly constrained, artificial environments.

## Part IV: The Tension Between Normative Optimization and Empirical Reality

The convergence of Gould and Lewontin’s biological critique with Stich’s cognitive critique exposes a profound internal tension within Dennett’s philosophy of mind. 

Dennett wants to maintain an instrumentalist account of intentionality that is liberated from the burden of proving that beliefs and desires correspond to distinct neurological micro-states. He achieves this by tying the instrumental success of the Intentional Stance to the macro-level teleology of evolutionary adaptation. The stance works, he claims, because evolution *has* designed organisms to be intentional systems that navigate their worlds rationally. 

However, this creates a tight conceptual circle:
1. We predict human behavior by assuming the agent is rational.
2. We justify the assumption of rationality by pointing to evolutionary optimization (Mother Nature as designer).
3. But evolutionary biology (via Gould, Lewontin, and modern evolutionary developmental biology) reveals that Mother Nature is an opportunistic tinkerer, not an optimizer, leaving organisms full of spandrels, constraints, and non-adaptive traits.
4. Concurrently, cognitive psychology (via Stich, Kahneman, Tversky, and others) demonstrates that actual humans are systematically irrational, prone to systematic cognitive failures, and driven by heuristics that flagrantly violate idealized norms of rationality.
5. Therefore, the evolutionary anchor of the Intentional Stance is broken, rendering the stance an unreliable predictive tool for real human behavior.

Dennett attempts to buffer his position by arguing that intentional systems are idealizations. Just as classical economics assumes perfectly rational actors with complete information without claiming that *every* individual is a walking calculator, Dennett suggests that the Intentional Stance operates under an idealizing assumption. 

Yet, this defense collapses under the weight of human cognitive architecture. In economics, the assumption of perfect rationality is increasingly recognized as a catastrophic failure mode when applied to actual human behavior, spawning the revolution of behavioral economics. Similarly, in the philosophy of mind, clinging to an idealized model of rationality when predicting human behavior leads to systemic predictive failures in precisely the domains that matter most: interpersonal conflict, moral decision-making, political polarization, and emotional trauma—areas where human beings are notoriously driven by irrational fears, cognitive dissonance, tribal loyalties, and unexamined biases.

If the Intentional Stance requires us to treat agents as rational optimizers, and if humans are manifestly *not* rational optimizers, then the stance must either be modified or supplemented with a framework that can accommodate human suboptimality.

## Part V: The Alternative: The Empathetic Predictive Strategy

If Dennett’s optimization-based Intentional Stance founders on the rocks of human suboptimality, what is the alternative? How do we successfully predict and explain human behavior in daily life? 

I propose that when humans predict the behavior of other humans, we do not typically adopt Dennett’s pure, normatively driven Intentional Stance, which calculates what the agent *ought* to do based on an objective assessment of goals and beliefs. Instead, we adopt an **empathetic predictive strategy**, encapsulated by the imaginative simulation question: 

> *“What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?”*

This formulation diverges sharply from Dennett’s model in two crucial respects:
1. It replaces the objective, third-person normative baseline of ideal rationality with a first-person, situated simulation.
2. It explicitly factors in the target’s specific idiosyncrasies, flaws, emotional states, and suboptimalities ("*if I were like him*"), rather than projecting an idealized, universally rational agent onto the subject.

### The Mechanics of Empathetic Simulation

To understand how this empathetic strategy operates, we can look to simulation theory in philosophy of mind (defended by philosophers like Robert Gordon and Alvin Goldman), which contrasts with Dennett’s theory-theory approach. According to simulation theory, we do not deploy a complex, folk-psychological theoretical framework (a "theory of mind" containing nomological generalizations about beliefs and desires) to predict others. Instead, we use our own cognitive and emotional machinery as a mental simulator.

When we want to predict what a friend, a colleague, or even a stranger will do, we feed our own decision-making apparatus with the perceived parameters of the other person’s situation:
* We imagine ourselves in their physical and social context.
* We provisionally adopt their perspective, including their specific background assumptions.
* Crucially, we adjust the simulation to account for their known deviations from ideal rationality—their specific anxieties, stubbornness, historical traumas, cognitive biases, or ideological blind spots.

If we are trying to predict the behavior of a colleague who is behaving defensively and irrationally during a budget meeting, Dennett’s pure Intentional Stance struggles. If we treat the colleague as an ideal rational maximizer whose goal is the health of the company, her defensive outbursts appear inexplicable, forcing us to invent elaborate, convoluted rationalizations for her behavior (e.g., attributing hidden, Machiavellian long-term desires).

By contrast, the empathetic strategy (“*What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?*”) handles this suboptimality with ease. We recognize that the colleague is insecure, feels professionally threatened, and is prone to emotional dysregulation under stress. We run a simulation of our own psyche under the influence of those exact stressors and flaws. The resulting prediction—that she will lash out defensively, reject constructive criticism, and double down on a flawed proposal—is accurate precisely *because* it incorporates human suboptimality rather than abstract optimization.

### Why Empathy Accommodates Evolutionary Spandrels

The empathetic strategy successfully sidesteps the biological critiques of Gould and Lewontin because it does not depend on the myth of evolutionary optimization. 

When we simulate another person, we are not assuming that their brain has been sculpted by natural selection into a flawless Bayesian inference engine. We are simulating a fellow evolutionary kludge. We implicitly understand that human beings are bundles of evolutionary spandrels—that our cognitive machinery is constrained by ancient emotional circuits (fear, anger, status-seeking) designed for ancestral savanna conditions rather than modern bureaucratic environments. 

Because the simulator *shares* these very same evolutionary constraints and cognitive biases, the simulation naturally captures the irrationalities of the target. We know what it feels like to be gripped by confirmation bias, because we suffer from it too. We know what it is like to succumb to sunk-cost fallacies, because our own decision-making is similarly compromised. Thus, the empathetic strategy leverages our shared suboptimality as an epistemic tool, rather than ignoring it in favor of an impossible standard of idealized rationality.

## Part VI: Objections and Counterarguments

A defender of Dennett’s framework might raise several significant objections to this critique and to the proposed empathetic alternative.

### Objection 1: The Charge of Subjectivity and Intractability
A primary objection to the empathetic strategy is that it appears thoroughly subjective, unscientific, and computationally mysterious. Dennett has long prided himself on offering a naturalized, externalist account of intentionality that avoids Cartesian introspection and the phenomenological black box of "simulation." To rest predictive success on empathetic simulation ("*What would I do...*") seems to regress into a cozy, uncheckable form of folk psychology that cannot be formalized or automated. If prediction relies on subjective simulation, how can we science-ify psychology or build artificial intelligence that interacts with humans?

### Response to Objection 1
This objection mistakes the pragmatic realities of human prediction for a foundational theory of mind. Dennett’s Intentional Stance was originally introduced precisely as a description of how *humans* successfully predict complex systems in daily life, before being expanded into a broader metaphysical thesis about intentionality. 

In everyday human interaction, prediction *is* largely empathetic, imaginative, and simulation-based. Artificial intelligence researchers have increasingly recognized that robots and autonomous systems interacting with humans cannot rely purely on game-theoretic optimization models; they must model human irrationality, emotional states, and cognitive biases explicitly. By incorporating "theory of mind" modules that simulate human heuristic failures (e.g., predicting when a human driver will panic or misjudge speed), modern AI systems move away from Dennett's pure rationality models toward architectures that mirror the empathetic, situated approach. 

Furthermore, subjectivity does not preclude empirical validity. Empathetic simulation is constantly calibrated and corrected through feedback loops in social interaction. When our simulation yields a false prediction, we update our model of the other person's specific psychological profile, refining our understanding of *how* they deviate from rationality.

### Objection 2: Isn't Empathy Just a Variant of the Intentional Stance?
Another powerful objection argues that the empathetic strategy is not a genuine alternative to Dennett’s framework, but merely a specialized, psychologically rich application of the Intentional Stance. After all, when we ask, “*What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?*”, we are still attributing beliefs, desires, and instrumental reasoning to an agent (ourselves, provisionally modified). Therefore, the empathetic strategy remains comfortably within the Dennettian tent.

### Response to Objection 2
This objection highlights a semantic overlap while ignoring a radical structural divergence. While both frameworks involve the attribution of mental states, the *normative baseline* and the *treatment of rationality* are fundamentally opposed.

Dennett’s Intentional Stance begins with the assumption of **normative optimization**: the agent believes what they *ought* to believe and does what they *ought* to do to maximize success. Suboptimality is treated as friction—an annoying deviation to be minimized or explained away.

The empathetic strategy begins from the premise of **situated suboptimality**: the agent (and the simulator) is recognized as inherently flawed, biased, emotionally driven, and shaped by evolutionary spandrels. Rationality is not assumed as a default baseline; rather, logical coherence is treated as a rare achievement that must be accounted for against a background of cognitive shortcuts and emotional interference.

To say that the empathetic strategy is "just" the Intentional Stance is equivalent to saying that behavioral economics is "just" classical economics because both study consumer choice. The shift from an optimization-based baseline to a suboptimality-embracing simulation changes the entire explanatory machinery, converting a rigid, brittle predictive tool into a flexible, psychologically realistic one.

---

## Conclusion

Daniel Dennett’s Intentional Stance has proven to be a remarkably fertile conceptual tool in the philosophy of mind, offering a pragmatic way to navigate the complexity of intentional systems without falling into the traps of dualism or radical reductionism. However, Dennett’s insistence on tethering the efficacy of this stance to an overarching evolutionary adaptationism creates a fatal vulnerability. 

By presuming that natural selection acts as an uncompromising optimizer of cognitive rationality, Dennett builds an idealized, normative baseline into the very foundations of his predictive framework. As the biological critiques of Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin demonstrate, this adaptationist paradigm is a biological fiction; organisms are not flawless optimization engines, but complex patchworks of historical contingencies, structural constraints, and evolutionary spandrels. Concurrently, the empirical findings marshaled by Stephen Stich and cognitive psychologists reveal that human beings are systematically irrational, driven by heuristics and biases that flagrantly violate the axioms of classical rationality.

When we attempt to predict human behavior using Dennett’s pure, optimization-driven Intentional Stance, we encounter systemic predictive failures whenever we confront human suboptimality, irrationality, and emotional complexity. 

To overcome these limitations, we must abandon the demand for universal normative rationality in behavioral prediction. By shifting toward an **empathetic predictive strategy**—one that asks, *“What would I do in that situation, if I were like him?”*—we ground our predictions in a shared, realistically flawed human nature. This empathetic simulation strategy embraces our status as evolutionary kludges rather than ideal calculators, providing a far more coherent, psychologically robust, and empirically adequate framework for understanding the wonderfully messy reality of human action.
