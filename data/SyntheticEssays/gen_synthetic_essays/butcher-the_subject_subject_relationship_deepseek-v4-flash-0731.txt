**Title: The Interval of Becoming: Communication Beyond Sartre’s Gaze**

**Introduction**

Human communication is a paradox. We reach out to the Other to be understood, yet in the very act of reaching, we risk reducing the Other to a set of predictable traits, a fixed image, an object of our comprehension. This tension is not a mere psychological inconvenience; it is an ontological crisis. Jean-Paul Sartre famously diagnosed this crisis as an inescapable conflict. For Sartre, the presence of the Other is a threat to my freedom; the Other’s gaze (le regard) fixes me in space and time, turning my fluid subjectivity into a static object. Conversely, my own gaze does the same to them. Communication, in this framework, is not a bridge between two consciousnesses but a battlefield where each self attempts to assert its transcendence by objectifying the other, rendering the other immanent, trapped, and known. If Sartre is correct, genuine person-to-person communication—a dialogue that respects both parties as free, evolving subjects—is an impossible dream, a mere pretense masking a fundamental drive toward domination.

Yet, the history of philosophy, particularly in the existential and phenomenological traditions, offers a counter-narrative. Thinkers like Paul Tillich and Luce Irigaray suggest that Sartre’s model, while profoundly insightful, is incomplete. They propose that we can move beyond this ontological conflict by re-conceiving the relational space between subjects. Tillich introduces the concept of *indeterminacy* within the subject-subject relationship, suggesting that the Other is never fully knowable or definable, and that genuine encounter requires the courage to dwell in this uncertainty. Irigaray, through her notion of *the two*, posits an irreducible interval of difference that prevents fusion and domination, allowing two subjects to coexist and communicate without one absorbing the other. This essay evaluates the claim that moving beyond Sartre requires a model of mutual transcendence, a model that does not naively eliminate objectification but rather recontextualizes it. I will argue that person-to-person communication does indeed require this philosophical shift. However, the path to mutual transcendence is not the abolition of objectification—which is impossible for finite, embodied beings—but rather the deliberate and disciplined management of it. The self must engage in a dialectical dance: it must utilize static approximations of the Other for preparation and practical navigation, yet it must radically relinquish these approximations during the dynamic event of actual dialogue. Genuine conversation succeeds in replacing the drive toward domination precisely when it embraces a dynamic anticipation of mutual change, a process that is structurally supported by Tillich’s indeterminacy and Irigaray’s interval of “the two.”

**Part I: The Sartrean Predicament – The Look and the Impossibility of Dialogue**

To understand the necessity of moving beyond Sartre, one must first fully inhabit his vision. In *Being and Nothingness*, Sartre grounds his theory of interpersonal relations in the fundamental structures of consciousness. Consciousness is defined as a nothingness (le néant) that nihilates the world, projecting itself toward possibilities. The self is a transcendence, a perpetual surpassing of what it is. The Other, however, presents an immediate and radical threat to this self-definition. When I am looked at by the Other, I am no longer the master of my own possibilities. The Other’s gaze seizes me, objectifies me. I become a body in *their* world, a thing with fixed attributes. The shame I feel when caught in an embarrassing act is the recognition that I am now a being-for-others, an object whose meaning is determined from the outside.

Sartre argues that this dynamic is reciprocal and inescapable. My attempts to reclaim my freedom necessarily involve objectifying the Other in turn. If I look at the Other, I reduce *them* to an object, reasserting my subjectivity. Consequently, human relations are a constant oscillation between two failed strategies: sadism and masochism. In sadism, I attempt to capture the Other’s freedom by making them a pure object, but I fail because their subjectivity always escapes my grasp. In masochism, I attempt to become an object for the Other, hoping to be absorbed into their freedom, but I fail because I cannot fully surrender my own subjectivity. Love, for Sartre, is the ultimate expression of this conflict. I desire to be loved because I want the Other to freely choose me as the absolute center of their world. But in doing so, I demand that their freedom be limited to choosing me, which paradoxically destroys the very freedom I sought to capture. Dialogue, in this framework, is merely a sophisticated tool for this ontological warfare. Every statement I make is an attempt to impose my interpretation of the world onto the Other. Every question I ask is an attempt to extract information that will give me power over them. There is no genuine reciprocity, only a zero-sum game where one subjectivity’s transcendence is purchased with the Other’s immanence.

Sartre’s model is compelling because it exposes the raw, egoistic undercurrents of social interaction. It rightly warns us against the naive optimism that assumes communication is inherently benevolent. However, it presents a closed system. If the fundamental structure of being-for-others is conflict, then any attempt at mutual transcendence is either self-deception or a temporary truce that will inevitably collapse back into hostility. The claim that we must move beyond this model is thus a claim that Sartre’s ontology is not exhaustive. It is a claim that there is a mode of encounter that does not require the annihilation of one subject for the flourishing of another. To find this mode, we must examine the nature of the space *between* subjects—a space Sartre leaves empty, but which Tillich and Irigaray seek to fill.

**Part II: The Necessary Objectification – The Static Approximation**

Before we can embrace the dynamic transcendence proposed by Tillich and Irigaray, we must honestly confront the inescapable presence of objectification in human cognition. The claim that we must move beyond Sartre does not mean we can discard objectification entirely. To exist as a finite, embodied consciousness in a world of other beings, we *must* objectify. This is not a moral failing but an epistemic necessity. Our perception is not a passive reception of raw data; it is an active structuring of the world according to categories, schemas, and expectations. When I encounter a new person, I do not see a pure, undifferentiated consciousness. I see a body, a face, a posture, a manner of speaking. I immediately categorize them: gender, age, profession, social class, emotional state. I rely on what Alfred Schutz called “typifications”—idealized models of how people like this typically behave. I use these typifications to navigate the social world, to predict reactions, and to formulate my own responses.

This process of objectification is a form of *static approximation*. It is a freezing of the Other’s fluid subjectivity into a set of fixed, knowable traits. I build a cognitive map of the Other, complete with landmarks (their interests, their history, their biases) and predicted routes (how they will respond to a joke, a criticism, or a confession). This map is essential for practical interaction. Without it, social life would be an overwhelming chaos of unpredictable stimuli. We cannot engage in a conversation without some prior assumptions about the Other. We prepare for a job interview by learning about the company and anticipating the interviewer’s questions. We prepare for a difficult conversation with a partner by rehearsing arguments and predicting their emotional responses. This preparatory objectification is the foundation of communicative competence.

However, the danger of the static approximation is that we mistake the map for the territory. We begin to believe that our typification of the Other *is* the Other. This is precisely the Sartrean trap. When we treat our static approximation as the final truth of the Other, we engage in a subtle form of domination. We refuse to allow the Other to surprise us, to contradict us, to reveal a depth that our schema cannot contain. We imprison them in our expectations. The Sartrean conflict arises not from the mere act of objectification, but from the *reification* of that act—the moment we treat the objectified image as the absolute essence of the person. Thus, the task of moving beyond Sartre is not to eliminate objectification, but to develop a relationship with our own objectifications. We must hold them lightly, as provisional hypotheses rather than fixed verdicts. This requires a philosophical framework that legitimizes uncertainty and change as the very ground of relational being. Tillich provides this framework through his concept of indeterminacy.

**Part III: Tillich’s Indeterminacy – The Courage to Risk the Subject**

Paul Tillich, a theologian and existential philosopher, offers a profound alternative to Sartre’s relational pessimism. While Sartre grounds his ontology in the conflict of individual freedoms, Tillich grounds his in the “ground of being” (das Sein selbst)—the ultimate, unconditioned reality that underlies all existence. For Tillich, every finite being participates in this ground, and this participation is the source of its power of being. Crucially, this ground of being is not an object; it is the very condition for the possibility of objectivity and subjectivity. Consequently, when I encounter another human being, I am not merely encountering a physical object that happens to have consciousness. I am encountering a being who, like me, is rooted in the infinite, who participates in a mystery that transcends all finite categories.

This ontological grounding leads Tillich to his concept of *indeterminacy* in human relationships. In his system, particularly in works like *The Courage to Be* and *Systematic Theology*, Tillich argues that a genuine subject-subject relationship is characterized by an irreducible indeterminacy. The Other is not a closed system of predictable behaviors; they are an open window onto the infinite. To treat the Other as fully determinate—as a fixed quantity that can be calculated and controlled—is to deny their participation in the ground of being. It is to reduce them to a mere thing, an object of technical manipulation. Conversely, to recognize the Other’s indeterminacy is to acknowledge that their depths are inexhaustible, that they always possess a surplus of meaning that escapes my grasp.

Tillich connects this indeterminacy to the concept of *courage*. The courage to be, for Tillich, is the courage to affirm one’s own being in the face of the threats of non-being (meaninglessness, guilt, death). In the context of interpersonal communication, this translates into the courage to encounter the Other without the armor of complete knowledge. To enter into a genuine dialogue with an indeterminate Other is to risk oneself. It is to risk having one’s assumptions shattered, one’s identity challenged, one’s worldview destabilized. The Other, precisely because they are indeterminate, has the power to change me. This is a terrifying prospect. The Sartrean subject seeks to avoid this terror by objectifying the Other, by controlling the encounter so that no change is possible. But Tillich argues that this avoidance is a form of spiritual death. By refusing to risk the encounter with the indeterminate Other, we cut ourselves off from the fullness of being. We retreat into a sterile, self-contained fortress of certainty, but we lose the vitality that comes from genuine relationship.

Tillich’s model of the subject-subject relationship is thus one of *dynamic mutual transcendence*. In a genuine encounter, both subjects are lifted beyond their current states of being. They do not remain static; they become something new through the act of communication. This is not a fusion where boundaries dissolve, but a reciprocal opening where each participant allows the Other to reveal new dimensions of reality. The indeterminacy of the Other is not a problem to be solved but a gift to be received. It is the guarantee that the conversation will never be stale, that there is always more to discover. This stands in stark contrast to Sartre’s vision, where the Other’s freedom is a threat to be neutralized. For Tillich, the Other’s freedom, expressed through their indeterminacy, is the very source of relational life.

**Part IV: Irigaray’s “The Two” – The Irreducible Interval**

While Tillich provides the ontological grounding for indeterminacy, Luce Irigaray offers a more concrete, embodied analysis of how this indeterminacy functions in the space between two specific subjects. Irigaray, a French feminist philosopher and psychoanalyst, is highly critical of Western philosophy’s tendency toward monism—the reduction of all difference to a single, universal principle. She argues that this monism is fundamentally masculine, a phallogocentrism that privileges a single, self-identical truth, often at the expense of the feminine and the Other. In her later works, particularly *I Love to You* and *The Way of Love*, Irigaray develops her concept of *the two* (le deux) as an alternative to the monistic model of relationship.

For Irigaray, the paradigm of all human difference is sexual difference. The relationship between a man and a woman is not a relationship between two versions of the same universal subject, but between two irreducibly different modes of being. This difference is not a lack or a deficiency in one compared to the other; it is a positive, generative feature of reality. Irigaray uses the metaphor of the *interval* (l’intervalle) to describe the space between the two. This interval is not an empty void, nor is it a bridge that erases difference. It is a living, breathing space of non-coincidence. It is the recognition that I am not you, and you are not me, and this non-coincidence is the very condition for the possibility of a relationship. If we were identical, there would be nothing to communicate; we would be a single, silent monolith. The interval is what allows for desire, for wonder, for the endless process of mutual discovery.

Irigaray’s notion of “the two” directly addresses the Sartrean problem of objectification. Sartre sees the Other’s difference as a threat because it is a difference that I cannot control. Irigaray reframes this difference as a gift. The interval between us is a sanctuary of alterity. It protects me from being absorbed by you, and it protects you from being absorbed by me. In this protected space, I can relate to you without the need to reduce you to an object of my comprehension. I can engage in what Irigaray calls “wonder” (l’admiration)—a state of awe and respect in the face of the Other’s irreducible mystery. Wonder is the opposite of the Sartrean gaze. The gaze seeks to fix, to define, to possess. Wonder seeks to behold, to appreciate, to let be.

Communication, for Irigaray, is not about speaking *for* the Other, or speaking *about* the Other, but speaking *to* the Other. This “to” is crucial. It presupposes the interval, the distance that must be respected. When I speak to you, I am not trying to make you into an image of myself. I am offering my words into the space between us, and I am inviting you to respond from your own unique position. This is a mutual transcendence because neither of us remains the same after the exchange. We are both transformed by the encounter, but we do not merge. We become more fully ourselves, precisely because we have been witnessed by an Other who is fundamentally different from us. Irigaray’s model thus provides a concrete, ethical framework for the indeterminacy that Tillich describes. The interval of “the two” is the physical, lived expression of the ontological ground of being that resists objectification.

**Part V: The Dialectical Balance – Suspending the Static, Embracing the Dynamic**

Having established the philosophical foundations of Tillich and Irigaray, we can now address the central tension of the essay: the balance between the necessary objectification of the Other-as-object and the recognition of the Other-as-subject. The solution is not a simple either/or, but a complex dialectic that operates across different temporal phases of the communicative act. We must distinguish between the *preparatory* phase and the *performative* phase of dialogue.

In the preparatory phase, static approximation is not only permissible but necessary. Before I enter a conversation, I must make use of typifications. I must anticipate the Other’s likely responses, prepare my arguments, and strategize my approach. This is the realm of the object. I treat the Other as a case study, a set of data points to be analyzed. This objectification is a form of respect for the practical demands of the world. It allows me to be an effective communicator, to avoid unnecessary offense, and to achieve my communicative goals. However, this preparatory objectification must be recognized for what it is: a heuristic fiction. It is a map, not the territory. The danger arises when I smuggle this preparatory map into the actual conversation and treat it as the final truth of the Other.

The performative phase—the actual, living dialogue—requires a radical shift. This is where Tillich’s indeterminacy and Irigaray’s interval become paramount. As I begin to speak and listen, I must engage in a deliberate *epoché*, a suspension of my static approximations. I must hold my objectified image of the Other loosely, ready to be shattered by the living reality of their speech, their tone, their pauses, their unexpected reactions. This is the act of *relinquishing* mentioned in the prompt. It is a profoundly difficult act because it requires vulnerability. To relinquish my approximation is to admit that I do not know the Other, that I am entering a space of uncertainty. This is the courage to be that Tillich describes. It is the courage to face the anxiety of the indeterminate, trusting that the ground of being will support me.

This relinquishment is not a passive surrender. It is an active, dynamic anticipation of mutual change. I do not simply empty my mind of preconceptions; I actively prepare to be transformed. I enter the dialogue with a posture of expectancy, knowing that the Other, as a free and indeterminate subject, has the power to reveal something new to me—not just about themselves, but about the world and about myself. This is where Irigaray’s wonder comes into play. I approach the Other with wonder, not with the desire to conquer, but with the desire to behold. I am attentive to the interval between us, respecting the distance that allows us to be two distinct beings.

The drive toward domination, which Sartre identifies as the core of human relations, is replaced by a drive toward *co-creation*. In a genuine conversation, I am not trying to impose my will on the Other; I am trying to build a shared world with them. This shared world is not a static consensus but a dynamic, evolving creation. It is a product of the mutual transcendence that occurs when two subjects risk encountering each other in their indeterminacy. The conversation becomes a dance, where each partner brings their own movements but responds to the other’s lead. The dance is not about one partner controlling the other; it is about the beauty that emerges from the interaction of two distinct, creative forces.

**Part VI: Resolving the Tension – Does Genuine Conversation Succeed?**

The final question is whether this model of mutual transcendence can genuinely succeed in replacing the Sartrean drive toward domination. The answer is yes, but with a crucial caveat: success is not a final destination but an ongoing process. Genuine conversation does not achieve a perfect, permanent state of mutual understanding where objectification is forever banished. Such a state would be impossible, as it would require a fusion of subjects that would eliminate the Irigarayan interval. Instead, genuine conversation is a continuous, disciplined practice of oscillation. We prepare with static approximations, we enter the dialogue and relinquish them, we are changed by the encounter, and then we must re-prepare for the next encounter with new approximations that are better informed but still provisional.

The success of this model lies in its ability to transform the *quality* of the relational space. In Sartre’s model, the space between subjects is a battlefield. In the Tillich-Irigaray model, it is a sanctuary. The shift is not in the elimination of objectification—which remains a necessary tool—but in the *intention* behind that objectification. When I objectify the Other in a Sartrean mode, I do so to control them, to render them predictable, to neutralize their threat to my freedom. When I objectify the Other in the preparatory mode of the mutual transcendence model, I do so to prepare myself for a respectful encounter. I am building a scaffold to help me reach the Other, but I know that the scaffold must be removed once I arrive.

Does this resolve the tension between preparation and relinquishment? Yes, because it reframes the relationship between the two. They are not contradictory but complementary stages of a single, cyclical process. Preparation without relinquishment leads to Sartrean domination—a conversation where I am merely talking to my own reflection, using the Other as a sounding board. Relinquishment without preparation leads to chaos—a conversation where I am so open that I have no grounding, no ability to make meaningful contact. The dialectical balance requires both. I must prepare thoroughly, but I must hold my preparations with humility, ready to abandon them the moment the living Other contradicts them.

Ultimately, the claim that person-to-person communication requires moving beyond Sartre is validated. Sartre’s model is a profound description of what happens when communication fails, when we are trapped in our own egos. But it is not a description of communication’s essence. The essence of communication is a mutual transcendence, a shared journey into the unknown. Tillich provides the ontological justification for this journey by grounding it in the indeterminate ground of being. Irigaray provides the ethical and relational structure for this journey by insisting on the irreducible interval of “the two.” Together, they offer a vision of communication where the Other is not an enemy to be conquered but a companion to be encountered. In this vision, the drive toward domination is not destroyed (it is a permanent possibility of human consciousness) but it is *sublimated*—redirected toward the higher goal of mutual becoming.

**Conclusion**

The philosophical journey from Sartre’s conflict to Tillich’s indeterminacy and Irigaray’s “the two” reveals a path out of the prison of mutual objectification. Sartre was correct to identify the ontological weight of the Other’s gaze and the inherent tension between two freedoms. However, his error was to treat this tension as the final word on human relations. By introducing the concept of indeterminacy, Tillich shows us that the Other is not a closed system but an open window onto the infinite, and that genuine encounter requires the courage to risk ourselves in that openness. By introducing the notion of “the two,” Irigaray shows us that the space between subjects is not a void of conflict but a generative interval of difference, a sanctuary that protects our alterity and allows for wonder.

The practical resolution lies in a dialectical balance. We must use static approximations to prepare for interaction, but we must relinquish these approximations during the dynamic event of dialogue. This relinquishment is not a loss of self but a gain of the Other. It is the active anticipation of mutual change, the willingness to be transformed by the encounter. Genuine conversation succeeds in replacing the drive toward domination not by erasing the self or the Other, but by elevating both into a shared space of co-creation. It is a perpetual becoming, a dance of two subjects who, in the words of Irigaray, remain forever two, yet are forever in relation. In this model, communication becomes not a weapon of war but an act of love—a love that respects the mystery of the Other and finds its deepest fulfillment in the endless, indeterminate, and wondrous dialogue between us.
