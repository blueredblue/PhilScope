## The Gene's Eye and the Soul's Tongue: How Ultra-Darwinian Explanations Annihilate the Emotions They Seek to Explain

**Abstract**

This essay evaluates the claim that ultra-Darwinian explanations of human emotion, as advanced by evolutionary psychology (EP), do not merely describe but actively undermine and transform their target phenomena. I argue that this claim is not only true but reveals a fatal flaw in the explanatory ambitions of socio-biology. The tension between the designative-instrumentalist view of language operative in EP and Charles Taylor’s expressivist framework exposes a fundamental incompatibility. While EP treats emotions as functionally defined mechanisms whose true nature is revealed by translating them into the language of inclusive fitness, Taylor demonstrates that emotions are constituted by subject-referring import-ascriptions that require a rich, expressive language for their very existence. By translating anger, love, or grief into the reductive lexicon of gene replication, EP does not merely describe these states; it *re-describes* them in a way that strips them of their constitutive meaning, thereby altering their qualitative character. This transformation constitutes an annihilation of the original object of inquiry. I resolve the ensuing dialectical problem by demonstrating that an explanatory framework which annihilates its target cannot, by definition, successfully account for it. The resolution lies not in a synthesis of the two frameworks, but in a critical demarcation: EP can explain the distal evolutionary origins of our emotional *capacities*, but it cannot account for the *constitutive nature* of human emotional life, which remains irreducibly hermeneutic.

### Introduction: The Paradox of the Explaining Gene

Consider the mother who cradles her newborn, overwhelmed by a love she describes as unconditional, transcendent, and essential to her very being. Now consider the evolutionary psychologist who, upon witnessing this scene, offers a translation: the mother’s love is an adaptation—a neurochemical reward mechanism designed to ensure the costly investment required for the survival of her genetic replicas. The mother’s feeling, the psychologist insists, is ultimately a strategy for inclusive fitness, a phenotypic expression of a gene’s drive for immortality. The question that haunts this juxtaposition is not merely whether the evolutionary explanation is *true* in some biological sense, but whether it is an explanation of the *same thing* the mother is experiencing. Has the psychologist explained her love, or has he, in the very act of explanation, dissolved it into something else—a mechanism, a signal, a strategy?

This essay contends that the ultra-Darwinian project, in its most ambitious forms, is fundamentally self-defeating. The claim that it "undermines and transforms" its target is correct, but this is not a mere epistemic side-effect; it is the logical consequence of its underlying philosophy of language and mind. The designative-instrumentalist view of language, which treats words as neutral labels for pre-existing objective facts, allows EP to assume that translating "love" into "pair-bonding mechanism" is a harmless terminological swap. However, as Charles Taylor powerfully argues, human emotions are not brute facts awaiting neutral designation. They are constituted by language, by the expressive articulations of subjects who grasp their own significance. When EP translates emotional experience into the language of genes, it commits a category error that annihilates the very phenomenon it purports to explain. Consequently, such a framework cannot successfully account for human emotional life, because the "life" it accounts for is a shadow—a functional abstraction—that bears no constitutive relation to the lived, qualitative reality of feeling.

To substantiate this thesis, I will first outline the core commitments of the ultra-Darwinian paradigm and its designative-instrumentalist view of language. I will then reconstruct Taylor’s expressivist alternative, which posits that emotions are irreducibly intentional states involving subject-referring import-ascriptions. Following this, I will analyze the specific transformative and undermining effects of translating everyday emotional experience into neo-Darwinian gene language, focusing on anger and love. Finally, I will confront the dialectical problem—whether an explanatory framework that annihilates its target can account for it—and offer a resolution that demarcates the legitimate scope of evolutionary explanation while preserving the irreducibility of the human lifeworld.

### I. The Ultra-Darwinian Paradigm: Language as a Designative Instrument

Evolutionary psychology, in its orthodox formulation (as championed by Cosmides, Tooby, Barkow, and Pinker), rests on a set of foundational assumptions. First, it is deeply adaptationist: it holds that the human mind is not a general-purpose learning device but a collection of specialized, domain-specific modules, each shaped by natural selection to solve a recurring adaptive problem faced by our Pleistocene ancestors. Second, it is universalist: these modules are posited as universal human nature, underlying the superficial diversity of cultural expression. Third, and most critically for our purposes, it is *reductionist* in its explanatory strategy. The ultimate explanation of any psychological trait—including an emotion—is to be found in its contribution to inclusive fitness. The proximate phenomenology (what it feels like to be angry or in love) is treated as a mere by-product, a subjective "user interface" that obscures the underlying algorithmic reality.

This explanatory strategy is predicated on a specific, deeply entrenched philosophy of language: the designative-instrumentalist view. In this view, language functions primarily as a tool for designating objects, properties, and relations in an objective world. Words are labels that map onto a pre-existing reality. The meaning of a term is its referent—the thing it picks out. For the sociobiologist, the "real" referent of the term "anger" is not the qualitative experience of outrage or the moral judgment of injustice; it is a specific neurophysiological and behavioral program. This program, in turn, is a functional adaptation. Thus, the language of evolutionary biology (genes, fitness, adaptation, strategy) is considered more accurate, more "scientific," than the language of folk psychology (anger, love, grief, shame). The latter is treated as a kind of false consciousness, a naive taxonomy that must be corrected by the objective vocabulary of the natural sciences.

Richard Dawkins’ "gene’s eye view" is the paradigmatic expression of this instrumentalism. The gene is the ultimate unit of selection, and organisms are merely "survival machines" built to propagate them. Emotions, in this framework, are strategic tools. Jealousy is a paternity-assurance mechanism; anger is a commitment device to deter future exploitation; grief is a signal to attract social support; love is a pair-bonding adaptation to facilitate biparental care. The instrumentalist view of language is evident in the way these explanations *translate* one vocabulary into another. The translation is presumed to be neutral—a mere exchange of synonyms that reveals the "real" meaning beneath the folk illusion. The language of the gene is privileged as the language of truth, while the language of the heart is demoted to the status of error or illusion.

This project is not merely descriptive; it is explicitly corrective. EP seeks to *replace* folk psychology with a scientific taxonomy. The goal is to explain why we have these feelings, and in doing so, to reveal their true function. However, this very project of replacement is where the trouble begins. For if language is merely designative, then changing the vocabulary does not change the object; it merely describes it more accurately. But if language is *constitutive*—if the way we articulate our feelings shapes what those feelings are—then the translation is not neutral. It is a transformative act that alters the very reality it seeks to describe. This is precisely the insight of Charles Taylor’s expressivist framework.

### II. Charles Taylor’s Expressivism: Emotions as Subject-Referring Import-Ascriptions

Charles Taylor’s philosophy offers a powerful and sustained critique of the designative-instrumentalist view of language, particularly as it applies to human agency and emotion. For Taylor, language is not merely a tool for designating pre-existing objects; it is the medium through which we constitute ourselves as persons. He distinguishes between two functions of language: the designative and the expressive. While the designative function allows us to refer to objects, the expressive function allows us to articulate what matters to us, to give voice to our inner lives, and in doing so, to shape them.

Central to Taylor’s account is the concept of an *import-ascriptive* emotion. Unlike brute sensations (e.g., a stomach ache), emotions are intentional states: they are *about* something. But more than that, they involve an *import*—a sense of what the situation means for the subject. To be angry is not merely to feel a certain physiological arousal; it is to feel that a wrong has been done, that one has been disrespected or unjustly treated. To be ashamed is not merely to feel discomfort; it is to feel that one has fallen short of a standard that one deeply values. To be in love is not merely to feel affection; it is to feel that the beloved is uniquely valuable, irreplaceable, and central to one’s own flourishing.

These imports are *subject-referring* in a crucial sense. The import of an emotion is not an objective property of the situation, independent of the subject’s self-understanding. Rather, the import is constituted by the subject’s own grasp of the situation in relation to their values, commitments, and identity. The shame I feel at failing an exam is not just a fact about the exam; it is a fact about my relationship to my own aspirations, my sense of self-worth, and the standards I hold myself to. The import is *for me*, and it depends on my capacity for self-interpretation.

This is where language becomes constitutive. Taylor argues that our emotional experiences are not fully formed prior to their articulation. We do not first have a raw feeling and then subsequently find words to describe it. Rather, the articulation of a feeling is part of what makes it the specific feeling it is. The difference between mere irritation and righteous anger is not a difference in physiological intensity; it is a difference in the *meaning* we ascribe to the situation, a meaning that is articulated in language. As Taylor writes, "We are aware of our emotions through their articulation." The language we use does not simply mirror an inner reality; it helps to *sculpt* that reality.

This is further developed through Taylor’s notion of *strong evaluation*. Human beings are not just subjects of simple desires or aversions; we are subjects who evaluate our desires. We distinguish between higher and lower, noble and base, worthy and unworthy. We ask not only "What do I want?" but "Is what I want good?" This capacity for strong evaluation is inseparable from language. We need a rich vocabulary of qualitative distinctions—words like dignity, integrity, authenticity, betrayal, and reverence—to make sense of our emotional lives. These words are not mere labels for pre-existing states; they are the very tools through which we constitute those states as significant.

For Taylor, then, the expressivist view holds that emotions are not brute facts awaiting neutral designation. They are *constituted* by our linguistic articulations of their import. To understand an emotion is not to reduce it to a mechanism, but to understand the meaning it holds for the subject. This meaning is irreducibly first-personal and hermeneutic. It cannot be captured by a third-personal, objectifying vocabulary that abstracts away from the subject’s self-understanding.

### III. The Transformative and Undermining Effect of Gene Translation

With Taylor’s expressivist framework in hand, we can now assess the claim that ultra-Darwinian explanations undermine and transform their objects. The claim is not merely that EP provides a *different* explanation of the same phenomenon, but that the translation itself is a *performative* act that changes the phenomenon. This happens in two distinct but related ways: transformation and undermining.

**Transformation of Qualitative Character**: Consider the emotion of anger. In the folk-psychological lexicon, anger is a moral emotion. It involves an import-ascruption: I am angry *because* I perceive a violation of justice, a disrespect of my person, or a betrayal of trust. The qualitative character of anger—its heat, its urgency, its demand for redress—is inseparable from this import. The anger is *about* the wrong; it is a protest against it.

Now, translate this into the language of evolutionary psychology. Anger is a "commitment device" designed to deter cheaters and free-riders. It is a mechanism that signals a credible threat of retaliation, thereby increasing the likelihood that others will cooperate with me in the future. The import is gone. There is no wrong, no justice, no disrespect—only a fitness-relevant cost-benefit analysis. The anger is no longer *about* a moral violation; it is *about* a strategic advantage.

This translation does not leave the qualitative character of anger untouched. If I come to believe that my anger is merely a genetic strategy, a programmed response to a fitness threat, the *meaning* of my anger changes. The moral outrage dissolves into a biochemical signal. The anger no longer feels like a righteous protest; it feels like a reflex. The intentional object has changed from "the wrong done to me" to "the stimulus that triggered my module." Consequently, the qualitative feel of the anger—its phenomenology—is altered. It is no longer a source of moral clarity but a suspect illusion. The translation has *transformed* the emotion into something qualitatively different.

**Undermining of the Phenomenon**: The more radical claim is that this translation *undermines* the very existence of the original phenomenon. If the expressivist view is correct, then the moral import of anger is not an accidental feature that can be stripped away; it is *constitutive* of anger. An anger that is not about a perceived wrong is not anger at all; it is something else—perhaps aggression or irritation. By translating anger into a functional mechanism, EP does not simply offer a rival description; it *denies the existence* of the original object. It claims that the "anger" the mother feels, or the lover feels, is an illusion—a folk-psychological fiction that obscures the real mechanism.

This is a performative contradiction. EP claims to be explaining human anger, but its explanation entails that the very thing it set out to explain (the subject-referring, import-laden emotion) does not exist. It has replaced the *explanandum* with a different object—a functional module—and then claimed to have explained the original. This is akin to explaining a symphony by reducing it to the physics of sound waves. The physics does not explain the symphony; it explains the sound. And if one insists that the symphony *is* just the sound waves, one has not explained the symphony; one has *eliminated* it. The aesthetic meaning, the structural coherence, the expressive power—all of which are constitutive of the symphony—are lost.

The case of love is even more striking. In the expressivist view, love is an import-ascruption of the beloved’s unique, irreplaceable worth. It involves a deep recognition of the other as a person, with their own projects, values, and dignity. It is a strong evaluation that places the beloved at the center of one’s moral and emotional universe. EP, however, explains love as a "pair-bonding mechanism" designed to facilitate biparental investment. The beloved is reduced to a set of fitness-relevant cues (symmetry, health, resource-holding potential). The love is a chemical cocktail (oxytocin, vasopressin) that ensures the parents stay together long enough to raise offspring.

If I internalize this explanation, my love is transformed. It is no longer a recognition of the beloved’s unique worth; it is a biological trick played on me by my genes. The "unconditional" nature of love is exposed as a conditional strategy. The sacred bond is demystified into a functional contract. The very *meaning* of my love—its import—is annihilated. I no longer love *this person* for who they are; I love a set of adaptive cues. The qualitative character of love—its warmth, its depth, its sense of transcendence—is replaced by a cynical awareness of its instrumental function. The translation has not merely described love; it has *killed* it.

This is the "undermining" effect. EP does not explain human emotional life; it *explains it away*. It provides a re-description that is incompatible with the constitutive features of the original phenomenon. The result is not a successful explanation but a *replacement* of the target with a different, impoverished object.

### IV. The Dialectical Problem and Its Resolution

We now arrive at the core dialectical problem: Can an explanatory framework that annihilates its original target phenomenon successfully account for human emotional life? The answer, I will argue, is a definitive *no*. But to see why, we must carefully diagnose the logical structure of this annihilation.

The dialectic proceeds as follows. **Thesis**: Human emotions are meaningful, import-laden experiences that are constituted by language and self-understanding (Taylor). **Antithesis**: Human emotions are functional adaptations that can be fully explained by their evolutionary origins (EP). The conflict arises because the antithesis does not merely contradict the thesis; it *presupposes* the truth of a designative view of language that denies the constitutive role of meaning. EP claims to have a better explanation of the *same* phenomenon. But if the expressivist view is correct, the "phenomenon" is not the same. EP’s re-description strips away the import, and in doing so, destroys the object. The question is how to resolve this conflict.

One possible resolution is *eliminativism*. This is the position that EP is right, and our folk-psychological emotions are illusions. We should abandon the language of anger and love and replace it with the language of modules and strategies. However, this resolution fails for a simple reason: the "illusion" itself is a real phenomenon. The fact that we *experience* anger as a moral protest is a fact about our psychology that needs explanation. If EP eliminates the experience, it has eliminated the very thing it needs to explain. The illusion is not a mistake to be corrected; it is the *datum* that any psychology must account for. An eliminativist EP would be left with nothing to explain, only a set of mechanisms that bear no relation to the lived experiences they supposedly cause.

A second resolution is *compatibilism* or *dual-levelism*. This position holds that EP explains the "hardware" (the neurophysiological mechanisms) while Taylor explains the "software" (the meaning). They operate at different levels and are therefore compatible. While this is more plausible, it fails to resolve the specific problem of *transformation*. The translation into gene language is not merely a claim about the hardware; it is a claim about the *nature* of the emotion itself. EP does not say "love is caused by a mechanism that also has a meaning." It says "love *is* the mechanism, and the meaning is a by-product." This is not a dual-level explanation; it is a reductionist identity claim. And it is this identity claim that annihilates the phenomenon.

The correct resolution, I contend, is to recognize that the annihilation is a *category error*—a confusion of different orders of explanation. EP is a legitimate science of *mechanism* and *function*. It can explain why we have the *capacity* for anger or love—why natural selection favored organisms that could experience these states. It can explain the distal causes, the adaptive pressures, and the neurochemical substrates. But it cannot explain the *constitutive nature* of these states, because that nature is constituted by meaning, and meaning is not a mechanistic property. It is a property of the *lifeworld*—the world of subjects who interpret themselves and their situations.

To resolve the dialectic, we must reject the imperialistic claim that EP provides a *complete* or *ultimate* explanation of human emotional life. We must instead adopt a *critical pluralism* that respects the irreducibility of the first-person perspective.
