# The Limits of Radical Fallibilism: Universal Revisability, Alethic Modal Logic S5, and the Paradox of Necessary Truth

## Abstract

W. V. O. Quine’s thesis that no statement is immune to revision—famously dubbed the dogma of universal revisability—constitutes a foundational pillar of modern empiricist pragmatism and naturalized epistemology. However, when formalized within the alethic modal logic $S5$, the thesis generates severe structural paradoxes under both wide-scope and narrow-scope interpretations. This essay evaluates the claim of universal revisability through a rigorous examination of these modal paradoxes, demonstrating that both readings result in logical contradiction and modal collapse. I analyze how the retention of necessary truths is structurally mandated by rational belief revision based on good reasons, and I assess whether restricting the revisability thesis via *ad hoc* exceptions can rescue the doctrine. Ultimately, I argue that universal revisability is either extensionally trivial or logically incoherent, forcing epistemology to recognize an irreducible class of modal truths that anchor rational inquiry.

---

## Introduction: The Quinean Legacy and the Modal Challenge

In his seminal 1951 essay, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," W. V. O. Quine delivered a radical critique of logical positivism, targeting the analytic-synthetic distinction and reductionism. Among the most enduring and disruptive corollaries of Quine's holism is the thesis of universal revisability: the claim that *no statement is immune to revision*. As Quine famously wrote, 

> "Any statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system. Conversely, by the same token, no statement is immune to revision."

For decades, this doctrine has functioned as the bedrock of epistemological fallibilism, assuring philosophers that even our most deeply entrenched logical and mathematical frameworks are, in principle, negotiable at the bar of pragmatic utility and experiential confrontation.

Yet, when we subject Quine’s thesis to formal scrutiny within the framework of alethic modal logic $S5$—the standard system governing metaphysical necessity and possibility—the intuitive appeal of radical fallibilism begins to fracture. The claim that "every statement is revisable" invites immediate meta-theoretical interrogation: Is the revisability thesis itself revisable? If so, what are the modal consequences of its possible falsification? More fundamentally, when we quantify over the statements of a conceptual scheme and modalize their susceptibility to revision, we uncover a deep structural tension between the pragmatist denial of unconditioned truths and the rigorous demands of modal semantics.

This essay evaluates the claim of universal revisability by addressing a central question: Do wide-scope or narrow-scope readings of Quine’s thesis lead to contradiction within alethic modal logic $S5$? I will demonstrate that both interpretations yield formal paradoxes and falsehoods. Furthermore, I will examine the indispensable role that necessary truths play in belief revision based on good reasons, and I will evaluate whether restricting the revisability thesis through *ad hoc* exceptions can salvage the doctrine from self-destruction.

---

## 1. Formalizing Universal Revisability in Alethic Modal Logic S5

To evaluate the logical coherence of Quine's thesis, we must first construct a rigorous formal framework. Let $\mathcal{L}$ be a standard first-order modal language extended with the alethic modal operators $\Box$ (necessity) and $\Diamond$ (possibility), interpreted under the semantics of system $S5$. The system $S5$ is characterized by the axioms of propositional logic, the distribution axiom $\Box(\phi \to \psi) \to (\Box\phi \to \Box\psi)$, and the characteristic modal axioms:
*   **($T$)**: $\Box\phi \to \phi$ (What is necessary is true)
*   **($4$)**: $\Box\phi \to \Box\Box\phi$ (If $\phi$ is necessary, its necessity is necessary)
*   **($5$)**: $\Diamond\phi \to \Box\Diamond\phi$ (If $\phi$ is possible, its possibility is necessary)

The accessibility relation $R$ in the Kripke frames for $S5$ is an equivalence relation: reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. This models a universe of possible worlds in which the modal status of every proposition is absolute across all accessible worlds.

Let $S$ be a variable ranging over the sentences or propositions of a theory $T$, and let $R(S)$ represent the predicate " $S$ is revisable." In epistemic and modal terms, to say that a proposition is revisable is to say that it is logically and epistemically possible that the epistemic agent or community abandons, rejects, or modifies their belief in $S$. Let us symbolize "It is possible that $S$ is not believed (or is rejected)" as $\Diamond \neg B(S)$, or, more abstractly in pure alethic modal terms regarding truth-value stability, as the modal operator asserting the contingency or non-necessity of $S$'s truth status within the system.

However, Quine's thesis is traditionally stated not merely as contingent revisability, but as a universal modal claim: *necessarily*, every statement is such that it can be revised. Alternatively, viewed from the perspective of the modal status of the propositions themselves, if a statement is a necessary truth (such as a logical or mathematical axiom), does the Quinean claim imply that its negation is possible? 

Let us examine the two primary ways philosophers have attempted to scope Quine’s universal quantification over statements and modal operators: the **wide-scope** interpretation and the **narrow-scope** interpretation.

---

## 2. The Wide-Scope Interpretation and Modal Collapse

### The Formulation of the Wide-Scope Thesis
Under the wide-scope reading, the universal quantifier scoping over the domain of statements is dominated by the modal operator of possibility or necessity, or the scope of the revisability operator encompasses the entire conditional structure of modal statuses. Formally, the wide-scope reading asserts that for any statement $\phi$, it is *possible* that $\phi$ is revised (or, more strongly, that the necessity operator does not apply unrestrictedly without admitting a counter-possibility):

$$\forall\phi \ \Diamond \neg\text{Revisable}(\phi)$$ 

Wait, let us be more precise about what Quine claims. Quine asserts that *no* statement is immune to revision. Immunization to revision is equivalent to modal necessity or unrevisability. Thus, "No $\phi$ is unrevisable" translates to:

$$\forall\phi \ \neg\Box(\text{Unrevisable}(\phi))$$

or simply, in terms of truth-value immutability:

$$\forall\phi \ \Diamond(\phi \text{ is abandoned})$$

More crucially, when applied to logical and mathematical truths—propositions traditionally held to be necessarily true ($\Box\phi$)—the wide-scope universal revisability thesis asserts:

$$\forall\phi (\Box\phi \to \Diamond\neg\phi)$$

This formulation states that for *any* proposition $\phi$, if $\phi$ is necessary, it is nevertheless possible that its negation is adopted or that $\phi$ fails to hold in some revised conceptual scheme.

### Deriving the Paradox in $S5$
Let us test this wide-scope formulation within the axioms of $S5$. 

1. Assume the wide-scope thesis for necessary truths: $\forall\phi (\Box\phi \to \Diamond\neg\phi)$.
2. Let $\psi$ be a specific necessary truth of the system, such as a logical tautology or an $S5$ theorem: $\Box\alpha$.
3. By universal instantiation on our thesis: $\Box\alpha \to \Diamond\neg\alpha$.
4. By modus ponens with our premise (2): $\Diamond\neg\alpha$.
5. However, by axiom ($T$) of $S5$, $\Box\alpha \to \alpha$, and consequently, the necessitation rule yields $\Box\alpha$. Furthermore, since $\alpha$ is a theorem, $\Box\alpha$ is a theorem of $S5$.
6. In $S5$, $\Diamond\neg\alpha$ is equivalent to $\neg\Box\alpha$.
7. Thus, we have both $\Box\alpha$ and $\neg\Box\alpha$, resulting in an explicit logical contradiction: $\Box\alpha \land \neg\Box\alpha$.

### The Fallacy of Modal Collapse
The contradiction above reveals a severe formal pathology. If we maintain that *every* necessary truth is susceptible to revision (i.e., its negation is accessible in some alternative theory or world), we violate the fundamental semantics of $S5$. In $S5$, if a proposition is true in all accessible worlds (necessity), there is no accessible world in which its negation is true. 

If a Quinean replies that the "possible worlds" invoked in belief revision are not possible worlds in the strict alethic modal sense, but rather "conceptual schemes" or "epistemic frameworks," we face a dilemma. If these conceptual schemes are logically inconsistent with $S5$ semantics, then Quinean pragmatism is not merely challenging traditional epistemology; it is operating outside the bounds of coherent formal logic. If, conversely, these frameworks are governed by standard modal logic, then the wide-scope thesis $\forall\phi (\Box\phi \to \Diamond\neg\phi)$ entails that *no* statement is strictly necessary—leading to radical modal skepticism where necessity is entirely evacuated from the language.

## 3. The Narrow-Scope Interpretation and Incoherence

### The Formulation of the Narrow-Scope Thesis
Under the narrow-scope reading, the modal operator scopes *inside* the universal quantification over statements, or the thesis asserts that for every statement, there *exists* a specific epistemic transformation under which it is revised, but this capacity for revision is itself treated as a contingent or necessary feature of the statement's status within the web of belief. Formally:

$$\forall\phi \, \Box (\phi \to \Diamond(\text{Revised}(\phi)))$$

Alternatively, focusing on the modal status of the revisability claim itself:

$$\forall\phi \, \text{Rev}(\phi)$$

where $\text{Rev}(\phi)$ is treated as an unconditional modal property of $\phi$. 

### The Self-Referential Paradox of Narrow-Scope Revisability
The narrow-scope interpretation encounters its most devastating critique when applied to the revisability thesis itself. Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the thesis of universal revisability: 

$$\mathcal{T} = \text{"Every statement is revisable."}$$

If $\mathcal{T}$ is a statement within the language of our theory, then by the narrow-scope universal revisability thesis, $\mathcal{T}$ itself must be revisable. 

Let us formalize this self-referential application within $S5$:
1. $\mathcal{T} = \forall\phi \, \text{Rev}(\phi)$.
2. By universal instantiation and the narrow-scope thesis, $\mathcal{T}$ is revisable: $\Diamond\neg\mathcal{T}$ (or $\text{Rev}(\mathcal{T})$).
3. To revise $\mathcal{T}$ means to adopt the belief in its negation: $\neg\mathcal{T}$.
4. The negation of "Every statement is revisable" is: $\exists\phi \, \neg\text{Rev}(\phi)$ ("There is at least one statement that is *not* revisable").
5. Therefore, the successful revision of $\mathcal{T}$ commits the agent to the existence of an unrevisable statement.

This creates an immediate pragmatic and logical paradox. If $\mathcal{T}$ is revised, the resulting epistemic state must affirm that *some* statement is immune to revision. But if *some* statement is immune to revision, $\mathcal{T}$ is false. Thus, the universal application of narrow-scope revisability to the thesis $\mathcal{T}$ undermines the very coherence of $\mathcal{T}$. 

### Modal Incoherence in $S5$
From an $S5$ perspective, let us examine the modal status of $\mathcal{T}$. Is $\mathcal{T}$ necessary or contingent?
*   If $\mathcal{T}$ is contingent ($\Diamond\mathcal{T} \land \Diamond\neg\mathcal{T}$), then there is a possible world (or conceptual scheme) where $\mathcal{T}$ is false—meaning there is a world where some statement is *not* revisable. But if the thesis of universal revisability is itself contingent, then universal revisability is false *globally* (as a metaphysical or structural thesis), for it fails to hold in all possible worlds.
*   If $\mathcal{T}$ is necessary ($\Box\mathcal{T}$), then by axiom ($T$), $\mathcal{T}$ is true. But if $\mathcal{T}$ is necessarily true, then *every* statement is necessarily revisable. 

Let $\chi$ be the thesis $\mathcal{T}$. If $\Box(\forall\phi \, \text{Rev}(\phi))$, then for any particular statement $\psi$, $\text{Rev}(\psi)$ is a necessary truth. However, if $\text{Rev}(\psi)$ means $\Diamond\neg\psi$, then $\Box\Diamond\neg\psi$. In $S5$, the characteristic axiom ($5$) states that $\Diamond\psi \to \Box\Diamond\psi$. The iteration of modal operators in $S5$ collapses modalities. If $\psi$ is a necessary truth ($\Box\psi$), then $\neg\psi$ is impossible ($\neg\Diamond\neg\psi$). But the narrow-scope thesis demands that $\Box\Diamond\neg\psi$, which directly contradicts $\neg\Diamond\neg\psi$ derived from $\Box\psi$.

Thus, whether interpreted with wide or narrow scope, universal revisability generates direct contradictions within the axiomatic architecture of $S5$.

## 4. Necessary Truths and Belief Revision Based on Good Reasons

The formal collapse of universal revisability in $S5$ is not merely an exercise in symbolic gymnastics; it reflects a profound epistemological reality concerning the nature of belief revision based on *good reasons*. 

### The Quinean Web of Belief vs. Epistemic Justification
In *Two Dogmas*, Quine presents his famous metaphor of the web of belief:

> "The totality of our so-called knowledge or beliefs... is a man-made fabric which impinges on experience only along the edges... A conflict with experience at the periphery occasions readjustments in the interior of the field... But the total field is so underdetermined by its boundary conditions, experience, that there has been a wide latitude of choice as to what statements to reevaluate in the light of any single contrary experience."

While this metaphor captures the holistic resistance of theories to empirical refutation, it obscures the normative dimension of epistemic justification. Rational belief revision is not merely a process of arbitrary triage where *any* belief can be sacrificed to save the periphery. Rational revision is governed by **epistemic warrant**, coherence, and **good reasons**.

### The Role of Logical and Modal Norms in Reasoning
When an agent revises their beliefs in light of new evidence or a logical contradiction, what guides their inferential steps? They rely upon logical laws—such as *Modus Ponens*, *Non-Contradiction* ($PV\neg P$ or $\neg(P \land \neg P)$), and structural constraints of probability and modal entailment. 

If *every* statement, including the fundamental laws of logic and alethic necessity, were genuinely revisable in the radical Quinean sense, the agent would possess no fixed standards of rationality by which to evaluate *good reasons*. As Laurence BonJour and other internalist epistemologists have argued, justification presupposes fixed normative criteria. If the rule of inference itself is perpetually up for grabs without stable structural anchors, the very concept of a "good reason" dissolves into coherentist circularity.

To revise a belief *on the basis of good reasons* requires that the reasons themselves are more secure, more stable, and more structurally foundational than the belief being revised. If we ascend to a meta-level where even the rules of deduction are subject to revision, we enter a state of global epistemic vertigo. 

### Why Necessary Truths Must Be Retained
Within $S5$, necessary truths ($\Box\phi$) represent propositions that are true in all possible worlds. Epistemically, they function as constitutive conditions of intelligibility. Consider the law of non-contradiction or the axioms of identity. Can an agent have a "good reason" to revise the law of non-contradiction? 

Any attempt to argue *for* the revision of the law of non-contradiction must utilize inferential patterns that already presuppose it (or collapse into trivialism, where a statement and its negation are asserted simultaneously). Therefore, rational belief revision does not operate in a vacuum of universal malleability; rather, it is anchored by a core of *necessary truths* that cannot be coherently abandoned without destroying the very framework of rational discourse.

## 5. Can *Ad Hoc* Exceptions Save Universal Revisability?

Confronted with the modal paradoxes and epistemological incoherence of unconditioned universal revisability, a defender of Quinean naturalism might seek refuge in qualification. Can we rescue the thesis by introducing *ad hoc* exceptions or stratified restrictions?

### The Strategy of Stratified Revisability
The modification proceeds by carving out a protected subclass of statements—such as logical tautologies, semantic meta-rules, or structural conditions of rationality—and declaring them immune to empirical or theoretical revision, while maintaining that *all empirical and theoretical statements* remain revisable. 

Formally, instead of:
$$\forall\phi \, \text{Rev}(\phi)$$
the restricted thesis posits:
$$\forall\phi (\neg\text{Immune}(\phi) \to \text{Rev}(\phi))$$
where $\text{Immune}(\phi)$ captures the class of necessary truths or foundational rules.

### Evaluating the Viability of the Restriction
While this maneuver avoids the catastrophic modal collapse of strict universal revisability, it comes at a severe theoretical cost: **it evacuates Quine's thesis of its radical philosophical force.**

1. **Trivializing the Thesis:** Quine’s original polemic against the analytic-synthetic distinction relied precisely on the denial of any principled, a priori boundary between statements that are immune to revision (analyticity) and statements that are vulnerable (syntheticity). If the defender of revisability now concedes that an irreducible core of statements *is* immune to revision, they have implicitly capitulated to a rehabilitated version of the analytic-synthetic distinction. They have reinstated the very dogma Quine sought to demolish.
2. **The *Ad Hoc* Problem:** What criteria determine which statements belong to the protected class of exceptions? If the criteria are grounded in logical necessity or transcendental preconditions of thought, we have abandoned naturalistic pragmatism in favor of rationalist foundationalism or Kantian transcendentalism. If the criteria are merely pragmatic ("we hold them immune because it is convenient to do so"), then the exceptions are vulnerable to future pragmatic shifts, dragging us back into the original modal paradox.
3. **The Logical Instability of Exceptions:** Furthermore, within $S5$, if we designate a set of propositions $\Gamma$ as strictly necessary ($\Box\gamma$ for all $\gamma \in \Gamma$), any attempt to treat them as revisable within the same system triggers the collapse theorems examined in Section 2. An exception cannot be half-pregnant: either a proposition is governed by $\Box$ (necessity), in which case its negation is impossible, or it is revisable (contingent/vulnerable), in which case it cannot serve as an unconditioned anchor for rational inference.

Thus, restricting the revisability thesis through *ad hoc* exceptions does not offer a logically viable resolution. It merely trades the Scylla of self-contradiction for the Charybdis of dogmatic truncation.

---

## Conclusion: Beyond Radical Fallibilism

The claim of universal revisability, when subjected to the formal rigor of alethic modal logic $S5$, proves to be logically untenable. Whether formalized under wide-scope or narrow-scope interpretations, the thesis generates fatal contradictions: wide-scope readings lead to modal collapse and the eradication of necessary truth, while narrow-scope readings trigger self-referential paradoxes that undermine the stability of the thesis itself.

Epistemologically, the dream of radical fallibilism—where every belief, rule, and logical axiom floats on a frictionless ocean of pragmatic revision—fails to account for the normative architecture of belief revision based on good reasons. Rational inquiry requires fixed structural anchors. Without an irreducible core of necessary truths that transcend local empirical negotiation, the very concepts of justification, inference, and rationality lose their meaning.

Ultimately, the attempt to salvage universal revisability through *ad hoc* exceptions collapses the distinctiveness of Quinean holism, forcing a return to a recognition of modal boundaries. Epistemology must move beyond the intoxicating extremes of positivist dogmatism and radical Quinean fallibilism, embracing a sophisticated realism that respects both the fallibility of human theorizing and the unyielding necessity of the logical structures that make thought possible in the first place.
