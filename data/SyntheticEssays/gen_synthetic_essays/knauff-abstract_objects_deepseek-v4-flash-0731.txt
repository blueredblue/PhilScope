### The Illusion of Ambiguity: Why Van Inwagen’s Reductio Against DAUP Survives the “Descartes” Objection

**Introduction**

In the landscape of contemporary mereology, few arguments carry the destructive force of Peter van Inwagen’s reductio ad absurdum against the Doctrine of Arbitrary Undetached Parts (DAUP). DAUP, the seemingly innocuous thesis that for any material object occupying a region, there exists a distinct material object occupying any arbitrary sub-region, has long been a cornerstone of a certain form of mereological plenitude. Van Inwagen, however, demonstrates that DAUP leads to a devastating contradiction involving the transitivity of identity, specifically through the thought experiment of Descartes and his left leg, or "D-minus." A common rejoinder to this argument claims that it fails due to an inherent ambiguity in the term "Descartes." The objection posits that "Descartes" can refer either to a material object (his body) or an abstract object (his mind, soul, or personhood), and that van Inwagen illicitly shifts between these interpretations to manufacture the paradox. This essay will argue that this objection is fundamentally misguided. While it is true that shifting between material and abstract interpretations of "Descartes" can create an *apparent* violation of the transitivity of identity, this shift is not present in van Inwagen’s argument, which is explicitly and consistently concerned with material objects. A rigorous analysis of the four formal identities generated by the thought experiment demonstrates that disambiguating the ontological status of the relata does not dissolve the paradox for material objects; it merely reveals that DAUP does not apply to abstract entities, which is trivially true. Consequently, maintaining a strict distinction between material and abstract objects fails to insulate DAUP from van Inwagen’s rejection. The argument remains a decisive refutation of DAUP for the material world, and the ambiguity objection is a red herring that mischaracterizes the logical structure of the reductio.

#### Section I: The Mechanics of Van Inwagen’s Reductio

To properly evaluate the ambiguity objection, one must first have a precise understanding of the argument being attacked. The Doctrine of Arbitrary Undetached Parts (DAUP) states that if an object, \(O\), occupies a spatial region \(R\), then for any proper sub-region \(R'\) of \(R\), there exists an object \(O'\) that occupies \(R'\) and is a proper part of \(O\). This doctrine is intuitive; it seems obvious that a table has a top half, a leg, and a set of molecules as parts, even if we do not ordinarily name them.

Van Inwagen’s reductio proceeds as follows. Let us consider a material object we will call "Descartes" (understood as his body). Descartes has a left leg, which occupies a sub-region of the region occupied by his entire body. According to DAUP, there exists an object that occupies exactly the region of Descartes’ body minus the region of his left leg. Let us call this object "D-minus." Before any surgical intervention, the following facts hold:

1.  Descartes exists.
2.  D-minus exists (by DAUP).
3.  D-minus is a proper part of Descartes (since Descartes has a leg that D-minus lacks).
4.  Therefore, Descartes is distinct from D-minus (as a proper part is never identical to the whole).

Now, imagine that Descartes undergoes a surgical procedure to remove his left leg. We can assume the leg is destroyed (e.g., cremated) so that no material overlap remains. After the operation, we have a new state of affairs. Let us call the object that is Descartes after the operation "Descartes-after" and the object that is D-minus after the operation "D-minus-after."

At this post-operative time, the region occupied by Descartes-after is exactly the region occupied by D-minus-after. Both objects are composed of exactly the same matter, occupying exactly the same spatial boundaries. Under a standard mereological principle—often referred to as the principle of material coincidence or mereological extensionality—if two material objects occupy the exact same region and are composed of the exact same proper parts, they are identical. Thus, we must conclude that Descartes-after is identical to D-minus-after.

Furthermore, we must consider the persistence conditions of these objects. D-minus, being a material object, persists through time as long as its constituent matter remains suitably arranged. Since the matter that composed D-minus before the operation is the same matter that composes D-minus-after (no atoms were lost or gained in the operation, only the leg was removed from the larger body), D-minus is identical to D-minus-after. Similarly, Descartes persists through the operation. The removal of a leg does not destroy the person or his body; Descartes before the operation is the same object as Descartes after the operation. Thus, Descartes is identical to Descartes-after.

Now, we can construct a formal chain of identities and distinctness:

- **Identity 1 (Persistence of D-minus):** \(D_{before} = D_{after}\).
- **Identity 2 (Coincidence after operation):** \(D_{after} = C_{after}\) (where \(C\) is Descartes).
- **Identity 3 (Persistence of Descartes):** \(C_{before} = C_{after}\).
- **Distinctness 1 (Before operation):** \(C_{before} \neq D_{before}\).

From Identities 1, 2, and 3, we can derive the transitive conclusion that \(C_{before} = D_{before}\). This directly contradicts Distinctness 1. Therefore, the conjunction of DAUP, the persistence of material objects through time, and the principle of material coincidence leads to a logical contradiction. Since the persistence and coincidence principles are generally accepted (or at least, far more plausible than DAUP), van Inwagen concludes that DAUP must be false.

#### Section II: The Ambiguity Objection and the Manufactured Paradox

The ambiguity objection targets the term "Descartes" in this argument. The critic argues that "Descartes" is a semantically loaded term. It might refer to the *person*—a composite of mind and body, or perhaps an immaterial soul (as Descartes himself famously argued). Alternatively, it might refer strictly to the *material body*. The objection claims that van Inwagen illicitly shifts between these two meanings to generate the contradiction.

Specifically, the critic might argue as follows: To establish Distinctness 1 (\(C_{before} \neq D_{before}\)), van Inwagen relies on the fact that Descartes has a leg while D-minus does not. This is a spatial, material fact. It requires "Descartes" to be a material object, because only material objects have spatial parts like legs. However, to establish Identity 3 (Persistence of Descartes, \(C_{before} = C_{after}\)), the critic claims we must rely on the persistence of the *person* or the *self*, which is an abstract or non-material entity. The person, as a thinking thing or a unified consciousness, persists through the loss of a leg. The material body, one might argue, changes significantly (it loses a part), and perhaps the body is not strictly identical before and after if we adopt a strict mereological essentialism. To avoid this, the argument implicitly shifts to an abstract interpretation of "Descartes" to ensure persistence through time.

If this shift occurs, then the argument commits the fallacy of equivocation. In Premise 1, "Descartes" refers to a material object (the body). In Premise 3, "Descartes" refers to an abstract object (the person/soul). If the relata are different, then the transitivity of identity cannot be applied across the chain. The apparent contradiction is manufactured by the ambiguity of the term. If we disambiguate—using "Descartes-body" for the material object and "Descartes-person" for the abstract object—the argument collapses. There is no single object \(C\) that is both distinct from D-minus and identical across time in the way required.

This objection is superficially plausible, but it fails on multiple levels. The first failure is a misreading of van Inwagen’s intent. Van Inwagen is a materialist about human beings. He explicitly argues that a person is a material object—a living organism. In his ontology, there is no immaterial soul. Therefore, when he uses "Descartes," he means the material organism, the living body. There is no shift. But even if we grant the critic a Cartesian dualist framework, the argument can be reformulated to avoid the ambiguity entirely.

Let us examine the four formal identities more closely, separating the material and abstract interpretations to see if the paradox truly dissolves.

#### Section III: Analyzing the Four Formal Identities Under Disambiguation

To determine whether disambiguation dissolves the paradox, we must systematically apply each interpretation to the four relata involved: \(C_{before}\) (Descartes before), \(C_{after}\) (Descartes after), \(D_{before}\) (D-minus before), and \(D_{after}\) (D-minus after).

**Case 1: The Strict Material Interpretation**

Suppose we disambiguate "Descartes" to mean strictly the material body. Let \(C\) be the material body of Descartes. Let \(D\) be the material object D-minus. Both are material objects.

- **Identity 1 (\(D_{before} = D_{after}\)):** This holds trivially. D-minus is a material object composed of a specific aggregate of atoms (the body minus the left leg). As long as those atoms persist in the same arrangement, the object persists. The operation does not affect the atoms of D-minus. Thus, \(D_{before}\) and \(D_{after}\) are the same material object.
- **Identity 2 (\(D_{after} = C_{after}\)):** After the operation, the material body of Descartes (\(C_{after}\)) is composed of exactly the same atoms as D-minus. They occupy the same region. Under the principle of material coincidence, they are identical. This is a standard materialist assumption. If one rejects this, one must explain what differentiates them—perhaps a "substantial form" or a "soul"—but that would be introducing a non-material element, which contradicts the material interpretation.
- **Identity 3 (\(C_{before} = C_{after}\)):** This is the persistence of the material body. Does a material body persist through the loss of a leg? Under standard materialist accounts of persistence (e.g., endurance or perdurance), yes. A body can lose parts and remain the same object. If we adopt a mereological essentialism that says any loss of parts destroys the object, then Identity 3 fails. But van Inwagen’s argument does not rely on mereological essentialism; it relies on the common-sense notion that a person/body survives an amputation. If the critic denies Identity 3, then they are denying that Descartes survives the operation, which is absurd. So Identity 3 holds.
- **Distinctness 1 (\(C_{before} \neq D_{before}\)):** Before the operation, \(C_{before}\) has a left leg, and \(D_{before}\) does not. They have different parts, so they are distinct material objects.

Under the strict material interpretation, all four premises hold, and the contradiction follows. The transitivity of identity is violated because we derive \(C_{before} = D_{before}\) from Identities 1, 2, and 3, contradicting Distinctness 1. Disambiguating to "material object" does not dissolve the paradox; it *constitutes* the paradox. The argument is sound for material objects.

**Case 2: The Strict Abstract Interpretation**

Now suppose we disambiguate "Descartes" to mean an abstract object—say, an immaterial soul or a purely mental substance (the "I" of the *Meditations*). Let \(C\) be this abstract soul.

- **Identity 1 (\(D_{before} = D_{after}\)):** D-minus is a material object. This identity is about material objects and holds as before.
- **Identity 2 (\(D_{after} = C_{after}\)):** This identity fails immediately. \(D_{after}\) is a material object occupying a spatial region. \(C_{after}\) is an abstract soul that does not occupy space. A material object cannot be identical to an abstract object. Therefore, \(D_{after} \neq C_{after}\).
- **Identity 3 (\(C_{before} = C_{after}\)):** This holds trivially for an abstract soul, which is immutable and persists through time.
- **Distinctness 1 (\(C_{before} \neq D_{before}\)):** This holds, but for a different reason. \(C_{before}\) is abstract, and \(D_{before}\) is material. They are distinct.

Under this interpretation, the argument fails because Identity 2 is false. The chain of identities is broken. The paradox is dissolved. However, this dissolution is entirely unhelpful to the defender of DAUP. DAUP is a thesis about *material objects*. It states that material objects have arbitrary material parts. An abstract soul has no material parts, so DAUP simply does not apply to it. By interpreting "Descartes" as an abstract object, the critic has changed the subject. They have shown that DAUP does not apply to souls, which is a trivial truth. They have not shown that DAUP holds for material objects.

**Case 3: The Hybrid Interpretation (The Equivocation Fallacy)**

The ambiguity objection claims that van Inwagen *shifts* between these two interpretations. Let us test this. To get Distinctness 1, we need \(C\) to be material (to have a leg). To get Identity 3, the critic claims we need \(C\) to be abstract (to persist through part-loss). If the argument uses "Descartes" as material in Premise 1 and abstract in Premise 3, then the \(C\) in Premise 1 is not the same \(C\) in Premise 3. The transitivity of identity cannot be applied across a chain that switches referents.

But does van Inwagen’s argument actually require this shift? No. As shown in Case 1, Identity 3 (\(C_{before} = C_{after}\)) holds perfectly well for a material object. A material body can persist through the loss of a leg. The persistence of a material object does not require an appeal to abstractness. The critic’s assertion that material objects cannot persist through part-loss is a non-standard, highly revisionary metaphysical view (mereological essentialism). Van Inwagen is not committed to this view. He is appealing to our ordinary intuition that a person survives an amputation. That intuition applies to the material body.

Therefore, the ambiguity objection fails because it relies on a false premise: that material objects cannot persist through the loss of parts. Once we recognize that material objects can persist through such changes, we see that the argument never needs to shift to an abstract interpretation. The argument is consistently about material objects, and the contradiction is real.

#### Section IV: Does the Strict Distinction Insulate DAUP?

The final question posed is whether maintaining a strict distinction between material objects and abstract objects can successfully insulate DAUP from van Inwagen’s rejection. Based on the preceding analysis, the answer is a resounding no.

If we maintain a strict distinction, we can say: "DAUP is a doctrine about material objects. Van Inwagen’s argument is about material objects. The argument shows that DAUP leads to a contradiction for material objects. Therefore, DAUP is false for material objects." The strict distinction does not help the defender of DAUP; it merely clarifies the scope of the damage. The damage is total for the material realm.
