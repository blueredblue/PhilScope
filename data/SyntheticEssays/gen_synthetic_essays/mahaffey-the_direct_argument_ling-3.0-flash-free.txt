# The Direct Argument, Transfer NR1, and the Bob Counterexample: Does the Direct Argument Collapse into an Indirect Argument?

## I. Introduction

The question of whether moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise has been one of the most vigorously contested issues in contemporary philosophy of action and moral psychology. At the center of this debate stands the Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP), which holds that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if they could have done otherwise in the exact circumstances in which they acted. For decades, the primary threat to PAP has come from Frankfurt-type counterexamples—carefully constructed scenarios in which an agent acts in a manner that would have occurred regardless of any alternative possibilities, yet intuitively seems to possess the kind of control necessary for moral responsibility. These cases have led many philosophers to abandon PAP and to seek a deeper, more robust account of the control requirements for moral responsibility.

It is against this backdrop that the Direct Argument for incompatibilism has emerged as a distinctive and influential line of reasoning. Associated most prominently with Peter van Inwagen, the Direct Argument purports to demonstrate that determinism is incompatible with moral responsibility without invoking PAP as a premise or relying upon any claim about alternate possibilities. Instead, the argument aims to establish its conclusion through a direct analysis of the nature of free action and control, showing that under determinism, agents simply cannot be the ultimate source of their actions in a way that grounds moral responsibility. The appeal of the Direct Argument lies precisely in its apparent independence from PAP: it promises to vindicate incompatibilism without needing to defend the truth of the Principle of Alternate Possibilities against the well-known Frankfurt-type objections.

However, recent philosophical work has raised serious doubts about whether the Direct Argument truly achieves this independence. Critics have argued that the argument's validity depends upon an inference rule—often referred to as Transfer NR1—that effectively encodes a commitment to the Principle of Alternate Possibilities, even if it does not explicitly invoke it. If this is correct, then the Direct Argument does not escape the Frankfurt problem but merely circumvents it at the cost of smuggling PAP back in through a side door. The question, then, is whether incompatibilists can neutralize this objection without ultimately defending PAP against Frankfurt-type counterexamples, thereby preserving the Direct Argument's distinctive character.

This essay evaluates whether the Direct Argument for incompatibilism successfully avoids relying upon PAP by examining the validity of Transfer NR1 through the lens of a particularly illuminating counterexample: the one-path scenario featuring Bob. In this scenario, Bob makes an undetermined choice that prevents a brain abnormality from operating, yet when a lightning strike intervenes, there are no alternative possibilities. The analysis reveals that the counterexample exploits precisely the features of Transfer NR1 that make the Direct Argument vulnerable, and that any attempt by incompatibilists to neutralize the counterexample without defending PAP faces significant philosophical difficulties. The essay concludes that the Direct Argument ultimately collapses into an indirect argument that remains vulnerable to traditional control requirements on moral responsibility, and that this collapse reveals a deep structural tension at the heart of incompatibilist attempts to articulate a PAP-independent account of moral responsibility.

## II. The Direct Argument and Its Relationship to PAP

To understand the stakes of the debate over the Direct Argument, it is essential to first reconstruct the argument itself and to clarify its relationship to the Principle of Alternate Possibilities. Van Inwagen's formulation of the incompatibilist thesis proceeds through a series of steps designed to establish that if determinism is true, no one is ever morally responsible for anything they do. The argument can be summarized as follows:

First, if determinism is true, then every event—including every human action—is entailed by the past state of the universe together with the laws of nature. Second, if determinism is true, then no one ever does otherwise than they actually do; in any given set of circumstances, there is exactly one possible course of action, and that course of action is the one the agent performs. Third, if no one ever does otherwise than they actually do, then no one ever acts freely in the sense required for moral responsibility. Fourth, if no one ever acts freely, then no one is ever morally responsible. Therefore, if determinism is true, no one is morally responsible.

The crucial premise in this argument is the third step: the claim that doing otherwise is a necessary condition for acting freely. This is where critics have traditionally focused their attention, because it appears to be equivalent to—or at least to presuppose—the Principle of Alternate Possibilities. If acting freely requires doing otherwise, and moral responsibility requires acting freely, then moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise, which is precisely PAP. The Frankfurt counterexamples, by presenting scenarios in which an agent acts freely and is morally responsible despite lacking alternate possibilities, seem to undermine this connection and thereby threaten the Direct Argument.

In response, defenders of the Direct Argument have insisted that the argument does not simply assert PAP as a premise. Rather, it derives the incompatibility of determinism and moral responsibility from a more fundamental analysis of what it means to act freely and to be the source of one's actions. The claim is that the Direct Argument succeeds because it targets the deeper structure of causal determination itself, showing that under determinism, the very notion of being a genuine source or origin of one's actions is undermined, independently of any claim about alternate possibilities. On this view, the Direct Argument is genuinely direct: it establishes the incompatibility of determinism and moral responsibility without passing through the intermediary of PAP, and therefore without needing to withstand the challenge posed by Frankfurt-type cases.

This claim of independence is central to the philosophical significance of the Direct Argument. If the argument genuinely avoids PAP, then incompatibilists have a powerful weapon in the debate over moral responsibility that does not require them to take a position on the truth or falsity of PAP in the face of Frankfurt counterexamples. They can simply argue that determinism is incompatible with the kind of control necessary for moral responsibility, full stop. However, as the next sections will explore, the independence of the Direct Argument from PAP is far from secure, and the validity of the inference rule Transfer NR1 lies at the heart of the problem.

## III. Transfer NR1 and Its Role in the Direct Argument

The inference rule Transfer NR1 has emerged as a focal point in the philosophical literature on the Direct Argument because it is the mechanism by which the argument moves from claims about what happens in one scenario to claims about what happens in another. Understanding the precise content and function of this rule is essential to evaluating whether the Direct Argument avoids reliance on PAP.

Transfer NR1 can be stated roughly as follows: if an agent is morally responsible for a particular action in a given set of circumstances, then that agent is morally responsible for that same action in any set of circumstances that is relevantly similar with respect to the causal history leading up to the action. In other words, moral responsibility transfers across scenarios when the causal chain leading to the action is preserved, even if the broader circumstances differ. This rule is what allows the Direct Argument to reason about moral responsibility in abstract, generalized terms—moving from the claim that no one acts freely under determinism to the claim that no one is morally responsible under determinism—without needing to examine each particular case individually.

The philosophical significance of Transfer NR1 lies in its role as an enabling inference. Without such a transfer principle, the Direct Argument would be limited to making claims about specific, concrete scenarios and could not generate the sweeping conclusion that determinism is incompatible with moral responsibility as a general phenomenon. Transfer NR1 is thus the logical engine that drives the Direct Argument from its premises to its conclusion, and any flaw in the rule would infect the entire argument.

The problem is that Transfer NR1 appears to encode something very much like the Principle of Alternate Possibilities. When we ask whether moral responsibility transfers from one scenario to another, we are implicitly asking whether the features that ground moral responsibility in the first scenario are also present in the second. If the only difference between the two scenarios is the presence or absence of alternate possibilities, and if moral responsibility transfers anyway, then Transfer NR1 effectively endorses the view that alternate possibilities are not necessary for moral responsibility—which is precisely the position that Frankfurt-type counterexamples are designed to establish. Conversely, if Transfer NR1 fails to transfer moral responsibility when alternate possibilities are absent, then the rule implicitly treats alternate possibilities as a necessary condition for responsibility, which is PAP.

This dilemma captures the fundamental tension at the heart of the Direct Argument. The argument aims to be independent of PAP, but the inference rule that makes it work seems to either presuppose PAP or to be vulnerable to counterexamples that target precisely the conditions under which responsibility transfers. The Bob counterexample, which will be examined in detail below, is designed to exploit this tension by constructing a scenario in which an agent's responsibility does not transfer even though the causal history is preserved, thereby demonstrating that Transfer NR1 is invalid.

## IV. The Bob Counterexample: A Detailed Analysis

The Bob counterexample is a one-path scenario specifically constructed to challenge the validity of Transfer NR1 and, by extension, the Direct Argument for incompatibilism. The scenario unfolds as follows:

Bob is a normal agent who, on a particular occasion, makes a choice that is undetermined—there is genuine indeterminacy in the decision-making process, and Bob's choice is not fixed by prior causes. This undetermined choice has a specific causal consequence: it prevents a latent brain abnormality from operating. The abnormality, if it were to become active, would cause Bob to perform some action for which he would not be morally responsible—perhaps a violent or self-harming act that Bob has no control over in the normal sense. Because Bob's undetermined choice prevents the abnormality from operating, Bob acts freely and is morally responsible for his behavior.

Now consider a second scenario that is relevantly similar to the first in all respects except one: a lightning strike occurs, which activates the brain abnormality regardless of Bob's choice. In this scenario, Bob makes the same undetermined choice, but it has no effect on the abnormality because the lightning strike overrides the causal chain. Bob acts in exactly the same way as he did in the first scenario, but now the abnormality is operating, and Bob has no alternative possibilities—he cannot prevent the abnormality from operating, and his action is causally determined by the abnormality's activity. In this one-path scenario, there is no fork in the road; there is only one possible trajectory, and Bob follows it.

The Bob counterexample is powerful because it isolates a specific feature that distinguishes the two scenarios: the presence or absence of alternative possibilities. In the first scenario, Bob's undetermined choice opens up a genuine alternative—he could have chosen differently, and that choice would have had different consequences. In the second scenario, the lightning strike eliminates this alternative, and Bob is left with exactly one possible course of action. Yet the two scenarios are identical up to the point of Bob's choice, and the choice itself is undetermined in both cases.

If Transfer NR1 were valid, then Bob's moral responsibility in the first scenario would transfer to the second scenario, because the causal history leading up to Bob's action is preserved. But intuitively, Bob is not morally responsible for his action in the second scenario. The lightning strike has introduced a causal mechanism that bypasses Bob's control, and even though Bob's choice is undetermined, it makes no difference to what happens. Bob is, in effect, a passive conduit through which the abnormality expresses itself, and there is nothing he could have done to alter the outcome. The absence of alternative possibilities in the lightning strike scenario seems to be precisely what undermines Bob's moral responsibility.

This is why the Bob counterexample is a one-path counterexample: it presents a scenario in which there is only one path of action, and yet the agent's choice is undetermined. The point is not to challenge PAP directly by showing that an agent can be responsible without alternate possibilities—that would be a Frankfurt-style challenge. Rather, the point is to show that Transfer NR1 is invalid because it would transfer moral responsibility into a scenario where the agent lacks the kind of control necessary for responsibility, even though the causal history is preserved. The counterexample thus targets the inference rule itself, not PAP as a philosophical thesis.

The Bob counterexample is particularly insidious for the Direct Argument because it does not rely on the truth of PAP. It does not claim that alternate possibilities are necessary for moral responsibility as a general matter. It simply claims that Transfer NR1 is an invalid inference rule because there are cases in which responsibility does not transfer even when the relevant causal conditions are preserved. The incompatibilist who wishes to defend the Direct Argument must therefore either show that Transfer NR1 is valid despite the Bob counterexample or abandon the Direct Argument's claim to independence from PAP.

## V. Can Incompatibilists Neutralize the Counterexample Without Defending PAP?

The central question that this essay has been building toward is whether incompatibilists can neutralize the Bob counterexample without defending the truth of PAP against Frankfurt-type objections. This question is of profound importance because it determines whether the Direct Argument ultimately collapses into an indirect argument that is vulnerable to the same traditional control requirements that PAP-based arguments face.

One possible response by the incompatibilist is to deny that the Bob counterexample actually presents a case in which moral responsibility fails to transfer. The incompatibilist might argue that Bob is morally responsible in the lightning strike scenario because his undetermined choice is part of the causal chain that leads to his action, and the fact that an additional causal factor (the lightning strike) is also involved does not diminish his responsibility. On this view, what matters for moral responsibility is not whether there are alternative possibilities but whether the agent's psychological states—their beliefs, desires, intentions, and character—are appropriately involved in the production of the action. Bob's undetermined choice reflects his psychological states, and those states are part of the causal explanation of his action, so Bob is responsible.

However, this response faces significant difficulties. The problem is that it seems to conflate two different things: the causal involvement of the agent's psychological states in the production of an action, and the kind of control that grounds moral responsibility. Frankfurt-type counterexamples have shown that an agent's psychological states can be causally involved in the production of an action without the agent having the kind of control necessary for moral responsibility. The neuroscientist who manipulates the agent's brain so that the agent's psychological states lead to a particular action is, on the Frankfurt view, not morally responsible for the manipulation because the agent's will is not the ultimate source of the action. Similarly, in the Bob scenario, the lightning strike may be seen as overriding Bob's will, such that even though Bob's psychological states are causally involved, they are not the ultimate source of the action. The incompatibilist who claims otherwise seems to be implicitly accepting that alternate possibilities are necessary for ultimate sourcehood—and thus for PAP.

A second possible response is to modify Transfer NR1 so that it includes additional conditions that are not satisfied in the Bob scenario. The incompatibilist might propose a restricted version of the transfer rule that requires not only the preservation of the causal history but also the preservation of some additional condition—perhaps the availability of alternative possibilities, or the presence of a certain kind of control, or the satisfaction of some criterion of ultimate sourcehood. If Transfer NR1 is restricted in this way, the Bob counterexample would not threaten it, because the additional condition would not be satisfied in the lightning strike scenario.

But this response is deeply problematic for the Direct Argument. If Transfer NR1 must be restricted to include a condition that is equivalent to or stronger than PAP—such as the availability of alternative possibilities or the satisfaction of a control requirement that entails PAP—then the Direct Argument is no longer independent of PAP. The argument would have effectively smuggled PAP back in through the modified transfer rule, and it would be vulnerable to the same Frankfurt-type objections that PAP-based arguments face. The incompatibilist would then be in the position of having to defend PAP against Frankfurt counterexamples, which is precisely what the Direct Argument was supposed to avoid.

A third possible response is to argue that the Bob counterexample is not a genuine threat to the Direct Argument because it relies on a misunderstanding of what indeterminism contributes to moral responsibility. The incompatibilist might claim that the undetermined nature of Bob's choice in the lightning strike scenario is irrelevant to responsibility because what matters is not whether the choice is determined or undetermined but whether the agent exercises the right kind of control. On this view, the Bob scenario does not show that Transfer NR1 is invalid; it shows that the indeterminacy of Bob's choice does not automatically confer moral responsibility when other factors undermine control. The incompatibilist would thus be claiming that Transfer NR1 is valid only when the right kind of control is present, and the Bob scenario fails to satisfy this condition.

But this response faces a dilemma. If the right kind of control is what grounds moral responsibility, then the incompatibilist must specify what that control consists in and show that it is incompatible with determinism. This specification will inevitably invoke conditions that look very much like the conditions for moral responsibility that are debated in the compatibilist literature—conditions involving reasons-responsiveness, hierarchical coherence, or sourcehood. And if these conditions are shown to be compatible with determinism (as many compatibilists have argued), then the incompatibilist has not succeeded in showing that determinism is incompatible with moral responsibility. The Direct Argument would have collapsed into an indirect argument that relies on contested claims about the nature of control, and it would remain vulnerable to the traditional compatibilist objection that the relevant control requirements can be satisfied even in a deterministic world.

The deeper issue is that the Bob counterexample exposes a structural weakness in the Direct Argument's attempt to avoid PAP. The Direct Argument aims to establish incompatibilism without relying on the truth of PAP, but the inference rule that enables this avoidance—Transfer NR1—is precisely the mechanism through which PAP-like considerations re-enter the argument. The Bob counterexample shows that Transfer NR1 cannot be accepted without restriction, and any restriction that is sufficient to block the counterexample will either encode PAP or invoke control requirements that are contested in the broader debate. In either case, the Direct Argument loses its distinctive character as a PAP-independent argument for incompatibilism and becomes vulnerable to the same objections that have traditionally been leveled against PAP-based incompatibilism.

## VI. Conclusion

The evaluation of the Direct Argument for incompatibilism through the lens of Transfer NR1 and the Bob counterexample reveals a deep and perhaps insurmountable difficulty for incompatibilist philosophers who seek to establish their position without relying on the Principle of Alternate Possibilities. The Direct Argument's claim to be a PAP-independent demonstration of the incompatibility of determinism and moral responsibility depends upon the validity of Transfer NR1 as an inference rule. But the Bob counterexample—a one-path scenario in which Bob makes an undetermined choice that prevents a brain abnormality from operating, yet leaves no alternative possibilities upon the occurrence of a lightning strike—demonstrates that Transfer NR1 is invalid because moral responsibility does not transfer to scenarios in which the agent lacks the kind of control necessary for responsibility, even when the causal history is preserved.

The incompatibilist's attempts to neutralize this counterexample without defending PAP face significant philosophical difficulties. Whether the incompatibilist denies that responsibility fails to transfer in the Bob scenario, restricts Transfer NR1 to include additional conditions, or claims that indeterminism is irrelevant to control without further specification, the result is the same: the Direct Argument either implicitly relies on PAP or becomes vulnerable to the traditional control requirements on moral responsibility that are contested in the compatibilist-incompatibilist debate. In the first case, the Direct Argument collapses into an indirect argument that is susceptible to Frankfurt-type objections. In the second case, the Direct Argument loses its distinctive character and becomes indistinguishable from other incompatibilist arguments that must engage with the contested nature of control.

The significance of this conclusion extends beyond the specific debate over the Direct Argument. It suggests a deep structural tension in incompatibilist attempts to articulate an account of moral responsibility that does not depend on alternate possibilities. The problem is not merely that Transfer NR1 is a problematic inference rule; it is that the very notion of moral responsibility seems to resist separation from the kind of control that alternate possibilities represent. Whether that control consists in the ability to do otherwise, in ultimate sourcehood, in reasons-responsiveness, or in some other condition, the incompatibilist must show that it is incompatible with determinism—and this demonstration, it seems, cannot be achieved without engaging the same philosophical terrain that the Principle of Alternate Possibilities occupies. The Direct Argument, for all its ambitions to be a direct and PAP-independent route to incompatibilism, ultimately collapses into an indirect argument that remains vulnerable to the traditional control requirements on moral responsibility.
