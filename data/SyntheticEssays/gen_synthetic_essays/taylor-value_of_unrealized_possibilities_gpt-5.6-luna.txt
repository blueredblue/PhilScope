# Futures, Persons, and the Wrongness of Killing: A Critical Assessment of Marquis

## Introduction

Don Marquis’s “future-like-ours” account offers one of the most influential secular arguments against abortion. Marquis argues that the central wrong-making feature of killing is not that it violates a social convention, frustrates a present desire, or damages a biologically mature human body. Rather, killing is seriously wrong because it deprives its victim of a valuable future: a future containing experiences, activities, relationships, and enjoyments that would have made the victim’s life worth living. Since a normal fetus has such a future, abortion is normally seriously wrong. The argument is attractive because it avoids several controversial assumptions. It does not depend on the claim that fetuses are already persons, that they possess sophisticated psychological capacities, or that they have present desires about continuing to live.

Nevertheless, the argument faces serious objections. Peter McInerney raises questions about personal identity and whether the fetus can appropriately be said to “have” the future of the later person. Robert Lovering emphasizes the importance of continuous consciousness and challenges the assumption that a mere biological or bodily continuity is sufficient to ground the relevant relation between an embryo and an adult. Walter Sinnott-Armstrong argues that the wrongness of killing must be connected to moral rights, interests, or claims possessed by the victim, and that merely having a valuable possible future may not be enough. These objections converge on a central issue: is killing wrong because it deprives an existing individual of an actualized future, or can it be wrong because it removes an unrealized possibility that would otherwise have become someone’s future?

The answer depends on how the notion of “having a future” is understood. If “having” means presently possessing a conscious interest in, or a psychological connection with, a future, then a fetus does not yet have a future like ours. If it means being the same continuing organism whose life would naturally unfold into that future, then the fetus does have such a future, even though it is not yet aware of it. The strongest version of Marquis’s argument is therefore not that every lost possibility is morally equivalent to killing, but that abortion deprives a particular developing individual of a determinate future that would have been its own. Yet this conclusion must be qualified: the future-like-ours argument establishes a powerful prima facie reason against abortion, but it does not by itself settle every abortion case, since rights, bodily autonomy, uncertainty about fetal identity, and conflicts between persons may defeat or outweigh that reason.

## Marquis’s future-like-ours account

Marquis begins with the assumption that killing adult human beings is normally seriously wrong. He then asks what explains this wrongness. Several familiar explanations are inadequate. Killing is not wrong merely because it causes pain, since painless killing can still be wrong. It is not wrong merely because it deprives someone of present experiences, since an unconscious person may still be wronged by being killed. Nor is it wrong only because it frustrates a strong present desire to live. Some people may lack such a desire because of depression, unconsciousness, immaturity, or cognitive impairment, yet killing them can remain seriously wrong.

Marquis’s answer is that killing deprives a person of “a future like ours.” Such a future includes the goods that ordinarily make human life valuable: conscious experiences, projects, relationships, satisfactions, achievements, and forms of appreciation. The harm of killing is not simply that the victim loses the next moment of consciousness. The victim loses the entire future that would have contained these goods. This explains why killing is generally worse than causing a temporary injury. It also explains why the wrongness of killing does not depend on how much the victim currently desires to live. A person can be deprived of a valuable future even when that future is not an object of present desire.

Marquis then applies this account to fetuses. A normal fetus, he argues, possesses a future that includes many of the same valuable experiences and activities as an ordinary adult human life. Abortion therefore deprives the fetus of a valuable future. Since this is the same kind of deprivation that makes killing adults wrong, abortion is normally seriously wrong.

The argument has two important features. First, it is a deprivation argument. The wrongness of killing lies primarily in what the victim loses, not in the killer’s motives or in the biological details of the killing. Second, it is not initially a personhood argument. Marquis does not need to show that the fetus is rational, self-conscious, autonomous, or capable of moral judgment. He needs to show only that the fetus is an individual that would otherwise continue into a valuable future.

The controversial step is the claim that the fetus’s future is its future. It is here that McInerney, Lovering, and Sinnott-Armstrong press their objections.

## Personal identity and McInerney’s challenge

McInerney’s criticism can be understood as challenging the identity relation implicit in Marquis’s argument. The phrase “a future like ours” sounds straightforward when applied to a normally functioning adult. If I am killed today, the future experiences I would have had tomorrow and next year are naturally described as my lost experiences. But the relation between a fetus and a future adult is less obvious. The fetus has no memories, plans, intentions, self-conception, or continuing consciousness. Why should the experiences of a future adult be attributed to the fetus rather than to a new person who comes into existence later?

This is not a trivial semantic worry. Deprivation requires a subject who is deprived. It is not enough to say that, had abortion not occurred, some valuable life would have existed. Many valuable lives fail to exist because of contraception, abstinence, or the decision not to conceive. If the mere loss of a valuable possible life were sufficient for serious moral wrongness, then ordinary reproductive choices would also appear to be seriously wrong. Marquis must therefore distinguish between a fetus and a merely possible person. The fetus must not merely be the predecessor of a future person; it must be the same individual who would have enjoyed that future.

McInerney’s personal-identity objection asks what grounds this sameness. A psychological account of personal identity emphasizes memory, character, intentions, and consciousness. On such an account, the fetus is not psychologically continuous with the later adult. There is no mental state in the fetus that develops into the adult’s memories or projects. The adult’s psychological life begins only after the fetus has acquired appropriate capacities. If psychological continuity is required for one person to own another time’s experiences, the fetus cannot be the victim of the adult future’s deprivation.

Marquis can respond by rejecting psychological continuity as the only basis of identity. Personal identity through time does not require uninterrupted consciousness. We regard a sleeping adult as the same person who will wake up, even though there is no continuous stream of conscious experience during sleep. Similarly, a person who undergoes anesthesia, a temporary coma, or substantial memory loss may remain the same individual despite a break in psychological continuity. Biological continuity appears to matter. On an organismic or animalist account, the fetus and the adult are the same living human organism at different stages of development. The absence of present consciousness does not imply the absence of identity.

This response is significant, but it does not entirely defeat McInerney. The relevant relation in Marquis’s argument may not be strict numerical identity. The moral harm of killing might depend on some relation of psychological connectedness rather than merely on being the same organism. Consider two beings that are biologically continuous but have no psychological connection at all. If a biological organism is destroyed before consciousness begins, is the deprivation morally equivalent to killing a self-conscious adult? The organismic account establishes that the fetus is the same animal, but it does not by itself establish that every good in the adult’s future is already possessed by the fetus in the morally relevant sense.

The distinction between identity and moral ownership is therefore crucial. A fetus may be numerically identical with the later adult without presently understanding, valuing, or owning its future in the way an adult does. Conversely, psychological continuity may be morally important without being necessary for numerical identity. Marquis’s argument is strongest if the harm of killing depends on the future belonging to the same organism, but weaker if it depends on a present subject’s psychological relation to that future.

McInerney’s objection also exposes a difficulty in Marquis’s use of the phrase “future like ours.” A fetus does not have a single inevitable future. It has a range of possible futures: it might be born and live a normal life, be born with a severe disability, die naturally, or develop into a person whose life contains more suffering than enjoyment. Even if one outcome is statistically most likely, it is not yet an actual future. The fetus has potential futures, but it does not yet possess the adult future as a psychological reality. The argument must therefore move from “the fetus could have a valuable future” to “this fetus would, if not aborted, have a valuable future.” That counterfactual is often plausible, but it is not metaphysically automatic.

## Continuous consciousness and Lovering’s objection

Lovering’s criticism sharpens the issue by focusing on continuous consciousness. The future-like-ours argument seems to derive its force from the value of conscious experiences. But the fetus has no consciousness at the earliest stages, and even later fetuses have no developed self-awareness or personal perspective comparable to that of a born human being. If the value of the future lies in its conscious experiences, why should that value be attributed to a subject that is not yet conscious?

One possible answer is that present consciousness is not necessary for having a morally significant future. Infants do not possess an adult’s self-consciousness, yet it would be wrong to kill them because they are capable of developing into conscious beings and because their lives contain valuable goods. Persons under anesthesia also lack present consciousness but do not thereby lose their right not to be killed. If temporary unconsciousness does not eliminate the wrongness of killing, then the mere absence of present consciousness in a fetus cannot settle the matter.

Lovering can reply that the analogy with sleeping or anesthetized adults is imperfect. An anesthetized adult has a developed psychological history and a previously existing consciousness. The adult’s present unconsciousness is temporary, and the person has already formed the mental structures that make the later conscious life continuous with the earlier one. A fetus before the emergence of consciousness lacks both a psychological past and a psychological present. The fetus is not a temporarily unconscious person; it is a preconscious organism. The difference may be morally important.

This objection identifies a genuine disanalogy. An adult in a coma has a past that grounds a personal narrative, while an early fetus has not yet developed any mental life. The fetus’s future may be biologically continuous with its present, but not psychologically continuous. If the moral subject of a future is a consciousness capable of having experiences, then the early fetus is not yet such a subject.

However, the continuous-consciousness objection also threatens to make the wrongness of killing depend too heavily on arbitrary temporal boundaries. Human consciousness is not a continuous uninterrupted phenomenon. People sleep every night, lose consciousness under anesthesia, and can experience temporary amnesia or severe neurological disruption. If a continuous psychological thread is required, many ordinary cases of identity and harm become uncertain. The objection must therefore distinguish between the absence of any consciousness yet and the temporary interruption of an already established consciousness. That distinction may be defensible, but it requires an additional theory of when moral status begins. Marquis’s argument alone does not provide it.

Lovering’s point can also be answered by noting that the value of a future need not be something the present subject consciously apprehends. A child may have a valuable future without understanding what that future contains. A person with profound cognitive impairment may be unable to formulate future-directed desires, but killing that person can still deprive them of experiences and relationships. The value belongs to the life that unfolds, not necessarily to a present representation of that life. If this is correct, then consciousness is the bearer of value in the future, but it need not already be present in the organism that will become conscious.

The deepest disagreement concerns whether moral status tracks present psychological capacities or diachronic biological development. Lovering’s position is more persuasive if persons are essentially centers of consciousness and if bodily continuity is merely a causal support for personal identity. Marquis’s position is more persuasive if human beings are organisms whose mental lives develop gradually and whose identity persists through radical psychological change. Neither view is established simply by pointing to consciousness or its absence.

## Moral rights and Sinnott-Armstrong’s objection

Sinnott-Armstrong’s criticism concerns the connection between deprivation and rights. Even if abortion prevents a valuable future from occurring, it does not immediately follow that abortion wrongs the fetus. Moral wrongdoing ordinarily involves violating a claim or right held by someone. If I prevent a merely possible person from existing, I have not obviously wronged that person, because there is no person there to possess a right. The question is whether the fetus has a right against being deprived of its future.

Sinnott-Armstrong’s challenge can be formulated in two ways. First, a valuable future may be insufficient for a right to continued life. Many things have valuable possible futures, but they do not thereby have a right not to be destroyed. A valuable painting that has not yet been created has no claim against the artist who decides not to paint it. A child who might have been conceived does not have a right to be conceived. Why should a fetus’s possible future generate a right if the future has not yet been actualized?

Second, rights may depend on interests or desires. A being that cannot have interests may not be capable of being wronged. The fetus may eventually develop desires and interests, but at the time of early abortion it has no desire to continue living and no understanding of what it would lose. If the wrongness of killing depends on frustrating a subject’s interests, then the future-like-ours argument appears to attribute interests before there is a subject capable of having them.

Marquis’s reply is that present desires cannot be the whole basis of the wrongness of killing. A person who is asleep, unconscious, severely depressed, or temporarily unaware of death may lack a present desire to continue living, but killing that person still deprives them of a valuable future. The right not to be killed cannot depend entirely on an explicitly expressed desire. It may instead be grounded in the person’s objective interests. A person has an interest in continuing a life containing valuable experiences even when that interest is not represented by a current desire.

This reply is powerful against a simple desire-based theory, but Sinnott-Armstrong’s objection is not thereby eliminated. There is a difference between lacking a present desire because one is temporarily unable to think and lacking any capacity for desire at all. The unconscious adult has a psychological history and a standing set of interests, even if those interests are not currently active. The early fetus has no interests in the psychological sense. Marquis must therefore defend an objective-interest theory according to which a being can have a morally relevant interest in a future before it can conceive of that future.

Such a theory is possible. Infants have interests despite lacking sophisticated desires. They have interests in nourishment, safety, affection, and development. These interests can be attributed to them because they are living beings whose flourishing can be helped or harmed. On this view, the fetus may have an objective interest in continued development even before it can formulate a desire. But the theory still must explain why the fetus has an interest in the entire future of an adult life rather than merely an interest in the next stage of development.

The distinction between rights and benefits is useful here. The fact that someone would benefit from being allowed to live does not by itself establish that others have a duty not to kill them. A right is a claim that imposes a duty on others. Marquis’s account supplies a reason why life is valuable to the fetus, but it does not independently show that the fetus’s claim overrides the pregnant woman’s bodily rights. Even if the fetus has a right not to be killed, rights can conflict. The future-like-ours account establishes the weight of the fetal loss; it does not settle the entire moral structure of pregnancy.

## The isolated hermit

The thought experiment involving an isolated hermit helps distinguish the value of a future from its social conditions. Imagine a hermit who lives alone, has no family, no friends, no social responsibilities, and no one who will miss him. He may have little influence on the world and no relationships that give his death social consequences. Is it nevertheless wrong to kill him?

Marquis’s answer is clearly yes if the hermit’s future contains experiences, pleasures, activities, and projects that he would otherwise enjoy. The wrongness of killing does not depend on the victim’s social importance or on the number of people affected. The hermit’s own loss is enough. This supports a deprivation account rather than a social-harm account. It also supports Marquis against theories that explain the wrongness of killing primarily through the disruption of relationships, contracts, or social practices.

The hermit case can, however, be used against Marquis if the hermit has no future-directed desires. Suppose the hermit is entirely indifferent to the future. He does not plan, hope, fear, or anticipate. If killing him is still wrong, then the wrongness cannot depend on a present desire for continued life. This is precisely the point Marquis wants the case to establish. The hermit can be wronged because his future would contain valuable experiences, whether or not he now wants them.

Sinnott-Armstrong may respond that an isolated hermit still has a developed consciousness and a history of interests. His lack of social relations does not imply a lack of personal interests. The hermit case therefore does not show that an entirely preconscious being has the same moral status. It shows only that social connection is not required for a person who already exists.

This response is correct as far as it goes. The hermit thought experiment does not prove the fetal conclusion. It establishes that killing can be wrong because of the victim’s own lost future, independently of social effects and perhaps independently of present future-directed desire. It does not establish that a fetus is the kind of subject who owns that future. The thought experiment thus supports the value premise of Marquis’s argument but leaves the identity and rights premises unresolved.

The hermit also clarifies the difference between an actual future and an unrealized possibility. The hermit is already an individual with a determinate life trajectory. If he is not killed, his future experiences would be parts of his continuing life. By contrast, before conception there is no individual whose future is being taken away. The fetus occupies an intermediate position: unlike a merely possible person, it is already a living individual; unlike the hermit, it lacks a developed mental life. The moral question turns on whether biological individuality is sufficient for future ownership.

## The paraplegic victim

The paraplegic case addresses a different challenge. Imagine a paraplegic person who cannot walk, whose life contains severe limitations, and whose future options are restricted. Suppose further that the person cannot pursue many activities that ordinary people regard as central to a flourishing life. Is it wrong to kill this person because killing deprives them of a future like ours?

Marquis’s answer should be yes, provided the person’s future still contains enough valuable experiences, relationships, and activities to make continued life worthwhile. The case illustrates that a valuable future need not be a maximally valuable or conventionally successful future. Disability does not eliminate the value of a person’s life. Nor does a reduction in available possibilities make killing permissible. A paraplegic may still have friendships, love, intellectual interests, pleasures, achievements, and personal projects. Depriving that person of these goods can be a serious harm.

The paraplegic case is especially relevant to the distinction between actualized futures and possibilities. A paraplegic victim may be unable to actualize some possible future—walking, traveling independently, or participating in certain physical activities. Yet killing remains wrong because the person has an actual future that includes goods still available to them. The moral significance of a future does not depend on preserving every possible option. What matters is the loss of the valuable life that the person would in fact have lived.

Sinnott-Armstrong might use the case to argue that the relevant wrong is not the loss of possibilities but the frustration of actual interests. A paraplegic person has present interests and a continuing perspective. Killing prevents the satisfaction of those interests. The fetus, by contrast, lacks present interests and therefore cannot be harmed in the same way.

Yet the case can also support Marquis. The paraplegic victim may not desire every element of the future being lost. They may not have formed plans concerning the next fifty years, and they may not be able to imagine all the experiences that would occur. Still, it would be wrong to kill them because their future has objective value for them. If objective value can ground a claim in the paraplegic’s case, then the absence of a present desire cannot by itself disqualify the fetus.

Again, the analogy is not conclusive. The paraplegic person has a conscious point of view, established interests, and a personal history. The fetus does not. The thought experiment therefore demonstrates that deprivation can be morally significant even when a person’s future is limited, uncertain, or not fully represented in present desires. It does not independently resolve whether a preconscious fetus is already a rights-bearing subject.

## Actualized futures and unrealized possibilities

The central dialectical tension can now be stated clearly. Marquis appears to rely on an actual future: the fetus would, if allowed to develop, have experiences that belong to it. His argument would be too broad if it treated every unrealized possibility as morally equivalent to a lost life. Yet the fetus’s future is not actual in the ordinary temporal sense. It is a counterfactual future, dependent on continued pregnancy, birth, survival, and development. Is depriving someone of such a future morally different from preventing a merely possible person from existing?

There are at least three relevant categories.

First, there is a merely possible future belonging to no existing individual. For example, a couple may decide not to conceive a child who might have had a happy life. No existing subject loses anything. The nonexistence of the child is not a deprivation suffered by that child, because there is no child to be deprived. Marquis’s argument should not condemn ordinary contraception or abstinence.

Second, there is the future of an existing individual whose life is already underway. The hermit and the paraplegic belong to this category. Their future is not merely possible in the relevant sense. It is the continuation of an actual life, even though particular events remain uncertain. Killing them deprives an existing individual of goods that would otherwise have been part of that life.

Third, there is the fetus. The fetus is an existing organism, but its personal and mental life is not yet developed. Its future is conditional and counterfactual, but it is not merely the future of an abstract possible person. If the fetus is the same organism that would later become the adult, then abortion prevents that organism from undergoing the valuable stages of life that would have followed. If identity requires psychological continuity, however, the fetus may be only a biological precursor of the future person.

The moral force of the future-like-ours argument thus depends on a bridge principle: when an existing organism would naturally develop into a conscious individual, the future goods of that individual count as goods of the organism now. Marquis assumes this bridge principle, but his critics challenge it. An organismic theory of identity supports it. A psychological theory resists it. A rights-based theory asks whether identity alone is sufficient for a claim against abortion.

The distinction between actualized futures and unrealized possibilities should not be drawn too sharply. Even adult futures are not actualized in advance. When someone is killed, they lose events that were still future and therefore unrealized. The morally relevant contrast is not between actual and unrealized in a temporal sense. It is between a future that is assigned to an existing subject by the continuity of that subject’s life and a possibility that belongs to no existing subject. A future can be unrealized yet still be someone’s future. The hermit’s tomorrow is unrealized today, but it is nevertheless his tomorrow. The question is whether the fetus stands in a sufficiently strong relation to its later life for that life to count as its future.

This formulation strengthens Marquis. He need not claim that every unrealized possibility has moral weight. He can argue that abortion is different from contraception because the fetus is already an individual whose development has begun. The fetus is not simply a possible person; it is a developing person or, at minimum, a developing human organism that would become a person without interruption. The force of the argument therefore rests on the continuity of an individual life, not on the value of possibilities as such.

Still, the argument may not justify the conclusion that abortion is always immoral. Even if the fetus possesses a valuable future, the pregnant woman also has an actual life, actual rights, and actual interests. Pregnancy requires the use of her body, and continuing it may impose serious physical, psychological, or social burdens. The future-like-ours account measures the loss caused by abortion but does not determine whether that loss outweighs the costs of gestation. Nor does it explain why a fetus’s right to life would include a right to another person’s bodily support. Thus, the account may establish that abortion involves a serious moral loss without establishing that every abortion is impermissible.

## Does the future-like-ours argument prove that abortion is immoral?

The answer depends on what “immoral” means. If it means that abortion is always wrong regardless of circumstances, Marquis’s argument does not succeed. The value of the fetus’s future may be substantial, but moral rights can conflict. In cases of serious threats to the pregnant woman’s life, abortion may be justified even if it deprives the fetus of a valuable future. Similarly, cases involving rape, catastrophic fetal abnormality, or extreme burdens may require a broader moral analysis than Marquis provides.

If “immoral” means that abortion normally involves the serious wrongful deprivation of something valuable, the argument is more successful. On a biological-continuity view of identity, a normal fetus is not merely a possible person. It is an existing organism with a future that would contain the goods of conscious human life. Abortion therefore deprives that organism of a future that would have been its own. This gives abortion a serious prima facie moral cost.

The objections show, however, that the conclusion is not independent of controversial assumptions. McInerney is right that Marquis must justify the attribution of the adult future to the fetus rather than simply assuming it. Lovering is right that the lack of consciousness creates a morally significant disanalogy between the fetus and an unconscious adult. Sinnott-Armstrong is right that deprivation alone does not automatically establish a right, and that the fetus’s claim must be explained rather than inferred from the value of a possible life.

The strongest defense of Marquis is therefore a moderate one. It should not claim that the fetus has exactly the same moral status as an adult merely because both have valuable futures. Instead, it should argue that the fetus possesses a serious moral claim grounded in the continuity of an individual organism toward a valuable conscious life. This claim may be weaker or differently structured than the claim possessed by a self-conscious adult, but it is not negligible. The fetus’s lack of present desires does not eliminate the loss, since objective deprivation can harm beings that cannot formulate future-directed preferences. The absence of continuous consciousness does not eliminate identity, since human organisms persist through sleep, anesthesia, and other interruptions. And the fetus differs from a merely possible person because it is already an existing developing individual.

Whether this is enough to establish full moral rights remains contestable. A personhood theorist can maintain that moral rights arise only with consciousness or sentience. An interests theorist can argue that a fetus acquires rights gradually as it develops the capacity for experience and desire. An animalist can maintain that the fetus has the right to life from the beginning because it is already the same human animal that will later possess psychological capacities. The future-like-ours argument is compatible with the last position but does not conclusively prove it.

## Conclusion

Marquis’s future-like-ours account correctly identifies an important dimension of the wrongness of killing. Killing is not wrong merely because it causes pain, frustrates a present desire, or disrupts social relationships. Its central harm is often the deprivation of the valuable life that the victim would otherwise have lived. The isolated hermit shows that this harm can be personal rather than social, while the paraplegic case shows that a future can remain valuable even when it is limited, uncertain, or lacking many conventional opportunities.

McInerney’s objection demonstrates that Marquis must defend the claim that the fetus and the future adult are one individual. Lovering’s objection shows that biological continuity does not automatically provide the psychological connection that many people regard as central to personal identity. Sinnott-Armstrong’s objection establishes that a valuable future is not by itself identical with a moral right, since the fetus’s interests and claims must be explained. These criticisms prevent the argument from functioning as a simple proof from “valuable future” to “absolute prohibition of abortion.”

Nevertheless, the objections do not decisively refute Marquis. The relevant contrast is not between actualized and unrealized futures in the temporal sense. Even an adult’s future is unrealized at the moment of killing. The relevant contrast is between a future that belongs to an existing individual and a mere possibility belonging to no one. If the fetus is understood as the same developing organism that would later become the conscious adult, then its future is not merely a possibility. It is an unrealized future of an existing individual. Abortion therefore constitutes a serious loss to that individual, even if the individual cannot yet understand or desire what is lost.

The most defensible conclusion is consequently qualified. The future-like-ours account provides a strong reason to regard abortion as morally serious and, in ordinary cases, as a wrongful deprivation. Its normative weight rests primarily on the loss of an individual’s valuable future, not on the loss of abstract possibilities. But the account does not, by itself, show that the fetus has an overriding right to life or that every abortion is immoral. To reach that stronger conclusion, one must add a theory of personal identity, a theory of the moral significance of consciousness, and a theory of rights in relation to bodily autonomy. Marquis’s account succeeds as a powerful argument about what abortion takes away; it is less successful as a complete account of why that loss must always determine what abortion is morally permissible.
