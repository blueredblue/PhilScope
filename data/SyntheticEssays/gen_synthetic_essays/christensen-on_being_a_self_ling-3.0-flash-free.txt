**The Formal and the Supersensible: Reconciling Kantian Selfhood with Practical Reason and Marion’s Givenness**

The claim that Kantian selfhood requires reconciling speculative possibility with practical reason and the supersensible experiences theorized by Jean-Luc Marion strikes at the very heart of the modern philosophical project. It posits that the Kantian transcendental subject—so rigorously defined by the formal conditions of experience—is not a closed system, but an open field requiring the dynamic input of moral agency and the overwhelming presence of supersensible phenomena to achieve its full realization. To evaluate this claim, one must first understand the precise nature of the restriction Kant places upon speculative possibility, the alternative accounts of possibility offered by Aristotle and Heidegger, the practical objection to formalist accounts of the self, and finally, the profound tension between inner sense and apperception when confronted with the saturated phenomenon. What emerges is a nuanced picture of the self not as a static formal unity, but as a dynamic existential field whose boundaries are perpetually stretched by the practical and the supersensible.

Kant’s critical philosophy fundamentally restricts speculative possibility through the formal conditions of experience. In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Kant dismantles the traditional speculative metaphysics of rationalism by establishing that human knowledge is bounded by the forms of intuition and the categories of the understanding. Speculative possibility—the realm of what *could* be thought apart from experience—is restricted because the mind does not passively receive representations but actively structures them through the a priori forms of space, time, and the pure concepts of the understanding (the categories). Any object of experience must conform to these formal conditions; that which cannot be intuited in space and time or thought through the categories is not merely unknown, but unknowable. Speculative reason, driven by the desire to know the unconditioned, inevitably falls into antinomy, producing contradictory propositions about the soul, the world, and God. 

This restriction means that speculative possibility is tethered entirely to the phenomenal realm. The self, in its speculative capacity, is restricted to being the transcendental unity of apperception—the formal "I think" that must be able to accompany all my representations. As a formal condition, this self provides the unity of consciousness, but it cannot know itself as it is in itself. The supersensible—the thing-in-itself, the noumenon—remains a limit concept, a boundary marker that prevents reason from overstepping its bounds. Speculative possibility, therefore, is a closed loop: it can only generate possibilities that remain within the framework of possible experience. It can speculate about the necessary conditions of experience, but it cannot access a reality that transcends the formal conditions of its own constitution. 

To appreciate the depth of this restriction, and the necessity of reconciling it with practical reason and Marion’s supersensible experiences, it is instructive to contrast Kant’s formal account of possibility with the dynamic and existential possibilities theorized by Aristotle and Heidegger. Aristotle offers a fundamentally different ontology of possibility through the concept of *dynamis* (potentiality). For Aristotle, possibility is not merely the logical absence of contradiction, nor is it a formal rule imposed by the mind. Instead, possibility is grounded in the internal actuality of a substance. A seed is potentially an oak tree because the form of the oak is immanent to the seed; the possibility is dynamic, driven by the inherent teleology of the substance toward its actualization. Possibility, for Aristotle, is an ontological feature of things themselves, tied to their material and formal causes. Unlike Kant’s static, formal restriction, Aristotelian possibility is dynamic and historical, unfolding according to the internal nature of the entity. It is not limited by the external forms of a transcendental subject, but by the actualizing potential of the substance itself.

Heidegger radicalizes this dynamic potential into existential possibility. For Heidegger, the restriction of Kant’s formal conditions represents a symptom of the broader metaphysical forgetting of the question of Being. Heidegger’s Dasein is not a transcendental subject governed by categories, but a being whose very essence is its existence—its Being is an issue for it. Existential possibility (*Existenz*) is not the logical capacity to conform to rules, nor the immanent teleology of a substance, but the fundamental projection of the self into the world. Dasein is thrown (*Geworfenheit*) into a situation, yet it is defined by its capacity to project (*Entwurf*) possibilities for its own Being. Possibility, in Heidegger’s terms, is the truth of Dasein; it is not a static condition, but an ongoing, historical, and existential unfolding. Where Kant restricts speculative possibility to the formal conditions of the understanding, and Aristotle grounds it in the dynamic actuality of substances, Heidegger relocates possibility to the existential structure of the self’s Being-in-the-world. The self is not a formal unity that restricts what can appear, but a dynamic horizon of projected existence that constitutes the world in the first place.

This contrast illuminates the force of the objection that ignoring practical sources of possibility renders accounts of the individual self incomplete. If the self is merely the formal unity of apperception, restricted to the bounds of possible experience, then the self is an epistemological spectator, not an agent. The formal conditions of experience can tell us how the self must think, but they cannot tell us how the self must act. By ignoring the practical source of possibility—freedom and the moral law—Kantian selfhood is reduced to a hollow shell, a formal structure devoid of existential weight. The objection argues that the individual self is not complete until it is grounded in practical reason. For Kant, practical reason does indeed bridge this gap, but it does so by positing the self as a noumenal agent. The moral law reveals the self as free, as the unconditioned ground of its own actions, a ground that cannot be reduced to the deterministic laws of the phenomenal realm. Practical reason thus introduces a second source of possibility: the possibility of the good, which is not constrained by the formal conditions of speculative experience. Without this practical source, the self is merely a phenomenal mechanism; with it, the self becomes a moral agent whose ultimate reality lies in the supersensible. 

It is precisely here that Jean-Luc Marion’s theory of the supersensible becomes indispensable for evaluating the claim of Kantian selfhood’s completeness. Marion, a phenomenologist of revelation, argues that the supersensible is not merely a hidden ground (as in Kant’s noumenon) or a logical limit (as in negative theology), but a phenomenon in its own right—the saturated phenomenon. The saturated phenomenon is givenness that overwhelms the noema; it is a phenomenon that gives itself without limits, without the mediation of concepts or the conditions of possible experience. In revelation, love, or the vision of the icon, the phenomenon saturates the intuition, refusing to be bounded by the categories or the forms of inner sense. 

Marion’s supersensible experiences challenge the Kantian restriction of speculative possibility by introducing a givenness that is not constituted by the formal conditions of the subject. The saturated phenomenon is not an object of speculative knowledge, nor is it merely a practical imperative; it is a sheer donation, a givenness that precedes and exceeds both theoretical and practical reason. This forces a radical reevaluation of the self: if the self is defined by its capacity to unify representations according to formal conditions, how can it account for an experience that saturates and overwhelms those very conditions? 

This brings us to the crux of the matter: the tension between inner sense and apperception, and the question of whether saturated phenomena constitute the self or merely appear within an individual halo of possibility. In Kant’s system, inner sense is the form of inner intuition—time—which provides the manifold of inner states, while apperception is the transcendental unity that synthesizes this manifold into a unified "I." The tension here is profound: inner sense gives us the *content* of our self-consciousness (our feelings, desires, thoughts), but apperception only provides the *formal* unity of that content. The self is the "I think" that must be able to accompany the representations, yet the inner sense remains an appearance, a phenomenal interiority, not the noumenal self itself. 

When we introduce Marion’s saturated phenomena into this Kantian tension, we must ask whether these phenomena constitute the self or merely appear within an individual halo of possibility. The "halo of possibility" can be understood phenomenologically as the horizon of the transcendental ego—the limits of what the formal unity of apperception can unify and what inner sense can contain. On one reading, saturated phenomena merely appear within this halo. They are overwhelming experiences—visions of divine love, mystical unifications—that nevertheless remain appearances to the self. Even the most saturated phenomenon must be given in time (inner sense) and unified by the "I think" (apperception). In this view, the self remains the formal condition of the phenomenon; the halo of possibility stretches to accommodate the saturation, but the self remains the spectator, and the phenomenon remains an object within the self’s horizon.

However, this reading fails to capture the transformative power of the saturated phenomenon as theorized by Marion. If the saturated phenomenon exceeds the noema and overwhelms the formal conditions of experience, it cannot merely appear *within* the existing halo of possibility; it must rupture and reconstitute it. The saturated phenomenon does not just extend the self’s horizon; it fundamentally alters the self’s mode of being. In the encounter with the supersensible—whether the radical givenness of love or the call of conscience—the self is not merely a spectator of an appearance, but is called into question, called to respond. The self is constituted *through* its practical response to the saturated phenomenon. Here, the tension between inner sense and apperception is resolved not by the self absorbing the phenomenon, but by the phenomenon demanding a practical appropriation that reshapes the self’s very possibility. 

Thus, the self is not constituted *by* the saturated phenomenon in the Kantian sense of providing the formal conditions of unity, but the self is constituted *for* the supersensible through its practical engagement with it. The saturated phenomenon appears within the halo of possibility, but it does so as a rupture—a givenness that exceeds the formal conditions and forces the self to expand its horizon of possibility into the practical and the supersensible. The inner sense provides the temporal horizon in which the phenomenon appears, and apperception provides the formal unity that initially frames it, but the saturated phenomenon demands a practical reason that goes beyond the formal. The self, in this dynamic, is not a static formal unity, but a field of possibility that is stretched, expanded, and redefined by its practical and supersensible encounters.

Ultimately, the claim that Kantian selfhood requires reconciling speculative possibility with practical reason and Marion’s supersensible experiences is validated by this analysis. The formal conditions of experience necessarily restrict speculative possibility, closing it off from the dynamic and existential possibilities of Aristotle and Heidegger, and leaving the self as a hollow formal unity if it ignores the practical sources of its own agency. The objection that ignoring practical sources renders accounts of the individual self incomplete is correct: without practical reason, the self is reduced to a phenomenal mechanism, unable to account for its own freedom or moral agency. Furthermore, the introduction of Marion’s saturated phenomena reveals that even practical reason is insufficient if it remains purely formal. The supersensible does not merely appear within the individual halo of possibility; it constitutes a new demand upon the self, forcing a practical reconciliation that transforms the self from a formal spectator into an existential agent. 

Kantian selfhood, therefore, is not a closed system of formal conditions, but an open dynamic that requires the practical reason to bridge the gap between the phenomenal and the noumenal, and the supersensible experiences theorized by Marion to rupture the limits of the halo of possibility. The self is reconciled not by speculating about the unconditioned, but by practically responding to the overwhelming givenness of the supersensible, thereby realizing its own completeness in the very act of moral and existential appropriation.
