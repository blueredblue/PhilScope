**The Dialectical Abyss: Evaluating Vlastos, Self-Predication, and Non-Identity in Plato’s Third-Man Argument**

The *Parmenides* stands as one of the most enigmatic and dialectically rigorous texts in the Western philosophical canon. While Plato’s early dialogues famously defend the Theory of Forms against the critiques of Protagoras and Thrasymachus, the *Parmenides* turns its dialectical artillery inward, subjecting the Theory to a devastating series of aporias. Among these, the Third-Man Argument (TMA) has long been regarded as the most formidable challenge to the ontology of Platonic Forms. In his seminal work *Plato’s Theory of Knowledge*, Gregory Vlastos offered a penetrating and influential interpretation of the TMA, arguing that it constitutes a genuine refutation by Plato of his own Theory. Vlastos’s analysis hinges on the tension between two fundamental principles governing Platonic ontology: the Principle of Self-Predication (SP) and the Principle of Non-Identity (NI). This essay evaluates Vlastos’s interpretation of the TMA by scrutinizing this very tension. It assesses whether Plato’s ontology inadvertently commits him to mutually inconsistent premises that generate an infinite regress, or whether alternative solutions parsing predication and identity can adequately resolve the dialectical problem without sacrificing historical plausibility. The argument advanced here is that while Vlastos correctly identifies the internal inconsistency generated by a rigid conjunction of SP and NI, the regress is not an inevitable logical collapse of Plato’s ontology. Rather, the TMA reveals the limitations of a simplistic, literalist reading of predication and identity. By parsing predication as a structural relation of essence and identity as a categorical distinction of genera, alternative solutions can neutralize the regress while preserving the historical plausibility of Plato’s core metaphysical commitments.

**Vlastos and the TMA as Genuine Refutation**

Vlastos’s interpretation of the *Parmenides* is distinguished by his conviction that Plato intended the TMA to function as a dialectical refutation of the Theory of Forms. Prior to Vlastos, many scholars viewed the TMA as a mere dialectical exercise, a logical puzzle that Plato posed and then dismissed, or even a critique of the Theory as held by some earlier Pythagorean or heterodox interlocutor. Vlastos rejected this reading, arguing that the argument’s force is precisely its capacity to undermine the very principle it relies upon. For Vlastos, the TMA is not an external attack but an internal critique, demonstrating that the premises required to sustain the Theory of Forms are mutually inconsistent.

The Third-Man Argument proceeds as follows: Suppose there is a Form of F-ness (let us say, the Form of Man). According to the Principle of Self-Predication (SP), the Form of Man is itself a man; the Form participates in itself. According to the Principle of Non-Identity (NI), the Form of Man is strictly distinct from the particular men that participate in it—Socrates, for instance, is a man, but Socrates is not the Form of Man. Because the Form of Man is a man, and because it is distinct from Socrates, there must be some further entity that makes both Socrates and the Form of Man man. This entity is the "Third Man" (M3), which is itself a man. But M3, being a man, must itself participate in the Form of Man. Since M3 is distinct from both Socrates and the Form of Man, it requires a Fourth Man (M4) to explain how it is man, and so on, generating an infinite regress. Vlastos argued that this regress is not merely an aesthetic or cosmological embarrassment; it is a logical contradiction that reveals the ontology is fundamentally flawed. The premises of SP and NI, when combined with the principle that participation requires a mediating Form, generate an inconsistency that the Theory cannot escape.

**The Engine of the Regress: Self-Predication and Non-Identity**

To evaluate Vlastos’s claim that the TMA commits Plato to mutually inconsistent premises, one must first understand precisely how Self-Predication and Non-Identity operate within the argument and why their conjunction is so potent. Self-Predication is the thesis that every Form is an instance of itself. In the context of the TMA, the Form of Man is a man. SP is not merely a formal logical requirement; it is the ontological engine that drives the regress. If the Form of Man were not a man—if it were a purely abstract, non-predicable entity—then the fact that Socrates is a man would not require the Form of Man to be a man in the same respect. The regress begins precisely because the Form shares the very property it is meant to explain. By making the Form an instance of the property it defines, SP ensures that the Form falls under the scope of its own definition, thereby necessitating a further explanatory link.

Non-Identity, conversely, is the thesis that the Forms are ontologically distinct from the particulars they explain, and distinct from one another in their specific roles. In the TMA, NI demands that the Form of Man is not identical to Socrates, and crucially, that the Form of Man is not identical to the Third Man. NI is the principle that preserves the distinctness of the ontological levels: the intelligible and the sensible must remain separate, and each Form must maintain its unique explanatory role. If NI were abandoned, and the Form of Man were simply identical to the Third Man, the regress would stop. But this would collapse the Theory into a form of pantheism or nominalism, where the Form is just another particular or where the Form loses its distinct explanatory power. Thus, NI is essential to the very structure of Platonic participation; it is what makes the Forms a separate, intelligible realm.

The tension between SP and NI creates a paradoxical bind. SP requires the Form to be an instance of the property it defines, which makes it an object of that property. NI requires the Form to be strictly distinct from the other objects of that property (the particulars and the subsequent Third Men). But if the Form is an object of the property, and distinct from other objects of the property, then it requires a further mediating entity to explain how it is an object of that property. The regress is not an accidental byproduct of the argument; it is the logical consequence of the ontological commitment to both principles simultaneously. Vlastos’s insight is that Plato’s ontology inadvertently forces this regress because it cannot consistently maintain both the self-exemplifying nature of the Forms (SP) and their strict ontological distinctness from the instances they explain (NI). The premises are, in Vlastos’s view, mutually inconsistent.

**The Inconsistency of the Premises**

If Vlastos is correct, Plato’s ontology commits him to a set of premises that generate an infinite regress, thereby undermining the coherence of the Theory of Forms. The inconsistency arises because the Theory attempts to serve two incompatible explanatory functions. On one hand, the Form of Man must explain why Socrates is a man; this requires the Form to be ontologically distinct from Socrates (NI). On the other hand, the Form of Man must be fully real and self-subsistent, which requires it to be an instance of itself (SP). The TMA shows that when the Form is an instance of itself, it becomes an object of the property it defines, and because it is distinct from Socrates, it falls into the same explanatory gap that Socrates does. The Form requires a Third Man to explain its manhood, and this Third Man requires a Fourth, and so on.

For Vlastos, this demonstrates that the Theory of Forms is not merely incomplete or in need of refinement; it is logically refuted. The premises that generate the regress—SP, NI, and the Principle of Mediation (that any relation between a Form and its instances requires a further Form)—cannot all be true. If Plato wishes to avoid the regress, he must abandon one of these principles. Yet, abandoning SP collapses the Forms into mere concepts, stripping them of their ontological independence. Abandoning NI collapses the Forms into particulars, destroying the dualistic distinction between the intelligible and the sensible. Abandoning the Principle of Mediation leaves the relationship between the Form and the particulars unexplained, rendering the Theory circular or vacuous. Thus, Vlastos concludes that the TMA is a genuine refutation: Plato’s ontology inadvertently commits him to mutually inconsistent premises, and the infinite regress is the symptom of this foundational inconsistency.

**Alternative Solutions: Parsing Predication and Identity**

Despite the compelling force of Vlastos’s analysis, the conclusion that Plato’s ontology is fundamentally inconsistent requires careful scrutiny. The TMA relies on a specific, somewhat rigid, parsing of what it means for a Form to be self-predicated and what it means for a Form to be non-identical to its instances. Alternative solutions that parse predication and identity differently can neutralize the regress without sacrificing historical plausibility, suggesting that the TMA is a dialectical trap rather than a refutation of Plato’s mature metaphysics.

The most promising alternative solutions involve rejecting the premise that Self-Predication entails self-exemplification in the manner required by the TMA. In the standard reading, SP means that the Form of Man is a man in exactly the same respect that Socrates is a man; the Form participates in the Form of Man, and Socrates participates in the Form of Man. If this reading is taken literally, the regress is inescapable. However, if SP is reinterpreted as a structural claim about the ontological status of the Form rather than a claim about self-instantiation, the dialectical pressure is relieved. On this reading, the Form of Man is not a "man" in the same categorical sense as Socrates is a man; rather, the Form *is* Man-ness itself. It does not "have" manhood; it *is* the essence of manhood. Predication, in this parsing, is not a relation of an instance to a universal, but a relation of a derivative entity to an essential reality. The Form does not participate in itself because it is not an instance of itself; it simply is what it is. By decoupling Self-Predication from self-exemplification, the need for a Third Man to explain the Form's manhood evaporates. The Form is man by its own essence, not by virtue of participating in a further Form of Man.

This alternative parsing of predication aligns with the more sophisticated ontological developments evident in Plato’s later dialogues, such as the *Sophist* and the *Statesman*. In these dialogues, Plato increasingly treats the Forms not as universals that subsist in a separate realm, but as the essential natures that structure reality. The Form of Man is not an object that can be predicated of Socrates in a purely predicative sense; it is the active principle of manhood that makes Socrates a man. If Self-Predication is understood as the Form being fully actualized and self-subsistent in its essence, rather than as the Form being an instance of itself, the TMA loses its logical force. The Form of Man does not require a Third Man to mediate its manhood because its manhood is intrinsic and essential, not relational or participatory in the manner the TMA assumes.

Similarly, alternative solutions can be found by parsing the Principle of Non-Identity in a more nuanced way. NI, as deployed in the TMA, demands that the Form of Man is numerically distinct from the Third Man, the Fourth Man, and so on. This strict numerical distinctness is what drives the regress forward, as each new Third Man is treated as a new entity requiring its own mediating Form. However, if Non-Identity is reinterpreted as a categorical or genus-level distinction rather than a strict numerical separation, the regress can be halted. The Third Man is not a new Form; it is the Form of Man itself, considered in its capacity as the unifying principle that relates the Form to the particulars. The Form of Man and the Third Man are the same entity, but they are not identical in all respects; they are distinct in their relational roles. The Form of Man is the essence, while the Third Man is the Form considered as the mediator of participation. Because they are the same entity, NI does not generate an infinite regress of distinct entities; it merely highlights the different aspects of the single Form as it relates to itself and to particulars.

This solution, which has been explored by scholars such as Mary Louise Gill and Lesley Penner, preserves the historical plausibility of Plato’s ontology by maintaining the dualistic structure of the Theory—the distinction between the intelligible and the sensible—without falling into the infinite regress that Vlastos identifies. It does so by refusing to treat the Third Man as a new, separate entity. Instead, it recognizes that the apparent "Third Man" is simply the Form of Man itself, viewed from the perspective of its relation to the particulars. The regress stops because the Form is not an additional entity that needs explaining; it is the ultimate explanatory ground.

**Historical Plausibility and the Spirit of the *Parmenides***

A crucial objection to these alternative solutions is that they may sacrifice historical plausibility by reading later, more sophisticated metaphysical concepts back into the early Theory of Forms. If Plato in the *Republic* genuinely believed that the Form of Man participates in the Form of Man, then any solution that denies self-exemplification is anachronistic. Furthermore, the *Parmenides* itself seems to present the TMA as a devastating critique, and if alternative solutions neutralize the critique, we risk diminishing the dialectical power of the *Parmenides*.

However, a careful reading of the *Parmenides* suggests that Plato’s intention may not have been to refute the Theory of Forms in its entirety, but rather to expose the inadequacy of a simplistic, literalist understanding of participation and self-predication. The aporias of the *Parmenides* are not meant to be fatal blows to the Theory, but dialectical provocations that force the philosopher to refine his concepts. The TMA reveals that if we cling to a naive realism about the Forms—where they are just another set of objects that must be related to one another and to particulars via a further set of objects—we fall into absurdity. The dialectical point is not that the Forms do not exist, but that our *understanding* of predication and identity must be deepened.

By parsing predication as a structural relation of essence and identity as a categorical distinction of relational roles, we do not abandon the Theory of Forms; we mature it. We move from a theory where Forms are objects that participate in one another, to a theory where Forms are the essential natures that ground the possibility of participation in the first place. This reading is historically plausible because it aligns with the trajectory of Plato’s thought. The early dialogues present a relatively straightforward Theory of Forms; the middle dialogues (*Republic*, *Phaedo*) deepen it with the introduction of the Form of the Good and the doctrine of recollection; the late dialogues (*Sophist*, *Statesman*) explicitly grapple with the problems of participation, non-identity, and self-predication, offering more nuanced accounts of the Forms as active principles of unity and differentiation. The TMA, on this reading, is Plato’s dialectical preparation for this maturation. It shows the inadequacy of the early, literalist interpretation, not the inadequacy of the ontological commitment itself.

Moreover, the alternative solutions do not violate the core historical commitments of Plato’s ontology. The dualism between the intelligible and the sensible remains intact; the Forms are still the ultimate objects of knowledge and the grounds of being. What changes is the logical mechanism of participation. Instead of a chain of entities (Socrates, Form of Man, Third Man, Fourth Man) requiring an ever-expanding network of Forms, the ontology posits a single, self-subsistent Form that is the ground of its own being and the being of its instances. This preserves the explanatory power of the Forms without the metaphysical extravagance of an infinite regress. The Form of Man explains why Socrates is a man, and it explains why it is a man, not by participating in a further Form, but by being the essence of manhood itself.

**Conclusion**

Gregory Vlastos’s interpretation of the Third-Man Argument provides a powerful and enduring framework for understanding the dialectical challenges facing Plato’s Theory of Forms. His identification of the tension between Self-Predication and Non-Identity as the engine of the infinite regress is a masterful piece of philosophical analysis. Vlastos correctly demonstrates that if we read SP and NI in a literalist, objectifying manner—where the Form is an instance of itself in the same way particulars are instances of it, and where the Form is strictly numerically distinct from any mediating entity—the premises of Plato’s ontology become mutually inconsistent. The regress is genuine, and the ontology is, on these terms, self-refuting.

However, the conclusion that Plato’s ontology is inevitably committed to this inconsistency is too strong. The TMA generates an infinite regress not because Plato’s ontology is inherently contradictory, but because the argument relies on a rigid and overly literal parsing of predication and identity. By interpreting Self-Predication as a structural claim about the essential self-subsistence of the Form, rather than a claim about self-exemplification, and by interpreting Non-Identity as a categorical distinction of relational roles rather than a strict demand for numerical separation, the dialectical problem can be resolved without sacrificing historical plausibility. The Third Man is not a new entity that demands a Fourth; it is the Form itself, considered in its mediating capacity. The regress is a product of a conceptual confusion, not a refutation of the Forms. Thus, the TMA does not demonstrate that Plato’s ontology is inconsistent; rather, it demonstrates that a simplistic reading of participation must give way to a more profound understanding of essence, predication, and identity—a transition that Plato himself, in his later dialogues, was already undertaking.
