# John Hick’s Pluralistic Hypothesis and the Problem of the Pre-Axial

John Hick’s religious pluralism is one of the most ambitious attempts in modern philosophy of religion to reconcile the apparent diversity of religious traditions with a realist account of religious truth. Hick rejects both exclusivism, according to which one religion alone possesses salvific truth, and a merely relativist pluralism, according to which all religious claims are equally human constructions with no transcendent reference. His alternative is a form of critical religious realism. The great religions, he argues, are historically and culturally diverse responses to one transcendent reality, which he calls “the Real.” Each tradition apprehends and interprets the Real through its own concepts, symbols, practices, and forms of religious experience. The plurality of religions is therefore not simply evidence of error. It is a consequence of the fact that the Real exceeds the conceptual and linguistic resources of any one tradition.

Yet Hick’s hypothesis raises a serious problem concerning its scope. In its mature form, Hick’s pluralism is primarily a theory about the major post-axial religious traditions: Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and perhaps other historically literate and institutionally developed traditions. Pre-axial religions, indigenous traditions, local ritual systems, shamanic practices, and forms of religion that do not fit the model of a universal path from self-centredness to Reality-centredness are often left outside the principal field of analysis. This restriction can appear unreasonably arbitrary. If religious diversity is explained by the plurality of human responses to the Real, why should the hypothesis apply only to religions that emerged during or after the so-called Axial Age? Why should Native American vision quests, African shamanic initiations, Aboriginal Australian ceremonies, or other indigenous traditions not count as responses to the same transcendent reality?

The difficulty becomes sharper when one considers the relation between Hick’s foundational principle and his criterion of religious validity. On the one hand, Hick maintains that religious experiences directed toward a transcendent reality provide justification for religious belief. On the other hand, he evaluates traditions principally by their soteriological fruits: the transformation of human beings from self-centredness to a more reality-centred life marked by compassion, humility, and moral openness. These two principles do not necessarily coincide. A tradition may contain profound experiences of transcendence without producing transformation according to Hick’s preferred model. Conversely, a tradition may produce ethical and communal flourishing without being easily described as a response to a single transcendent Real. The question is whether Hick’s emphasis on post-axial traditions is supported by his theory or whether it reflects an unacknowledged cultural bias.

The most defensible conclusion is that Hick’s restriction is not inevitably arbitrary, but it is insufficiently justified in its original form. It can be made more coherent only if the category of the Real is expanded, the criterion of religious validity is reformulated in terms of ethical fruitfulness rather than a single psychological pattern, and the alleged pre-axial/post-axial distinction is treated as historically useful but philosophically non-exclusive.

## Hick’s Pluralistic Hypothesis

Hick’s pluralism begins with a rejection of the assumption that religious disagreement entails that most religions must be false. Religious traditions make apparently incompatible claims. Christianity affirms that God is personal and that salvation is mediated through Christ; Buddhism often rejects the notion of a creator God and conceives liberation in terms of awakening to dependent origination and the cessation of craving; Hindu traditions may describe ultimate reality in personal, impersonal, or non-dual terms; Islam emphasizes submission to the one God; Judaism emphasizes covenantal relationship and historical revelation. If these claims are treated as straightforward descriptions of an independently accessible object, they appear mutually exclusive.

Hick’s response is to distinguish between the Real as it is in itself and the Real as humanly experienced and interpreted. He draws, in a broad way, on the Kantian distinction between the noumenal and phenomenal. The Real is not an object within the world that can be observed from a neutral standpoint. It is the transcendent source or ground of religious experience, but it is encountered under different forms shaped by human conceptual schemes. The Real is experienced as personal by some traditions and as non-personal by others. It is understood through the categories of theistic theism, non-theistic spirituality, emptiness, Brahman, Allah, or other symbols. These are not simply arbitrary inventions, but culturally conditioned modes of response to a reality that transcends them.

Hick therefore claims that religious traditions can be “equally salvific” without being doctrinally equivalent. They are historically distinct paths in which human beings are transformed in relation to the transcendent. Religious truth is not measured solely by the literal correctness of doctrinal propositions. It is also manifested in the capacity of a tradition to generate spiritual transformation. A religion is authentic insofar as it enables people to move from self-centredness toward a more reality-centred existence. The signs of this transformation include compassion, justice, humility, courage, forgiveness, generosity, and a reduction of egotism.

This position has a powerful philosophical appeal. It preserves religious realism while acknowledging religious pluralism. It avoids the imperialistic claim that every tradition must be interpreted as an incomplete version of Christianity. At the same time, it avoids the conclusion that all religions are merely subjective projections. The traditions are different, but their difference is explained by the interaction between the Real and the historically situated structures of human consciousness.

Nevertheless, Hick’s project is not simply descriptive. It includes a normative judgment about which traditions qualify as genuine responses to the Real. This judgment is where the problem of scope emerges. The theory is most confidently applied to “the great world religions,” especially those that arose in the post-axial period. The reason for this emphasis must be examined rather than assumed.

## The Axial Age and the Post-Axial Religious Paradigm

The term “Axial Age” is associated principally with Karl Jaspers, who used it to describe the period roughly between 800 and 200 BCE in which major religious and philosophical transformations occurred in China, India, Israel, and Greece. During this period, traditions associated with Confucius, the Hebrew prophets, the Upanishads, the Buddha, and the Greek philosophers developed new forms of reflection on the self, morality, transcendence, and the structure of reality. These movements often challenged local political and ritual systems and articulated more universal conceptions of human existence.

Hick’s religious pluralism is naturally suited to this model of religion. The post-axial traditions tend to present themselves as universal paths of salvation or liberation. They address the individual as an agent capable of moral and spiritual transformation. They establish practices that can be followed beyond the boundaries of a single tribe or locality. They generate texts, institutions, and explicit theological or philosophical reflection. Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, and Hinduism can therefore be compared as alternative responses to ultimate reality. Each has a recognizable soteriology, a body of teachings, a community of practice, and a historical development that permits comparative analysis.

By contrast, many pre-axial or indigenous traditions are not organized around a sharp distinction between ordinary life and a separate religious sphere. Their sacred realities are embedded in land, kinship, seasonal cycles, animals, ancestors, political relations, and ritual obligations. They may not offer a universal doctrine or a singular path of salvation. Their central question may not be how an individual is liberated from self-centredness, sin, ignorance, or samsara. Instead, the central concern may be the maintenance of right relations among human beings, animals, ancestors, spirits, and the land.

Hick might therefore defend his emphasis on post-axial traditions by arguing that these traditions provide clearer evidence of universal religious transformation. They contain explicit accounts of conversion, enlightenment, salvation, liberation, or reconciliation. They are also more readily comparable across cultures because they have developed systematic accounts of ultimate reality and the human predicament. The post-axial focus could be methodological rather than metaphysical: Hick begins with traditions that most clearly display the phenomenon he wants to interpret.

However, a methodological restriction becomes problematic when the theory’s rhetoric is universal. Hick does not merely claim that post-axial religions are interesting examples. He presents religious pluralism as an account of the relationship between humanity and the Real. If the Real is genuinely transcendent and if human beings encounter it in diverse historical forms, there is no obvious reason to expect such encounters only among traditions that fit the Axial model. The distinction between post-axial and pre-axial religion may identify differences in social structure, literary form, and conceptual self-consciousness, but it does not by itself establish differences in spiritual authenticity.

The danger is that the category “great world religions” may reflect modern academic and Western assumptions about what counts as religion. Traditions with scriptures, universal claims, large populations, centralized institutions, and explicit doctrines are more likely to appear in comparative theology. Local and indigenous traditions are more likely to be classified as myth, ritual, magic, folklore, or culture. Hick’s limitation may thus reproduce the very asymmetry that pluralism is supposed to overcome.

## The Foundational Principle: Experience and Transcendent Reference

At the foundation of Hick’s thought is the idea that religious experience is not simply a private feeling but can be interpreted as an encounter with the transcendent. He is influenced by the claim that perception is always interpreted through conceptual schemes. Religious experience, like ordinary sense experience, is mediated by the perceiver’s categories. A Christian may experience divine love through the image of Christ; a Buddhist may experience liberation as nirvanic awakening; a Hindu may experience the unity of the self and ultimate reality. The conceptual differences do not necessarily imply that one experience is veridical and the others illusory. They may represent different modes in which the Real becomes available to human consciousness.

This principle appears to provide a strong basis for including pre-axial traditions. Native American vision quests, for example, may involve fasting, isolation, dream, encounter with a spirit helper, and a transformed understanding of one’s place within a network of human and non-human relations. African shamanic initiations may involve illness, symbolic death, visionary experience, communication with ancestors or spirits, and the acquisition of powers used for healing or communal service. Whether or not these experiences should be described as “the Real” in Hick’s preferred vocabulary, they plainly present themselves as encounters with a reality that exceeds ordinary ego-consciousness.

If the justification for religious belief rests partly on the way religious experience functions as a form of contact with transcendence, then the form of the tradition should not be decisive. A vision quest need not contain a universal doctrine of salvation in order to be an experience of transcendence. A shamanic initiation need not distinguish sharply between religion and ecology in order to mediate a transformation of the person. The relevant philosophical question is whether such experiences exhibit features that justify interpreting them as responses to a reality beyond the individual. These features might include a sense of givenness, authority, alterity, depth, intelligibility, and lasting transformation.

Of course, religious experience is not self-authenticating. Experiences can be generated by psychological, neurological, social, or cultural causes. They can be contradictory, manipulated, or interpreted in harmful ways. Hick’s appeal to experience does not establish the truth of every belief associated with an experience. But this qualification applies equally to post-axial traditions. Christian mystical experiences can be distorted by institutional power; Buddhist experiences can be interpreted through competing doctrines; Islamic, Hindu, or Jewish experiences can produce conflicting claims. If the possibility of error does not disqualify the major religions, it should not disqualify indigenous traditions merely because their experiences are framed differently.

The foundational principle therefore pushes toward inclusiveness. It suggests that the Real may be encountered in multiple cultural forms, including forms that do not fit the conceptual grammar of post-axial theology. Hick’s pluralism becomes difficult to defend if it accepts the epistemic significance of religious experience in principle but recognizes it only where the experience has been translated into the textual and doctrinal structures of major religions.

## Soteriological Transformation as the Criterion of Validity

Hick attempts to avoid this difficulty by giving soteriological transformation a central role. The truth of a religion is not demonstrated by the mere occurrence of extraordinary experiences. It is shown by the fruits of the tradition in human life. The crucial question is whether a religion produces a movement away from self-centredness and toward Reality-centredness. This is a deeply important insight. Religious experience can be deceptive, self-aggrandizing, or socially destructive. A criterion based on moral and spiritual transformation enables critical evaluation of religious claims.

Yet Hick’s criterion also creates a tension. The principle of transcendent experience is expansive, whereas the criterion of soteriological transformation is comparatively narrow. It is narrow in at least three senses.

First, Hick’s language of “self-centredness” and “Reality-centredness” presupposes a particular understanding of the religious problem. It resembles the concerns of post-axial traditions, especially Christianity, Buddhism, and Hinduism, where liberation is frequently described as a transformation of the ego, the overcoming of desire, or the reorientation of the self toward God or ultimate reality. But not every religious tradition understands the primary human problem in these terms. In many indigenous traditions, the problem may be relational disorder, rupture with ancestors, imbalance between human and non-human communities, violation of ritual obligations, or alienation from the land. The relevant transformation may be restored reciprocity rather than the overcoming of ego-centredness.

Second, Hick’s criterion can appear overly individualistic. Even when he speaks of compassion and justice, his formulation often focuses on the transformation of persons. Indigenous traditions may locate religious validity in the restoration of communal, ecological, and intergenerational relationships. A vision quest may be valid not because it produces an individual who feels inwardly “reality-centred,” but because it enables that person to assume a responsibility to the community, to heal illness, to protect the land, or to renew a broken relationship with the ancestors. The soteriological unit may be a people, a household, a place, or an entire network of life rather than an isolated individual.

Third, the criterion may be too abstract to discriminate adequately among traditions. Compassion, humility, generosity, and justice are admirable goods, but traditions can produce these fruits in different ways and alongside practices that outsiders may regard as problematic. No religion has an uncontested moral record. If ethical fruitfulness is used as a criterion, it must be applied historically, socially, and comparatively rather than through idealized portraits of post-axial traditions.

The tension, then, is not that religious experience and ethical transformation are incompatible. Rather, the problem is that Hick appears to move from one to the other without fully explaining the transition. Religious experience grounds the possibility that beliefs are directed toward the Real. Soteriological transformation then becomes the test of whether a tradition is an authentic response. But why should the test take the specific form of movement from self-centredness to Reality-centredness? Why not use a broader notion of flourishing, relational repair, moral responsibility, or ecological reciprocity?

If the answer is that the Real necessarily transforms the self in a particular way, Hick risks making his criterion stipulative. He defines genuine religion as what produces the kind of transformation he takes to be genuinely religious. If the answer is that this pattern is empirically visible across post-axial traditions, the evidence may be insufficient to exclude traditions whose soteriology is organized around different categories.

## Native American Vision Quests and African Shamanic Initiations

The significance of indigenous practices can be illustrated through the examples of Native American vision quests and African shamanic initiations, though both categories encompass many distinct traditions and should not be treated as uniform.

A vision quest commonly involves withdrawal from ordinary social life, fasting, prayer, solitude, dreams, and encounters with animals, ancestors, or spiritual powers. The person may seek guidance, a name, a vocation, healing, or confirmation of a role within the community. The quest is not merely an attempt to acquire private mystical knowledge. It is often embedded in a system of obligations. The vision imposes duties; it does not simply confer status. Its authenticity may be judged by whether the person returns with wisdom that benefits the community and by whether the person lives in accordance with the responsibilities disclosed through the encounter.

This structure can be interpreted in Hickian terms. The quest involves an experience of a reality that exceeds the ordinary ego. It leads to a reorientation of life. It may produce humility, courage, gratitude, and service. It can therefore satisfy both of Hick’s principles: transcendent experience and moral transformation. If Hick excludes it because it lacks a universal doctrine or a post-axial form of salvation, the exclusion appears arbitrary.

At the same time, the vision quest may challenge Hick’s vocabulary. The distinction between “the Real” and its cultural manifestations may not map neatly onto a tradition in which animals, ancestors, land, and spiritual powers are not merely symbolic appearances of a single metaphysical object. The quest may disclose a plurality of real agencies rather than one noumenal reality behind all religious forms. A participant might not regard the vision as an encounter with “the Real as such,” because the relevant reality is relational and local. It is encountered in a particular place, through a particular animal, and in relation to a particular lineage. Hick must either permit a more plural ontology of transcendence or treat these concrete realities as culturally conditioned manifestations of the Real.

African shamanic initiations similarly resist a simple post-axial paradigm. Initiation may be triggered by illness, misfortune, recurring dreams, possession, or a calling from ancestral or spiritual powers. It often includes symbolic death and rebirth, apprenticeship, ritual discipline, and the acquisition of healing knowledge. The initiate’s authority is validated through service, successful healing, moral conduct, and recognition by the community. Again, the pattern resembles religious transformation: an ordinary identity is disrupted, a deeper reality is disclosed, and the person assumes a vocation oriented toward others.

But the category “shamanism” itself is contested and can obscure differences among African traditions. Many African religious systems do not have a single figure corresponding to the Siberian model of the shaman. There may be diviners, healers, mediums, priests, spirit specialists, and ritual experts with different roles. The philosophical point, however, remains. These traditions frequently understand transcendence as continuous with social and ecological relationships rather than as a universal doctrinal object. Their religious epistemology is embodied, communal, performative, and vocational.

If Hick’s criterion is ethical and soteriological transformation, these practices should be evaluated by their fruits: Do they cultivate healing, reconciliation, courage, responsibility, and care for the community? Do they reduce destructive forms of selfishness? Do they establish right relations with humans and non-humans? Their lack of a post-axial theology should not count against them. Indeed, their ritual forms may reveal that “salvation” need not mean escape from the world or attainment of an individual state of enlightenment. It may mean restored participation in a living order.

## Is Hick’s Restriction Unreasonably Arbitrary?

The charge of arbitrariness can now be stated precisely. Hick’s pluralism claims that religious diversity is a set of culturally conditioned responses to the Real. Yet it privileges traditions that possess certain historical and conceptual characteristics: universal scope, textual articulation, institutional continuity, explicit soteriology, and reflective doctrines about ultimate reality. The theory therefore risks confusing the conditions under which a tradition becomes recognizable to modern comparative philosophy with the conditions under which it becomes religiously valid.

Still, not every limitation is arbitrary. A philosopher may reasonably restrict an argument for practical purposes. Hick could say that his hypothesis is an initial model, not a complete account of every form of religion. He might focus on post-axial traditions because these are the traditions that have developed explicit and comparable accounts of salvation and ultimate reality. The fact that a theory does not address every case does not by itself refute it.

The problem arises when the restriction is not acknowledged as provisional or methodological but functions as a substantive boundary. If pre-axial and indigenous traditions are excluded because they are supposedly less developed, less universal, or less morally reflective, then Hick must offer an argument. The mere fact that they differ from post-axial religions cannot justify their exclusion, since the whole purpose of pluralism is to explain religious difference without reducing it to error or deficiency.

There is also a danger of evolutionary prejudice. The Axial Age model can imply that history moves from tribal, ritual, and mythic religion toward morally universal and spiritually interior religion. This narrative may privilege literate, urban, centralized, and philosophically articulated traditions over oral, local, ecological, and embodied ones. Yet moral universalism is not absent from indigenous traditions, and post-axial traditions are not free from violence, domination, or ethnocentrism. The capacity for compassion cannot be inferred simply from the presence of scripture or abstract theology.

Moreover, “pre-axial” can be a misleading category. Indigenous traditions have not remained frozen in a prehistoric stage. They have changed in response to colonization, migration, political transformation, and interaction with world religions. Some contain highly developed ethical reflection without the institutional forms that define the Axial paradigm. The categories “pre-axial” and “post-axial” describe historical tendencies, but they should not be used as rankings of spiritual maturity.

Hick’s restriction is therefore unreasonably arbitrary if it claims to define the complete range of authentic religious responses to the Real. It is not necessarily arbitrary if presented as a limited comparative focus. But once indigenous traditions display the same relevant features that Hick values—transcendent encounter, transformation, communal responsibility, and moral fruitfulness—the rationale for excluding them disappears.

## Ethical Fruitfulness as a Modification

One possible modification is to make ethical fruitfulness the primary criterion of religious validity. Rather than asking whether a tradition fits the post-axial model or whether it expresses a particular doctrine of salvation, we could ask whether it reliably generates forms of life characterized by compassion, justice, reconciliation, humility, respect for persons, and care for the more-than-human world.

This modification has several advantages. It preserves Hick’s concern that religious truth should be visible in life rather than confined to metaphysical assertion. It allows indigenous traditions to be assessed according to their own categories of transformation. A vision quest may be religiously valid if it deepens responsibility to the community and the land. An initiation may be valid if it forms a healer who reduces suffering and restores social balance. The criterion becomes sensitive to communal and ecological forms of salvation rather than imposing an individualistic model.

Ethical fruitfulness also provides a basis for criticism within pluralism. Not all religious practices can be accepted simply because they are sincere or culturally important. Traditions can sanction oppression, violence, exclusion, exploitation, or abuse. A pluralism without normative evaluation collapses into relativism. Ethical fruits offer a way to distinguish between life-giving and destructive interpretations of religious experience.

However, ethical fruitfulness must be formulated carefully. It cannot mean conformity to a single modern liberal morality without argument. Nor can it be reduced to social usefulness, since a religion may promote group solidarity while treating outsiders unjustly. The criterion should include both internal and external dimensions: the transformation of persons, the quality of communal relations, the treatment of outsiders, and the relation to the natural world. It should also be historically sensitive. Traditions should be evaluated through their concrete patterns of life and not merely through their ideals.

A further problem is that ethical consequences may be difficult to attribute to religion alone. Social conditions, political institutions, economic practices, and psychological factors also shape behavior. A tradition may contain both liberating and oppressive tendencies. Ethical fruitfulness is therefore better understood as a criterion of interpretation than as a simple empirical test. It asks which interpretations of a religious tradition best embody its orientation toward transcendence and human flourishing.

Most importantly, ethical fruitfulness should not replace transcendent reference entirely. If religion is defined solely by good consequences, secular moral philosophies and political movements could count as religions, while contemplative or mystical traditions whose benefits are less immediately measurable might be excluded. Ethical fruitfulness should function as a practical confirmation or manifestation of a tradition’s orientation toward the Real, not as a substitute for religious experience and transcendent reference.

## An Expanded Formulation of the Real

A second modification concerns Hick’s conception of the Real itself. Hick often speaks as though there is one ultimate reality that appears under personal and non-personal forms. This model helps explain the relationship between theistic and non-theistic religions, but it may be too thin or too abstract to accommodate indigenous traditions.

The Real could be reconceived not as a single metaphysical “thing” behind all religious appearances, but as the inexhaustible depth or source of reality encountered through multiple relational configurations. The Real would not be an object that remains identical across all traditions in a way that makes local spiritual beings merely symbolic masks. It would be the transcendent depth that is disclosed through personal, communal, ecological, ancestral, and cosmic relations. This formulation would allow the Real to be encountered through a sacred landscape, an ancestor, an animal power, a divine person, emptiness, or a ritual pattern of reciprocity.

Such an expansion would preserve Hick’s realism while avoiding an overly monistic picture. It would recognize that some traditions do not distinguish between a transcendent realm and the natural world in the manner presupposed by modern Western theology. For them, transcendence may be immanent in the land, the body, the community, and the cycles of life. The Real is not necessarily “elsewhere.” It can be the depth of the relations in which beings participate.

This proposal raises a question about religious contradiction. If one tradition affirms one God, another affirms many spiritual beings, and another rejects gods altogether, can all these perspectives refer to the Real? Hick’s answer is that they refer to the Real under different categories. But an expanded formulation must clarify whether the plural realities are independent beings, manifestations, symbols, or relational disclosures. It may not be possible to preserve all metaphysical claims literally. Pluralism requires some distinction between the transcendent reality and the particular interpretations that represent it.

The solution may be a form of ontological humility. The Real is not fully captured by either monism or pluralism as ordinarily understood. It may be one in a way that does not exclude relational plurality, or plural in appearance without being reducible to a single object. Human concepts divide the Real into forms that are appropriate to particular ways of life. The point is not that all religious claims are interchangeable, but that no tradition can exhaust the depth to which the claims respond.

This expanded account also makes indigenous traditions philosophically significant rather than merely supplementary. They can correct the tendency to imagine transcendence as detached from the world. They suggest that the Real may be encountered through obligations to place, kinship, ancestors, animals, and future generations. Their contribution would not consist simply in providing additional examples of Hick’s theory. They would require the theory itself to change.

## A Revised Pluralism

A coherent revision of Hick might therefore involve three claims.

First, religious experiences in all cultures can possess prima facie epistemic significance when they present themselves as encounters with a reality beyond ordinary self-consciousness and when they are embedded in disciplined communal practices. Their credibility is defeasible, not absolute. They must be assessed in relation to consistency, testimony, consequences, and alternative explanations. But no tradition should be excluded merely because its experience is oral, ritual, visionary, or ecological rather than doctrinal and textual.

Second, religious validity should be assessed through a pluralized account of transformation. The movement away from self-centredness remains important, but it should not be treated as the only form of salvation. Transformation may involve awakening, reconciliation, healing, covenantal responsibility, liberation from oppression, restored relation to ancestors, or renewed participation in the land. The common structure is not a single psychological state but a reorientation of life toward realities and relationships that exceed the isolated ego.

Third, the Real should be understood as an inexhaustible transcendent depth disclosed through multiple forms of relation. This preserves realism without requiring that every tradition’s language be translated into the metaphysical vocabulary of the major post-axial religions. The Real can be encountered personally, impersonally, ancestrally, ecologically, ritually, and communally. These forms are not automatically equivalent, but they may be authentic and partial disclosures.

Such a revision would transform Hick’s pluralism from a theory of “world religions” into a more comprehensive philosophy of religious diversity. It would also require abandoning the assumption that the Axial Age marks a universal threshold of religious maturity. The Axial transformations remain historically important, especially because they developed forms of moral universalism and critical self-reflection. But they are not the exclusive bearers of transcendence or salvation. Indigenous traditions may preserve insights that post-axial traditions have neglected, particularly concerning ecological interdependence, embodied ritual, and the communal nature of spiritual vocation.

## Conclusion

John Hick’s pluralistic hypothesis offers a compelling alternative to both religious exclusivism and relativism. Its central insight is that the great religious traditions may be understood as culturally diverse responses to one transcendent reality. The Real exceeds every human conceptual scheme, and the plurality of religious forms reflects the plurality of ways in which human beings encounter and interpret transcendence. Hick’s further insistence that authentic religion should transform human life gives pluralism a necessary ethical dimension.

Yet the restriction of this hypothesis to post-axial religions creates a serious problem. It is difficult to reconcile an inclusive theory of transcendent religious experience with the exclusion of traditions whose forms are local, oral, ritual, ecological, or shamanic. Native American vision quests and African shamanic initiations can plausibly be interpreted as encounters with a reality beyond the ordinary ego, and they can produce forms of moral, communal, and spiritual transformation. Their difference from post-axial religions does not show that they lack religious validity.

The tension arises because Hick moves from a broad foundational principle—the possibility that religious experience is grounded in the transcendent—to a narrower soteriological criterion defined primarily as movement from self-centredness to Reality-centredness. That criterion is valuable but insufficiently universal. It reflects the categories of post-axial religions more clearly than those of indigenous traditions. If applied rigidly, it turns the theory into a self-confirming model: traditions count as authentic when they resemble the traditions from which the criterion was derived.

The best response is not to abandon Hick’s realism or his emphasis on transformation. It is to revise both. Ethical fruitfulness should be broadened to include personal, communal, social, and ecological forms of flourishing. The Real should be conceived not merely as a single noumenal reality behind all religious appearances but as an inexhaustible depth disclosed through diverse relations—with God, emptiness, ancestors, land, animals, community, and the cosmos. Religious traditions should be evaluated critically, but none should be excluded merely because it lacks the textual or doctrinal structure of a post-axial world religion.

Hick’s pluralism is thus guilty of unreasonable arbitrariness only if its post-axial restriction is treated as a principled boundary. As a provisional focus, the restriction may be defensible. As a comprehensive account of religious plurality, it is not. The internal coherence of Hick’s project requires a pluralism capacious enough to recognize that transcendence may be mediated not only through universal doctrines and individual conversion, but also through vision, ritual, land, ancestry, healing, and the restoration of relationships. Only then can the hypothesis fully realize its own foundational insight: that the Real is greater than any single form in which human beings have encountered it.
