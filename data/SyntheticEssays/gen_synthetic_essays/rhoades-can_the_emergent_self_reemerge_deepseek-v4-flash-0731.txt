### The Fractured Field: Hiatuses, Identity, and the Incoherence of Hasker’s Emergent Dualism

The problem of personal identity across time is one of philosophy’s most enduring puzzles, but it achieves a particularly acute form when confronted with the possibility of a genuine hiatus in consciousness. Consider the case of a patient who undergoes deep hypothermic circulatory arrest—their heart stopped, their brain electrically silent, their conscious experience utterly extinguished—only to be resuscitated twenty minutes later. Or consider the more speculative scenario of cryonic suspension, where a body is preserved at liquid nitrogen temperatures, its cellular machinery frozen, awaiting a future technology capable of revival. In both cases, there is a period during which no mental activity occurs. Upon revival, the individual exhibits memories, character traits, and a physical continuity with the pre-hiatus person. Yet, was there a continuous subject of experience? Did the *same person* survive the gap, or was a numerically distinct individual created at the moment of revival?

William Hasker’s theory of emergent dualism offers a compelling and sophisticated answer to the mind-body problem, one that seeks to preserve the unity of consciousness and libertarian free will while maintaining a naturalistic (non-Cartesian) ontology. Hasker posits that the conscious self is an emergent field of consciousness, produced by the brain in a manner analogous to a magnetic field produced by a magnet. This field is not reducible to its physical base, yet it is wholly dependent upon it for its existence and continued functioning. However, Hasker’s theory faces a profound challenge when we apply it to the phenomena of hiatuses. The very mechanics of his magnet analogy, which ground his claim that the field of consciousness is the criterion for personal identity, generate an insurmountable trilemma. Confronted with resuscitation, coma, or cryonic suspension, Hasker must either (1) abandon the field of consciousness as the criterion for identity in favor of physical brain continuity, thereby collapsing into a form of physicalism he explicitly rejects; (2) accept that numerical identity is lost across any hiatus in consciousness, leading to absurd ethical and eschatological consequences; or (3) invoke divine intervention to preserve identity, which collapses his naturalistic emergentism into a Cartesian-style interaction problem. Ultimately, an analysis of the causal mechanics of the magnet analogy reveals that none of these horns is satisfactory, rendering Hasker’s emergent dualism incoherent when faced with the reality of interrupted consciousness.

#### I. The Metaphysics of the Emergent Field

To understand the depth of the trilemma, one must first grasp the precise ontology Hasker proposes. In his seminal work, *The Emergent Self*, Hasker argues that the human mind is not a separate, injected soul (as in Cartesian dualism), nor is it merely a set of physical brain states (as in physicalism). Instead, it is an *emergent* entity—a new thing that arises from the complex organization of the brain’s matter. His central analogy is the magnet. A magnet generates a magnetic field, a real physical entity with its own causal powers. The field is not identical to the magnet; it is something over and above the mere arrangement of iron atoms. Yet, the field cannot exist without the magnet. If the magnet is destroyed, the field ceases to exist. Similarly, Hasker argues, the brain generates a "field of consciousness"—a unified, subjective, and qualitative field that constitutes the self. This field is a substance in its own right, possessing causal powers, particularly the power of "top-down" causation, whereby the conscious field can influence the brain’s neural events, thus preserving libertarian free will.

Crucially, Hasker asserts that this field of consciousness is not merely an epiphenomenon or a property. It is the *subject* of experience. When I see a red apple, the redness is a property of my conscious field, and that field is me. Consequently, the criterion for personal identity over time is the continuity of this field. A person at time *t1* is identical to a person at time *t2* if and only if they share the same field of consciousness. This is not a psychological continuity criterion (which could be satisfied by memory), nor a physical continuity criterion (which could be satisfied by the brain), but an ontological continuity criterion. The self is a particular, concrete entity—the field—and its persistence is what constitutes my persistence.

This view is designed to solve several problems. It avoids the "homunculus" problem of Cartesian dualism by grounding the mind in the natural world. It avoids the "binding problem" of physicalism by positing a unified field that integrates disparate sensory inputs. And it preserves free will by giving the field causal power. However, the elegance of this solution relies entirely on the specific mechanics of the generation and persistence of the field. The magnet analogy is not merely a heuristic; it dictates the logical structure of the theory. And it is precisely this structure that proves fatal when we introduce a hiatus.

#### II. The Mechanics of the Magnet and the Problem of Cessation

The magnet analogy dictates that the field of consciousness is *generated* by the brain. This generation is a causal process. Under a deterministic reading of Hasker’s emergentism, the existence and specific character of the field is a function of the brain’s state. If the brain is in state *B1*, it generates field *F1*. If the brain is in state *B2*, it generates field *F2*. The field is supervenient on the brain, though not reducible to it. Under an indeterministic reading—which Hasker favors to secure free will—the field has a degree of causal autonomy, but its *existence* is still contingent on the brain providing the necessary substrate. The brain is the *sine qua non* of the field. Without the brain, there is no field.

Now, consider a hiatus. During clinical death, the brain ceases to function. Electrical activity flatlines. The complex organization of neurons that sustained the field is disrupted. Under Hasker’s model, what happens to the field? The analogy is clear: if you destroy the magnet, the magnetic field vanishes. It does not persist in a latent state; it does not hover in the vicinity awaiting the magnet’s reconstruction. It is gone. The field is an event-like substance, a state of affairs that exists only in relation to its generating base. Therefore, when the brain ceases to function, the field of consciousness ceases to exist. The person, *qua* field, is annihilated.

When the brain is subsequently revived—through CPR, defibrillation, or in the future, rewarming from cryonic suspension—the brain returns to a similar, perhaps even identical, functional state *B1*. According to Hasker’s mechanics, this state *B1* will generate a new field, *F1'*. The crucial question is: is *F1'* numerically identical to the original *F1*?

The answer, based on the logic of causation and identity, must be no. Numerical identity requires continuity of existence. *F1* existed from time *t1* to *t2*, and then ceased to exist. *F1'* begins to exist at time *t3*. There is a temporal gap. An entity that ceases to exist cannot be the same entity as one that begins to exist later, any more than a candle that is extinguished and then relit is the same flame. The flame is numerically distinct because its causal history is different—the second flame is caused by the match, not by the first flame. Similarly, *F1'* is caused by the revived brain, not by the prior existence of *F1*. It is a *de novo* creation. Even if *F1'* is qualitatively identical to *F1*—possessing the same memories, the same personality, the same qualitative feel—it is numerically distinct. Leibniz’s Law of the Identity of Indiscernibles does not apply here because they are *not* indiscernible; they have different temporal properties and different causal origins. *F1* was present at *t2*, while *F1'* was not.

This problem is exacerbated when we consider the indeterministic element Hasker introduces to preserve free will. If the field has genuine causal autonomy, then its specific character is not strictly determined by the brain. The field makes choices, and these choices affect the brain. This means that the field has a unique, historical trajectory. It is a particular individual with a particular causal history. When the brain is destroyed, that historical trajectory is terminated. When a new field is generated, it begins a new trajectory. Even if we could perfectly replicate the brain state, we could not replicate the field’s *history* of free choices, because those choices were contingent and not determined by the brain. The new field would have to start from scratch, lacking the accumulated causal history of the original. It would be a new person, a new subject of experience, with no ontological link to the one who died.

Thus, the mechanical structure of Hasker’s theory—the generation of a field by a base, and the cessation of that field upon the base’s destruction—directly entails that any hiatus in brain function results in the annihilation of the self and the creation of a new, numerically distinct self upon revival. This is not a peripheral objection; it is the logical consequence of the very analogy Hasker uses to define his ontology.

#### III. Horn 1: The Retreat to Physical Brain Continuity

Faced with this devastating consequence, Hasker might attempt to salvage personal identity by shifting the criterion away from the field and towards the physical brain. He could argue that while the field is the *subject* of experience, the *identity* of the person is anchored in the continuity of the physical organism, the brain. In this view, the revived patient is the same person because their brain is the same brain, even though the field of consciousness was destroyed and recreated.

However, this move is fatal to Hasker’s project. If identity is anchored in the brain, then the field of consciousness is demoted to a mere transient state, an epiphenomenon of the brain’s activity. It becomes a property or an event, not a substance. But Hasker explicitly argues that the field is a substance, the subject of experience, and the locus of free will. If the brain is the criterion of identity, then what is the ontological status of the field? Is it merely a phase of the brain? If so, Hasker has collapsed into a form of non-reductive physicalism, or even property dualism, which he spends considerable effort arguing against.

Moreover, this retreat undermines the very motivation for emergent dualism. Hasker’s primary goal is to preserve the unity of consciousness and libertarian free will. If the field is not the self, but merely a product of the brain that can be destroyed and recreated without affecting personal identity, then the field’s causal powers become suspect. If the brain is the criterion of identity, then it is the brain that persists, and the field is just a tool it uses. But how can a transient field exert top-down causal influence on the persistent brain in a way that constitutes *my* free will? The free will would belong to the brain, not the field. This collapses into a compatibilist or even epiphenomenalist account, which Hasker rejects.

Furthermore, this horn fails to solve the problem it was meant to address. If the brain is the criterion of identity, then we must ask: what constitutes the continuity of the brain across a hiatus? The brain is a collection of atoms that are constantly being replaced. Over a lifetime, the brain’s matter is entirely exchanged. If identity is anchored in the brain, it must be anchored in its *form* or *organization*, not its matter. But if the brain is destroyed to the point of flatlining, and then revived, is it the same organization? In the case of cryonics, the brain is frozen, which damages cellular structures. Upon rewarming, the brain is not in the exact same state as before; it has been altered. Does this alteration break identity? If we require exact physical continuity, then even a minor chemical change during a coma would break identity. If we require mere functional continuity, then we are back to a psychological or functional criterion, which Hasker’s ontology of the field was supposed to supersede. The retreat to physicalism does not resolve the trilemma; it merely abandons the field as the criterion, thereby conceding that Hasker’s emergent dualism is not a theory of personal identity at all, but merely a theory of the generation of a phenomenal state.

#### IV. Horn 2: Accepting the Loss of Numerical Identity

The second horn of the trilemma is to accept the logical consequence of the magnet analogy: that numerical identity is lost across any hiatus in consciousness. Hasker could bite the bullet and say, "Yes, if you die and are resuscitated, you are a different person. The original person is dead, and a new person has been created." While this is logically consistent with his mechanics, it is philosophically and theologically catastrophic.

First, consider the ethical implications. If a person is resuscitated after a cardiac arrest, are they morally responsible for crimes committed before the arrest? If they are a new person, then punishing them would be punishing an innocent individual for the actions of another. This would destroy the foundations of retributive justice. Similarly, promises made before the hiatus would be void, contracts dissolved, and marriages annulled, because the person who made those commitments no longer exists. The social fabric would be torn asunder by the simple act of a successful CPR.

Second, consider the problem of sleep and anesthesia. Hasker might argue that sleep does not constitute a hiatus because the brain remains active, generating a continuous field, albeit a dreamless one. But what about deep anesthesia? Under general anesthesia, the brain’s activity is severely suppressed, and consciousness is completely absent. Is the field destroyed? If so, then every surgical procedure results in the death of the patient and the creation of a new person. This is absurd. Hasker would likely respond that the field is not destroyed during anesthesia, but merely "suspended" or "dormant." But this response is ad hoc and unsupported by the magnet analogy. A magnetic field does not become dormant when the magnet is cooled or altered; it either exists or it does not. If the brain is functioning at a level that does not generate a field, then there is no field. Hasker has no mechanism to explain how a field could persist without a functioning base. He cannot have it both ways: either the field is generated by the brain, and ceases when the base is insufficient, or it is a persistent entity independent of the brain, which is Cartesian dualism.

Third, and most critically, this horn destroys any hope of an afterlife or resurrection. Hasker is a Christian philosopher who wishes to reconcile his metaphysics with theological doctrines of bodily resurrection. If the field is annihilated at death, then the person is annihilated. A new field created at the resurrection would be a *new* person, not the same person who lived and died. The promise of eternal life would be empty, as the individual who is resurrected would not be the one who died, but a perfect replica. This is a classic problem of duplication, and Hasker’s emergent dualism has no resources to solve it. He cannot appeal to a soul that survives death, because he rejects the soul. He cannot appeal to the brain, because it decays. He is left with the grim conclusion that death is the absolute end of the individual, and resurrection is a creation of a new individual. This is a conclusion Hasker would find deeply unpalatable, but it is the only one his mechanics allow if he refuses to invoke divine intervention.

#### V. Horn 3: Divine Intervention and the Cartesian Collapse

The third horn, which Hasker might be tempted to take to preserve both his dualism and his theology, is to invoke divine intervention. He could argue that God, in His omnipotence, preserves the field of consciousness during the hiatus, or that God recreates the field with the exact same identity upon revival. This move, however, is fraught with internal contradictions and collapses into the very Cartesianism Hasker sought to avoid.

Let us examine the "preservation" option. If God preserves the field of consciousness during the period of brain death, then the field exists without its generating base. This is a direct violation of the emergentist principle that the field is dependent on the brain. If the field can exist without the brain, then it is not emergent; it is a separate, immaterial substance—a soul. Hasker would be reintroducing Cartesian dualism through the back door. Furthermore, if God preserves the field, how does it interact with the body upon resurrection? The field would have to re-engage with a physical brain that has been dead for years. This is the classic interaction problem. How does a non-physical entity (the preserved field) causally influence a physical entity (the revived brain) without violating the laws of physics? Hasker’s emergent dualism avoided this problem by positing that the field and the brain are intimately connected through the generation process. But if the field is detached and preserved, that connection is severed, and the interaction problem returns in full force.

Now consider the "recreation" option. God could, at the moment of revival, recreate the exact field that existed before the hiatus. But as we established earlier, a recreated entity is numerically distinct from the original. To claim identity, God would have to do more than recreate a similar field; He would have to ensure that the new field is the *same* field. But how can this be? Identity requires causal continuity. If the new field is caused by God’s creative act, not by the prior field, then it is a new entity. Hasker might argue that God can "transfer" the identity, but this is a meaningless metaphysical assertion. Identity is not a property that can be transferred like a baton in a relay race. It is an intrinsic relation of self-sameness. If entity A ceases to exist and entity B begins to exist, B cannot be identical to A simply because God wills it so. God would be creating a perfect duplicate, but a duplicate is not the original. This is the classic problem of the Ship of Theseus or the teletransporter paradox. If I am destroyed in London and a perfect replica is created in New York, I have not survived; I have been replaced.

Moreover, invoking divine intervention to solve this specific problem is deeply ad hoc. It reveals a fundamental flaw in the naturalistic mechanics of emergent dualism. If God has to intervene at every moment of unconsciousness to preserve or recreate identity, then the theory is no longer a naturalistic account of the mind. It becomes a supernaturalist account, where the emergence of the field is merely a front for divine action. This undermines the scientific and philosophical credibility that Hasker claims for his view. It also creates a new problem: if God can preserve the field, why does He allow it to be destroyed in the first place? Why not just make the field inherently immortal? The invocation of divine intervention is a desperate measure that highlights the incoherence of the underlying ontology rather than solving it.

#### VI. Synthesis: The Inescapable Incoherence

Having traversed the trilemma, we find that each horn is impaled. Horn 1 (retreat to physicalism) abandons the core claim of emergent dualism, rendering the field a mere epiphenomenon and collapsing into the very physicalism Hasker critiques. Horn 2 (accepting loss of identity) is logically consistent but leads to absurd ethical consequences, the dissolution of personal responsibility, and the annihilation of any hope for an afterlife. Horn 3 (divine intervention) violates the naturalistic emergence principle, reintroduces Cartesian interaction problems, and relies on an ad hoc supernatural mechanism that cannot establish numerical identity.

The root of this incoherence lies in Hasker’s failure to reconcile the *substantial* nature of the self with the *event-like* nature of the field. A substance, by definition, is a persistent entity that can undergo change while remaining the same. Hasker wants the field to be a substance—the subject of experience—but he grounds its existence in a generative process that makes it fragile and contingent. The magnet analogy is instructive precisely because it shows the flaw. A magnetic field is not a substance; it is a state of space. It does not persist through the destruction of its source. Hasker’s field of consciousness, if it is truly generated by the brain, suffers the same fate. It is a state of the brain, not a persistent entity. To make it a substance, Hasker must either give it independent existence (which he rejects) or give it a mode of persistence that survives the cessation of its base (which the analogy does not allow).

The phenomena of hiatuses—resuscitation, coma, cryonic suspension—are not edge cases that can be ignored. They are the ultimate test of any theory of personal identity. A theory that cannot account for a temporary cessation of consciousness is a theory that cannot account for death, and death is the one certainty we all face. Hasker’s emergent dualism fails this test. It offers a beautiful and intuitive picture of the mind as a field, but the field is too fragile to carry the weight of personal identity. When the brain is silent, the self is gone, and what returns is a stranger wearing the same face. The coherence of emergent dualism is shattered by the very hiatuses that challenge our deepest intuitions about who we are. Hasker cannot have his emergent field and his persistent self too. He must choose, and each choice leads to ruin.

In conclusion, the tension between the field as the criterion of identity and the mechanics of the magnet analogy is insurmountable. The trilemma is exhaustive and each horn is fatal. Hasker’s emergent dualism, despite its initial appeal, ultimately provides no coherent account of how a person can survive a hiatus in consciousness. It either reduces to physicalism, dissolves into a radical discontinuity of the self, or collapses into a Cartesian dualism propped up by divine intervention. The field of consciousness, it turns out, is not a bridge over the abyss of unconsciousness; it is a fragile bubble that pops the moment the brain stops, leaving behind only the empty promise of a self that never truly persists.
