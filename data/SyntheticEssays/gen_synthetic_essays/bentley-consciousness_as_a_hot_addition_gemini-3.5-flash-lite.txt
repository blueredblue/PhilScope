# The Architecture of Awareness: A Critique of Higher-Order Theories and the Sufficiency of Object Awareness

## Introduction

The nature of phenomenal consciousness—what Thomas Nagel famously characterized as *what it is like* to be a subject undergoing a mental state—remains one of the most recalcitrant enigmas in philosophy of mind. Within analytic philosophy, theories of consciousness are broadly divided into first-order approaches, which locate the qualitative character of a mental state within its intrinsic or representational properties, and higher-order approaches, which argue that a mental state is conscious only in virtue of being the target of a suitable higher-order mental state. 

Among higher-order theories (HOTs), the HOT model advanced by philosophers such as David Rosenthal has achieved considerable prominence. Rosenthal’s core thesis is that for a mental state to possess intransitive consciousness—that is, for it to be a conscious mental state—it must be accompanied by a higher-order thought directed at it. This model introduces a demanding structural architecture to the mind, distinguishing between first-order awareness of objects or properties in the world and higher-order awareness of the mental states themselves.

A pivotal question in this debate concerns the precise taxonomy of awareness. Following Fred Dretske’s foundational distinction, philosophy must differentiate between *object awareness* (awareness of an entity or thing) and *fact awareness* (awareness that a state of affairs obtains). Applying this distinction to the architecture of consciousness, some theorists argue that state consciousness requires both object awareness and fact awareness. Others, most notably Derek Browne, have launched a formidable critique against this dual-requirement view. Browne contends that object awareness alone is sufficient to confer consciousness upon a mental state, and that the addition of fact awareness—operationalized as a higher-order thought—is entirely unnecessary.

This essay evaluates the claim that state consciousness requires both object awareness and fact awareness, with a specific focus on whether a higher-order thought (understood as fact awareness) is strictly necessary alongside direct acquaintance. I will analyze Derek Browne’s objection, which leverages Dretske’s taxonomy to argue that object awareness is sufficient for consciousness. Following this, I will assess standard counterexamples in the literature—specifically, visual blindsight and the phenomenon of unconscious driving—to determine whether object awareness alone can indeed render a mental state conscious or whether it collapses into unconscious sub-personal processing without the addition of a suitable HOT. Ultimately, I will defend the thesis that while object awareness is a necessary component of sensory-perceptual consciousness, it is not *sufficient*. State consciousness is fundamentally a higher-order phenomenon, requiring the conceptual framework of fact awareness to elevate raw representation into the luminous domain of subjective experience.

## The Taxonomy of Awareness: Dretske, Browne, and the HOT Framework

To evaluate the structural requirements of state consciousness, we must first unpack the terminology of awareness. Fred Dretske, in his influential work on perception and knowledge, drew a sharp and enduring distinction between two modes of awareness: *simple awareness* (or *object awareness*) and *epistemic awareness* (or *fact awareness*). 

Object awareness is non-conceptual, direct, and non-propositional. To be object-aware of something—say, a red apple on a desk—is simply for that object to stand in a certain perceptual relation to one's sensory apparatus. It does not require that the subject possess the concepts "red," "apple," or "desk," nor does it require that the subject entertain any proposition about the object. A cat or a human infant can be object-aware of the apple in this robust, perceptual sense. 

Fact awareness, by contrast, is propositional, conceptual, and cognitive. To be fact-aware is to be aware *that* something is the case. For example, to be fact-aware of the apple is to be in a mental state that represents the proposition *that the apple is red and resting on the desk*. Fact awareness requires conceptual capacities; one cannot be aware that an object is a desk unless one possesses the concept of a desk.

When we map this distinction onto the philosophy of mind, particularly the debate surrounding intransitive consciousness (the property of a mental state being a conscious mental state, as opposed to transitive consciousness, which is a subject being conscious *of* an object), a crucial theoretical fault line emerges. 

Higher-order thought theories, pioneered by David Rosenthal, posit that a mental state $M$ is conscious if and only if the subject has a higher-order thought to the effect that they are currently in mental state $M$. In Rosenthal's formulation, this HOT is paradigmatically propositional and conceptual. It is an internal judgment or thought of the form: *I am currently in mental state M* (or, more specifically, *I am seeing a red patch*). Consequently, within the HOT framework, state consciousness is fundamentally a matter of *fact awareness*. The first-order mental state—say, a visual sensation of red—provides the raw sensory representation (which we might describe as object awareness of the red color property), but this first-order state is unconscious in itself. It becomes conscious *only* when a higher-order thought makes the subject fact-aware of the occurrence of that first-order mental state.

However, this architecture invites a severe challenge. If first-order states are merely passive representational vehicles, why should the addition of a propositional, higher-order thought be required to "illuminate" them? Why can't the intrinsic properties of the first-order state, or the direct object awareness it affords, suffice for consciousness?

This is precisely the vector of Derek Browne’s critique. Browne argues that the orthodox higher-order requirement of fact awareness is an overcomplication born of a faulty philosophy of mind. Drawing directly on Dretske’s taxonomy, Browne contends that object awareness is not only necessary for sensory consciousness; it is *sufficient*. On Browne's view, when a subject visually experiences a red object, the subject has object awareness of the red surface. This object awareness *is* the conscious mental state. The invocation of a higher-order thought (fact awareness) to make this state conscious is redundant, conceptually problematic, and phenomenologically unsupported. For Browne, consciousness does not require a meta-representational spotlight cast by a HOT; the light is already on in virtue of the first-order state's object-directedness.

## Derek Browne’s Objection: Object Awareness as Sufficient for Consciousness

To fully appreciate Browne’s objection, we must examine the mechanics of his argument against higher-order theories. Browne targets the central motivation behind HOTs: the explanatory gap between physical/functional processing and phenomenal experience. HOT theorists argue that a purely first-order state—such as the activation of early visual cortex neurons representing edge orientations—can occur entirely in the dark, devoid of any subjective feel. To cross the threshold into consciousness, the system must become aware of this state.

Rosenthal and other HOT theorists claim that this awareness must be higher-order and conceptual. But Browne counters that this assumes an untenable dichotomy between unconscious mechanical processing and conscious propositional thought. According to Browne, sensory states are inherently conscious because they put the subject in direct relation to objects and their sensible properties. 

In developing his position, Browne utilizes Dretske’s distinction to reframe the debate. He argues that the error of the HOT theorist lies in misidentifying the kind of awareness required for state consciousness. The HOT theorist assumes that because consciousness involves a subjective "knowing" or "presence," it must be modeled on epistemic fact awareness (*knowing that*). But perceptual consciousness, Browne insists, is fundamentally rooted in *object awareness* (*knowing of* or *being acquainted with*).

Consider a visual experience of a sunset. According to Browne, the phenomenal character of this experience—the blazing oranges, the fading purples, the gradient of the horizon—consists in the subject being object-aware of these chromatic properties. The mental state is a conscious state because its representational content is object-directed. It presents the sensible qualities of the external world directly to the subject. 

Why, then, does Browne deem fact awareness (and by extension, the HOT) unnecessary? 
1. **Redundancy and Regress:** If a first-order state already presents an object with qualitative properties to the subject, adding a higher-order thought (*I am now having a visual experience of a sunset*) adds an extra layer of cognitive processing. If this higher-order thought itself needs to be conscious to perform its illuminating function, we are immediately launched into an infinite regress of higher-order thoughts. If the HOT does *not* need to be conscious, it is mysterious how an unconscious thought could confer consciousness upon an otherwise unconscious first-order state.
2. **Phenomenological Immediacy:** When we are absorbed in an experience—listening to a symphony or feeling the warmth of a fire—our attention is directed outward toward the music or the warmth (object awareness), not inward toward our own mental states (fact awareness). Indeed, as introspection reveals, focusing on our mental states often disrupts or alters the raw phenomenological character of the experience. To insist that a background HOT is constantly tracking our mental states is to impose a hyper-reflective, Cartesian ego structure upon minds that are fundamentally tuned to the external environment.
3. **Phylogenetic and Ontogenetic Plausibility:** Many non-human animals and pre-linguistic human infants clearly possess rich sensory consciousness. They feel pain, see colors, and hear sounds. Yet, it is highly contentious whether they possess the complex conceptual architecture required for higher-order thoughts (i.e., the capacity to represent one's own mental states *as* mental states). If state consciousness required fact awareness via a HOT, we would be forced to deny consciousness to infants and animals—a consequence many philosophers find deeply counterintuitive.

Thus, Browne’s objection concludes that object awareness is entirely self-sufficient. A mental state is conscious if it successfully establishes a direct, acquaintance-based relation to an object or its properties, rendering further fact awareness superfluous.

## The Counterexamples: Blindsight, Unconscious Driving, and the Necessity of the HOT

To rigorously evaluate whether object awareness alone is sufficient or whether fact awareness (via a HOT) is strictly necessary, we must test Browne's thesis against complex empirical phenomena where object awareness and fact awareness diverge. Two classic test cases dominate this literature: **visual blindsight** and **unconscious driving** (or automatism).

### Visual Blindsight

Blindsight is a neurological condition resulting from damage to the primary visual cortex (V1). Patients with blindsight report being completely blind in a specific region of their visual field (a scotoma). When stimuli are presented within this blind field, the patients honestly report seeing nothing. However, if forced to guess (or "blindly point") at the location, orientation, or motion of the stimulus, their forced-choice responses are remarkably accurate—often well above chance.

How do we analyze blindsight through the lens of object awareness and fact awareness?
* **The First-Order/Object Awareness Interpretation:** A blindsight patient processes visual information from the scotoma. Their visual system detects edges, directions of motion, and even wavelengths. Does the patient have *object awareness* of the stimulus? In a deflationary, information-processing sense, yes: the visual system registers the object's properties and guides motor behavior accordingly. Yet, the patient vehemently denies any visual experience. There is *what it is like* to be in the blindsight state.
* **The HOT Interpretation:** For the HOT theorist, blindsight provides a striking confirmation of their model. The patient possesses first-order visual representations (and arguably, a low-level form of functional object registration), but they entirely lack a higher-order thought directed at those representations. Because there is no HOT (no fact awareness *that* one is seeing a stimulus in the scotoma), the first-order visual state remains strictly unconscious. Blindsight demonstrates precisely that first-order object registration—even when it drives accurate behavioral responses—is insufficient for state consciousness.

### Unconscious Driving

Consider the everyday phenomenon of highway hypnosis or unconscious driving: a motorist drives for miles along a familiar route while their mind is entirely absorbed in a complex daydream or argument. Suddenly, they "snap to" and realize they have no memory of the past twenty minutes of driving, despite having successfully navigated traffic, changed lanes, and braked for obstacles.

* **The Object/First-Order Analysis:** During this episode, the driver’s visual and motor systems were intensely active. They were object-aware of the road, other cars, and traffic signs in order to execute precise driving maneuvers. Browne and first-order theorists would argue that the driver *was* conscious during those miles—they had sensory experiences of the road, but these experiences lacked robust attention, memory consolidation, or higher-order reflection. 
* **The HOT Analysis:** The HOT theorist counters that the driving behavior was executed via unconscious sensorimotor routines. While the visual and motor cortices processed environmental inputs (functioning as a sophisticated biological autopilot), the subject lacked higher-order thoughts monitoring these specific perceptual states at the time of occurrence. The absence of a HOT explains why there was no phenomenal awareness of the drive and why the episodes are absent from autobiographical memory. The driving was unconscious because it lacked fact awareness.

### Assessing the Counterexamples

Do these cases vindicate the HOT or support Browne's object awareness view? 

Let us examine Browne’s potential reply to blindsight. A defender of object awareness might argue that blindsight *does* involve a very degraded, marginal form of consciousness—perhaps a "cold" awareness where the subject registers the object without the qualitative "heat" usually associated with normal vision. However, this move strains credulity. Blindsight patients do not merely report a dim or fuzzy experience; they report a total absence of visual phenomenological presence. The qualitative difference between seeing something and blind-pointing at something is absolute, not merely a matter degree.

Furthermore, consider the architecture of visual processing. In blindsight, subcortical pathways (such as the superior colliculus and the pulvinar nucleus of the thalamus) route visual information directly to motor areas without passing through the damaged striate cortex and the frontoparietal networks associated with metacognition and working memory. This anatomical dissociation maps impeccably onto the distinction between first-order processing and higher-order access. 

If first-order object registration can occur completely in the absence of any subjective experience (as in blindsight), then object awareness alone cannot be sufficient for state consciousness. Something more is required to bridge the gap between mere physiological registration of an object and the subjective manifestation of *what it is like*. That "something more" is precisely what the higher-order thought provides: it elevates the blind, non-conscious representation into the light of conscious awareness by making the subject *fact-aware* of their own mental state.

## Defending the Higher-Order Thesis: Why Fact Awareness is Strictly Necessary

Having scrutinized Browne’s object-awareness sufficiency thesis and evaluated the empirical pressures of blindsight and unconscious driving, I will now defend the thesis that consciousness is fundamentally a higher-order addition—specifically, that fact awareness (operationalized as a HOT) is strictly necessary alongside direct acquaintance to confer intransitive consciousness upon a mental state.

### 1. The Explanatory Demands of Subjectivity

The core difficulty of the mind-body problem is explaining how physical systems can generate subjective experience. A purely first-order representational theory—even one enriched with Browne's notion of direct object awareness—struggles to articulate *why* representation should feel like anything. 

Representation is ubiquitous in the natural world. A thermometer represents ambient temperature; a DNA molecule represents amino acid sequences; a security camera represents the perimeter of a building. None of these systems are phenomenally conscious. To move from mere representation (even complex, multi-modal, first-order representation) to *phenomenal consciousness*, a qualitative shift must occur. 

The higher-order thought model provides a brilliantly parsimonious solution to this problem. A mental state does not become conscious in virtue of its intrinsic physical or representational complexity; it becomes conscious in virtue of its *epistemic status* relative to the subject. By taking a first-order mental state as the intentional object of a higher-order thought, the mind establishes a reflexive loop. The subject becomes aware *that* they are in that state. This meta-representational framing introduces the subjective perspective. It transforms an objective processing event into a subjective experience by embedding it within the subject's conscious self-model.

### 2. The Metacognitive Nature of Consciousness

Human consciousness is deeply intertwined with metacognition—the capacity to monitor, evaluate, and reflect upon one's own cognitive and perceptual states. While animals and infants may occupy a more primitive rung of this ladder, the structural continuity of the mind suggests that phenomenal consciousness and higher-order monitoring share a common architectural root.

Consider the phenomenon of pain. A first-order theory views pain as a sensory state representing bodily damage. But as many philosophers have noted, what makes pain conscious is not merely the physiological detection of tissue damage (which can occur unconsciously, as in congenital insensitivity to pain or during deep shock), but the affective, distressing dimension. Within a higher-order framework, pain becomes conscious when the subject is introspectively aware of being in that aversive sensory state. The HOT (*I am experiencing a sharp, distressing pain*) binds the raw sensory signal to the subject's conscious ego, rendering it a phenomenally potent experience.

### 3. Addressing the Regress Objection

A standard objection to HOT theories, echoed in Browne's critique, is the threat of an infinite regress: if a mental state $M_1$ requires a higher-order thought $M_2$ to be conscious, must $M_2$ itself be conscious? And if so, does $M_2$ require an $M_3$, ad infinitum?

Rosenthal handles this objection by making a vital distinction: a mental state does not need to be a *conscious* mental state in order to make another mental state conscious. An unconscious higher-order thought can confer intransitive consciousness upon a first-order state. The HOT itself is simply a mental state that represents another mental state. It achieves its function not by being endlessly reflected upon, but by virtue of its representational content. Thus, the regress is cleanly severed. 

Furthermore, we experience this in everyday cognition all the time. We can be aware of our surroundings or our internal states without needing to enter an infinite loop of meta-metacognition. The HOT operates as a background mental state that casts a subjective glow over the first-order representation, without requiring an endless stack of subsequent thoughts.

---

## Conclusion

The debate between first-order defenders of object awareness and proponents of higher-order fact awareness cuts to the very heart of philosophy of mind. Derek Browne’s ambitious objection, drawing upon Fred Dretske’s elegant taxonomy, challenges us to reconsider whether the mind requires a complex, meta-representational machinery to experience the world. Browne argues that direct object awareness—the unmediated acquaintance with objects and their sensible properties—is fully sufficient for state consciousness, rendering higher-order thoughts redundant and phenomenologically ungrounded.

However, when we interrogate this claim against empirical pathologies like visual blindsight and everyday anomalies like unconscious driving, the cracks in the object-awareness sufficiency thesis become visible. Blindsight demonstrates unequivocally that complex first-order object registration and sensorimotor guidance can occur in total, unmitigated darkness, devoid of any subjective feel. Object awareness, in the absence of higher-order cognitive tracking, collapses into sub-personal information processing.

Therefore, we must conclude that object awareness alone is insufficient. State consciousness requires both the raw material of direct acquaintance (object awareness) and the structural elevation provided by the conceptual framework of fact awareness (the higher-order thought). By synthesizing these two modes of awareness, the higher-order model successfully explains how objective physical processes are transformed into the luminous, subjective tapestry of human experience. Consciousness is not merely a passive mirror reflecting the world; it is an active mind reflecting upon its own reflection.
