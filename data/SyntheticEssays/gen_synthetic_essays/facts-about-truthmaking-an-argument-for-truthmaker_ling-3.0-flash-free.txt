# Truthmaker Necessitarianism and the Problem of Higher-Order Truthmaking: A Dialectical Analysis

## I. Introduction

The truthmaker principle stands as one of the most enduring and influential theses in contemporary metaphysics. In its canonical form, it asserts that every truth requires something in reality—some entity, state of affairs, or fact—that makes it true. This principle, in its various formulations, has served as a powerful constraint on ontological theorizing, promising to discipline metaphysical speculation by tying every claim about what exists to something that grounds its truth. Yet beneath this seemingly straightforward principle lies a deep and persistent dialectical tension concerning the nature of the truthmakers themselves. Specifically, must truthmakers be necessary beings, or may they be contingent entities? This question—whether truthmaker theory requires a necessitarian commitment—has generated substantial philosophical debate, and its implications extend into the most fundamental questions about the structure of reality and the nature of grounding.

The thesis of truthmaker necessitarianism, most prominently associated with the work of David Armstrong, holds that truthmakers must be necessary entities. Only necessary states of affairs, on this view, can serve as the ontological foundations of truth, providing stable and non-circular grounds for the truths they ground. The non-necessitarian tradition, by contrast, permits contingent entities to function as truthmakers, arguing that the truthmaker principle need not impose a necessity constraint on its bearers. This essay evaluates the thesis of truthmaker necessitarianism by examining a particularly acute problem for non-necessitarians: the question of how they account for the truthmakers of higher-order truthmaking facts. In doing so, it analyzes the dialectical tension between Armstrong's formulation of truthmaker necessitarianism and the non-necessitarian rejection of necessitating truthmakers for universal generalizations and negative existentials, ultimately assessing whether non-necessitarians inevitably face an infinite regress of contingent truthmakers or must collapse into ontological commitments equivalent to those of the necessitarian orthodoxy.

## II. Armstrong's Formulation of Truthmaker Necessitarianism

David Armstrong's truthmaker theory is grounded in a robustly realist ontology of states of affairs. For Armstrong, states of affairs are the fundamental ontological constituents of reality—uninstantiated as well as instantiated—and they serve as the truthmakers for all true propositions. A proposition that "S is F" is true if and only if the state of affairs of S's being F exists. This is a powerful and elegant thesis, but it is the further claim about the necessity of these truthmakers that constitutes Armstrong's necessitarian commitment.

Armstrong's necessitarianism emerges from his broader commitment to a Humean principle of universals and a strong realism about the ontology of properties and relations. The states of affairs that serve as truthmakers are themselves constituted by universals instantiated in particulars. Since universals, on Armstrong's view, are necessary entities—they exist necessarily and are not contingent in their existence—the states of affairs they compose are also necessary in their being. The truthmaker for any true proposition, then, is a necessary state of affairs. This is not merely an empirical observation about which states of affairs happen to exist; it is a metaphysical requirement of the truthmaker principle itself.

The rationale for this necessitarian commitment is subtle but important. Armstrong argues that if contingent entities served as truthmakers for truths, then the truth of those propositions would depend on the existence of contingent entities in a way that threatens the stability and objectivity of truth itself. If a truthmaker could fail to exist, then the truth it grounds would become false, or worse, the truth would lack any ground at all. More fundamentally, Armstrong contends that contingent truthmakers would generate a circular or regressive problem: contingent truthmakers themselves would require truthmakers, and if those truthmakers were also contingent, the chain would never reach a stable foundation. Necessity, on this picture, provides the terminus of the grounding chain—the necessary state of affairs that grounds contingent truths without itself requiring grounding.

It is important to note that Armstrong's necessitarianism is not merely a thesis about the ontological status of truthmakers; it is also a thesis about the direction of explanation. Truthmakers explain truths, and necessary truthmakers explain contingent truths without themselves needing explanation. The necessity of the truthmaker is what licenses the explanatory priority of the grounded over the grounder. Without necessitarianism, the explanatory structure of truthmaker theory becomes vulnerable to charges of circularity or infinite regress.

## III. The Non-Necessitarian Rejection and Its Motivations

Non-necessitarians reject the claim that truthmakers must be necessary entities. This rejection is motivated by several considerations. First, there is the intuitive appeal of allowing the truthmaker for a contingent truth to itself be contingent. If the truth that "Socrates is mortal" is made true by Socrates's being mortal, it seems natural to suppose that Socrates himself—contingent, perishable, existing only in certain possible worlds—serves as the truthmaker. To require that Socrates be a necessary entity in order to serve as his own truthmaker would seem to introduce an implausible metaphysical commitment that has no independent motivation.

Second, non-necessitarians often appeal to a broader grounding-theoretic framework in which grounding relations themselves are not restricted to necessary facts. In contemporary grounding debates, philosophers such as Jonathan Schaffer and others have argued that grounding is a non-causal explanatory relation that can hold between contingent facts. If grounding can hold between contingent facts, then there is no need to restrict truthmakers to necessary entities. The contingent fact that the cat is on the mat can be grounded in the contingent fact that the cat is situated in a certain spatial relation to the mat, and neither fact requires necessity as a condition of its serving as a truthmaker or a ground.

Third, non-necessitarians often point to the problem of universal generalizations and negative existentials as cases where necessitarianism generates implausible ontological commitments. Consider the universal generalization "All ravens are black." On Armstrong's necessitarian account, the truthmaker for this universal generalization would need to be a necessary state of affairs. But what could this be? The state of affairs of every raven's being black seems to depend on the contingent existence of ravens and the contingent fact that they are black. To render this a necessary state of affairs, one would need to posit necessary universals and necessary relations in a way that may seem metaphysically extravagant. The non-necessitarian can simply allow that this universal generalization is made true by the contingent fact that all actual ravens are black—or, more sophisticatedly, by the totality of raven-instances and their blackness—without requiring that this fact be necessary.

Similarly, negative existentials such as "Pegasus does not exist" pose a challenge for necessitarianism. The truthmaker for this negative existential cannot be an instantiated state of affairs, since Pegasus does not exist. Armstrong and other necessitarians have appealed to uninstantiated states of affairs or negative states of affairs as truthmakers, but these entities must themselves be necessary if they are to serve as stable truthmakers. The non-necessitarian can instead appeal to the absence of Pegasus in the actual world as a contingent fact that requires no necessitarian machinery. The absence is itself contingent—Pegasus might have existed—and yet it serves as the truthmaker for the negative existential without requiring necessity.

## IV. The Problem of Higher-Order Truthmaking Facts

The central dialectical tension of this essay concerns a problem that arises when we consider the truthmaker for truthmaking facts themselves. Truthmaking facts are propositions of the form "The truthmaker for p is t." These facts are themselves truths—they describe the relationship between truthmakers and the truths they ground—and therefore, if the truthmaker principle is universal, they require truthmakers of their own. The question is: what kind of entities serve as truthmakers for these higher-order truthmaking facts?

For the necessitarian, this question has a relatively straightforward answer. The truthmaking facts are themselves grounded in necessary states of affairs, and the relationship between a necessary truthmaker and the contingent truth it grounds is itself a necessary fact. The truthmaking relation, on Armstrong's account, is a necessary feature of the ontological structure of reality: states of affairs ground truths in a way that is metaphysically necessary and non-accidental. The truthmaker for the truthmaking fact "The truthmaker for 'Socrates is mortal' is Socrates's being mortal" is the necessary state of affairs of Socrates's being mortal, which itself is a necessary entity in Armstrong's ontology. Higher-order truthmaking facts are thus grounded in the same necessary ontology that grounds first-order truths, and no special problem arises.

For the non-necessitarian, the situation is considerably more complex. If truthmakers can be contingent, then truthmaking facts can also be contingent truths about contingent relationships between contingent entities and contingent truths. But then the truthmaker for these higher-order truthmaking facts must itself be either necessary or contingent. If it is necessary, the non-necessitarian has introduced a necessary entity into their ontology for the specific purpose of grounding truthmaking facts—a move that seems to undermine the motivation for rejecting necessitarianism in the first place. If it is contingent, then the non-necessitarian faces a regress: the contingent truthmaker for the higher-order truthmaking fact requires its own truthmaker, which in turn requires its own truthmaker, and so on indefinitely.

This higher-order problem exposes a fundamental vulnerability in non-necessitarian truthmaker theory. The non-necessitarian's rejection of necessitating truthmakers for first-order truths—universal generalizations, negative existentials, and so forth—seems perfectly reasonable at the level of basic ontological commitments. But when the truthmaker principle is applied to its own outputs, the non-necessitarian finds themselves in a position where they must either accept necessitarian truthmakers for higher-order facts or face an infinite regress of contingent truthmakers. Neither option is comfortable, and both seem to impose ontological costs that challenge the coherence of the non-necessitarian position.

## V. The Dialectical Tension: Universal Generalizations and Negative Existentials

The dialectical tension between Armstrong's necessitarianism and the non-necessitarian rejection of necessitating truthmakers becomes particularly acute when we examine the cases of universal generalizations and negative existentials in light of higher-order truthmaking.

Consider the universal generalization "All Fs are G." The non-necessitarian wants to say that the truthmaker for this generalization is some contingent fact or collection of facts—perhaps the totality of F-instances and their G-ness, or perhaps a contingent universal that is instantiated in all Fs. But now consider the truthmaking fact itself: "The truthmaker for 'All Fs are G' is the totality of F-instances and their G-ness." This is a higher-order truthmaking fact, and it too requires a truthmaker. If the truthmaker for this fact is itself contingent—if it is the same totality of F-instances and their G-ness that grounded the first-order truth—then we have a circularity problem. The truthmaker for the truthmaking fact is the same entity as the truthmaker for the original truth, which means the truthmaker is explaining itself through the truthmaking relation, and the truthmaking relation is itself being explained by the same entity. This is a deeply problematic circularity that threatens the coherence of the non-necessitarian's account.

Alternatively, the non-necessitarian might appeal to a distinct contingent entity as the truthmaker for the higher-order truthmaking fact. But then we have an infinite regress: the truthmaker for the first-order truth is contingent, the truthmaker for the truthmaking fact is contingent, the truthmaker for the truthmaker of the truthmaking fact is contingent, and so on. Each contingent truthmaker requires its own truthmaker, and there is no necessary entity to serve as a terminus.

The case of negative existentials is structurally similar but raises additional difficulties. The non-necessitarian might claim that the truthmaker for "Pegasus does not exist" is the contingent fact of Pegasus's absence from reality. But the truthmaking fact "The truthmaker for 'Pegasus does not exist' is the fact of Pegasus's absence" requires its own truthmaker. If the truthmaker is the absence itself, we face the same circularity problem. If the truthmaker is a distinct contingent fact about absence, we face the regress problem. And if the non-necessitarian appeals to a necessary entity—such as a negative state of affairs or a uninstantiated universal—to serve as the truthmaker for higher-order negative existential truths, they have effectively reintroduced necessitarian commitments into their ontology.

Armstrong's necessitarianism handles these cases more elegantly, precisely because it does not face the tension between first-order and higher-order truthmaking. For Armstrong, the truthmaker for any truth—first-order or higher-order—is a necessary state of affairs, and the truthmaking relation itself is a necessary feature of the ontological structure of reality. Universal generalizations are made true by necessary states of affairs involving universals; negative existentials are made true by uninstantiated states of affairs, which are also necessary. Higher-order truthmaking facts are made true by the same necessary ontology that grounds first-order truths, and the regress is arrested at the level of necessary states of affairs.

## VI. The Infinite Regress Problem for Non-Necessitarians

The infinite regress problem for non-necessitarians deserves careful examination, as it represents the most serious objection to their position. If truthmakers can be contingent, and if every truth requires a truthmaker, then the truthmaker for a contingent truth is contingent, the truthmaker for the truthmaking fact is contingent, and so on. The chain of truthmakers never terminates in a necessary entity, and each link in the chain requires its own further explanation.

Non-necessitarians have developed several strategies to resist this regress. The first strategy is to appeal to foundational contingent truths or brute facts. On this view, some contingent truths are simply ungrounded—they have truthmakers, but those truthmakers do not themselves require truthmakers. The truthmaker for a foundational contingent truth is brute and unexplained, serving as a terminus of the grounding chain. This strategy is philosophically defensible, particularly in the context of contemporary grounding debates, where some philosophers argue that not every fact needs a further ground. However, it comes at the cost of accepting brute ontological facts, which many metaphysicians find deeply unsatisfying.

The second strategy is to appeal to the totality of contingent truths as a collective truthmaker. On this view, the totality of all contingent truths and their truthmakers forms a unified ground that explains itself without requiring further grounding. This strategy has been defended by philosophers who argue that the totality can serve as a non-factive truthmaker—it is the totality that grounds the truths, not any individual contingent entity within it. However, this strategy faces the objection that it is unclear how a collection of contingent entities can function as a necessary truthmaker. If the totality is contingent—i.e., if it might have been otherwise—then it cannot serve as a stable ground for necessary truths or for the truthmaking facts that describe the grounding relations themselves.

The third strategy is to collapse the distinction between first-order and higher-order truthmaking facts, treating truthmaking as a holistic or coherentist relation rather than a linearly ordered grounding relation. On this view, truthmaking facts do not require separate truthmakers; rather, the truthmaking relation is a fundamental feature of reality that holds among truths and their truthmakers in a mutually supporting network. This strategy avoids the regress but at the cost of abandoning the grounding-theoretic structure that makes truthmaker theory philosophically interesting in the first place. If truthmaking is not a hierarchical explanatory relation, then truthmaker theory becomes a merely correlational or coherence-based account of truth, which is a very different kind of thesis.

Each of these strategies has significant philosophical costs, and none fully escapes the tension that the higher-order truthmaking problem generates for non-necessitarianism. The foundationalist strategy introduces brute facts that many metaphysicians reject; the totality strategy struggles with the contingency of the totality; and the coherentist strategy abandons the grounding structure that distinguishes truthmaker theory from alternative accounts of truth.

## VII. Collapse into Necessitarian Orthodoxy?

The question that emerges from this analysis is whether non-necessitarians inevitably face an infinite regress or must collapse into ontological commitments equivalent to those of the necessitarian orthodoxy. The answer, I will argue, is that non-necessitarians face a dilemma in which neither horn is stable, and that the most plausible non-necessitarian strategies either implicitly adopt necessitarian commitments or accept ontological costs that are equivalent to those of the necessitarian position.

Consider the non-necessitarian who attempts to account for higher-order truthmaking facts by introducing necessary truthmakers specifically for those facts. This move effectively concedes the necessitarian thesis for higher-order truths while maintaining non-necessitarianism for first-order truths. But this hybrid position is unstable: if higher-order truthmaking facts require necessary truthmakers, then the truthmaking relation itself is partly constituted by necessary entities and necessary facts. This means that the structure of truthmaking is not purely contingent but has a necessary core—a core that serves as the foundation for all truthmaking, including the truthmaking of first-order contingent truths. The non-necessitarian who introduces necessary truthmakers for higher-order facts has thereby collapsed into a form of necessitarianism, even if they deny the label.

Alternatively, consider the non-necessitarian who refuses to introduce necessary truthmakers for higher-order facts and instead appeals to brute contingent truthmakers or foundational contingent truths. This position avoids the necessitarian commitment but accepts brute facts at the foundation of the ontological structure. The question is whether this position is genuinely distinct from necessitarianism or whether it is equivalent to it in substance. If the brute contingent truthmakers function as termini of explanation—entities that require no further grounding—then they are serving a role structurally identical to that of necessary truthmakers in Armstrong's theory. The difference is merely that the terminus is contingent rather than necessary, but the explanatory function is the same. In this sense, the non-necessitarian who accepts brute contingent truthmakers has adopted an ontological commitment equivalent to necessitarianism, differing only in the modal status of the foundational entities.

The coherentist or holistic non-necessitarian who treats truthmaking as a non-hierarchical relation faces a different but equally serious problem. If truthmaking is not a grounding relation that proceeds from truthmakers to truths in a directional manner, then truthmaker theory loses its explanatory force. The truthmaker principle, in its standard formulation, is a grounding thesis: it claims that truthmakers explain truths in a specific direction. If this directionality is abandoned, then truthmaker theory becomes a merely structural or formal thesis about the correspondence between truths and entities, which is a significantly weaker claim than the necessitarian or non-necessitarian versions of the theory. The non-necessitarian who adopts a coherentist truthmaking theory has effectively abandoned the grounding-theoretic ambitions of truthmaker theory and retreated to a weaker ontological thesis that does not engage the higher-order problem in a substantive way.

## VIII. Conclusion

The dialectical tension between truthmaker necessitarianism and non-necessitarianism, particularly as it manifests in the problem of higher-order truthmaking facts, reveals deep structural challenges for both positions. Armstrong's truthmaker necessitarianism offers an elegant and unified account in which necessary states of affairs serve as truthmakers for all truths, including higher-order truthmaking facts, and the regress of truthmakers is arrested at the level of necessary ontology. The cost of this elegance is a robust commitment to the necessity of states of affairs and universals—a commitment that many metaphysicians find metaphysically extravagant and independently unmotivated.

Non-necessitarianism, by contrast, offers a more modest ontology in which contingent entities can serve as truthmakers for contingent truths, avoiding the need for necessary universals and uninstantiated states of affairs. But this modesty comes at a steep price when the truthmaker principle is applied to its own outputs. The problem of higher-order truthmaking facts forces non-necessitarians into a dilemma: either they introduce necessary truthmakers for higher-order facts, thereby collapsing into necessitarianism, or they accept an infinite regress of contingent truthmakers or brute contingent foundations that are structurally equivalent to necessitarian termini. Neither option preserves the distinctive motivation of non-necessitarianism, and both impose ontological costs that challenge the coherence of the position.

The most plausible conclusion is that truthmaker necessitarianism possesses a dialectical advantage over non-necessitarianism in virtue of its ability to provide a unified account of truthmaking across all levels of ontological complexity. The non-necessitarian who wishes to avoid both infinite regress and collapse into necessitarian orthodoxy must develop a conception of truthmaking that is either non-hierarchical in a way that preserves explanatory force or that accepts a form of foundationalism that is indistinguishable from necessitarianism in its structural commitments. Until such a conception is developed, the thesis of truthmaker necessitarianism remains the most coherent and defensible version of truthmaker theory, even if its ontological costs are substantial. The problem of higher-order truthmaking facts thus serves not merely as a technical challenge within the truthmaker literature but as a deep structural constraint on any theory that seeks to ground truth in being without collapsing into either infinite regress or necessitarian orthodoxy.
