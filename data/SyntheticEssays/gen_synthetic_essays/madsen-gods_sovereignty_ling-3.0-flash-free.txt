The philosophical trajectory of Augustine of Hippo represents one of the most profound and consequential intellectual evolutions in Western thought. Nowhere is this evolution more starkly delineated than in his shifting perspective regarding the human will, a shift that unfolded across his lifelong polemical engagements with the Manichees and the Pelagians. Augustine’s early formulation of an uncaused free will in *On Free Choice of the Will* (*De Libero Arbitrio*) stands in apparent opposition to his later deterministic stance in *On the Predestination of the Saints* (*De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*). The question of whether this evolution represents a fundamental contradiction or a coherent defense of divine sovereignty is not merely a matter of historical theology, but a deep philosophical puzzle concerning the metaphysics of freedom, the problem of evil, and the nature of moral responsibility. This essay argues that Augustine’s shift is not a fundamental contradiction but a dialectical progression, a coherent defense of divine sovereignty that adapts its philosophical mechanism to the specific polemical threat at hand. However, while subordinating human moral agency to the paramount attribute of divine supremacy successfully unifies Augustine’s theology, it ultimately compromises the classical, libertarian conception of human moral responsibility.

To understand the necessity of Augustine’s early formulation, one must first grasp the strategic objective of his polemical engagement with the Manichees. The Manichean cosmology was fundamentally dualistic, positing two eternal, co-equal principles: the principle of light (good) and the principle of darkness (evil). In this framework, evil was not a mere privation or defect, but a positive, material substance that actively warred against the good. For Augustine, this dualism posed an intolerable theological problem: if evil were a co-eternal substance, then God could not be the supreme, omnipotent creator of all that exists. God would be locked in a cosmic struggle, and the origin of evil would remain an unsolved mystery. Furthermore, the Manichean view of the human will was deeply materialistic; they posited that the human soul was a mixture of light and dark particles, meaning that human beings were not fully responsible for their moral failings, as evil was an inherent, material compulsion rather than a product of the will’s choice.

It is precisely here that Augustine’s early *De Libero Arbitrio* serves its strategic objective of exonerating God from the problem of evil. Augustine constructs a rigorous defense of the will as a rational faculty, distinct from the material body. The central philosophical move in the early Augustine is the assertion that the human will is *uncaused* in its moment of choice. In the first book of *De Libero Arbitrio*, Augustine argues that the will is a rational nature capable of self-determination. It is not pushed by external material forces, nor is it compelled by God. Because the will is an uncaused prime mover in its choices, it possesses libertarian free will—the capacity to choose between alternative possibilities without prior necessity. 

This formulation is strategically brilliant for exonerating God. If the will is uncaused, then the origin of evil cannot be traced back to God’s creative act. God created the will as a good, rational substance, but the will’s subsequent turning away from the *summum bonum* (the highest good, which is God) toward lesser, created goods is entirely the will’s own doing. Evil, therefore, is not a substance created by God, but a privation—a deficiency of good—born of the will’s misdirection. By positing an uncaused free will, Augustine creates a metaphysical buffer between the divine and the diabolic. God is exonerated from the authorship of evil because the evil will is an autonomous agent of its own corruption. The strategic objective is achieved: the Manichean dualism is dismantled, God’s absolute goodness and sovereignty are preserved, and the problem of evil is reframed as a problem of creaturely misuse rather than divine deficiency.

However, the philosophical satisfaction of this early formulation was not to last. As Augustine’s theological maturity developed, he encountered a new and seemingly insurmountable polemical threat from the Pelagians, most notably the rigorous moral perfectionist Julian of Eclanum. If the Manichees had made the will a victim of material determinism, the Pelagians made the will an autonomous savior of the self. Pelagius and his followers, particularly Julian, argued that human nature was fundamentally good and that the will retained its pristine capacity to choose sinlessness without the necessity of divine grace. They posited that Adam’s sin affected only Adam himself, and that subsequent human beings are born with the exact same capacity for moral perfection that Adam possessed prior to the Fall. Grace, in this framework, is merely a helpful aid—a provision of the law, Christ’s example, and external assistance—but it is not ontologically necessary for the will to achieve moral perfection or salvation.

Julian of Eclanum’s moral perfectionism posed a direct and devastating threat to the paramount attribute of divine sovereignty. If the human will can achieve sinless perfection and merit salvation entirely through its own autonomous effort, then divine grace is rendered superfluous. God’s role in salvation becomes merely that of a facilitator, and human moral agency usurps the place of divine causality. It is against this backdrop that Augustine’s perspective on the will undergoes a dramatic shift, culminating in the deterministic stance of *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*. 

In his later anti-Pelagian works, Augustine radically reinterprets the human will. The will is no longer an autonomous, uncaused prime mover, but a faculty in bondage. Augustine argues that the Fall did not merely weaken the human will; it corrupted its very essence. The will is now enslaved to sin and incapable of choosing the true good—God—without an internal, transformative intervention of divine grace. In *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*, Augustine prioritizes absolute divine grace against the moral perfectionism of Julian. The will is not merely assisted by grace; it is moved by grace. God does not merely offer the will the possibility of good; God actively causes the will to will the good. The will’s turning toward God is not an uncaused choice, but the direct result of God’s irresistible and efficacious grace. Furthermore, Augustine argues for predestination: God, in His absolute sovereignty, elects certain individuals to receive this grace, while passing over others, leaving them in their justly deserved bondage to sin. 

The question that naturally arises from this evolution is whether it represents a fundamental contradiction. On the surface, the early Augustine posits an uncaused free will to exonerate God from evil, while the late Augustine posits a determined will to prioritize divine grace. Libertarian free will and theological determinism appear mutually exclusive. However, to label this a fundamental contradiction is to misunderstand the underlying philosophical and theological unity of Augustine’s project. The evolution is not a contradiction of principle, but a dialectical progression of defense. In both phases, the paramount attribute of divine sovereignty is being defended against a specific threat. 

In the early phase, divine sovereignty is threatened by the Manichean notion that evil is a co-eternal substance, which would make God dependent on or limited by the forces of darkness. Augustine exonerates God by making the will uncaused, thereby severing the causal link between God and evil. In the late phase, divine sovereignty is threatened by the Pelagian notion that the human will can achieve salvation autonomously, thereby making grace unnecessary and subordinating God’s lordship to human moral effort. Augustine reasserts divine sovereignty by making the will determined by grace, ensuring that even the human turn toward God is a product of divine causality. In both instances, the coherence of Augustine’s theology lies in his unwavering commitment to the absolute supremacy of God. The will is never allowed to usurp the place of God. In the early Augustine, the will is free from material determinism but still subordinate to God as the creator of the good; in the late Augustine, the will is free from Pelagian autonomy but entirely dependent on God as the cause of salvation. The evolution is a coherent defense of divine sovereignty, adapting its philosophical mechanism—libertarian freedom versus deterministic grace—to protect the same ultimate theological conviction: that God is the *causa prima* of all reality.

Yet, this unification of theodicy and soteriology under the banner of divine sovereignty comes at a profound philosophical cost. By subordinating human moral agency to the paramount attribute of divine supremacy, Augustine successfully unifies his theology, creating an airtight system where God is the author of all good and the ultimate determinant of all outcomes. Theodicy is secured because evil is the privation of the will, never God’s creation; soteriology is secured because salvation is entirely a gift of grace, never a human achievement. However, this unification ultimately compromises human moral responsibility in a way that strains the very fabric of Augustinian thought.

The early Augustine had a robust, intuitive account of moral responsibility: the agent is responsible because they are the uncaused cause of their evil actions. The will chooses freely, and therefore the agent justly merits condemnation or praise. In the later Augustine, this account is dismantled. If the will is moved by God’s grace or bound by sin’s corruption, in what sense can the human agent be said to possess moral responsibility in the libertarian sense? Augustine attempts to navigate this tension by arguing that humans are responsible for their desires, even if those desires are corrupted or caused by God. He argues that God does not force the will against its nature; rather, God moves the will according to its nature. For the fallen will, God’s grace does not violate its will but heals it, causing it to will what it already secretly desires. 

However, this compatibilist maneuver ultimately compromises human moral responsibility by collapsing the distinction between ultimate and proximate causality. If God is the ultimate cause of every human choice—whether the choice to sin or the choice to repent—then the human agent is reduced from a primary moral agent to a secondary conduit of divine causality. The moral responsibility of the agent becomes a function of divine decree rather than autonomous praxis. The moral agent no longer originates the act; they merely execute it under the determination of a higher will. While Augustine can maintain that humans are justly punished for sin because they sin willingly, the philosophical depth of that "willingness" is hollowed out. It is no longer a free choice among alternatives, but a determined movement of the will. This compromises the classical understanding of moral responsibility, which requires that the agent be the ultimate source of their actions, possessing the capacity to do otherwise.

In conclusion, Augustine’s shift in perspective regarding the human will—from the uncaused free will of *De Libero Arbitrio* to the deterministic grace of *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*—is not a fundamental contradiction, but a coherent and dialectical defense of divine sovereignty. Each formulation is a strategic adaptation to a specific polemical threat: the Manichean dualism of evil and the Pelagian autonomy of the will. In both cases, Augustine subordinates human moral agency to the paramount attribute of divine supremacy, successfully unifying his theology into a comprehensive system where God is the absolute sovereign of both the origin of evil and the accomplishment of salvation. Yet, this theological unification is purchased at the expense of philosophical coherence regarding human moral responsibility. By subordinating the will to divine causality, Augustine ultimately compromises the libertarian, autonomous agent, reducing the moral subject to a determined instrument of divine or sinful causality.
