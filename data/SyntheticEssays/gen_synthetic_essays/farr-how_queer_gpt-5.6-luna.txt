# Mackie’s Argument from Queerness and the Plausibility of Objective Moral Values

J. L. Mackie’s argument from queerness is one of the most influential objections to objective moral values. It is not a direct argument that particular moral judgments are false. Rather, it challenges the metaphysical and epistemological foundations that would be required if moral judgments were objectively true. According to Mackie, objective values would be “entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort,” unlike anything else in the universe. They would possess a peculiar kind of intrinsic action-guiding force, and human beings would need an equally peculiar faculty of moral intuition in order to know them. Since such entities and faculties would be metaphysically and epistemically “queer,” Mackie concludes that we have strong reason to reject objective moral values and to adopt moral error theory.

The argument is powerful because it draws attention to features of morality that are not easily accommodated by a naturalistic picture of the world. Moral values appear to be authoritative, categorical, and reason-giving in a way that ordinary descriptive properties are not. Yet the argument is also controversial. The fact that a proposed property is strange, unfamiliar, or theoretically costly does not by itself show that it does not exist. Science has repeatedly expanded our ontology by introducing entities and forces that initially seemed implausible. The crucial question, therefore, is whether moral properties are merely unfamiliar, or whether their alleged features generate a distinctive and decisive explanatory problem.

This essay argues that Mackie’s argument from queerness provides a serious challenge to robust, non-naturalist moral realism, especially when combined with concerns about moral epistemology and the apparent causal inertness of moral facts. However, it does not constitute a conclusive refutation of objective moral values. The presumption against queerness is a legitimate methodological consideration, but it becomes unwarranted if it is treated as a proof that reality must conform to familiar categories. Scientific analogies show that strangeness alone is not sufficient grounds for rejection. At the same time, moral properties differ from scientific posits in important respects: they seem to possess intrinsic normativity, lack an obvious causal role, and present unusual problems concerning how we could know them. The argument from queerness therefore succeeds as a defeasible argument from theoretical cost, not as a demonstration that objective moral facts are impossible.

## The structure of Mackie’s argument

Mackie’s moral theory begins with a distinction between ordinary moral discourse and the metaphysical status of moral claims. People commonly say that actions are wrong, duties are binding, and states of affairs are good or bad. These statements often appear to assert facts that hold independently of human attitudes, conventions, or desires. If torturing an innocent person merely for amusement is wrong, then it seems wrong even if the torturer approves of it, the victim does not object, and an entire society endorses the practice. This appearance of objectivity is what Mackie rejects.

His position is usually called moral error theory. It combines two claims. First, ordinary moral judgments purport to refer to objective and prescriptive values. Second, there are no such values. Moral judgments are therefore systematically false, even though they may express attitudes, recommendations, or socially useful norms. Mackie’s argument from queerness supports the second claim by arguing that objective values would be ontologically and epistemologically extraordinary.

The metaphysical version of the argument concerns the nature of objective values. If something is objectively good or wrong, its value must not simply consist in being desired, approved, or commanded. Otherwise, its moral status would depend on human attitudes or divine decisions, and it would not be objective in the relevant sense. Objective values would instead have to be intrinsic features of things or actions. Yet they would also have to be inherently action-guiding. To recognize that an action is wrong would not merely be to discover a neutral fact; it would be to become subject to a reason or demand to avoid the action. Moral properties would thus combine a descriptive or world-involving status with an irreducibly normative force.

Mackie sees this combination as deeply unusual. A stone can be heavy, a person can be intelligent, and an action can cause pain. These properties may influence what we do, but none of them is intrinsically authoritative. The property of causing pain gives us a reason to prevent pain only in conjunction with some evaluative or motivational background. By contrast, objective wrongness would supposedly provide a reason independently of anyone’s desires, interests, or social practices. It would be both part of the fabric of reality and inherently prescriptive.

Mackie’s point is not merely that moral properties are non-natural in the sense of being invisible or immaterial. Many perfectly respectable scientific entities are unobservable. His stronger claim is that objective values would have to be unlike ordinary properties because they would possess an intrinsic “to-be-pursuedness” or “to-be-avoidedness.” They would resemble commands embedded in reality. The world would contain facts that, simply by being known, necessarily exerted practical authority over us.

The epistemological version of the argument follows from this metaphysical claim. If objective values existed, human beings would need some way of becoming aware of them. Ordinary sense perception does not seem to reveal wrongness or goodness as it reveals color, shape, or sound. Nor does empirical science appear to detect moral properties as it detects chemical or physical ones. Mackie therefore argues that moral knowledge would require a special faculty of moral intuition. This faculty would be capable of apprehending objective values directly, and its deliverances would motivate or guide action.

Mackie regards this proposed faculty as epistemically queer. It would be difficult to explain how it works, how it could be tested, and why its deliverances should be trusted. Moral intuition appears especially suspect because different people and cultures produce persistent and extensive moral disagreement. If moral truths were directly available to rational intuition, it is unclear why human beings would disagree about them so frequently. Furthermore, moral judgments are evidently influenced by upbringing, emotion, social position, and evolutionary history. These influences make it difficult to distinguish genuine perception of moral facts from psychological projection.

The argument can therefore be formulated as follows:

1. Objective moral values would have to be metaphysically unusual because they would be intrinsically prescriptive or action-guiding.
2. Knowledge of such values would require an epistemically unusual faculty of moral intuition.
3. We have no independent reason to believe in such metaphysically and epistemically unusual entities and faculties.
4. Therefore, we should reject objective moral values.

The conclusion is not supposed to follow deductively from the mere fact of strangeness. Mackie presents queerness as a reason for disbelief, grounded in a broader commitment to ontological economy and explanatory plausibility. The argument is thus best understood as an inference to the best explanation: if we can explain moral practice without objective values, then we should not populate our ontology with entities that are unlike anything else we have reason to believe in.

## What exactly is “queer” about moral properties?

An initial difficulty is that “queer” may refer to several different kinds of strangeness. A property could be queer because it is unfamiliar, because it is difficult to observe, because it is not reducible to known physical properties, or because it has an implausible metaphysical role. These forms of strangeness should not be treated as equivalent.

Unfamiliarity is a weak objection. Many things were once unfamiliar but are now accepted. Non-observability is also not decisive, since scientific theories routinely posit unobservable entities. Non-reducibility may be more serious, but even here it does not establish nonexistence. Consciousness, mathematical objects, and modal properties are often said to resist straightforward reduction to physics, yet their existence remains a live philosophical question.

The most serious form of queerness concerns the alleged intrinsic normativity of moral properties. Moral values are not merely supposed to be unusual objects; they are supposed to have a fundamentally different kind of power. They would make demands on agents. If an act is objectively wrong, then an agent has a reason not to perform it regardless of personal desire or social approval. This reason would not be merely causal, emotional, or prudential. It would be a normative reason, and its authority would arise from the moral fact itself.

This is a genuine philosophical puzzle. Consider the fact that an action causes unnecessary suffering. That fact is descriptive. It tells us what the action does. To move from it to the conclusion that the action is wrong, however, seems to require a normative principle. A moral realist may say that unnecessary suffering is objectively bad, or that causing it is objectively wrong. But Mackie asks what kind of property this badness or wrongness would be. If it is simply another natural property, why does it generate obligation? If it is not natural, how does it connect with the natural world and with human motivation?

The realist may reply that there is no mystery in the existence of normative reasons. A fact can be reason-giving without being causally coercive. The fact that one has promised to meet a friend may provide a reason to keep the promise even if one has no desire to do so. The fact that one’s child is in danger may provide a reason for action independently of one’s current inclinations. On this view, Mackie’s argument assumes that reasons must be reducible either to desires or to causal forces. But a realist might reject that assumption. Normative authority is not supposed to push or compel us like a physical force; it is supposed to rationally justify action.

This reply reveals a central issue. Is normativity genuinely mysterious, or merely conceptually distinct from causation? If objective reasons are part of reality, their authority need not be understood as a supernatural force. A fact can count in favor of an action without producing behavior mechanically. Rational recognition and physical causation may be different relations. The realist can argue that Mackie’s language of “queerness” makes objective normativity seem more exotic than it is by treating every non-causal form of practical significance as metaphysically suspect.

Nevertheless, this response does not completely dissolve the problem. It explains what normativity means, but it does not yet explain how normative properties exist or how we know them. Saying that an objective reason is a reason that applies independently of desire may accurately characterize the concept, but it does not establish that such reasons exist. The question remains whether the world contains stance-independent facts that possess this kind of authority.

## The epistemological challenge

Mackie’s epistemological argument is arguably stronger than the metaphysical one. Even if objective moral values are coherent, how could human beings know them?

A non-naturalist realist cannot simply appeal to observation in the ordinary empirical sense. We do not see wrongness with our eyes, hear justice with our ears, or measure goodness with scientific instruments. The realist may therefore appeal to rational intuition. According to this view, some moral truths are self-evident or can be grasped through reflection. For example, one might claim that gratuitous cruelty is wrong, that suffering is bad, or that equals should be treated equally. Moral knowledge would be analogous to knowledge of logical or mathematical truths rather than empirical observation.

Mackie’s objection is that this proposal introduces an unexplained faculty. Why should we trust moral intuition? How does it distinguish truth from culturally inherited prejudice, emotional response, or evolutionary conditioning? Moral intuitions vary across individuals and societies, and they are often unstable or manipulable. People who grow up in slave-owning societies, caste systems, or patriarchal cultures may sincerely regard oppressive practices as morally permissible. This makes it difficult to claim that moral judgment is a transparent form of perception.

The realist has several possible replies. First, disagreement does not show that there is no truth. People disagree about mathematics, history, and science, but disagreement does not eliminate those domains. Second, moral reasoning may involve correcting unreliable intuitions rather than simply consulting immediate feelings. Just as perception can be improved through training and reflection, moral judgment may become more reliable through consistency, attention to evidence, and awareness of bias. Third, the existence of evolutionary or cultural influences on moral cognition does not by itself show that moral beliefs are false. Our cognitive faculties have evolutionary histories, but that does not make all their deliverances unreliable. Evolution may have selected creatures capable of recognizing certain objective truths, particularly truths concerning cooperation, harm, and social life.

These responses show that the epistemological argument cannot rest solely on disagreement or causal genealogy. However, the challenge of moral epistemology remains substantial. In science, there are publicly accessible methods for testing theories, correcting error, and accumulating evidence. In mathematics and logic, there are formal procedures for proving propositions. Moral reflection has no universally accepted procedure of comparable power. Philosophers may appeal to coherence, reflective equilibrium, universalizability, impartiality, or the avoidance of contradiction, but these methods do not produce agreement on all fundamental values.

Moreover, moral beliefs are closely entangled with motivation and emotion. An individual’s judgment that cruelty is wrong is not normally a detached perception. It is associated with aversion, indignation, empathy, or concern. A moral realist may say that these psychological responses are caused by recognition of objective value, but the reverse explanation is also available: human beings developed aversions to violence and systems of condemnation because these dispositions promoted cooperation and group survival. If evolutionary and cultural explanations can account for moral belief without appealing to objective values, then the realist’s appeal to moral intuition may look explanatorily redundant.
