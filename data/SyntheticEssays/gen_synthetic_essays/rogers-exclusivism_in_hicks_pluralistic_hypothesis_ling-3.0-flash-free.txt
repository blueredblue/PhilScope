John Hick’s pluralistic hypothesis stands as one of the most ambitious and influential attempts to reconcile the enduring diversity of global religious traditions under a unified metaphysical framework. At its core, Hick proposes that the world’s great religions are distinct yet equally valid human responses to a single, transcendent Reality—which he famously calls the Real. To account for this diversity, Hick draws upon Immanuel Kant’s epistemology, arguing that the Real, as a noumenal transcendent, is utterly inaccessible to unaided human cognition. Consequently, all religious believers encounter the Real through the filter of their culturally conditioned, phenomenal selves, resulting in the myriad deities, cosmologies, and soteriological goals that characterize the world’s faiths. 

However, Hick’s pluralism is not without its profound internal tensions and boundaries. One of the most conspicuous and philosophically troubling aspects of his hypothesis is its restrictive scope. Hick deliberately confines his pluralistic hypothesis to the post-Axial Age religions—Buddhism, Hinduism, Confucianism, Taoism, Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. By excluding pre-Axial traditions and indigenous religious expressions, such as Native American vision quests or African shamanic initiations, Hick’s hypothesis appears to commit what can be characterized as unreasonable arbitrariness. This exclusion creates a severe tension between Hick’s foundational principle—that religious experiences grounded in the transcendent justify the veridicality of religious beliefs—and his reliance on soteriological transformation as the exclusive criterion for evaluating religious validity. This essay will evaluate whether this exclusion undermines the internal coherence of Hick’s pluralism, and it will examine whether ethical fruitfulness or an expanded formulation of the Real offers a viable modification to resolve this exclusion.

To understand the tension at the heart of Hick’s pluralism, one must first distinguish between his foundational epistemological principle and his soteriological criterion. Hick’s foundational principle asserts that religious experiences are veridical encounters with the transcendent Real. Just as a physical object causes sensory experiences that justify our belief in its existence, the Real causes religious experiences that justify the believer’s conviction that their religious tradition points toward an ultimate reality. This principle is universalist in its implications: if all authentic religious experiences are encounters with the same Real, then the beliefs grounded in those experiences possess a prima facie claim to veridicality. The diversity of religious concepts is thus explained not by the diversity of the Real, but by the diversity of the human cognitive and cultural apparatus that interprets the Real.

Yet, Hick does not merely assert that religious experiences are veridical; he imposes a strict criterion to determine *which* experiences and traditions qualify as authentically religious in the first place. This criterion is soteriological transformation. For Hick, the ultimate purpose of religion is not merely ethical living, cosmological explanation, or communal cohesion, but soteria—salvation, liberation, or the ultimate transformation of the human person from self-centeredness to Reality-centeredness. In the post-Axial religions, this transformation takes specific forms: moksha or nirvana in the Indian traditions, union with God or the Beatific Vision in the Abrahamic faiths. Hick’s pluralism is thus structured around the idea that all post-Axial religions are different soteriological paths up the same mountain.

The tension arises because Hick’s foundational principle of veridicality and his soteriological criterion operate at cross-purposes when applied to pre-Axial and indigenous traditions. If the Real is the ultimate source of all religious experience, and if all authentic experiences of the Real are veridical, then why should the scope of veridicality be restricted to those traditions that explicitly articulate a soteriological goal of individual or cosmic liberation? Native American vision quests, for instance, are profound encounters with the transcendent—often involving the soul’s journey to the spirit world, encounters with guardian spirits, and the receipt of sacred knowledge. African shamanic initiations involve dramatic, transformative encounters with ancestral spirits and the divine, often resulting in profound psychological and communal integration. These traditions undeniably involve experiences grounded in the transcendent; by Hick’s foundational principle, they should be considered veridical. Yet, Hick dismisses them as lacking the explicit soteriological framework of ultimate liberation or salvation that characterizes the Axial Age faiths. 

This dismissal constitutes unreasonable arbitrariness for several interconnected reasons. First, it imposes a historically and culturally contingent boundary—the Axial Age—as a universal criterion for religious validity. Karl Jaspers, who coined the term, acknowledged the Axial Age as a heuristic concept, not an absolute theological boundary. By adopting it uncritically, Hick privileges a specific, Eurocentric historical narrative over the lived reality of religious experience. The boundary between the "Axial" and the "pre-Axial" or "indigenous" is historically fuzzy and culturally arbitrary; the spiritual practices of indigenous peoples are not "pre-Axial" relics, but living, dynamic traditions that have evolved and adapted over millennia. To exclude them is to impose an artificial temporal and geographical limitation on the scope of the Real, which contradicts Hick’s own insistence that the Real is universal and transcends cultural boundaries.

Second, the exclusion undermines the internal coherence of Hick’s pluralism by violating his own principle of epistemic parity. Hick’s pluralism demands that we treat the post-Axial religions as equally valid, mutually cross-interpretable paths to the same soteriological goal. However, by excluding indigenous traditions, Hick implicitly establishes a hierarchy: the post-Axial religions are "true" religions because they possess the correct soteriological aim, while indigenous traditions are relegated to the status of "primitive" or "magical" precursors that lack the philosophical sophistication to grasp ultimate reality. This move betrays the very pluralism Hick seeks to defend. If the Real is the ultimate reality, it cannot be confined to the cultural and historical milieus that produced the Axial Age texts. The Real, by definition, must be accessible to all human beings, regardless of their historical epoch or level of socio-political organization. By restricting the scope of his hypothesis to post-Axial religions, Hick effectively argues that the Real only manifests itself in certain times and places, thereby undermining the universalist foundation of his pluralism.

The reliance on soteriological transformation as the exclusive criterion further exposes this arbitrariness. Hick uses soteriology to filter out traditions that do not fit his mold, yet he simultaneously argues that soteriological goals are themselves culturally conditioned interpretations of the Real. The criterion is thus circular: a tradition is valid because it aims for salvation/liberation, and salvation/liberation is the ultimate goal of religion, which is defined by the post-Axial religions. Indigenous traditions are excluded not because their experiences are demonstrably non-veridical, but because their soteriological frameworks do not map neatly onto the Axial categories of moksha or theosis. Yet, shamanic traditions undeniably involve profound transformations of the self—transformations from sickness to health, from alienation to integration with the community and the cosmos. If soteriological transformation is the criterion, why not recognize the ecological and communal salvation offered by the vision quest or the shamanic initiation? Hick’s refusal to do so reveals that his soteriological criterion is not a neutral, universal standard, but a philosophical preference for the metaphysical frameworks of the literate, Axial Age elite over the embodied, ecological, and communal frameworks of indigenous peoples.

Given that the exclusion of pre-Axial and indigenous traditions severely undermines the internal coherence of Hick’s pluralism, the question becomes whether modifications can resolve this exclusion. Two primary modifications have been proposed in the secondary literature: adopting ethical fruitfulness as a criterion, or expanding the formulation of the Real. Both offer potential pathways, but they also carry significant philosophical costs.

One viable modification is to replace, or supplement, soteriological transformation with ethical fruitfulness as the exclusive criterion for evaluating religious validity. If the truth of a religion is not measured by its ability to produce individual liberation from samsara or union with a personal God, but by its capacity to generate love, justice, compassion, and ecological harmony, then indigenous traditions would readily pass Hick’s test. Native American vision quests, for instance, are fundamentally oriented toward restoring balance, healing the community, and fostering a deep ethical relationship with the natural world. African shamanic initiations similarly aim to integrate the individual into a web of communal and ancestral obligations that prioritize harmony and mutual care. By shifting the criterion from soteriological liberation to ethical fruitfulness, Hick could include indigenous traditions without violating his foundational principle of veridicality. 

However, this modification fundamentally alters the nature of Hick’s pluralistic hypothesis. Hick’s pluralism is explicitly a metaphysical and soteriological theory, not merely an ethical one. The entire point of his hypothesis is that different religions converge on the same ultimate Reality, not merely on the same ethical outcomes. If the criterion is reduced to ethical fruitfulness, Hick’s pluralism collapses into a form of ethical pluralism or secular humanism, losing its theological ambition. The Real ceases to be the ultimate noumenal ground of being and becomes merely a useful heuristic for promoting moral behavior. Furthermore, ethical fruitfulness is notoriously difficult to measure and compare. Many highly soteriological traditions have produced profound ethical fruits, but they have also sanctioned violence and oppression. Conversely, some indigenous traditions, while highly ethical within their immediate contexts, may lack a universalizing ethical impulse that extends beyond their tribal boundaries. Thus, while ethical fruitfulness resolves the exclusion of indigenous traditions, it does so at the cost of gutting the metaphysical core of Hick’s project, rendering it a different theory altogether.

A more promising, yet still challenging, modification is to expand the formulation of the Real itself. Instead of defining the Real strictly as the noumenal ground of being that is interpreted through post-Axial soteriological categories, one could expand the Real to encompass the full breadth of indigenous cosmologies, including the land, the ancestors, and the spirit world. In this expanded formulation, the Real is not merely an abstract, transcendent noumenon that is veiled by cultural interpretations; it is also immanent, ecological, and communal. The vision quest is not merely a culturally conditioned interpretation of an abstract Real; it is a direct, veridical encounter with the Real as the living land and the ancestral spirits. Similarly, African shamanic initiation is a genuine soteriological transformation—not of the individual’s ultimate metaphysical status in relation to an abstract Brahman or God, but of the individual’s integration into the cosmic, communal, and ecological order. 

By expanding the Real in this manner, Hick could include indigenous traditions as equally valid, fully soteriological responses to the same transcendent reality, albeit a reality that is understood more holistically than the strictly monistic or personalistic Real of the Axial Age. This modification preserves the metaphysical ambition of Hick’s pluralism while rectifying its ethnocentric boundary. It allows soteriological transformation to remain the criterion, but it broadens the definition of soteriology to include ecological restoration, communal healing, and ancestral harmony. 

Yet, this expanded formulation of the Real is not without its own severe difficulties. The primary risk is that by expanding the Real to encompass the myriad spirits, ancestors, and ecological forces of indigenous cosmologies, Hick’s pluralism loses its monistic unity. Hick’s pluralism relies on the premise that all religions are different interpretations of *one* Real. If the Real of the vision quest is the land and the ancestors, and the Real of the Abrahamic faith is a personal creator God, and the Real of Buddhism is the impersonal void, then Hick has not unified the Real; he has merely multiplied the phenomenological manifestations of reality without demonstrating their ontological identity. The expanded Real risks becoming a vague, all-encompassing pantheism or polytheism that renders the concept of the Real meaningless. If the Real can be anything that any tradition considers ultimate, then Hick’s hypothesis that all religions are responses to the *same* Real is vacuously true and philosophically vacuous. The hypothesis loses its explanatory power and its capacity to generate genuine cross-cultural dialogue, as it no longer posits a specific, shared ontological ground.

Furthermore, expanding the Real raises the question of whether indigenous soteriologies are truly soteriological in the sense Hick intends. Hick’s original framework relies on the idea that soteriology is about the ultimate transformation of the self from its isolated, ego-centric state to its true, Reality-centric state. Indigenous traditions, particularly shamanic ones, often emphasize the maintenance of cosmic balance and the service of the community rather than the radical, individual dissolution of the ego. While one could argue that restoring cosmic balance is a form of ultimate transformation, it is a transformation of the *world* rather than the *self* in the Axial sense. To force indigenous traditions into Hick’s soteriological mold, even with an expanded Real, risks committing the very reductionism that pluralism seeks to overcome. It demands that indigenous traditions speak the language of Axial soteriology, thereby perpetuating the epistemic violence that Hick’s hypothesis ostensibly seeks to cure.

Ultimately, the evaluation of Hick’s pluralistic hypothesis reveals a deep and perhaps irresolvable tension between its universalist aspirations and its culturally specific boundaries. The restriction of Hick’s hypothesis to post-Axial religions is undeniably guilty of unreasonable arbitrariness. It relies on a historically contingent and Eurocentric demarcation—the Axial Age—to determine which encounters with the transcendent are veridical and which are merely cultural curiosities. This exclusion violates Hick’s own foundational principle that the Real is universally accessible and that religious experiences grounded in the Real are inherently veridical. By imposing an a priori soteriological filter that only the post-Axial traditions can pass, Hick transforms his pluralism from a theory of universal religious epistemology into a sectarian ideology that privileges literate, historical, and metaphysically abstract traditions over embodied, ecological, and communal ones.

Neither modification fully resolves the dilemma without sacrificing a core tenet of Hick’s hypothesis. Ethical fruitfulness resolves the exclusion of indigenous traditions but strips the theory of its metaphysical and soteriological depth, reducing it to a secular ethical framework. An expanded formulation of the Real preserves the metaphysical ambition but risks destroying the monistic unity that makes the Real a single, shared object of religious experience, thereby rendering the pluralism trivial or forcing indigenous traditions into an alien soteriological paradigm. 

The deepest lesson of this critique is that Hick’s pluralism, for all its sophistication, remains tethered to the very Western philosophical and historical frameworks it seeks to overcome. The Axial Age is a construct of Western historiography; the criterion of soteriological transformation is heavily shaped by the Greco-Christian and Indian metaphysical traditions that emerged from that Age. To construct a truly universal pluralistic hypothesis, one must be willing to dismantle the very categories that make the hypothesis intelligible. If the Real is truly the ultimate reality, it must be capable of generating the vision quest, the shamanic initiation, and the Axial revelation alike—not as variations on a single soteriological theme, but as radically diverse, yet equally valid, manifestations of the human encounter with the transcendent. Until Hick’s hypothesis can accommodate this radical diversity without collapsing into arbitrariness or triviality, it will remain a brilliant but ultimately limited attempt to map the infinite variegation of religious experience within the narrow confines of Western philosophical categories.
