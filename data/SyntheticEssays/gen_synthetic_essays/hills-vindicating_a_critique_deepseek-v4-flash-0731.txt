**Title: The Pragmatic Shield: Donnellan, Kripke, and the Limits of Russell’s Theory of Descriptions**

**Introduction**

The philosophy of language in the twentieth century is largely defined by a foundational dispute over the nature of definite descriptions—phrases of the form “the so-and-so.” Bertrand Russell’s theory of descriptions, first articulated in his landmark 1905 paper “On Denoting,” stands as a monument of analytic philosophy. Russell argued that definite descriptions are not referring expressions at all, but rather incomplete symbols that are logically analyzed as quantified statements. This theory elegantly solved puzzles about empty descriptions, negative existentials, and the failure of substitutivity in intensional contexts. However, in 1966, Keith Donnellan challenged Russell’s account with a now-famous distinction between the *referential* and *attributive* uses of definite descriptions. Donnellan argued that in many ordinary utterances, a description like “the man drinking the martini” is used not to assert a unique existence condition, but simply to pick out a particular individual, even if that individual does not actually satisfy the description. This, Donnellan claimed, showed that Russell’s theory was fundamentally mistaken about the semantics of descriptions.

Yet this challenge did not go unanswered. Saul Kripke, in his 1977 paper “Speaker’s Reference and Semantic Reference,” mounted a powerful defense of Russell. Kripke argued that Donnellan’s distinction, while real, is a *pragmatic* phenomenon—a matter of speaker’s intentions and conversational context—rather than a *semantic* ambiguity in the description itself. According to Kripke, Russell’s theory correctly specifies the semantic reference of a definite description, while Donnellan’s referential use is simply a case where the speaker’s reference diverges from the semantic reference. Thus, Kripke concluded, Donnellan’s examples do not constitute counterexamples to Russell’s theory.

This essay evaluates whether Donnellan’s distinction successfully refutes Russell’s theory. I will argue that it does not. However, I will also argue that the debate reveals a deeper truth: while Kripke’s pragmatic defense successfully shields Russell’s *semantic* theory, it simultaneously exposes a significant incompleteness in Russell’s account of *reference* as a whole. The distinction between speaker’s reference and semantic reference, while correct, proves that Russell’s theory is not a complete account of how language actually refers in communicative contexts. The central mechanism of analysis—whether we posit semantic ambiguity or pragmatic ambiguity—must be resolved in favor of pragmatic ambiguity, but this resolution comes at a cost. It forces us to recognize that Russell’s theory, though true for the semantics of sentences, fails to account for the full truth-conditional content of utterances when speaker intentions override descriptive accuracy. Ultimately, I will defend the viability of pragmatic ambiguity as the correct explanatory framework, while arguing that this framework does not rescue Russell’s theory from the charge of being incomplete—it merely refines the nature of that incompleteness.

**Section 1: Russell’s Theory of Descriptions**

To evaluate Donnellan’s challenge, we must first understand precisely what Russell’s theory claims. For Russell, a definite description such as “the present King of France” is not a proper name or a logically proper name. It does not function as a directly referring term that picks out an object. Instead, it is a *quantificational* expression that can be paraphrased away. The sentence “The present King of France is bald” is analyzed as follows:

(1) There exists an x such that x is presently King of France, and for any y, if y is presently King of France, then y = x, and x is bald.

In logical notation: ∃x(Kx & ∀y(Ky → y=x) & Bx).

This analysis has several crucial features. First, it eliminates the description altogether; the logical form contains only variables, predicates, and quantifiers. Second, it introduces an *existence* claim and a *uniqueness* claim as part of the truth conditions. If there is no present King of France, or if there are two, then the sentence is false—not meaningless, but false. Third, the description has a *scope*. In complex sentences, such as those involving negation or propositional attitudes, the description can take either a *primary* or *secondary* occurrence. For example, “The present King of France is not bald” can be read as (i) “There exists a unique present King of France, and he is not bald” (primary scope, where the description falls outside the negation) or (ii) “It is not the case that there exists a unique present King of France who is bald” (secondary scope, where the description falls inside the negation). This scope distinction was Russell’s solution to the problem of substitutivity in opaque contexts. For instance, in “George IV wondered whether Scott was the author of *Waverley*,” the description “the author of *Waverley*” can take a secondary scope, so that the sentence does not entail that George IV knew that Scott was the author. The description is not a referring term, so it does not have a referent that can be substituted.

Russell’s theory is a *semantic* theory about the truth conditions of sentences containing definite descriptions. It tells us what the world must be like for such a sentence to be true. It does not claim that speakers always use descriptions in this way; rather, it claims that the *meaning* of the sentence—its propositional content—is given by the quantificational analysis. The theory is elegant, parsimonious, and solves a host of philosophical puzzles. It avoids Meinongianism by denying that empty descriptions refer to subsistent entities. It handles negative existentials by showing that “The present King of France does not exist” is not a statement about a non-existent object, but rather a denial of the existence claim embedded in the description. And it explains why substitution of co-referring terms fails in intensional contexts: because descriptions are not terms, they do not have a referent to substitute.

**Section 2: Donnellan’s Distinction**

In 1966, Keith Donnellan published “Reference and Definite Descriptions,” in which he argued that Russell’s theory conflates two distinct ways in which a definite description can be used. Donnellan distinguished between the *attributive* use and the *referential* use.

In the *attributive* use, a speaker uses a description to speak about whatever object happens to satisfy the description. The description is essential to identifying the object. Donnellan’s famous example: suppose Smith has been brutally murdered, and his body is found. A detective says, “Smith’s murderer is insane.” Here, the speaker does not know who the murderer is; they are using the description to assert that whoever committed the murder is insane. The description “Smith’s murderer” is used attributively because the speaker intends to refer to the person, whoever he is, who fits the description. If it turns out that the murderer is perfectly sane, the detective’s assertion is false. The truth conditions are exactly those given by Russell’s theory: there exists a unique murderer of Smith, and that person is insane.

In the *referential* use, however, the speaker uses a description to refer to a particular individual, even if that individual does not actually satisfy the description. Donnellan’s classic example: at a party, someone points to a man across the room who is holding a martini glass and says, “The man drinking the martini is a famous philosopher.” Unbeknownst to the speaker, the man is actually drinking water from a martini-shaped glass. The description “the man drinking the martini” is false of the man—he is not drinking a martini. Yet the speaker clearly intends to refer to that specific person, and the audience knows which person is being referred to. Donnellan argues that in this case, the statement is *true* if the man is indeed a famous philosopher, regardless of the fact that he is not drinking a martini. The description is merely a tool for picking out the referent; it is not part of the proposition asserted.

Donnellan’s point is that in the referential use, the description functions more like a proper name or a demonstrative—it is a device of *direct reference*. The speaker’s intention to refer to a particular object is what determines the reference, not the satisfaction of the description. Consequently, the truth of the statement depends on the properties of the object referred to, not on whether the object satisfies the description. This directly contradicts Russell’s theory, which holds that the truth conditions of any sentence containing a definite description include the existence and uniqueness of an object satisfying the description. In Donnellan’s referential example, the existence and uniqueness conditions are not part of the truth conditions; they are merely part of the speaker’s communicative strategy.

**Section 3: The Challenge to Russell’s Theory**

Donnellan’s distinction poses a serious challenge to Russell for two reasons. First, it appears to show that Russell’s truth conditions are too strong. If a speaker uses a description referentially, and the object referred to does not satisfy the description, Russell’s theory would predict that the sentence is false (because the existence condition fails). But Donnellan argues that the sentence can be true, because the speaker is making a claim about the object, not about the description. Thus, Russell’s theory fails to capture the truth-conditional content of the utterance.

Second, Donnellan’s distinction seems to undermine Russell’s scope analysis in intensional contexts. Consider the sentence, “The man drinking the martini is believed to be a spy.” If used attributively, this means that whoever is drinking the martini is believed to be a spy—a de dicto reading. If used referentially, it means that the speaker has a particular man in mind, and that man is believed to be a spy—a de re reading. Russell’s theory can handle this via scope: the primary scope reading gives the de re reading, and the secondary scope reading gives the de dicto reading. However, Donnellan argues that the referential/attributive distinction is not equivalent to the primary/secondary scope distinction. In a referential use, the description is not part of the proposition at all; it is merely a vehicle for picking out the referent. Even if we give the description primary scope, the resulting proposition still contains the description as a constituent, which is not what the speaker intended. The speaker is not saying “There exists a unique man drinking a martini, and he is believed to be a spy.” The speaker is saying, “That man—the one I am pointing at—is believed to be a spy.” The description is eliminable without loss of propositional content.

Thus, Donnellan’s challenge is not merely that Russell’s truth conditions are wrong in some cases; it is that Russell’s entire approach—treating descriptions as quantificational structures—misses the fundamental fact that descriptions can be used as referring expressions. If Donnellan is right, then Russell’s theory is not just incomplete; it is a mistaken account of the semantics of definite descriptions in their referential use.

**Section 4: Kripke’s Pragmatic Objection**

Saul Kripke’s response, in “Speaker’s Reference and Semantic Reference,” is a masterful defense of Russell. Kripke begins by distinguishing between *semantic reference* and *speaker’s reference*. The semantic reference of an expression is determined by the rules of the language—the conventional meaning of the expression. The speaker’s reference is what the speaker intends to refer to on a particular occasion of use, which may diverge from the semantic reference. Kripke argues that Donnellan’s referential/attributive distinction is not a distinction in the semantics of definite descriptions, but rather a distinction in the pragmatics of speaker meaning.

Kripke points out that the phenomenon Donnellan describes is not unique to definite descriptions. Consider the sentence, “The man over there is my uncle.” Suppose the speaker points to a man who is actually a woman in disguise. The speaker uses the description “the man over there” to refer to the person, even though the person is not a man. In this case, the semantic reference of “the man over there” is determined by the description—there is no man over there, so the description has no semantic referent. Yet the speaker’s reference is to the woman. Does this show that “the man over there” has two semantic senses—one meaning “the male person over there” and another meaning “the person I am pointing at”? Obviously not. The description has a single semantic meaning, but it can be used to refer to something that does not satisfy that meaning. This is a general feature of language: we often use descriptions loosely, metaphorically, or incorrectly to refer to things.

Kripke argues that Donnellan’s referential use of “the man drinking the martini” is exactly analogous. The semantic reference of the description is determined by the quantificational analysis: there is a unique man drinking a martini. Since there is no such man, the description has no semantic referent. However, the speaker’s reference is to the man holding the water glass. The speaker has made a mistake about the facts, but the audience understands the speaker’s intention. This is a case of speaker’s reference overriding semantic reference, not a case of semantic ambiguity.

Kripke’s central claim is that Russell’s theory correctly gives the *semantic reference* of a definite description. The truth conditions of the *sentence* are those specified by Russell. If the description is empty or non-unique, the sentence is false. However, the *statement* the speaker makes—the proposition the speaker intends to communicate—may be different. This is a pragmatic matter, governed by Gricean maxims of conversation, shared background assumptions, and the speaker’s intentions. The fact that a speaker can successfully communicate a true proposition using a false description does not show that the sentence is true; it shows that the speaker’s meaning is not identical to the sentence’s meaning.

Kripke’s objection is powerful because it preserves the elegance of Russell’s theory while accommodating Donnellan’s observations. It does not deny that there is a referential/attributive distinction; it merely reclassifies it as a pragmatic distinction. Kripke argues that this is the correct classification because the distinction applies to many other expressions, such as proper names, demonstratives, and pronouns. For example, if I point at a man who is not wearing a hat and say, “The man with the hat is my brother,” I am using the description referentially. But no one would claim that the description “the man with the hat” has a semantic sense that means “the man I am pointing at.” The description has its ordinary semantic meaning, and the speaker is using it incorrectly. The same applies to definite descriptions.

**Section 5: Intensional Contexts and Truth-Values**

The debate between Donnellan and Kripke becomes particularly acute when we consider intensional contexts—contexts involving belief, desire, necessity, or other propositional attitudes. These contexts are where Russell’s scope distinctions are most important. Russell argued that descriptions can take primary or secondary scope, which yields different truth conditions. For example, “The author of *Hamlet* is believed to be a genius” can be read as:

(2) There exists a unique author of *Hamlet*, and that person is believed to be a genius. (Primary scope, de re)
(3) It is believed that there exists a unique author of *Hamlet* who is a genius. (Secondary scope, de dicto)

These readings have different truth conditions. If someone believes that Shakespeare wrote *Hamlet* but does not believe that Shakespeare is a genius, then (2) is false (because the person is not believed to be a genius), but (3) might be true if the belief is about the description itself. Russell’s theory thus handles intensional contexts by appealing to scope.

Donnellan’s challenge is that the referential/attributive distinction does not map neatly onto the primary/secondary scope distinction. In a referential use, the speaker is not asserting an existential claim at all. Consider the sentence, “The man drinking the martini is believed to be a spy.” If the speaker uses this referentially, pointing at Jones, who is actually drinking water, the speaker is saying that *Jones* is believed to be a spy. The description is not part of the proposition. If we apply Russell’s primary scope reading, we get: “There exists a unique man drinking a martini, and he is believed to be a spy.” This is false, because there is no man drinking a martini. But the speaker’s intended proposition—that Jones is believed to be a spy—might be true. Thus, Russell’s theory seems to fail to capture the truth-value of the speaker’s utterance.

Kripke’s response is to insist that the truth-value of the *sentence* is what Russell’s theory gives. The sentence is false. The speaker’s *utterance*—the act of saying something—may be true, but that is because the speaker is using the sentence to make a different claim than the sentence’s literal meaning. Kripke draws a parallel to the case of a speaker who says, “The man over there is my uncle,” pointing at a woman. The sentence is false, but the speaker has successfully communicated that the woman is his uncle. The communication succeeds because the audience recognizes the speaker’s intention to refer to the woman. But this does not change the truth-value of the sentence.

Kripke’s position has a subtle implication for intensional contexts. He argues that the truth-value of the *sentence* in an intensional context is determined by the scope of the description, as Russell said. However, the truth-value of the *speaker’s claim* may be different if the speaker is using the description referentially. This means that there is a gap between sentence meaning and speaker meaning. Kripke acknowledges this gap but argues that it is a general feature of language, not a defect of Russell’s theory. The theory of semantic reference is not supposed to tell us what the speaker intends; it is supposed to tell us what the words mean.

**Section 6: Defending Pragmatic Ambiguity over Semantic Ambiguity**

The central mechanism of analysis in this debate is whether we should treat the referential/attributive distinction as a semantic ambiguity (two distinct meanings of the definite description) or a pragmatic ambiguity (a single meaning used in different ways). I will defend the viability of pragmatic ambiguity, following Kripke, on grounds of parsimony, explanatory power, and theoretical coherence.

First, semantic ambiguity would require positing that a definite description like “the F” has two distinct senses: one quantificational (Russellian) and one referential (directly referring). This would multiply meanings without explanatory necessity. Consider the analogy with indexicals like “I” or “you.” These expressions have a fixed semantic content (the speaker, the addressee) but their reference varies with context. No one would say that “I” has two meanings—one for when I am speaking and one for when you are speaking. The meaning is constant; the context determines the referent. Similarly, a definite description has a constant semantic meaning given by Russell’s analysis. The referential use is a context-dependent phenomenon where the speaker exploits the description to pick out a particular object, regardless of whether the description is satisfied. This is not a different meaning; it is a different use.

Second, pragmatic ambiguity has greater explanatory power because it accounts for the fact that the referential/attributive distinction is not limited to definite descriptions. As Kripke notes, we can use proper names referentially (e.g., calling someone “John” when his name is actually “James” because we are confused). We can use demonstratives referentially (e.g., pointing at a chair and saying “that table” when it is a chair). The phenomenon of speaker’s reference overriding semantic reference is ubiquitous. If we were to treat the referential use of definite descriptions as a semantic ambiguity, we would be forced to treat every expression that can be used referentially as having a second semantic sense. This would lead to an explosion of meanings and would obscure the distinction between semantics and pragmatics.

Third, pragmatic ambiguity is theoretically coherent because it aligns with the standard Gricean distinction between sentence meaning and speaker meaning. Grice argued that what a speaker means by an utterance is not always identical to what the sentence means. The speaker can mean something by uttering a sentence that is false, and the audience can infer the speaker’s meaning through conversational implicatures. The referential use of a definite description is a classic case of this. The speaker utters a sentence with a false description, but the audience infers that the speaker intends to refer to a particular object. The inference is based on the context, the speaker’s gestures, and shared background knowledge. This is a pragmatic process, not a semantic one.

Therefore, I defend the viability of pragmatic ambiguity. It is the correct mechanism for analyzing Donnellan’s distinction. It preserves the simplicity of Russell’s semantic theory while acknowledging the complexity of language use.

**Section 7: Does Speaker’s Reference vs Semantic Reference Prove Russell’s Theory Incomplete?**

The final question is whether the distinction between speaker’s reference and semantic reference proves that Russell’s theory lacks a complete account of reference. I will argue that it does, but not in the way Donnellan intended.

Russell’s theory is a theory of *semantic reference*—it tells us what a definite description denotes in virtue of its meaning, given the facts of the world. It does not purport to be a theory of *speaker’s reference*—that is, a theory of how speakers use descriptions to refer to objects in communicative acts. This is a crucial distinction. If we demand that a theory of descriptions explain all aspects of reference, including speaker intentions, then Russell’s theory is indeed incomplete. It cannot explain how a speaker can successfully refer to an object using a description that is false of that object. It cannot explain how the truth-value of an utterance can differ from the truth-value of the sentence. It cannot explain the pragmatic mechanisms by which audiences identify the speaker’s intended referent.

However, this incompleteness is not a refutation of Russell’s theory as a *semantic* theory. Russell’s theory is correct about the meaning of the sentence. The sentence “The man drinking the martini is a famous philosopher” is false if there is no man drinking a martini. The fact that a speaker can use this sentence to communicate a true proposition about a man drinking water does not change the sentence’s truth-value. It merely shows that communication relies on more than semantic content.

So, does the distinction prove that Russell’s theory is incomplete? Yes, if we interpret “complete account of reference” broadly. Russell’s theory does not account for the full phenomenon of reference in natural language, because it abstracts away from pragmatics. But this is not a flaw in the theory; it is a limitation of its scope. Russell never claimed to be offering a theory of speaker meaning. He was offering a theory of the logical form of propositions expressed by sentences containing descriptions. That theory is correct.

Yet there is a deeper sense in which Russell’s theory is incomplete, and this is where Kripke’s defense is not entirely satisfying. Kripke argues that the semantic reference of a description is given by Russell’s analysis, and the speaker’s reference is a pragmatic matter. But this raises a question: what is the *truth-value* of the utterance? Kripke would say that the sentence is false, but the speaker’s claim is true. However, this creates a bifurcation between the content of the sentence and the content of the utterance. If the utterance is true, then the proposition expressed by the utterance is not the proposition expressed by the sentence. This means that the sentence does not fully determine the truth conditions of the utterance. In that sense, Russell’s theory is incomplete as a theory of *utterance truth*.

Consider a concrete example. Suppose Jones is drinking water from a martini glass. The speaker says, “The man drinking the martini is a famous philosopher.” The speaker intends to refer to Jones. Jones is indeed a famous philosopher. Is the speaker’s utterance true? Intuitively, yes. But Russell’s theory says the sentence is false. Kripke would say that the sentence is false, but the utterance is true because the speaker’s reference is to Jones, and Jones is a famous philosopher. This is a coherent position, but it requires us to distinguish between the proposition expressed by the sentence and the proposition expressed by the utterance. The latter is a function of speaker intention, not just linguistic meaning. This means that the *complete* truth-conditional content of an utterance is not determined by the semantics of the sentence alone. It requires pragmatic supplementation.
