## The Incomplete Symbol and the Substance of Logic: A Critical Evaluation of Gyekye’s Thesis

The history of philosophy is replete with attempts to retroactively legitimize ancient thought through the lens of contemporary logic. Few such attempts are as provocative or as philosophically fertile as Kwame Gyekye’s thesis that Aristotle’s ontology, particularly his doctrine of primary and secondary substances, anticipates the central insight of modern predicate logic—namely, that universal terms function as “incomplete symbols” whose meaning is only realized in relation to the concrete particulars that instantiate them. Gyekye, drawing upon the Fregean distinction between saturated objects and unsaturated functions, argues that Aristotle’s insistence on the ontological priority of primary substances (e.g., this individual man, this individual horse) over secondary substances (e.g., man, animal) and accidents (e.g., white, wise) mirrors the logical priority of singular terms over predicates. In this reading, the universal “man” is not a self-subsistent entity but a logical placeholder, a function that requires an argument to yield a complete proposition. This thesis, if correct, would bridge the chasm between Aristotelian hylomorphism and the extensionalist ambitions of modern logic.

However, this elegant synthesis faces a formidable challenge from George Englebretsen, a contemporary proponent of term logic, who argues that Gyekye has committed a category error of the most fundamental kind: conflating the *semantic meaning* of a proposition with its *ontological truth conditions*. Englebretsen contends that while a universal term like “wisdom” is indeed ontologically dependent on a substance for its existence, it possesses a complete and determinate semantic content independent of any particular instantiation. The incomplete symbol in Frege’s sense is incomplete *in meaning*—an unsaturated function awaiting an argument to produce a truth-value. Aristotle’s universals, by contrast, are complete in meaning; their incompleteness is purely a matter of *being*, not of *signification*. This dialectical tension—between the reductionist pull of Gyekye’s logical reading and the anti-reductionist stance of Englebretsen’s semantic critique—forces us to interrogate the very foundations of Aristotle’s project. Specifically, we must ask whether Aristotle’s prohibition of non-substantial subjects in the *Posterior Analytics* (APo) successfully prevents an infinite regress of predication, or whether this prohibition inadvertently undermines his own theory of definitions and the crucial distinction between synonymous and homonymous predication. Ultimately, I will argue that while Gyekye’s thesis offers a valuable heuristic analogy, Englebretsen’s critique correctly identifies a fatal conflation. Yet, the apparent conflict between ontological reductionism and the necessity of universal knowledge is resolved not by rejecting Aristotle’s ontology, but by recognizing that the APo prohibition operates on an ontological plane distinct from the logical plane of predication, and that the distinction between synonymous and homonymous predication provides the key to reconciling the dependence of universals with the necessity of scientific knowledge.

### I. Gyekye’s Thesis: Aristotle as a Proto-Fregean

To understand Gyekye’s thesis, we must first grasp the fundamental architecture of Aristotle’s *Categories* (Cat.). Aristotle distinguishes between primary substances—individual entities like this man or this horse—and everything else. Secondary substances (species and genera, such as man and animal) and accidents (qualities, quantities, relations, etc., such as white or two-cubits-long) are all said *of* or exist *in* primary substances. Primary substances are the ultimate subjects of predication; they are not predicated of anything else, and they are not in anything else. Everything else, Aristotle insists, is either said of a primary substance (as a universal is said of its particulars) or in a primary substance (as an accident inheres in a subject). This ontological hierarchy is the bedrock of Aristotle’s realism, a direct repudiation of Platonic separate Forms.

Gyekye’s innovation lies in translating this ontological hierarchy into the formal language of modern predicate logic. In the Frege-Russell tradition, a predicate like “…is wise” is an incomplete symbol. It is a function that maps objects to truth-values. The expression “wise” alone signifies nothing determinate; it is an open sentence, a gap awaiting saturation by a singular term. Russell famously extended this to definite descriptions and even to ordinary proper names, arguing that they are disguised descriptions and thus incomplete symbols themselves. Gyekye sees a direct parallel in Aristotle. Just as Frege’s function is incomplete without an argument, so too, Gyekye argues, Aristotle’s secondary substances and accidents are incomplete without primary substances. The universal “man” does not exist *per se*; it exists only in individual men. The accident “white” does not exist *per se*; it exists only in a white object. Therefore, the universal term in an Aristotelian proposition is logically analogous to a predicate function. The primary substance is the logical subject, the saturated object. The proposition “Socrates is wise” is not a concatenation of two complete entities—Socrates and wisdom—but rather the saturation of the incomplete function “x is wise” by the argument “Socrates.” The truth-value of the proposition is determined by whether the object (Socrates) falls under the concept (wisdom).

Gyekye’s reading has several attractive features. First, it explains why Aristotle insists that primary substances are *tode ti* (a “this”), whereas universals are *poion* (a “such”). In modern logic, only singular terms can be the subject of a singular proposition; predicates denote properties or classes, not individuals. Second, it provides a rigorous account of ontological dependence. The universal is not a separate entity but a principle of unity instantiated in particulars. The incomplete symbol, in being saturated, does not create a new entity but rather actualizes a potential. Third, it aligns Aristotle’s project with the anti-Platonic, nominalist or conceptualist tendencies of modern logic. If universals are incomplete symbols, then they have no independent ontological status; they are merely ways of grouping particulars. This reading, if sustained, would make Aristotle a precursor to the extensionalist program of Quine, who famously declared, “To be is to be the value of a variable.” In Gyekye’s Aristotle, to be is to be a primary substance that can serve as the argument for a predicate function.

Yet, this very elegance raises a red flag. Gyekye’s thesis relies on a conflation that Englebretsen is quick to expose. The Fregean incomplete symbol is incomplete *in sense*—it is a function that requires an argument to yield a *proposition*. The predicate “x is wise” is not a complete thought; it is a mere fragment of a thought. Aristotle’s universal, however, is not a fragment of a thought. The term “wise” in “Socrates is wise” has a complete meaning—it signifies the quality of wisdom. It is not an open sentence awaiting an argument; it is a term that, when combined with another term (the subject), forms a proposition. The incompleteness of the universal in Aristotle is not a logical or semantic incompleteness but an *ontological* one. The universal cannot *exist* without a substance, but it can be *conceived* without one. This distinction between existence and conception is precisely what Englebretsen seizes upon.

### II. Englebretsen’s Critique: The Semantic and the Ontological

George Englebretsen, a staunch advocate of term logic as a viable alternative to predicate logic, argues that Gyekye’s thesis is anachronistic and philosophically destructive. His core objection is that Gyekye conflates two distinct levels of analysis: the level of *semantic meaning* (what a term signifies) and the level of *ontological truth conditions* (what must exist for a proposition to be true). Englebretsen insists that a term like “wise” has a complete semantic content. It signifies a determinate property. The proposition “Socrates is wise” has a meaning that is fully intelligible to a speaker who understands the terms “Socrates” and “wise” and the copula “is.” The *truth* of this proposition, however, requires that there exists a particular, Socrates, who instantiates this property. The truth condition is that Socrates *has* wisdom. But this truth condition does not render the term “wise” semantically incomplete. The meaning of “wise” is not a gap; it is a concept.

To illustrate, consider the proposition “The present King of France is bald.” According to Russell’s theory of descriptions, this proposition is meaningful but false, because there is no present King of France. Russell treated the definite description as an incomplete symbol that is analyzed away into a complex of quantifiers and predicates. Gyekye might argue that Aristotle’s universals are similarly incomplete symbols, because they lack independent existence. But Englebretsen would counter that Aristotle’s universals *do* have existence—they exist in re, in the particulars. The problem is not that the universal is a mere logical fiction, but that it is an ontological dependent. The term “wise” is not an incomplete symbol in the sense of lacking a referent; it refers to a universal that is instantiated. The meaning of the term is the universal itself, which is a complete entity (in the sense of being a determinate nature), even if its mode of existence is parasitic on primary substances.

Englebretsen further argues that Gyekye’s reading fundamentally misconstrues the nature of Aristotelian predication. In term logic, a proposition is a combination of two terms—a subject term and a predicate term—united by a copula. The predicate term is not a function; it is a term that is asserted *of* the subject. The proposition “Socrates is wise” asserts that the term “wise” applies to the term “Socrates.” This is a relation of inherence (for accidents) or identity (for essential definitions). The predicate term is semantically complete; it signifies a nature or a quality. The act of predication is an act of *assertion* that links these two complete terms. This is fundamentally different from the Fregean model, where the predicate is a function that, when applied to an object, yields a truth-value. In Frege’s model, the predicate is not a term in the same sense as the subject; it is a different logical category (function vs. object). In Aristotle’s logic, both subject and predicate are terms (horoi), albeit of different ontological statuses.

Therefore, Englebretsen concludes that Gyekye’s thesis is a profound misinterpretation. Aristotle does not anticipate predicate logic; he embodies a distinct, and arguably superior, term logic. The incompleteness of universals is an ontological fact, not a semantic one. To conflate the two is to collapse the distinction between logic and ontology. Logic deals with the structure of propositions and inferences; ontology deals with the structure of reality. While they are related—truth conditions connect them—they are not identical. Gyekye’s error is to think that because universals depend on substances for their existence, they must also depend on them for their meaning. This is a fallacy of equivocation on the word “depend.”

This critique is powerful and, in my view, largely correct. However, it does not fully resolve the deeper tension that Gyekye’s thesis attempts to address: the apparent conflict between Aristotle’s ontological reductionism (only primary substances exist in the primary sense) and the necessity of universal knowledge (science is about universals).
