## The Fragile Scaffolding of Merit: A Critical Evaluation of Scott Hill’s Desertist Solution to Petitionary Prayer

The puzzle of petitionary prayer is one of the most persistent and vexing problems in the philosophy of religion. It arises from a seemingly intractable tension between the classical attributes of God—omniscience, omnipotence, and perfect goodness—and the common religious practice of asking God for specific goods. If God is omniscient, He already knows what we need. If God is omnipotent, He can bring about any state of affairs without our assistance. If God is perfectly good, He will already desire to give us what is genuinely good for us. Given these premises, petitionary prayer appears at best redundant and at worst a form of magical thinking that implies God is either ignorant, impotent, or capricious. The philosophical challenge is to explain how petitionary prayer can be meaningful, efficacious, and non-pointless without compromising divine perfection.

In a recent and provocative attempt to resolve this puzzle, philosopher Scott Hill has defended a "desertist" solution. Hill argues that petitionary prayer, when offered in a respectful and non-demanding manner, generates a minuscule amount of *prima facie* desert. This tiny moral claim, when combined with other factors, enables an agent to *overall* deserve a good that God has, from eternity, already disposed to give. This solution aims to preserve divine immutability while granting prayer a genuine causal and moral role in the divine economy. My task in this essay is to critically evaluate Hill’s desertist framework, engaging with the historical insights of Thomas Aquinas and Gregory the Great to illuminate its strengths and weaknesses. I will argue that while Hill’s solution is philosophically ingenious, it ultimately fails as a robust defense of petitionary prayer because its central mechanism—the generation of desert through effort—is vulnerable to a devastating Rawlsian critique regarding the moral arbitrariness of desert bases. However, I will further argue that the *spirit* of Hill’s solution can be salvaged, but only by abandoning the secular, merit-based language of desert in favor of a Thomistic and Gregorian model of *dispositional fittingness* and *participatory grace*. This reframing, however, requires the very theological and metaphysical premises that Hill seeks to avoid, meaning that his specific desertist mechanism does not successfully rescue petitionary prayer from pointlessness without importing controversial assumptions.

### I. The Puzzle and Hill’s Desertist Mechanism

The standard formulation of the puzzle of petitionary prayer is well-known. Let us posit that God is essentially omniscient, omnipotent, and morally perfect. If God is omniscient, He knows the content of all future prayers and all future needs. If He is omnipotent, He can satisfy those needs without any creaturely cooperation. If He is morally perfect, He desires the best for His creatures. Consequently, the outcome of any petition is already fixed by God’s perfect will and knowledge. If the good will be given regardless of the prayer, the prayer is causally superfluous. If the good is contingent on the prayer, then God’s will is somehow contingent upon human action, which seems to compromise His aseity and immutability. This is the core dilemma.

Hill’s solution attempts to thread this needle. He introduces the concept of *prima facie* desert—the idea that an agent can have a genuine, albeit weak, moral claim to a good based on some action or property. Hill’s central thesis is that the act of petitionary prayer itself, when performed respectfully and without demanding that God comply, constitutes a form of effort or spiritual exercise. This effort, however modest, generates a minuscule amount of *prima facie* desert. The petitioner, by virtue of this effort, becomes slightly more deserving of the good in question. God, from eternity, has decreed that He will grant the good *if and only if* the agent generates this desert. Thus, the prayer does not change God’s mind; it simply fulfills a condition that God has eternally set for the distribution of His grace. The prayer is not pointless because it does real moral work—it makes the agent *overall* deserving of a good they would not otherwise deserve.

Hill is careful to specify the nature of this desert. It is *prima facie*, meaning it is a pro tanto reason that can be outweighed by other considerations. It is *minuscule*, meaning it is extremely small—perhaps so small that it would be negligible in ordinary human moral accounting. Yet, in the divine calculus, this tiny bit of desert is sufficient to tip the scales, allowing God to give the good without violating the principle that He rewards agents according to their merits. Hill’s solution is elegant because it avoids the problematic notion that prayer coerces or manipulates God. Instead, it frames prayer as a cooperative venture in which the human agent genuinely contributes to the moral worthiness required to receive a divine gift.

To understand the philosophical weight of this proposal, we must contrast it with the classical theological tradition. Both Thomas Aquinas and Gregory the Great grappled with the same puzzle, but their solutions took a markedly different trajectory—one that does not rely on the concept of desert at all.

### II. The Classical Alternative: Aquinas, Gregory, and the Dispositional Account

Thomas Aquinas, in his *Summa Theologica* (II-II, Q. 83), addresses the question of whether prayer is necessary or useful. His answer is rooted in his broader metaphysics of providence and secondary causality. Aquinas argues that God’s providence does not merely decree the *ends* of things but also the *means* by which those ends are achieved. God has ordained that certain effects will come about through the cooperation of secondary causes—including human free will. Prayer, for Aquinas, is not a means of informing God of our needs or of changing His eternal decree. Rather, it is a secondary cause that God has chosen to use in the distribution of His gifts. Aquinas writes: “We do not pray in order to change the divine disposition, but in order that, by our prayers, we may obtain what God has disposed to be accomplished by our prayers.” This is remarkably similar to Hill’s claim that God has disposed to give the good *because of* the prayer. However, Aquinas’s justification for this is not based on *desert*. He explicitly argues that prayer is not a meritorious act in the sense of earning a reward. Instead, prayer is *useful* because it *disposes the soul* to receive the divine gift. Prayer cultivates humility, faith, and hope—virtues that make the petitioner a fitting receptacle for grace. The primary effect of prayer, for Aquinas, is internal transformation, not external causation.

Gregory the Great, writing centuries earlier, offers a similarly dispositional account. In his *Pastoral Rule* and *Dialogues*, Gregory emphasizes that prayer is a form of spiritual exercise that aligns the human will with the divine will. He famously states that “prayer is the lifting up of the mind to God,” and he stresses that the efficacy of prayer depends not on the eloquence or quantity of words, but on the *affectus*—the disposition of the heart. Gregory argues that God does not need our prayers to know our needs, but we need prayer to *become* the kind of people who can receive His gifts. Prayer is a means of purification and sanctification. For Gregory, the purpose of petitionary prayer is not to generate a claim against God but to transform the petitioner, making them capable of participating in the divine life.

This classical tradition offers a stark contrast to Hill’s desertist model. For Aquinas and Gregory, prayer is not a mechanism for *earning* a good; it is a mechanism for *becoming* a suitable recipient. The good is given not because the prayer has created a moral debt, but because the prayer has changed the agent’s ontological and moral state. This distinction is crucial for evaluating Hill’s solution. The classical model avoids the need to posit a desert base, and it does so by importing a robust theology of grace and participation. Hill, by contrast, attempts to secularize the mechanism by grounding it in a purely ethical concept—desert. It is here that the philosophical tension arises.

### III. The Rawlsian Objection: The Arbitrariness of Effort as a Desert Base

Hill’s solution hinges on the claim that the effort exerted in petitionary prayer generates *prima facie* desert. But what exactly is the desert base? Hill suggests it is the effort or the act of respectful petition itself. This is a classic meritocratic intuition: if you work for something, you deserve it. However, this intuition faces a formidable challenge from John Rawls’s influential critique of desert bases.

In *A Theory of Justice*, Rawls argues that desert claims are morally problematic when they are based on factors that are themselves arbitrary from a moral point of view. Specifically, Rawls targets the notion that individuals deserve rewards for their efforts or achievements. His argument is that our natural talents, our social circumstances, and even our capacity for sustained effort are largely determined by factors beyond our control—our genetic makeup, our upbringing, our early environment. If I am naturally diligent, disciplined, and focused, I can exert more effort in prayer than someone who is naturally lazy or who has suffered trauma that undermines their capacity for sustained concentration. But I did not choose my natural temperament or my formative environment. These are brute facts about my history. Consequently, the effort I exert is not something for which I can claim ultimate moral responsibility. Rawls concludes that desert claims based on effort are unjustified because they rely on a foundation of moral arbitrariness.

How does this apply to Hill’s solution? The effort to pray respectfully and non-demandingly is not a pure act of free will. It is conditioned by a host of unearned factors. A person raised in a devout, contemplative household may find it easier to pray with genuine humility and focus than someone raised in a secular, chaotic environment. A person with a naturally calm and patient temperament may find it easier to avoid demandingness than someone with a naturally anxious and controlling disposition. A person with a high IQ and extensive theological education may understand the nuances of non-demanding prayer better than someone with intellectual disabilities. All these factors are morally arbitrary—they are not chosen by the agent, and they are not deserved. Therefore, the effort that generates the alleged *prima facie* desert is itself grounded in arbitrary contingencies. If the desert base is arbitrary, then the desert claim is unjustified.

Hill might respond that the desert he posits is *minuscule*, and that the arbitrariness is correspondingly negligible. But this response misses the point. The Rawlsian objection is not about the *quantity* of desert but about the *validity* of the desert base. If the base is morally arbitrary, then the desert claim is categorically invalid, regardless of its size. It is akin to saying that a person deserves a penny because they blinked, but blinking is causally determined by involuntary muscle spasms. The amount of the reward does not fix the problem that the reward is not deserved. Similarly, if the effort to pray is not something the agent can claim as their own in a morally robust sense, then it cannot generate any desert, however small.

Furthermore, Hill’s reliance on effort as a desert base creates a theological problem. If God’s distribution of goods is contingent upon the generation of desert, and if that desert is contingent upon factors that are morally arbitrary, then God’s distribution of goods is also morally arbitrary. This would seem to compromise God’s perfect justice. A God who gives more grace to those who happen to be born with a talent for focused prayer, and less to those who struggle due to no fault of their own, would not be perfectly just. Hill’s solution, when pressed, seems to make God a respecter of arbitrary persons, which is precisely what the New Testament denies.

### IV. A Defense via Reframing: From Desert to Dispositional Fittingness

Given the Rawlsian critique, it appears that Hill’s specific desertist mechanism is philosophically untenable. However, I want to suggest that Hill’s solution can be *rescued* if we are willing to reinterpret what he means by "desert." The rescue requires us to abandon the Rawlsian framework of merit and adopt a Thomistic/Gregorian framework of *dispositional fittingness* or *participatory grace*.

Let us reconsider the act of prayer. When a person prays respectfully and non-demandingly, they are not merely exerting effort; they are performing an act that has a specific *teleological orientation*. They are aligning their will with the divine will. They are expressing trust, humility, and dependence. According to Aquinas, this act of alignment is not a meritorious work that *earns* a reward; it is a *dispositive cause* that makes the agent *fitting* to receive a grace that God freely bestows. The good is given because the agent has become the kind of being who can receive it without harm. For example, if a prideful person were given great wealth, it might destroy them. If a humble person were given the same wealth, it might be a blessing. The prayer of humility does not *deserve* the wealth; it *fits* the person to receive it.

In this reframed model, the "desert" Hill speaks of is not a moral claim against God but a *congruence* between the agent’s state and the good being given. This is not subject to the Rawlsian critique because it does not rely on the agent having earned the good through arbitrary effort. Instead, it relies on the agent’s current *ontological state*—their character, their virtues, their openness to grace. While it is true that our character is partly shaped by arbitrary factors, the *fittingness* of receiving a good is not about moral credit; it is about the *suitability* of the recipient. A person who is humble is *fitting* to receive grace, not because they deserve it, but because they will be able to appropriate it properly. This is a teleological, not an ethical, claim.

Gregory the Great’s emphasis on the *affectus* of the heart supports this reframing. For Gregory, the purpose of prayer is to cultivate the right disposition. The prayer does not change God’s mind; it changes the *pray-er*. The good is given because the prayer has made the person capable of receiving it. This is not a matter of earning but of *becoming*. The effort of prayer is not a desert base; it is a *transformative practice*.

If we adopt this reframing, Hill’s solution becomes much more robust. The prayer does not generate a minuscule *prima facie* desert; rather, it generates a *dispositional state* that makes the agent fitting to receive the good. God, from eternity, has decreed that He will give the good to those who are in this dispositional state. The prayer is causally relevant because it is the *means* by which the agent enters this state. This aligns perfectly with Aquinas’s secondary causality and Gregory’s pastoral theology.

### V. Does This Successfully Rescue Petitionary Prayer from Pointlessness?

The crucial question, however, is whether this reframed version of Hill’s solution—or Hill’s original version—can rescue petitionary prayer from pointlessness *without relying on controversial theological or metaphysical premises*. I will argue that it cannot, and that this is the fatal flaw of Hill’s project.

Let us first consider Hill’s original desertist mechanism. As I have argued, it fails on Rawlsian grounds. But even if we set that objection aside, the mechanism itself is philosophically problematic. To claim that a minuscule amount of *prima facie* desert can *overall* justify God granting a significant good requires a very specific metaphysical framework. It requires that God operates according to a retributive or distributive justice that is sensitive to infinitesimal differences in moral worth. This is not a neutral philosophical premise; it is a highly specific theological claim about the nature of divine justice. Hill might argue that this is a plausible interpretation of God’s justice, but it is certainly controversial. Why would a perfectly loving God condition His gifts on such trivial moral distinctions? Why would God require a tiny bit of desert to give a good He already desires to give? This seems to make God’s love conditional in a way that many theologians would find objectionable.

Now consider the reframed dispositional model. This model successfully avoids the Rawlsian critique because it does not rely on desert. However, it relies on a different set of controversial premises. Specifically, it relies on the premise that God is a personal agent who desires a *relationship* with His creatures, and that He has ordained that our free participation in this relationship—through practices like prayer—is a genuine means of receiving grace. This is a deeply theological premise. It assumes that God values the *process* of human transformation, not just the *outcome*. It assumes that God has chosen to make His grace available through secondary causes, including human acts of faith and devotion. It assumes that there is such a thing as *grace*—a supernatural gift that elevates the human person.

Are these premises "controversial"? In one sense, they are the standard teachings of orthodox Christianity. But in the context of a purely philosophical defense of petitionary prayer, they are highly controversial. A philosopher who wants to defend petitionary prayer without appealing to revelation or specific theological doctrines will find this model unsatisfying. The dispositional model does not provide a *secular* justification for prayer; it provides a *theological* one.

Hill’s original project, I believe, was an attempt to provide a secular, ethical justification for prayer. He wanted to show that prayer is meaningful even if we bracket out specific theological claims about grace and participation. He wanted to ground the efficacy of prayer in a universal moral concept—desert—that any rational agent could accept. This is a noble endeavor, but it fails for two reasons. First, the desert base is invalid due to Rawlsian arbitrariness. Second, even if we accept the desert base, the mechanism itself requires a controversial theology of divine justice that is no less controversial than the theological premises Hill seeks to avoid.

The dispositional model, on the other hand, is theologically honest. It admits that petitionary prayer is only meaningful if we accept a robustly personal and relational God who has chosen to work through human cooperation. This model does not *rescue* petitionary prayer from pointlessness in a purely philosophical sense; rather, it *explains* why petitionary prayer is meaningful *within* a specific religious worldview. This is not a failure of the model; it is a recognition of the limits of philosophical naturalism.

### VI. Conclusion: The Necessity of Theological Premises

In conclusion, Scott Hill’s desertist solution to the puzzle of petitionary prayer is a creative and philosophically sophisticated attempt to resolve a deep theological tension. By positing that respectful and non-demanding prayers generate a minuscule amount of *prima facie* desert, Hill aims to give prayer a genuine causal and moral role in the divine economy while preserving God’s immutability and omniscience. However, this solution ultimately fails as a robust philosophical defense. The central mechanism—effort as a desert base—is vulnerable to the Rawlsian critique of moral arbitrariness. Our capacity for effort is shaped by unearned natural and social contingencies, making it an invalid foundation for desert claims. Consequently, the minuscule desert Hill posits is not merely small; it is categorically unjustified.

I have argued that the *spirit* of Hill’s solution can be salvaged by reinterpreting his "desert" as a Thomistic and Gregorian notion of *dispositional fittingness* or *participatory grace*. In this reframed model, prayer does not *earn* a good; it *transforms* the petitioner, making them suitable to receive a gift that God freely bestows. This model avoids the Rawlsian objection because it does not rely on merit. However, this reframing comes at a significant cost: it requires the explicit acceptance of theological premises about the nature of God, grace, and human participation in the divine life.

Therefore, my position is that Hill’s desertist framework does not successfully rescue petitionary prayer from pointlessness without relying on controversial theological or metaphysical premises. The attempt to find a purely secular, ethical justification for prayer fails because it either relies on an invalid desert base or imports a theology of divine justice that is just as controversial as the premises it seeks to replace. Petitionary prayer is not pointless, but its pointfulness cannot be established through a purely philosophical analysis of desert. It can only be established through a robust theological and relational framework—one that affirms that God is a personal agent who desires our transformation and has graciously chosen to make our prayers a means of receiving His gifts. Hill’s solution is a valuable contribution to the debate, but it ultimately demonstrates the limits of trying to solve a mystery of divine grace with the fragile scaffolding of human merit.
