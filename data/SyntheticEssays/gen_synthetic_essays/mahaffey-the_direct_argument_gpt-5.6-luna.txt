# The Direct Argument, Transfer NR1, and the Limits of One-Path Reasoning

The incompatibility of moral responsibility and determinism has traditionally been defended by means of the Principle of Alternate Possibilities, or PAP. In its familiar formulation, PAP states that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if the agent could have done otherwise. If determinism is true, however, the past and the laws of nature jointly fix every later event. Since no one can alter the past or the laws, no one can alter the events that follow from them. The incompatibilist therefore concludes that, under determinism, agents lack the alternatives required for moral responsibility.

The Direct Argument is designed to reach the same incompatibilist conclusion without relying on PAP. Rather than arguing that determinism deprives agents of alternate possibilities, it argues that determinism transfers nonresponsibility from the past and the laws of nature to every determined action. The argument is therefore “direct” because it purports to show that determinism itself undermines responsibility, independently of any principle concerning what an agent could have done otherwise.

The central question is whether this strategy succeeds. Much turns on the validity of the inference rule usually called Transfer NR1, a transfer principle governing the movement from nonresponsibility for one fact to nonresponsibility for another. A one-path counterexample involving Bob appears to challenge the rule. Bob makes an undetermined choice that prevents a brain abnormality from operating. A lightning strike then occurs, and after the strike there is no alternative possible continuation. Nevertheless, Bob may remain responsible for what happens because his earlier choice prevented the abnormality from determining his conduct. The case is intended to show that an unavoidable event need not transmit its lack of responsibility to an agent’s subsequent action.

The force of this counterexample is not merely that it supplies one more Frankfurt-style example in which responsibility exists without alternatives. Its deeper significance is that it threatens the principle of inference on which the Direct Argument depends. If incompatibilists respond by denying that Bob is responsible, they may have to appeal to a traditional control requirement that is structurally similar to PAP. If they instead insist that Bob is responsible despite the absence of alternatives, they appear to concede the very point that the Direct Argument was meant to establish without reliance on PAP.
