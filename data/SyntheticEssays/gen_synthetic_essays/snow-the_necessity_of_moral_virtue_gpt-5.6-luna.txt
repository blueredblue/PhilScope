# Moral Virtue and Human Happiness in Aristotle: Inclusion, Contemplation, and the Shape of a Mortal Life

Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* appears to offer two different answers to the question of what human happiness is. In the opening books, happiness (*eudaimonia*) is presented as activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, and the virtues discussed at length are predominantly moral virtues: courage, temperance, generosity, justice, and the excellences of practical reasoning that govern conduct and emotion. The good life therefore seems to be a life of character, action, friendship, and participation in a human community. Yet Book X concludes that the happiest life is the life of contemplation. The activity of theoretical wisdom is said to be superior, more continuous, more self-sufficient, more pleasant, and more divine than any practical activity. Aristotle even suggests that we should live, as far as possible, “according to the highest thing in us,” and that a life organized around intellect is not merely one good life among others but the best and happiest life.

This apparent tension has generated a major interpretive controversy. On the so-called inclusive interpretation, defended in different ways by J. L. Ackrill and Timothy Roche, Aristotle’s chief good is a comprehensive or inclusive end: happiness consists in a complete life containing the exercise of all the principal human excellences, including moral virtue, practical wisdom, friendship, and contemplation. On the dominant intellectualist interpretation, associated especially with John Cooper, Aristotle distinguishes the happiness proper to human beings as practical and political agents from a superior or ultimate happiness constituted by contemplation. Moral virtue is indispensable for ordinary human flourishing and for preparing the conditions under which contemplation can occur, but it is not necessarily an intrinsic constituent of the highest good. It belongs to the humanly necessary background of the contemplative life rather than to its defining activity.

The controversy cannot be settled simply by selecting either the practical books or Book X. The central issue is what Aristotle means by the chief good and by the term *teleion*. If *teleion* means a final, dominant end that excludes subordinate activities from the definition of happiness, Book X appears to support intellectualism. If it means a complete or comprehensive end that embraces a plurality of activities ordered within a whole life, then the function argument and the structure of the *Ethics* support an inclusive account. The most plausible conclusion is that moral virtue is both intrinsically valuable within the complete human good and instrumentally necessary for the realization of its highest form. It is not identical with the highest activity, contemplation, but neither is it merely an external means to it. Mortal happiness is a layered unity: theoretical activity provides the highest measure of fulfillment, while moral virtue makes possible the stable, rational, socially embodied life in which human beings can pursue that activity.

## The chief good and the ambiguity of *teleion*

Aristotle begins the *Nicomachean Ethics* with the claim that every craft, inquiry, action, and choice aims at some good. Since ends are arranged hierarchically, the highest end must be that which is chosen for its own sake and for the sake of which all other things are chosen. This end is happiness. Aristotle’s initial description of happiness is formally rather than substantively stated: happiness is “activity of soul in accordance with virtue,” or, if there are several virtues, in accordance with the best and most complete virtue, over a complete life.

The word *teleion* is crucial here. It can mean “complete,” “perfect,” “finished,” or “final.” These meanings are related but not identical. If Aristotle means that happiness is the final end in the sense of a single dominant activity, the later identification of happiness with contemplation seems natural. The activity of contemplation is the end for which practical life and moral virtue might be organized. But if *teleion* means complete in the sense of comprehensive, happiness could be a whole made up of several activities and goods, none of which alone exhausts it.

Ackrill’s inclusive interpretation rests heavily on this second possibility. He argues that Aristotle’s conception of the chief good is not necessarily that of a single activity or possession but of a complete life. A human being does not achieve happiness by performing one isolated act, however excellent. Happiness is the fulfillment of a life whose different elements contribute to its completeness. The reference to a “complete life” in Book I is therefore not an incidental qualification. It indicates that happiness must be assessed across the full range of human functioning: moral conduct, rational deliberation, friendship, political participation, bodily health, and, where possible, intellectual contemplation.

The inclusive reading also draws support from Aristotle’s distinction between ends that are pursued as means and ends that are pursued for their own sake. Some activities, such as medical treatment or military strategy, are subordinated to further ends. But an inclusive end can contain activities that are each worthwhile for their own sake while also being ordered into a larger whole. The fact that contemplation is the highest activity does not entail that all other activities are merely instrumental. A complete human life may include subordinate goods that are genuine constituents of happiness rather than simple tools.

Roche develops a related argument through the function argument. Aristotle asks what the distinctive function (*ergon*) of a human being is. Since life as such belongs to plants and perception belongs to animals, the human function must be an activity of the soul involving reason. The good of a human being will therefore be the excellent performance of this function: activity of soul in accordance with virtue. Roche emphasizes that the argument does not establish one narrowly delimited intellectual function as the whole of the human good. The human function is rational activity in a broad sense. It includes not only theoretical understanding but also the rational governance of desire and action. The moral virtues are therefore not alien additions to the human function. They are modes in which the distinctively human capacity for reason is actualized in conduct.

This point is significant because Aristotle divides virtue into intellectual and moral virtue. Intellectual virtues perfect the rational part directly; moral virtues perfect appetite, emotion, and character through habituation and practical reason. A person who acts courageously, temperately, and justly is not merely satisfying social expectations. Such conduct expresses a specifically human mode of activity: desire and emotion have been brought into conformity with reason. On Roche’s interpretation, the function argument supports a comprehensive account because it points to the excellence of the human being as a whole, not merely to theoretical cognition abstracted from embodied and social life.

The function argument, however, does not by itself settle whether contemplation is the final specification of human happiness. Aristotle distinguishes between reason that commands and reason that contemplates. One might therefore argue that the human function is rational activity generally, while the best human function is theoretical activity specifically. The inclusive interpreter must show that the hierarchy within rational activity does not eliminate the lower forms from the good life. Ackrill and Roche can reply that hierarchy is compatible with inclusion. A musical composition can have a highest note without consisting only of that note. Similarly, contemplation can be the highest activity within a complete life without being the sole constituent of happiness.

## The case for the intellectualist interpretation

The intellectualist reading has formidable textual support. In Book I, Aristotle says that happiness is activity in accordance with the best and most complete virtue. If the highest virtue is wisdom, happiness should be activity in accordance with wisdom. Book X then explicitly identifies the contemplative life with the highest happiness. Contemplation is superior because the intellect is the highest element in us, its objects are the highest objects, its activity is the most continuous, and it is the most self-sufficient. The life of contemplation is also most akin to divine activity, since the gods are characterized by eternal intellectual activity rather than by political action or moral struggle.

Aristotle’s praise of contemplation is not modest. He does not simply say that contemplation is one component of happiness among others. He says that it is “complete” happiness, and he treats the practical virtues as belonging to a secondary kind of happiness. The moral virtues concern the composite human being, especially the relation between reason and desire. But the intellect, insofar as it contemplates truth, is less dependent upon bodily and external conditions. It is closer to the divine and therefore closer to the ideal of self-sufficiency that Aristotle associates with happiness.

John Cooper’s interpretation gives systematic form to this intellectualist pressure. Cooper distinguishes between the happiness attainable by human beings as practical, embodied, and political agents and a higher happiness realized in contemplation. On this view, Aristotle’s ethical theory contains two related but distinct conceptions of the good life. The first is the life of moral virtue, practical wisdom, friendship, and political activity. This is human happiness in the ordinary and properly human sense. The second is the contemplative life, which is superior because it actualizes the best part of the human soul and participates in a mode of activity that is divine in character.

Cooper’s distinction helps explain why Book X can both praise practical virtue and subordinate it. Aristotle need not retract the earlier books. Moral virtue remains indispensable to a good human life. But the contemplative life is not simply the fullest version of the practical life. It is a different mode of activity, organized around theoretical wisdom rather than practical reasoning. The human being is composite: capable of political action and moral formation, but also capable of an intellect whose activity resembles that of the divine. The tension is therefore internal to human nature itself.

On this reading, moral virtue is necessary primarily in two ways. First, it is necessary for the ordinary human good. A person who is unjust, cowardly, intemperate, or vicious cannot live well, even if that person possesses theoretical knowledge. Second, moral virtue and practical order are necessary conditions for a contemplative life. A person consumed by uncontrolled appetites, political conflict, or injustice will not possess the leisure, stability, or psychological order required for sustained thought. Moral education provides the disciplined character that allows reason to govern the rest of the soul.

But these forms of necessity do not make moral virtue part of the defining essence of ultimate happiness. It may be compared to the foundation of a building. The foundation is necessary for the building’s existence, but it is not the same as the activity that takes place in the building. Likewise, practical wisdom and moral virtue may establish the conditions for contemplation without constituting contemplation itself. Once the highest activity is identified, moral virtue becomes subordinate in the final structure of happiness.

The intellectualist interpretation also draws strength from Aristotle’s remarks about self-sufficiency. Contemplation requires fewer external goods than political action. A just or generous action requires other people, resources, and circumstances. Contemplation, by contrast, can continue so long as the intellect and its objects are available. The contemplative person is therefore less dependent on fortune. This seems to support the thesis that contemplation is not merely one part of a composite life but the activity that most fully realizes the formal characteristics of happiness.

## The inclusive response: completeness rather than exclusivity

The inclusive interpretation challenges the inference from superiority to exclusivity. Aristotle can hold that contemplation is the highest activity without holding that moral virtue is not a constituent of happiness. The fact that one good is highest does not imply that other goods are only means. The complete human good may be hierarchically organized, with contemplation at its apex and moral virtue forming part of the life that is perfected by it.

Ackrill’s central insight is that Aristotle’s conception of a final end is not necessarily that of one isolated goal. An end can be final because it is chosen for its own sake while also containing other activities chosen for their own sake. Happiness, on this account, is not like money, which is desired merely as a means to other ends. It is more like a fulfilled life, whose elements are not external instruments but internal aspects of its realization. Courage, justice, friendship, and contemplation can each be worthwhile for their own sake, even though they occupy different positions within the whole.

This interpretation makes better sense of Aristotle’s extensive treatment of moral virtue. The *Nicomachean Ethics* devotes several books to the analysis of character, choice, responsibility, pleasure, justice, friendship, and practical wisdom. If all of these were merely preparatory devices for contemplation, Aristotle’s detailed discussions would seem disproportionately elaborate. The ethical life would be a kind of training program that disappears once its goal is reached. Yet Aristotle repeatedly presents virtuous activities as inherently noble and pleasant. The courageous person does not merely tolerate danger as a necessary step toward wisdom; courage is itself an admirable way of acting. The just person does not practice justice only to gain the leisure required for theory; justice expresses a form of human excellence.

The inclusive view also fits Aristotle’s account of virtue as activity rather than as a passive state. Possessing a virtuous disposition is not enough; happiness requires exercising it. If moral virtue were merely an instrumental condition for contemplation, its exercise would have a secondary and perhaps disposable status. But Aristotle’s definition of happiness as activity in accordance with virtue suggests that the activities of virtue belong to the substance of flourishing. A life in which moral dispositions exist but are never enacted would not be fully happy. Nor would a life devoted to thought but systematically deficient in justice, temperance, and practical judgment obviously satisfy the requirements of Aristotle’s earlier definition.

Roche’s use of the function argument reinforces this point. The human function is not simply the reception of theoretical truths. It is rational activity, and human rationality operates through both thought and action. Practical reasoning is not a degraded substitute for theoretical reason. It is the form of rationality appropriate to a creature whose desires, emotions, body, and social relations require governance. To act courageously or justly is to realize human reason in a distinctively embodied manner. Moral virtue therefore has a claim to intrinsic value because it is an exercise of the human function, not merely an arrangement of circumstances for another activity.

The inclusive reading can also interpret the language of “most complete virtue” without denying the superiority of contemplation. “Most complete” may indicate the virtue that orders or crowns the other virtues, rather than a virtue that excludes them. Practical and moral virtue can be subordinate in rank while still necessary to the completeness of the life. If *teleion* means “complete,” then the chief good is not a single point but a structured totality. Contemplation is the highest element in that totality, but the lower elements remain genuinely good.

## Is moral virtue intrinsic or merely developmental?

The deepest issue is not whether moral virtue is useful. Both sides agree that it is useful and necessary in some sense. The issue is whether its necessity is constitutive or merely developmental. Does moral virtue belong to happiness itself, or does it merely make possible the activity that constitutes happiness?

The intellectualist has a powerful argument. In Book X, Aristotle appears to define happiness by reference to an activity, not by reference to the psychological and social conditions that support it. If contemplation is the activity of the best part of the soul, then the highest happiness should be constituted by contemplation. Moral virtue concerns the non-rational part of the soul insofar as it can listen to reason. It may be indispensable for a human being who must live in the world, but the contemplative intellect, precisely as intellect, does not need moral virtue to think. The truth of geometry or metaphysics is not made more true by courage or justice.

There is also a conceptual difference between theoretical and practical wisdom. Practical wisdom deliberates about what is good for a human being and determines how to act in contingent circumstances. Theoretical wisdom concerns necessary and eternal truths. A person could, in principle, possess knowledge of the highest objects without being morally excellent. If these are distinct intellectual excellences, then moral virtue cannot be a constituent of theoretical activity as such.

Yet this argument risks treating the contemplator as an abstract intellect rather than as a mortal human being. Aristotle does not describe the contemplative person as disembodied. The person who contemplates still has a body, desires, needs, relationships, and a place in a political community. Even if contemplation itself does not logically entail courage or justice, its sustained realization in human life requires a certain practical order. Moreover, Aristotle’s conception of happiness concerns a life, not an instantaneous act of cognition. A life cannot be reduced to its most elevated moments while ignoring the way the person lives when not contemplating.

This is where the inclusive position is strongest. Moral virtue may not be an ingredient in the content of a theorem, but happiness is not merely the occurrence of theoretical cognition. It is the successful activity of a human life. Since human life is embodied and social, practical virtue belongs to its fulfillment. The fact that contemplation is highest does not imply that the rest of life is morally irrelevant. It implies that the different dimensions of human excellence must be ordered around a highest activity.

Still, the inclusive position should not collapse all distinctions. It would be misleading to say that moral virtue and contemplation contribute equally to happiness. Aristotle clearly gives contemplation a special status. Nor should one claim that the contemplative life is simply the sum of moral and intellectual activities. The life has an ordering principle, and that principle is theoretical reason. Moral virtue is intrinsic to the complete life, but it is not constitutive in exactly the same way or at the same level as contemplation. Its intrinsic value is the value of a mode of human activity; its instrumental value is the value of making the highest activity possible.

A useful distinction is therefore between constitutive inclusion and defining priority. Moral virtue is constitutively included in a complete human life, but contemplation has defining priority in the sense that it expresses the highest fulfillment of the human capacity for reason. The moral virtues are not merely external tools, yet they do not determine the ultimate standard of excellence. They are subordinate constituents of a hierarchically ordered good.

## The problem of divine contemplation and mortal life

Book X creates a further difficulty by comparing contemplation to divine activity. If the gods contemplate eternally and without bodily needs, while human beings must act, deliberate, eat, work, form relationships, and confront fortune, how can a mortal life genuinely participate in contemplation? Does Aristotle’s appeal to divine activity imply that the highest human happiness is inhuman?

The intellectualist answer is that human beings should live according to the best element in themselves, even if they cannot sustain contemplation continuously. The divine comparison is normative rather than descriptive. It tells us what should govern human life, not what human beings can accomplish without interruption. The contemplative ideal is therefore a measure of excellence that mortal beings approximate. Practical activity remains necessary because of our embodiment, but it should occupy a subordinate place wherever circumstances permit.

Cooper’s distinction between human happiness and ultimate divine contemplation makes this structure intelligible. Human beings have a practical form of happiness appropriate to their composite nature. They also have access to an activity that exceeds ordinary human functioning because intellect is, in a sense, divine in us. The contemplative life is not the normal condition of human existence, but it is the highest possibility available to human beings. Human happiness is therefore internally divided between what is characteristically human and what is divine-like.

The inclusive interpreter can respond that Aristotle’s divine comparison need not separate contemplation from the rest of human life as sharply as the intellectualist suggests. The fact that contemplation is divine-like does not make moral virtue worthless; rather, it clarifies the order of goods. Human beings imitate divine activity imperfectly and intermittently, but they remain human beings. Their perfection must therefore include the rational governance of mortal conditions. A human being who abandons justice, friendship, and temperance in the name of contemplation would not become divine. Such a person would fail to exercise reason in the practical sphere where human beings are responsible for their conduct.

The notion of a complete life is especially important here. A human life includes periods when one is not contemplating: moments of action, deliberation, rest, friendship, and care. If happiness is a characteristic of a life, then its content cannot be exhausted by the highest activity performed during only some of those periods. Aristotle’s requirement that happiness extend over a complete life indicates that stability and continuity matter. Moral virtue contributes to that continuity by forming a character capable of acting well across changing circumstances. It gives practical shape to the life whose highest activity is contemplation.

This does not mean that every moment of a happy life must be morally virtuous in the same way, still less that practical action is equal to theory. Rather, it means that the life must be coherent. Contemplation cannot simply be juxtaposed with a vicious mode of living. The contemplator must have enough temperance to resist distraction, enough justice to avoid corrupting relationships, and enough practical wisdom to arrange external affairs so that intellectual activity can flourish. The virtues of character establish the harmony within which intellect can function.

## Practical reasoning and theoretical wisdom

The reconciliation of moral virtue with contemplation depends on a proper understanding of practical reasoning. Aristotle’s practical philosophy is not merely a lower form of cognition, useful only because human beings are imperfect. Practical reasoning has its own objects and standards. It concerns action in situations where circumstances are variable and where the agent must determine what is appropriate here and now. The practical intellect does not produce eternal truths, but it produces intelligent action.

Theoretical wisdom and practical wisdom are therefore complementary rather than simply competitive. Theoretical wisdom reveals what is highest and most intelligible. Practical wisdom translates an understanding of human goods into decisions within the world of contingency. A person who knows that contemplation is the highest activity still needs practical judgment to determine how to live, what responsibilities to accept, how to use resources, and how to maintain relationships. Without practical wisdom, the ideal of contemplation could not be embodied in a human life.

Moral virtue is similarly dependent upon practical reasoning. Aristotle’s virtuous person does not merely possess good habits; the person acts from a correct understanding of what is appropriate. Courage is not reckless exposure to danger, and temperance is not the suppression of all pleasure. Justice requires judgment about what is owed to others. The moral virtues thus constitute a domain in which rationality governs desire and action. They reveal the human function as a unity of thought, feeling, and conduct.

The intellectualist might object that this practical order remains only a condition of leisure. Once the conditions for contemplation are secured, moral activity has no further role. But this conclusion underestimates Aristotle’s insistence that virtues are exercised in activity. A person who is just only because justice happens not to interfere with contemplation is not fully just. Virtue is not simply a set of restraints imposed on appetite. It is a mode of acting well, and acting well is itself a form of human fulfillment.

At the same time, the inclusive interpretation must recognize that practical reasoning cannot provide the ultimate criterion of human happiness. Practical wisdom deliberates within the framework of human purposes, but theoretical wisdom concerns the highest objects of knowledge. Aristotle’s hierarchy is not erased by saying that both are valuable. Theoretical wisdom determines what is highest; practical wisdom determines how a mortal agent can live in light of that hierarchy. Moral virtue makes the practical life rationally ordered, while contemplation gives that life its highest orientation.

This relation can be expressed through the idea of subordination without reduction. Moral virtue is subordinate to contemplation because contemplation is a higher activity. But it is not reducible to a means, because the virtuous exercise of practical reason is itself a perfection of human nature. The practical life has its own excellence even when ordered toward a higher end. Thus, Aristotle can consistently say that contemplation is the best happiness and that moral virtue is necessary for happiness without treating moral virtue as merely preparatory.

## External goods, friendship, and the limits of self-sufficiency

The debate is also illuminated by Aristotle’s treatment of external goods. Happiness requires some measure of health, resources, friends, and political stability. The contemplative life may be more self-sufficient than the political life, but it is not absolutely independent of external conditions. A person cannot contemplate while deprived of all bodily necessities or subjected to constant violence. The dependence of human activity on external goods complicates the intellectualist ideal of self-sufficiency.

The same applies to friendship. Aristotle devotes an entire book to friendship and describes it as necessary and beautiful. Friendship is not merely useful for obtaining the leisure required for contemplation. It is itself a form of shared activity and mutual recognition. The highest friendship is grounded in the appreciation of virtue, and friends participate in one another’s lives. A happy person, Aristotle suggests, needs friends not simply as instruments but because human beings are social and because shared activity enhances awareness and enjoyment of the good.

This supports the inclusive conception of happiness. If friendship is a genuine human good, then a life entirely detached from practical relationships would be deficient even if it contained periods of contemplation. The intellectualist can respond that friendship is part of human happiness but not of its ultimate, contemplative form. This is a coherent distinction, but it comes at the cost of positing two levels of happiness rather than one unified chief good.

The question is whether Aristotle’s ethics is fundamentally about the best life possible for human beings or about the ideal activity of the intellect considered independently of ordinary life. The opening of the *Ethics* suggests the former. Aristotle is writing an ethical and political inquiry concerned with how human beings should live. His discussion of legislation, education, habituation, friendship, and justice assumes that a good life must be lived within a community. Book X elevates contemplation, but it does not abolish the practical framework in which ethical life occurs.

The inclusive position is therefore better able to preserve the unity of the work. It explains why the practical books are not merely preliminary and why the contemplative conclusion can nevertheless be decisive. The intellectualist interpretation, by contrast, offers a sharper account of Aristotle’s hierarchy but risks making the ethical theory bifurcated: one theory for human life and another for the divine-like intellect. Cooper’s distinction is valuable precisely because it identifies this bifurcation rather than concealing it. But whether Aristotle ultimately endorses two separate goods or one comprehensive good remains contested.

## A qualified resolution

The best resolution is neither a simple inclusive reading nor an uncompromising intellectualism. Aristotle’s account should be understood as a hierarchical inclusivism. Happiness is a complete human life whose activities are ordered by the superiority of contemplation. Moral virtue is intrinsically valuable as the excellent exercise of practical rationality and as a constituent of a fulfilled human character. It is also instrumentally necessary because it establishes the stable, disciplined, and socially responsible conditions required for contemplation. These two roles are not mutually exclusive.

This conclusion respects Ackrill’s insight that a final end may be comprehensive rather than exclusive. Happiness is not a single episode of thought but the completed activity of a life. It also respects Roche’s interpretation of the function argument: the human function is rational activity in a broad sense, including the rational formation of desire and action. The moral virtues therefore belong to the human good because they actualize human rationality in the practical world.

At the same time, the conclusion preserves Cooper’s distinction. Contemplation is not simply one more moral activity. It is superior because it actualizes the highest and most divine-like capacity in human beings. The person who lives contemplatively realizes a form of happiness that exceeds the practical happiness of political virtue. In this sense, Aristotle does distinguish ordinary human happiness from ultimate or divine-like contemplation. But the superiority of contemplation does not remove moral virtue from the complete good of a mortal life.

Moral virtue is thus intrinsic at the level of the whole life but instrumental in relation to the highest activity. The distinction may be illustrated by the relation between health and athletic performance, though the analogy must be handled carefully. Health is not the performance itself, yet it is also not merely an external tool. It is part of the athlete’s flourishing as an embodied person. Similarly, moral virtue is not contemplation, but it is part of the excellent human life in which contemplation is realized. It gives the life order, integrity, and practical rationality.

This view also explains why Aristotle does not require uninterrupted contemplation. Human beings cannot live as gods. They must act, deliberate, maintain bodies, form friendships, and respond to circumstances. Their happiness is therefore not the possession of an eternally contemplative substance but the organization of a mortal life around the highest available activity. Practical reasoning reconciles mortal existence with contemplation by arranging necessary actions in accordance with a rational hierarchy. The point is not to eliminate practical life but to prevent it from becoming the whole of life.

The contemplative ideal consequently functions as both an activity and a standard. As an activity, it is intermittent and limited in mortal beings. As a standard, it determines the orientation of the life as a whole. Moral virtue allows the agent to inhabit the world without being dominated by its lower pressures. Temperance moderates distractions, courage withstands obstacles, justice orders relations with others, and practical wisdom arranges action. These virtues do not produce contemplation in the way a technical skill produces an artifact. Rather, they create a life capable of receiving and sustaining its highest fulfillment.

## Conclusion

The necessity of moral virtue in Aristotle’s account of happiness depends on what kind of necessity is at issue. If the question is what activity defines the highest form of happiness, Book X gives an intellectualist answer: contemplation is supreme. It is the activity of the highest part of the soul, the most self-sufficient and continuous, and the closest approximation available to divine life. Cooper is therefore right to distinguish the practical happiness of human beings as political agents from the higher happiness of theoretical contemplation.

If the question is what belongs to the complete good of a mortal human life, however, moral virtue cannot be reduced to a mere instrument. Ackrill’s analysis of *teleion* shows that finality may involve completeness rather than exclusivity. Roche’s interpretation of the function argument shows that human excellence includes the rational governance of desire and action, not only the contemplation of eternal truth. Moral virtue is an intrinsically valuable actualization of human rationality, and the *Nicomachean Ethics* would lose much of its meaning if justice, courage, temperance, and friendship were treated merely as disposable preparations.

The most defensible interpretation is therefore hierarchical inclusivism. Contemplation is the highest constituent and governing ideal of happiness, but moral virtue is a genuine constituent of the complete human good and an indispensable foundation for its realization. Practical reasoning does not compete with theoretical wisdom as an equal final end. It mediates the conditions under which theoretical wisdom can be embodied in mortal life. Human beings reconcile practical action with pure intellectual activity by ordering the former toward the latter without denying the independent excellence of virtuous action.

Aristotle’s final vision is consequently neither the life of the isolated sage nor the life of the merely active citizen. It is a life in which the practical virtues make the person worthy, stable, and capable of rational activity, while contemplation crowns the whole by actualizing the highest human power. Moral virtue is necessary not because it is identical with the supreme good, but because no mortal being can reach the supreme good except through the rational and ethically ordered life that moral virtue makes possible.
