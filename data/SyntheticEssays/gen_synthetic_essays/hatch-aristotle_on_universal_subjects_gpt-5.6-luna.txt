# Substance, Predication, and the Limits of Aristotle’s Logical Anticipation

Kwame Gyekye’s interpretation of Aristotle presents an intriguing historical and philosophical thesis: Aristotle’s ontology anticipates certain fundamental ideas of modern predicate logic because it treats universal subjects as incomplete symbols whose significance depends upon their relation to primary substances. On this interpretation, Aristotle’s distinction between primary and secondary substances is not merely a classification within an ancient metaphysics of being. It also supplies an implicit theory of reference. Individual substances—this man, this horse, this tree—function as the ultimate subjects of predication, while universal terms such as “man” or “animal” acquire their logical and semantic force through their predicability of such individuals. Universal terms are therefore not self-subsisting subjects in the same way that particular substances are. Their intelligibility is relational and incomplete.

George Englebretsen’s critique places pressure on this interpretation by arguing that Gyekye conflates two distinct questions. One concerns the semantic meaning of a sentence: what its linguistic components signify and how those components combine. The other concerns the ontological truth conditions of the sentence: what must exist or obtain in the world for the sentence to be true. A universal expression may be semantically meaningful without being an ontologically dependent “incomplete symbol” in anything like the modern logical sense. Conversely, an individual substance may serve as an ontological subject without thereby functioning as the sole or even primary bearer of sentence meaning. The dialectical tension between Gyekye and Englebretsen thus concerns the level at which Aristotle’s theory should be interpreted. Is Aristotle developing an ontology whose structure has logical consequences, or is he developing a theory of predication whose semantic grammar should not be identified with an inventory of entities?

The issue becomes especially acute in Aristotle’s prohibition of an endless regress of predication in the *Posterior Analytics*. Aristotle insists that predication cannot proceed indefinitely. If every subject were itself predicated of another subject, there would be no secure starting point for demonstration or definition. Yet the attempt to stop the regress by making substance the ultimate subject threatens to complicate Aristotle’s theory of universal knowledge. Scientific knowledge concerns universals, causes, and necessary connections, while primary substances are particular and perceptible. If only primary substances are genuine subjects, how can definitions and demonstrations possess the universal character Aristotle requires? Does the prohibition of non-substantial subjects successfully ground knowledge in being, or does it undermine the distinction between synonymous and homonymous predication by reducing the logical role of universals?

A satisfactory evaluation must resist two opposite conclusions. Aristotle is not simply a disguised modern predicate logician, and his ontology cannot be reduced to a theory of sentence structure. Yet Gyekye is right to see that Aristotle’s account of predication anticipates a central modern insight: the logical role of an expression depends upon the position it occupies in a proposition, and the same term can behave differently depending upon whether it is functioning as a subject, predicate, name, or universal expression. Englebretsen is also right that semantic function and ontological status cannot be identified without qualification. The conflict is ultimately resolved not by choosing between ontological reductionism and universal knowledge, but by recognizing that Aristotle operates with several overlapping notions of subject: a grammatical subject, a logical subject, a metaphysical subject, and a subject of scientific inquiry.

## Gyekye’s Thesis and the Aristotelian Background

The opening chapter of Aristotle’s *Categories* distinguishes synonymous, homonymous, and paronymous expressions. Things are synonymous when they share a name and the same account corresponding to that name. A human being and an ox, for example, are called “animals” synonymously if “animal” has the same definition in both cases. Things are homonymous when they share a name but not the same account, as in the different senses of “bank” in modern English or, in Aristotle’s examples, a real human being and a painted human being called “animals.” Paronymous expressions derive their names from other things, as “grammarian” derives from “grammar.”

The distinction is initially linguistic, but it has ontological consequences. Aristotle then distinguishes things that are “said of a subject” from things that are “in a subject.” A universal such as “man” is said of a subject, since it can be predicated of Socrates. A particular quality such as a certain whiteness is in a subject, since it exists in a particular body. Primary substances—individual men and individual horses—are neither said of a subject nor in a subject. They are not predicated of something more fundamental, nor do they exist as properties in another subject. Secondary substances, such as “man” and “animal,” are said of subjects but are not in subjects in the same way that qualities are.

Gyekye’s interpretation draws attention to the asymmetry in this arrangement. Primary substances are ontologically independent subjects, whereas universals are dependent upon their instances for their exercise in predication. A universal can be predicated of many individuals, but it does not appear as an independently existing subject in the same manner as an individual substance. The universal “man” is intelligible as a universal only through its relation to individual men and through its role in predicating what they are. In this respect, Gyekye reads Aristotle as anticipating the idea that certain expressions are incomplete when considered apart from the propositions in which they occur. They require completion by reference to an appropriate object or domain.

The comparison with modern predicate logic must, however, be made carefully. In contemporary logic, a predicate such as “is human” is not ordinarily treated as an incomplete symbol in the same sense as a definite description in Russell’s theory. It is an expression that becomes a proposition when supplied with an argument, as in “Socrates is human” or “Human(x).” Gyekye’s phrase “incomplete symbol” may therefore be understood broadly: a universal term does not possess its full logical function in isolation but acquires determinate application through its connection to subjects. Aristotle’s universal is not a modern predicate variable, and his “said of” relation is not identical with the formal relation between an open formula and its argument. Nevertheless, both theories recognize that predication requires an organized relation between an expression and what it is about.

This anticipation is more than a superficial resemblance. Aristotle’s ontology gives priority to the subject-predicate structure of discourse. Individual substances are the ultimate answers to the question, “What is being predicated of what?” The structure of reality is disclosed through the structure of predication, even if it is not exhausted by it. In this respect, Aristotle stands at the origin of a tradition in which logical form is connected to ontological form. The proposition “Socrates is musical” involves a subject that can exist independently and a predicate that characterizes it. The proposition “Socrates is a man” involves a universal that is said of the individual. The direction of dependence appears to run from the individual to the universal: the universal is instantiated in individuals, whereas the individual is not simply a property of the universal.

Gyekye can therefore claim that Aristotle’s account has a proto-logical sophistication. It distinguishes between expressions that function as ultimate subjects and expressions whose role is derivative. It also recognizes that a universal’s significance is not equivalent to the existence of a separate universal object standing alongside its instances. The universal is conceptually and predicatively available because there are individuals of which it can be said.

## Englebretsen’s Critique: Meaning and Truth Conditions

Englebretsen’s criticism challenges the movement from Aristotle’s ontology to modern logical semantics. The central worry is that Gyekye treats the semantic incompleteness of a universal expression as if it were the ontological dependence of a universal entity. These are not the same claim.

Consider the sentence “Socrates is a man.” Its meaning is not simply the sum of two independently complete items, “Socrates” and “man.” The predicate “is a man” has a function that is completed by being applied to a subject. In that semantic sense, it is incomplete when detached from a sentence. But it does not follow that the property or universal humanity is ontologically dependent on Socrates in the same way that the grammatical predicate depends upon a subject for sentence completion. Nor does it follow that the truth of the sentence is secured merely by the existence of Socrates. Socrates might exist without being a man if, for example, the term “man” were applied differently or if the world were arranged otherwise. The truth condition involves Socrates’ belonging to a certain kind.

Englebretsen’s point is that semantic dependence concerns the role an expression plays in language, whereas ontological dependence concerns what must exist for a fact to obtain. The expression “man” may be meaningful even in a context in which no human beings exist. A speaker could understand the term while imagining an empty world. Likewise, the sentence “There are no humans” is meaningful precisely because it uses the universal concept “human,” even though its truth would require the absence rather than the presence of human beings. Thus, the semantic meaningfulness of a universal does not require actual instantiation.

This criticism exposes an ambiguity in Gyekye’s use of the analogy with incomplete symbols. In modern logic, an expression may be semantically incomplete because it requires an argument, but this does not make its referent ontologically incomplete. The open formula “x is human” requires assignment of a value to “x” for evaluation as true or false, but the predicate itself can be defined independently of any particular individual. In some logical systems, predicates can even be interpreted as empty. The predicate “is a unicorn” remains meaningful although it may have no actual instances. A purely extensional account would treat its extension as the empty set, but that still does not make the expression meaningless.

Aristotle’s own theory is not straightforwardly extensional. Definitions are accounts of what a thing is, and scientific knowledge depends upon necessary relations rather than mere lists of actual instances. Yet this makes the distinction between meaning and truth conditions even more important. The definition of “triangle” does not become meaningful only because particular triangles exist. Rather, particular triangles are recognized as triangles because the concept and definition are already available. The universal is not simply an ontological shadow cast by individual substances. It has an explanatory and epistemic role that cannot be reduced to its being predicated of them.

Englebretsen therefore introduces a productive dialectical tension. If Gyekye is correct that Aristotle treats universals as incomplete in relation to primary substances, then Aristotle seems to anticipate modern logic’s concern with the conditions under which terms acquire determinate function. But if Englebretsen is correct that Gyekye moves too quickly from semantic dependence to ontological dependence, then the interpretation risks making Aristotle more nominalist or reductionist than he actually is. Aristotle’s universal knowledge cannot be explained if universals are merely incomplete names whose content is exhausted by their instances.

The tension is not eliminated by saying that Aristotle means both things. The difference between semantic meaning and ontological truth conditions must remain visible. Still, the distinction does not force a complete separation between language and being. Aristotle’s theory presupposes that linguistic predication is answerable to real structures. The fact that “man” is semantically meaningful without any particular man being present does not show that the term lacks ontological significance. It shows only that the semantic and ontological relations operate at different levels.

## The Regress of Predication in the *Posterior Analytics*

The *Posterior Analytics* deepens the problem by connecting predication to demonstration. Scientific knowledge requires demonstration from first principles. Demonstrations proceed through middle terms, and definitions articulate the essence of the things about which science reasons. But if every subject in a predication were itself predicated of another subject, the explanatory chain would never arrive at a first subject. There would be no stable basis for demonstration.

Aristotle’s concern resembles an argument against an infinite regress. Suppose that “musical” is predicated of “man,” “man” of “animal,” “animal” of “living thing,” and so on. If each term in the chain were merely a predicate of something else, then no term would serve as an ultimate subject. The chain would lack a subject whose nature is being characterized. Aristotle therefore insists that predication must terminate in substance. Some things are subjects without being predicates of further subjects. This is the metaphysical role of primary substance in the *Categories* and the logical role of ultimate subjects in the *Posterior Analytics*.

The argument has real force. Scientific explanation cannot consist of an endless sequence of answers to the question “What is this?” If every answer referred to a further characterization without ever reaching the thing itself, then the account would never explain anything. The regress would be especially destructive to definition. A definition must say what something is, not merely state that it belongs under another term that itself remains undefined. The termination of predication is therefore necessary if essence and demonstration are to be intelligible.

Yet the apparent solution also creates a difficulty. Aristotle’s science is universal. Demonstration does not prove merely that this individual possesses a property; it establishes that a property belongs necessarily to a kind as such. The conclusion “this triangle has angles equal to two right angles” is not scientific in the fullest sense. The scientific conclusion is that every triangle, insofar as it is a triangle, has this property. Scientific knowledge therefore concerns universals, while ultimate subjects in the ontology appear to be particulars.

This produces a structural tension between the metaphysical subject and the scientific subject. Primary substances are the ultimate bearers of properties, but universal substances or forms are what definitions and demonstrations principally investigate. If Aristotle treats only primary substances as genuine subjects, then scientific knowledge seems forced to rest on what it cannot itself directly know as universal. If, by contrast, universals are allowed to function as subjects in their own right, then the regress threatens to resume. The universal “animal” may be predicated of man, but if it is also a subject of predication, what is it a subject of? Is it an independent entity, a concept, a form, or merely a linguistic term?

The distinction between different types of predication helps Aristotle respond. Not all predicates are related to their subjects in the same way. Some are accidental, some essential, and some express genus, species, or differentia. “Socrates is musical” states an accidental predication. “Socrates is a man” states an essential predication in the sense that being a man belongs to what Socrates is. “Man is an animal” expresses a relation between species and genus, but it need not mean that “animal” is an independently existing subject in the same manner as Socrates.

The regress argument is therefore strongest when directed against the demand that every predication be grounded in a prior predication of exactly the same type. Aristotle can distinguish a chain of linguistic predication from an order of ontological dependence. “Man” may be predicated of Socrates, and “animal” of man, but the relationship between animal and man is not simply another instance of the relationship between a quality and an individual substance. The logical hierarchy of genus and species is not reducible to a series of ontological subjects and predicates.

This distinction also shows why the prohibition of non-substantial subjects must be interpreted narrowly. Aristotle cannot mean that no universal can ever appear as a grammatical or logical subject. He routinely discusses propositions such as “man is an animal,” and scientific reasoning often treats mathematical objects, species, genera, and properties as subjects of inquiry. The prohibition must instead concern the attempt to make every term in an explanatory order a subject that requires another subject beneath it. It blocks an infinite regress of ultimate grounding, not the ordinary use of universals as subjects in discourse.

## Does the Prohibition Undermine Definition?

The deepest challenge concerns Aristotle’s theory of definition. Definition is concerned with the essence of a thing, and Aristotle commonly analyzes definitions through genus and differentia. “Man” may be defined as a rational animal. But if “man” is a universal and universals are not primary substances, what exactly is being defined? Is the definition of man a definition of a universal entity, a common nature instantiated in individuals, or a term used to classify individuals?

Aristotle’s answer is deliberately complex. Definitions are not primarily of accidental features. They state what a thing is. In the case of natural substances, the species is more definitionally fundamental than the individual. We can define man, horse, or triangle, but it is difficult to define Socrates as an individual in the same strict sense. The individual is known through the universal form or essence that makes it the kind of thing it is. Thus, Aristotle’s theory of definition requires universals to possess a reality and intelligibility beyond their status as mere predicates.

At the same time, Aristotle refuses to treat universals as substances in the same way as individuals. In the *Categories*, the species “man” is called a secondary substance, but it is not a primary substance. It is “substance” in a derivative and classificatory sense. It tells us what the primary substance is, but it does not exist as a separate individual alongside Socrates. This intermediate status is crucial. The universal is neither a mere sound nor an independently existing object of the Platonic kind. It is the form or essence that can be present in many individuals and expressed in a definition.

If one interprets “incomplete symbol” as implying that universals have no ontological reality beyond their actual instances, Aristotle’s theory of definition is undermined. The definition of man would become a convenient linguistic summary of the individuals called men. Such a summary could support classification, but it would not explain why certain predicates belong necessarily to men. It could not ground the universal and necessary character of science. The distinction between essential and accidental predication would also be weakened. “Man is an animal” would differ from “Socrates is musical” only in the number of individuals to which the terms apply, not in metaphysical status.

Aristotle’s distinction between synonymous and homonymous predication shows why that reduction is inadequate. When “animal” is predicated synonymously of man and horse, the same account of animality applies in both cases. The universal term does not merely have two similar uses; it retains one definitional content across its instances. Homonymous predication, by contrast, involves a shared name with different accounts. The same word may be applied to different things without establishing a common nature.

If universals were only incomplete expressions dependent upon individual substances, the difference between synonymous and homonymous predication would be difficult to secure. One might say that synonymous predication occurs when a word is applied to individuals in a sufficiently regular way, while homonymous predication occurs when the applications differ. But this would make synonymy a fact about linguistic convention or extension rather than a relation grounded in a common essence. Aristotle’s insistence on the same account indicates that synonymy is not merely a matter of the same word applying to multiple things. It requires a shared intelligible structure.

The danger, then, is that ontological reductionism destroys the basis for universal knowledge. If only individuals are real, and universals are semantically incomplete devices, then science can at best record patterns among individuals. It cannot know necessary truths about what things are. Aristotle’s science, however, requires more than inductive generalization. It seeks causes and essences. The prohibition of non-substantial subjects cannot therefore mean that universals are ontologically empty. It must mean that universals do not constitute ultimate substances independently of the individuals and forms in which they are realized.

## Synonymy, Homonymy, and the Unity of Predication

The distinction between synonymy and homonymy is not an incidental semantic classification. It is a test of whether Aristotle can preserve a unity between language, thought, and being.

In synonymous predication, a universal term is said of many things according to the same account. “Man” is said of Socrates and Plato in the same sense. This does not require the existence of a separate universal man hovering beyond them. It requires that Socrates and Plato possess the same form or essence in the relevant respect. The universal is real as a common intelligible structure, even if it is not a numerically one individual substance.

Homonymous predication presents the opposite case. “Healthy” may be said of an animal, a diet, and a complexion, but not in precisely the same way. A diet is healthy because it produces health; a complexion is healthy because it manifests health; an animal is healthy because it possesses health. The term has a unified reference through a focal relation, but not through one definition equally applicable to all its subjects. Aristotle’s later discussions of focal meaning develop this insight.

Both forms of predication challenge a simplistic reduction of universals to incomplete symbols. In synonymous predication, the universal must preserve a common definition across multiple subjects. In homonymous predication, the term retains enough unity to support meaningful discourse even though the account varies. In neither case is meaning exhausted by the existence of a particular primary substance. The universal expression has a semantic and explanatory organization that exceeds its occurrence in any one proposition.

Nevertheless, Aristotle’s ontology prevents this semantic unity from becoming free-floating. The common nature is not an independently existing subject in the way a particular substance is. It is realized in individual substances and known through abstraction or definition. This is the point at which Gyekye’s insight can be retained without accepting a reductive interpretation. Universals are dependent upon primary substances for their concrete realization, but they are not dependent upon them for every aspect of their intelligibility. They are ontologically inseparable from individuals in the natural world, yet conceptually and scientifically they have a relative autonomy.

This relative autonomy is also what allows Aristotle to distinguish the subject of a proposition from the subject of an ontology. In “Socrates is a man,” Socrates is the primary subject in the proposition. In “Man is an animal,” “man” is the grammatical subject, but it functions as a species under a genus. In a scientific discussion, “triangle” may be the subject of a theorem, although no individual triangle is being discussed. The same expression can occupy different roles without generating a contradiction. The regress arises only if these roles are collapsed.

## Ontological Reductionism and Universal Knowledge

The apparent conflict can now be formulated precisely. Ontological reductionism says that only primary substances are ultimately real, while universals are dependent features of thought or language. Universal knowledge says that science must grasp general essences and necessary relations. If the first claim is interpreted strongly, the second becomes impossible. If the second is interpreted strongly, the first must be qualified.

Aristotle’s own position is not a simple version of either extreme. He rejects Platonic separation, according to which universals exist as independent entities apart from sensible things. But he also rejects an account on which universals are arbitrary names imposed upon radically unrelated individuals. The form is in the individual substance, and the intellect can know it as universal. The universal is not a separate thing, but neither is it nothing.

This position permits Aristotle to maintain both the priority of primary substances and the objectivity of universal knowledge. Primary substances are ontologically primary because they exist independently and serve as bearers of properties and forms. Species and genera are secondary substances because they answer the question of what primary substances are. They are not primary bearers of existence, but they are primary bearers of intelligibility. The individual exists first in the order of concrete being; the universal is prior in the order of explanation and knowledge.

The *Posterior Analytics* presupposes this double priority. Demonstration requires subjects that are not endlessly predicated of other subjects, but it also requires universal terms that can serve as premises and conclusions. The ultimate subject of predication grounds the chain of being, while the universal form organizes the chain of knowledge. Aristotle therefore need not choose between the ontological priority of individuals and the epistemic priority of universals. The two priorities belong to different orders.

This answer also limits the force of Englebretsen’s criticism. It is correct that semantic meaning cannot be read directly as ontology. But Aristotle’s theory does not treat meaning as an isolated linguistic phenomenon either. A definition has meaning because it expresses an essence; a true predication is true because a form or property belongs to a subject in reality. The separation between meaning and truth conditions is necessary, but not absolute. Meaning provides the intelligible content through which truth conditions can be grasped, while ontology supplies the structure that makes true predication possible.

Gyekye’s thesis is therefore best understood as an analogy about structural dependence, not as a claim that Aristotle possessed modern formal semantics. Aristotle anticipates predicate logic insofar as he recognizes that predicates require subjects, that universal expressions can be applied to multiple particulars, and that logical analysis must distinguish the function of an expression from the material constitution of the entities it signifies. He does not anticipate predicate logic insofar as modern logic abstracts from substantial form, essence, and causal explanation. Modern predicate logic treats its variables and predicates formally; Aristotle treats subjects and predicates as elements in a metaphysical order.

## The Dialectical Resolution

The tension between Gyekye and Englebretsen can now be resolved dialectically. Gyekye begins from an important truth: Aristotle’s ontology is organized around dependence relations that resemble the argument-predicate structure of modern logic. Primary substances are not predicated of further subjects, while universals are said of subjects. A universal’s logical role is incomplete unless it is related to an appropriate subject. This makes Aristotle a significant predecessor of later theories of predication.

Englebretsen identifies the danger in turning this resemblance into an identity. The semantic incompleteness of a predicate is not the same as the ontological dependence of a universal. A term can be meaningful without actual instantiation, and a sentence’s truth conditions cannot be inferred merely from the grammar of its subject and predicate. Aristotle’s “said of” relation therefore cannot be equated with the formal semantics of a modern predicate.

The synthesis is that Aristotle’s distinctions operate across, rather than within only one, level of analysis. The language of predication reflects an ontological asymmetry, but it does not mirror ontology without remainder. The universal is semantically available as a predicate because it expresses a form that is instantiated in substances; it is ontologically non-primary because it does not exist as an independent individual; and it is epistemically indispensable because science apprehends the form as universal. These claims are compatible once semantic, ontological, and epistemic dependence are distinguished.

The prohibition of non-substantial subjects succeeds in preventing an infinite regress only if it is understood as a demand for ultimate explanatory grounding, not as a ban on every universal subject in discourse. Aristotle must allow universals to serve as subjects of definition, demonstration, and inquiry. What he denies is that their role as subjects entails that they are primary substances requiring further ontological subjects beneath them. The subject of a theorem is not necessarily the ultimate subject of existence.

This qualified prohibition does not undermine the theory of definitions. On the contrary, it makes definition possible by distinguishing between what is ultimately instantiated and what is universally intelligible. A definition captures the essence of a species, while the species is realized in primary substances. The definition is not a mere report about the use of a word; it articulates the form through which individuals are what they are. Nor does the prohibition abolish synonymy. Synonymous predication remains grounded in the common account of a form, whereas homonymous predication reflects the unity of a name across different accounts or relations.

The resolution is therefore neither a victory for Gyekye nor a refutation by Englebretsen. Gyekye’s thesis is persuasive as a claim about formal anticipation, provided “incomplete symbol” is used metaphorically or structurally. Englebretsen’s critique is decisive against any interpretation that identifies semantic dependence with ontological nonexistence. Aristotle’s philosophy retains a layered conception of subjecthood that neither modern reductionism nor a simple linguistic formalism can capture.

## Conclusion

Aristotle’s ontology anticipates modern predicate logic only in a limited and transformed sense. His analysis of primary and secondary substances recognizes that some expressions function as ultimate subjects while others are predicatively dependent upon them. Universal terms such as species and genera are not independent individuals; they are said of primary substances and acquire their concrete application through those substances. This structure provides a genuine predecessor to the modern insight that predicates are incomplete outside the propositions in which they are applied.

Yet Aristotle does not thereby reduce universals to meaningless or merely instrumental signs. Englebretsen rightly warns that the meaning of a sentence and its ontological truth conditions are distinct. The intelligibility of “man” does not depend upon the presence of a particular man, and the truth of “Socrates is a man” involves more than the existence of Socrates. It involves the real possession of a form or essence. Semantic dependence does not entail ontological reduction.

Aristotle’s prohibition of non-substantial subjects is successful against the regress of predication only when directed at the demand for ultimate explanatory grounds. It cannot prohibit universals from serving as grammatical, logical, or scientific subjects, since definitions and demonstrations necessarily concern universal forms. The ultimate subject of being and the subject of scientific knowledge are not identical. Primary substances ground instantiation and existence; universals ground intelligibility, classification, and necessary demonstration.

The distinction between synonymous and homonymous predication confirms this layered account. Synonymy requires a common definition and therefore a real unity of form; homonymy permits shared names without a single account. Neither distinction can be preserved if universals are reduced to incomplete symbols in a purely semantic or nominalist sense. But neither requires universals to exist as separate substances. Aristotle’s middle position—universal form immanent in individuals and abstractable by intellect—allows him to affirm both ontological dependence and universal knowledge.

The resulting philosophy is not modern predicate logic in ancient dress. It is a metaphysics of substance whose account of language contains proto-logical insights. Gyekye reveals the structural proximity between Aristotle’s subject-predicate ontology and later logical theories; Englebretsen prevents that proximity from becoming an anachronistic identification. The conflict is resolved by distinguishing the orders of being, meaning, and knowledge while acknowledging their Aristotelian interdependence. Primary substances terminate the regress of ontological predication, but universal forms terminate neither into nothingness nor into separate entities. They remain the indispensable medium through which what is individually real becomes universally knowable.
