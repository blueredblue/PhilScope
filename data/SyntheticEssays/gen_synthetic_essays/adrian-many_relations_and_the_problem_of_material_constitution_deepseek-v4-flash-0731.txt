**Title:** The Form of the Problem: Evaluating the Five-Place Constitution Relation in Hylomorphic Partism

**Introduction**

The ancient doctrine of hylomorphism, which holds that composite objects are structured unities of matter and form, has experienced a significant resurgence in contemporary analytic metaphysics. In its modern, mereologically-inflected guise—Hylomorphic Partism—this view seeks to reconcile the intuitive appeal of composition as a genuine metaphysical operation with the rigorous demands of formal mereology. However, this synthesis faces two notoriously intractable puzzles: the problem of the many and the puzzle of the statue and the clay. These puzzles expose a deep dialectical tension in any three-dimensionalist Partist View, particularly the influential account defended by Hud Hudson. In response, some contemporary hylomorphists have proposed a novel formalization involving a *five-place constitution relation*, which relativizes parthood to a specific form (the “in-F” relation). This essay will evaluate whether this five-place relation successfully resolves the dialectical tension that defeats Hudson’s account. I will argue that while the five-place relation offers a sophisticated and initially promising solution to the statue/clay puzzle by decoupling identity from co-location, it ultimately fails to resolve the underlying tension. Specifically, it fails to provide a principled solution to the problem of the many, it relies on a conception of form that is either too weak to serve as a principle of unity or too strong to avoid a vicious regress, and its commitment to multiple hylomorphic compounds occupying the exact same spacetime region leads to an unacceptable ontological overpopulation. Consequently, the introduction of the “in-F” relation is an ad hoc modification that masks, rather than resolves, the fundamental conflicts inherent in a three-dimensionalist hylomorphic mereology.

**Section 1: The Dialectical Tension in Hudson’s Three-Dimensionalist Partist View**

To understand the purported solution offered by the five-place relation, we must first grasp the precise nature of the dialectical tension it aims to dissolve. Hud Hudson’s view, which he develops in works like *The Metaphysics of Hyperspace* and various papers on mereology, is a form of three-dimensionalist Partism. He accepts that objects are three-dimensional, enduring through time by being wholly present at each moment. He also accepts a classical or quasi-classical mereology, where parthood is a primitive, transitive, and antisymmetric relation, and where composition is either unrestricted or governed by a specific principle (such as maximality). Within this framework, the puzzle of the statue and the clay presents a stark dilemma.

Consider a statue, *Statue*, and the lump of clay, *Clay*, that constitutes it at time *t*. They occupy the exact same spacetime region. They share all the same physical parts—the same molecules, atoms, and particles. However, they appear to have different modal and persistence conditions. *Statue* cannot survive being squashed into a ball; if it is squashed, it ceases to exist. *Clay* can survive being squashed; it remains the same lump of clay. By Leibniz’s Law, if *Statue* and *Clay* are identical, then they must share all properties. Since they do not share the property of “can survive being squashed,” they cannot be identical. Therefore, they must be distinct objects.

If they are distinct, however, the mereological relationship between them becomes problematic. Since they occupy the same region and share all material parts, it seems natural to say that *Clay* is a proper part of *Statue* (or vice versa). If *Clay* is a proper part of *Statue*, then *Statue* has *Clay* as a part. But if *Statue* is squashed, *Clay* survives. This means that a whole (*Statue*) can cease to exist while one of its proper parts (*Clay*) continues to exist. This is not inherently contradictory—a car can be destroyed while its engine survives—but it raises a deeper issue. The proper part *Clay* has the property of surviving the squashing, while the whole *Statue* does not. The whole is composed of the part, yet the whole lacks a property that its part possesses. More critically, if *Clay* is a proper part of *Statue*, then *Statue* is not identical to *Clay*, but *Statue* is *made of* *Clay*. This suggests that *Statue* is a distinct entity that has *Clay* as a constituent. But what is *Statue* over and above *Clay*? If it is just the sum of *Clay* plus some other parts, we have a problem of infinite regress. If it is just *Clay* plus a property, then *Statue* is not a mereological sum but a complex of a part and a property, which is ontologically odd.

Hudson attempts to solve this by relativizing parthood to times. He might say that *Clay* is a part of *Statue* at *t*, but not at *t’* (after squashing). This handles the persistence condition. However, the problem of the many exposes a deeper issue. Consider the lump of clay. It is composed of billions of particles. There are millions of aggregates of these particles that differ by only one particle. Each of these aggregates is a candidate for being the lump of clay, and hence a candidate for being the material constituent of the statue. Which one is the *real* lump? Hudson’s maximality principle might say that only the maximal aggregate (the one containing all the particles) is the lump. But this is arbitrary. Why should maximality be privileged? The problem is that there is no non-arbitrary way to select one aggregate over another without invoking some principle that is not purely mereological.

The dialectical tension that defeats Hudson’s view is thus twofold. First, the statue/clay puzzle forces a choice between identity (violating Leibniz’s Law) and distinctness (leading to an unexplained co-location and a problematic proper parthood relation). Second, the problem of the many shows that mereological principles alone cannot identify which aggregate of particles constitutes the statue or the clay. Hudson’s Partist View, which relies on standard mereology, cannot resolve this tension because it treats parthood as a single, univocal relation that is not sensitive to the kind of object being composed. The introduction of a form-based, five-place relation is intended to address this exact shortcoming.

**Section 2: The Five-Place Constitution Relation and the “in-F” Parthood**

The proposed Hylomorphic Partist solution introduces a five-place constitution relation. Instead of the standard two-place parthood relation (*x* is a part of *y*) or the three-place temporal relativization (*x* is a part of *y* at *t*), the hylomorphist posits a relation of the form:

*C*(*x*, *y*, *r*, *t*, *F*)

This is read as “*x* constitutes *y* at region *r* and time *t* in virtue of the form *F*.” Alternatively, we can define a relativized parthood relation:

*P*(*x*, *y*, *r*, *t*, *F*) – “*x* is a part of *y* at region *r* and time *t* in virtue of form *F*.”

The crucial novelty is the final term, *F*, which represents the substantial form of the compound. The “in-F” relation is a parthood relation that is relativized to a specific form. This allows the hylomorphist to say that the clay particles are parts of the statue *in virtue of the statue-form*, while the statue is a part of the clay *in virtue of the clay-form*. More precisely, we can say that the matter (the clay) constitutes the statue in virtue of the statue-form, and the statue constitutes the clay in virtue of the clay-form. These are distinct constitution relations.

How does this resolve the statue/clay puzzle? The hylomorphist can now assert that *Statue* and *Clay* are distinct objects. They are distinct because they have different forms. However, they do not stand in an unrelativized proper parthood relation to each other. Instead, the clay is a *part of* the statue *in virtue of the statue-form* (i.e., the clay is the matter informed by the statue-form). The statue is a *part of* the clay *in virtue of the clay-form* (i.e., the statue is a mode of the clay). This avoids the asymmetry problem. Neither is a proper part of the other in an absolute sense; they are parts of each other relative to different forms. This is a form of *relativized parthood* that is not merely temporal but also formal.

The modal properties are also handled. The statue has the property “cannot survive squashing” *qua* statue (i.e., in virtue of the statue-form). The clay has the property “can survive squashing” *qua* clay (i.e., in virtue of the clay-form). Since these are distinct objects with distinct forms, they have distinct properties. Leibniz’s Law is not violated because they are not identical. The tension between identity and distinctness is dissolved by positing distinctness, but the problematic proper parthood relation is replaced by a formal relativization.

**Section 3: Does the “in-F” Relation Resolve the Statue/Clay Puzzle?**

At first glance, the five-place relation appears to successfully navigate the statue/clay dilemma. It avoids the Scylla of identity (which violates Leibniz’s Law) and the Charybdis of unrelativized proper parthood (which leads to the whole lacking properties of its parts). The introduction of the form as a relativizer allows for a clean separation of the two objects. The statue is not identical to the clay, but it is not merely a sum of clay plus a property either; it is a distinct hylomorphic compound.

However, a closer examination reveals that this resolution is largely verbal. The hylomorphist has simply defined a new relation, *P*(*x*, *y*, *r*, *t*, *F*), and asserted that it holds in the relevant cases. But what grounds this relation? Why is it true that the clay is a part of the statue *in virtue of the statue-form*? The answer, presumably, is that the statue-form organizes the clay into a statue. But this is just a restatement of the problem. The puzzle of the statue and the clay is not merely about the *terminology* of parthood; it is about the *ontological status* of the two objects. Are they two objects or one? The hylomorphist says they are two. But if they are two, then we need to know what makes them two. The answer is “the form.” But what is the form? Is it a part? Is it a property? Is it a separate entity?

If the form is a property of the matter, then the statue is just the clay with a certain property. But then the statue is not a distinct *object*; it is a mode of the clay. This collapses into a form of bundle theory or a deflationary account of co-location. If the form is a separate entity, then it must be a part of the statue. But then we have a three-part composition: matter, form, and the composite. This leads to a regress—what unifies the matter and the form into a composite? We would need a second form, and so on.

The five-place relation does not answer these questions. It simply stipulates that the relation *C*(*x*, *y*, *r*, *t*, *F*) holds. The dialectical tension in Hudson’s view was not just about the *form* of the parthood relation, but about the *metaphysical ground* of distinctness. Hudson’s view failed because it could not explain why two objects with the same parts could have different persistence conditions. The hylomorphic view explains this by appealing to form. But the appeal to form is only explanatory if we have a clear account of what form is and how it relates to matter. The five-place relation provides a formal notation but not a metaphysical explanation. It is a schema for a solution, not a solution itself.

**Section 4: The Problem of the Many and the Selection Problem**

The most devastating challenge for the five-place relation comes from the problem of the many. The problem of the many asks: given a lump of clay, which of the millions of overlapping aggregates of particles is the *real* lump? The hylomorphist might respond that the real lump is the one informed by the clay-form. But which aggregate is informed by the clay-form? The form is a universal or a principle. It cannot pick out a specific aggregate unless it is instantiated in that specific aggregate. But the form is instantiated in *all* the aggregates that have the right shape and structure.

Consider the statue. There is a maximal aggregate of particles that has the statue-shape. But there are also millions of aggregates that differ by one particle, and each of these also has the statue-shape (if we define “statue-shape” loosely enough). The hylomorphist might say that only the maximal aggregate is informed by the statue-form. But why? What is the principle of maximality? If it is a mereological principle, then we are back to Hudson’s problem. If it is a formal principle, then the form itself must have a principle of maximality built into it. But then the form is not just a principle of unity; it is a principle of *selection*. This is a heavy burden.

The “in-F” relation does not solve this. It simply says that *x* is a part of *y* in virtue of *F*. If *F* is instantiated in multiple aggregates, then all of them are statues. This leads to a proliferation of statues—the very problem of the many. To avoid this, the hylomorphist must say that *F* is instantiated in exactly one aggregate. But what determines that? The form itself cannot determine it because the form is a universal. If the form is a particular (a trope), then it is a part of the composite. But then we have a regress: which aggregate of matter plus the form-trope is the statue? The form-trope is a part, so it must be unified with the matter by another form, and so on.

The five-place relation simply relocates the problem of the many. Instead of asking “which aggregate of particles is the statue?”, we ask “which aggregate of particles is informed by the statue-form?”. The answer is no more forthcoming.
