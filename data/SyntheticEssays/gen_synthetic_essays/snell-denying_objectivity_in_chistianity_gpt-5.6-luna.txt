# Objective Inquiry and the Passion of Faith: Kierkegaard’s Challenge in the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*

Johannes Climacus’s *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* presents one of the most radical accounts of the relation between reason, history, and religious faith in modern philosophy. Its central thesis is not simply that faith cannot be proved in the manner of a mathematical theorem. It is that objective inquiry and authentic Christian faith are, at a fundamental level, oriented toward different kinds of achievement. Objective inquiry seeks certainty about an object independently of the investigator’s personal relation to it. Christian faith, by contrast, is an existential commitment in which the individual must appropriate a truth inwardly and stake his or her life upon it. Because faith requires passionate commitment under conditions of uncertainty, inquiry that successfully eliminates uncertainty appears to eliminate the very situation in which faith can exist.

This thesis is developed especially through two related arguments. The first is the “passion-venture” argument: objective inquiry tends to remove the uncertainty that makes passionate faith possible. The second is the argument from inspiration: historical and philological investigation may clarify what the Bible says, when it was written, and how its manuscripts developed, but it cannot establish that the text is divinely inspired. The fact that a document is historically reliable is not the same as the fact that it is the word of God. In both arguments Climacus insists that Christian faith cannot be secured by an external foundation that would compel assent independently of the believer’s inward appropriation.

Yet Climacus’s position also generates serious questions. Does increased historical certainty really destroy authentic Christian commitment? Might historical evidence strengthen faith without replacing it? Is objective inquiry always directed toward detached observation, or can inquiry itself become a form of passionate existential engagement? And if faith is defined too strongly by uncertainty, does Christianity become dependent on ignorance rather than truth? These questions reveal both the power and the limits of Climacus’s opposition between objective inquiry and subjective faith.

The most charitable interpretation of Climacus is not that he rejects historical scholarship, logical reasoning, or empirical evidence altogether. Rather, he denies that any such method can perform the existential function that faith must perform. Objective inquiry may establish propositions, probabilities, or historical facts. It cannot, by itself, transform an individual’s existence. The issue is therefore not whether evidence is useful, but whether evidence can produce the kind of inward passion through which eternal happiness is appropriated. Climacus’s answer is fundamentally negative. Still, the force of that answer depends on a distinction between certainty as an epistemic condition and commitment as an existential act. Once that distinction is made, increased historical certainty need not destroy authentic faith, although it cannot substitute for faith either.

## The Meaning of Objective and Subjective Inquiry

The terms “objective” and “subjective” in Climacus do not simply correspond to fact and opinion. Subjectivity does not mean arbitrary preference, and objectivity does not mean truth in every context. The distinction concerns the relation between the knower and what is known. Objective inquiry attempts to understand an object by abstracting from the personal existence of the investigator. Its ideal is detached correctness: the proposition should be true whether or not the investigator is emotionally invested in it. Subjective inquiry, in the existential sense, concerns how an individual relates to a truth and allows that truth to shape a life.

A person may objectively know that Christianity teaches love of one’s neighbor while remaining inwardly indifferent to that teaching. Another person may possess less historical knowledge about Christianity but strive, with anxiety and devotion, to live according to its demand. The first may have more information, but the second may have a more authentically Christian relation to the truth. Climacus therefore distinguishes knowing what Christianity is from becoming a Christian. Christianity is not merely a doctrine to be mastered. It is a mode of existence requiring repentance, self-renunciation, obedience, and trust.

This distinction becomes especially important because Christianity concerns what Climacus calls “eternal happiness.” Eternal happiness is not simply an object that one can inspect from a distance. It is a transformation of the individual’s existence in relation to God. To know that such happiness is possible is not yet to possess it, and to possess a detailed intellectual account of Christian doctrine is not yet to be reconciled to God. The central question is not merely, “What is Christianity?” but, “How am I to become a Christian?” Objective inquiry can address the first question, but Climacus doubts that it can answer the second.

This is why he characterizes Christianity as an “existential” problem. The individual cannot remain an impartial spectator. A spectator may investigate Christianity historically, compare its doctrines with other doctrines, and assess the evidence for its origins. But the Christian demand concerns the whole person. It calls for a decision in which the individual’s self-understanding, conduct, and hope are placed at stake. The relation to Christianity is therefore not complete when one has reached intellectual clarity. Indeed, intellectual clarity may become a way of evading the more difficult demand of commitment.

## The Passion-Venture Argument

The most striking form of Climacus’s claim is the passion-venture argument. Faith requires both uncertainty and passion. The believer must be passionately committed to something that cannot be made objectively certain. If the object of faith were established with the same kind of necessity as a mathematical proposition, then the individual would no longer be making a venture. The relation would become one of knowledge rather than faith.

Climacus’s formula is that truth, in the specifically religious sense, is “objective uncertainty held fast in an appropriation-process of the most passionate inwardness.” This formula should not be interpreted as saying that any passionately held uncertainty is true. Fanaticism is not faith merely because it is intense. The point is instead that Christian faith possesses a distinctive structure: the individual must commit absolutely without possessing an objective guarantee proportionate to the absoluteness of the commitment. The uncertainty is objective because it concerns the limits of publicly demonstrable evidence; the passion is inward because the individual nevertheless relates to the possibility as decisive for existence.

The concept of a “venture” expresses this structure. To venture is to risk oneself. If an action is guaranteed in advance, it is not really a venture. A person who invests money in a project with complete certainty of success is not taking a risk in the meaningful sense. Likewise, if Christian truth could be demonstrated so conclusively that disbelief became irrational in the same way that denial of a logical contradiction is irrational, then Christian commitment would lose the character of a venture. The person might assent, but the assent would be compelled by evidence rather than animated by faith.

Climacus’s point is not that believers should seek confusion or suppress evidence. It is that the kind of certainty objective inquiry seeks is structurally inappropriate to faith. Objective inquiry aims at removing uncertainty. Faith, however, must maintain the tension between uncertainty and infinite concern. The believer is not merely curious about Christianity. The believer cares infinitely about whether Christianity is true and whether he or she stands rightly before God. This infinite passion cannot be generated by objective evidence alone because evidence does not itself dictate how one must exist.

Consider a historical investigation into the resurrection of Christ. Suppose that the evidence is strong enough to make the resurrection highly probable. Such evidence might reasonably alter a person’s beliefs. But probability, however high, does not itself produce repentance, love, obedience, or trust. Even if the historical conclusion were accepted, the individual would still have to determine what that fact means for his or her life. One might regard the resurrection as an extraordinary event while remaining spiritually indifferent. Conversely, one might be moved by the Christian proclamation before possessing a detailed historical argument. The transition from historical judgment to existential commitment is not automatic.

Climacus therefore fears that the attempt to provide an objective foundation for faith can become a form of evasion. The investigator may postpone commitment while waiting for a final result. One may spend a lifetime studying manuscripts, chronology, authorship, and evidential probabilities, believing that one has not yet reached the point at which a personal decision is necessary. But the demand of Christianity is precisely that one must decide as an existing individual, not as an infinitely patient observer. Objective research can become a shield against the risk of faith.

There is also a more subtle danger. If a person comes to believe that Christianity is objectively proved, then Christian commitment may become merely a rational response to overwhelming evidence. The individual may say, “I believe because the proof compels me.” But Climacus would ask whether such a person has truly entered into the passionate inwardness of faith or has simply accepted a conclusion. The issue is not whether rational assent is present. It is whether assent has become a lived relationship to God.

This argument is powerful because it identifies a gap between recognizing a truth and staking one’s existence upon it. A person can know that generosity is morally admirable and yet remain miserly. A person can know that mortality is unavoidable and yet live as though death were irrelevant. Similarly, a person can know Christian propositions without becoming Christian. Objective certainty does not guarantee existential transformation.

Nevertheless, the passion-venture argument depends on an important qualification. It assumes that objective certainty and passionate commitment are inversely related. But this is not always true. People can be passionately committed to things they regard as certain. Love, moral conviction, and political responsibility do not necessarily require epistemic uncertainty. A parent may be certain that a child exists and still feel profound passion toward the child. A person may be certain that injustice is wrong and nevertheless be passionately committed to opposing it. Certainty does not automatically flatten emotional or existential significance.

Climacus’s more defensible claim is not that certainty destroys passion as such, but that certainty of a certain kind cannot create faith. If Christianity became an externally demonstrable fact, faith would no longer be required as the mode of relating to it. The believer could still love God, obey God, and live religiously, but the original venture-like structure would be changed. Faith would no longer involve an absolute commitment under objective uncertainty. It would become a form of knowledge accompanied by devotion.

This distinction preserves the force of Climacus’s argument without making the implausible claim that certainty necessarily produces indifference. Historical certainty could intensify a believer’s passion, but it could not be the source or essence of the passion that makes faith faith.

## The Inspiration Argument and the Limits of Historical Inquiry

The second major argument concerns the Bible. Christianity is historically mediated. It depends on events, persons, texts, and testimony. Christianity cannot simply retreat into a timeless inward experience detached from history. The Christian claims that something happened: that Christ lived, taught, suffered, died, and was raised. The Bible is also treated as a central witness to those events. This creates a temptation to think that historical research can establish the divine authority of Scripture.

Climacus argues that this temptation must be resisted. A philologist may investigate manuscripts, compare linguistic forms, reconstruct textual variants, identify probable authors, and establish the historical circumstances in which biblical texts emerged. Such work can be valuable and intellectually rigorous. But no amount of philological success can prove that the Bible is inspired by God. Historical inquiry can establish features of a document as a human artifact; it cannot, by historical means alone, establish its divine status.

The reason is conceptual. Inspiration is not an ordinary historical property. To say that a text was written in a certain language, at a certain time, by a certain community, is to make a claim of the sort that historical evidence can address. To say that God speaks through the text, or that the text possesses divine authority, is to make a claim about the relation between the text and God. That relation is not observable in the same way as authorship, transmission, or date. A historian may discover that a biblical book is ancient, coherent, influential, and connected to a remarkable religious movement. None of these facts entails that it is inspired.

The philologist thought experiment makes this point vivid. Imagine a scholar who devotes an entire life to the Bible. The scholar reconstructs the most accurate possible text, resolves questions about grammar and vocabulary, identifies sources, and shows how the documents were transmitted. At the end of the investigation, what has been established? A great deal about the Bible as a historical object. But the scholar has not thereby established that the text is God’s word. The question of inspiration remains beyond the method’s reach.

This does not mean that inspiration is irrational or meaningless. It means that inspiration cannot be inferred as a necessary conclusion from historical data. The scholar may become a believer, but the decisive movement will involve faith, not merely philology. The individual must relate the text inwardly as a divine demand and promise. That relation cannot be produced by the accumulation of textual facts.

Climacus’s point becomes sharper when one considers two possible scenarios for biblical verification. In the first, suppose historical inquiry succeeds in establishing the Bible’s textual authenticity to an extraordinarily high degree. Scholars determine what the original manuscripts most likely said, identify the earliest witnesses, and demonstrate that later traditions have not substantially distorted the text. This would answer an important question: what did the earliest Christian communities write and believe? But it would not answer the question of whether what they wrote came from God. Textual certainty does not become divine certainty merely by being increased.

In the second scenario, suppose historical inquiry goes further and establishes the reliability of the biblical witnesses. Imagine that the testimony of the apostles or evangelists is shown to be sincere, consistent, and grounded in events that they themselves experienced. This would strengthen the historical case for Christianity. Yet even here, the conclusion would not be forced. Reliable witnesses may report an extraordinary event, but their reliability does not by itself establish the theological meaning of that event. The resurrection, even if historically probable, does not automatically compel the existential conclusion that one must repent, believe, and follow Christ.

These scenarios expose two different gaps. The first is the gap between a text’s historical identity and its divine inspiration. The second is the gap between the historical occurrence of an event and the individual’s religious appropriation of its meaning. Historical inquiry can narrow uncertainty about both, but it cannot eliminate the need for faith.

One might add a third scenario: suppose a person had lived at the time of Christ and directly witnessed the central events of the Christian story. Would immediate eyewitness perception eliminate the need for faith? Climacus’s answer would again be negative. Seeing a miracle would be an empirical experience, but the interpretation of that experience would still involve a decision. One could witness an extraordinary event and remain resistant, confused, or indifferent. The historical nearness of the event would not guarantee inward appropriation. Indeed, the New Testament itself portrays contemporaries who witnessed Jesus’ actions yet did not become believers.

The point is not that evidence is irrelevant. Eyewitness testimony matters for historical judgment. It can make belief more reasonable. But objective evidence remains external to the transformation that Christianity demands. A witness may confirm that something happened; only faith can establish what the event means for the individual’s eternal existence.

## Eternal Happiness: Subjective Inwardness or Empirical Proof?

The conflict between subjective inwardness and empirical proof becomes most intelligible when viewed through the idea of eternal happiness. If Christianity were only a theory about the ancient world, historical inquiry might be sufficient. We could ask whether the theory is true, compare evidence, and settle the matter according to scholarly standards. But Christianity claims to offer reconciliation with God and eternal happiness. It therefore concerns the individual in the most absolute way.

Climacus is not saying that eternal happiness is merely a subjective feeling. Rather, he is saying that the appropriation of eternal happiness is subjective because it must occur in the individual’s existence. No one can possess eternal happiness on behalf of another. A scholar may prove that Christianity has a certain historical origin, but the scholar cannot believe in the existential sense for the student. A church may preserve doctrine, but it cannot substitute institutional membership for inward commitment.

Empirical proof is public and transferable. If a scientific experiment is valid, anyone with the necessary competence can reproduce or verify it. Faith, by contrast, cannot be transferred in the same manner. One person’s faith may inspire another, but it cannot replace the other’s own relation to God. The personal character of faith is therefore not an accidental psychological feature. It follows from the nature of the good being sought. Eternal happiness is not an external possession but a mode of existing before God.

This makes subjective inwardness indispensable. The individual must care infinitely about the truth and must allow the truth to become decisive for conduct. The question is not only whether Christianity is historically credible but whether one is willing to exist as a Christian. Such willingness involves risk because no objective proof can guarantee that the individual’s commitment will be rewarded in a way visible beforehand. Faith is thus not an irrational leap into arbitrary belief. It is a passionate response to a possibility whose significance exceeds the available evidence.

The phrase “possibility” is crucial. The Christian does not possess a complete objective grasp of God. If God were available as an object fully present to detached inspection, God would become one item within the world of objects. The relation to God would then be comparable to the relation to a scientific fact. Climacus believes that this would misunderstand the absolute difference between God and the human knower. God is not an object that can be mastered by investigation. The individual must instead stand in a relation of dependence, trust, and obedience.

Still, one might object that empirical proof could support inwardness rather than replace it. Knowledge that a person’s spouse exists does not prevent love; knowledge that a historical event occurred does not prevent moral response. Why should knowledge of Christ’s resurrection destroy faith? Perhaps evidence simply removes unnecessary doubt, allowing believers to devote themselves more fully to spiritual life.

This objection identifies a real weakness in a simplistic reading of Climacus. In actual religious life, believers often use historical arguments as support for faith without supposing that arguments alone produce conversion. Historical evidence can protect faith from arbitrariness, answer objections, and make commitment intellectually responsible. It can also deepen a believer’s understanding of what he or she is committed to. There is no reason why scholarship must be spiritually corrosive.

Climacus can respond by distinguishing support from foundation. Evidence may support faith, but it cannot become an objective foundation proportionate to the absolute character of Christian commitment. Even a very high probability leaves the existential decision to the individual. More importantly, evidence cannot determine the inward form of the response. One may accept the evidence and remain unchanged. Thus empirical inquiry can accompany faith, but it cannot replace subjective appropriation.

## The Role of the Philologist and the Biblical Scenarios

The philologist thought experiment has a further function beyond showing that inspiration cannot be proven. It reveals the danger of confusing historical proximity with existential immediacy. A scholar may move backward through layers of textual transmission and approach the earliest available witness. Yet chronological closeness to the origins of Christianity does not equal spiritual closeness to Christ. The philologist may possess the most accurate text while remaining distant from its demand.

This is important because Christianity is often treated as though the main problem were uncertainty about what the original text said. If only textual corruption could be removed, it is assumed, faith would follow. Climacus rejects that assumption. Even a perfect text would not compel faith. The obstacle is not merely textual ambiguity; it is the individual’s relation to the truth.

The two verification scenarios similarly distinguish different kinds of certainty. In the first scenario, the Bible’s wording and transmission are verified. In the second, the historical witnesses and events are verified. The first concerns the reliability of the medium; the second concerns the reliability of the message’s historical basis. Neither establishes inspiration or creates faith.

The scenarios also suggest that the problem cannot be solved by moving closer to the original event. Suppose one says that later believers lack the advantage of the first disciples because they were not present. The response is that even contemporaneity would not remove the need for inward appropriation. An individual who saw Jesus directly would still confront the question of how to interpret what was seen and whether to submit to its meaning. The historical distance of later believers may increase objective uncertainty, but it does not create the essential structure of faith. The first disciples too had to venture.

This point is especially relevant to the Christian doctrine of the incarnation. If God became a human being in a particular historical life, then divine revelation is necessarily exposed to the ambiguities of history. The event can be described, remembered, contested, and investigated. But the divine significance of the event cannot be extracted from its historical surface as though it were a measurable property. The incarnation is a paradox: the eternal appears in time, and the absolute becomes accessible through a finite individual. Historical inquiry can encounter the finite manifestation, but faith is required to relate to it as the manifestation of God.

The philologist therefore cannot achieve by scholarship what the believer achieves through faith. But this does not make the philologist’s work worthless. It clarifies the conditions under which the work is relevant. Philology can tell us what claim is being made and whether the historical record supports it. It cannot decide whether we will allow the claim to govern our lives.

## Does More Historical Certainty Destroy Authentic Commitment?

The most important counter-argument concerns the alleged destructive effect of increased historical certainty. Why assume that greater certainty undermines faith? One could argue that authentic Christian commitment does not require uncertainty as such. It requires trust, love, and obedience. If historical evidence made Christian claims more certain, believers could still trust God and live in devotion. Indeed, eliminating false doubts might make faith more stable and sincere.

This counter-argument is persuasive against the claim that uncertainty is intrinsically valuable. There is no virtue in believing something merely because evidence is lacking. Nor is faith necessarily more authentic when it is poorly informed. A believer who studies history, theology, and textual criticism may possess a richer faith than one who accepts Christianity without reflection. In some cases, historical inquiry may purify faith by exposing superstition, correcting false assumptions, and distinguishing central Christian claims from later accretions.

Moreover, Christian faith is not simply a commitment to an unknown possibility. It is faith in a God who has acted in history. If historical claims are irrelevant, Christianity risks becoming a purely inward projection. The believer would no longer be responding to God’s revelation but to an existential symbol. The Christian tradition itself insists that the resurrection is not merely a subjective experience but an event. Therefore, some connection to objective reality seems essential.

A further objection is that Climacus may conflate “objective certainty” with “objective knowledge.” In ordinary life, knowledge and commitment coexist. Knowing that a friend is trustworthy does not eliminate the meaningfulness of trusting the friend. Knowing that a promise is likely to be kept does not make reliance on it meaningless. Trust is not simply a response to ignorance; it is a relationship to a person. Perhaps faith likewise could survive increased knowledge because its object is God, not an abstract proposition.

These objections require a revision rather than a total rejection of Climacus. Increased historical certainty does not inherently destroy authentic Christian commitment. It destroys faith only if faith is defined as a passionate response whose essential condition is the absence of objective certainty. But that definition may be too narrow. There are multiple dimensions of Christian faith. Faith can mean assent to historical claims, trust in God, commitment to Christ, participation in a community, and a way of life oriented toward forgiveness and love. Historical certainty might affect the first dimension without eliminating the others.

Climacus’s position remains defensible if understood as a claim about the insufficiency of objective certainty, not its automatic incompatibility with faith. Objective inquiry cannot generate the inward passion of faith because it cannot make a decision for the individual. It may remove certain obstacles, but it cannot take the individual’s place. Even if the historical evidence for Christianity were overwhelming, the person would still have to decide whether to live in relation to God. The certainty would not perform the existential act.

We can distinguish three possibilities. First, objective inquiry may undermine faith by showing that central Christian claims are false or highly implausible. In that case, inquiry can certainly threaten commitment. Second, objective inquiry may support faith by showing that Christian claims are historically responsible and intellectually defensible. In that case, inquiry may strengthen commitment. Third, objective inquiry might become so conclusive that belief is compelled as a detached conclusion. In that hypothetical case, the epistemic status of faith would change, but the individual could still possess love, obedience, and devotion. What would disappear is not all religious commitment but the specific venture structure emphasized by Climacus.

This distinction also reveals that Climacus’s criticism is aimed less at evidence than at the fantasy of postponing existence until evidence becomes absolute. No historical method can deliver a conclusion that automatically becomes an existential life. The individual cannot escape responsibility by saying, “I will commit only when the evidence has become infinite.” Historical evidence is finite, while the Christian demand is absolute. The disproportion between finite evidence and infinite commitment is precisely what makes a venture necessary.

At the same time, a believer should not deliberately cultivate uncertainty in order to preserve the emotional drama of faith. Authenticity cannot consist in refusing knowledge. Faith is not made more genuine by ignoring historical facts. The right conclusion is that faith and inquiry have different roles. Inquiry investigates whether the Christian witness is credible; faith determines whether the individual will exist in relation to that witness. Neither role should be confused with the other.

## Evaluation of Climacus’s Thesis

Climacus’s thesis is strongest as a critique of reductionism. It challenges the idea that Christianity can be converted into a scientific hypothesis and then accepted without existential transformation. It also resists the apologetic ambition to prove Christianity in a way that would make personal commitment unnecessary. His emphasis on passion explains why a person can possess extensive religious knowledge without being religious, and why a less informed person can nevertheless relate to Christianity with seriousness and devotion.

His argument from inspiration is equally valuable. It exposes a category mistake in attempts to prove divine authorship by historical means. A text may be ancient, accurate, and historically influential without being inspired. The claim that God speaks through Scripture involves a theological interpretation and a relation of faith that cannot be established by philology alone. The philologist thought experiment effectively illustrates the limits of scholarly expertise.

Yet Climacus risks overstating the opposition between objectivity and subjectivity. Objective inquiry is not always detached in the pejorative sense. Historians and philologists may investigate Christianity because they are personally concerned with its truth. Their inquiry can be existentially motivated without ceasing to be methodologically objective. Conversely, inwardness can be self-deceptive. Passion does not guarantee truth. A person may be deeply committed to a false belief. Climacus’s formula therefore needs an ethical and rational supplement: subjective passion must be directed toward a truth that is not simply invented by the individual.

There is also a danger that defining faith through uncertainty makes faith vulnerable to the charge that it thrives on insufficient evidence. Christianity does not usually present itself as a commitment to an unknowable possibility detached from history. It claims that God has revealed himself. Revelation may never become an object of complete mastery, but it is not nothing. The believer’s passion must be responsive to something believed to have occurred, not merely to an inward need for meaning.

The most balanced conclusion is therefore a qualified one. Objective inquiry and authentic Christian faith are incommensurable if “objective inquiry” means a method that seeks to settle Christianity as an external object and if “authentic faith” means the passionate inward appropriation of an absolute relation to God. Under those definitions, the two cannot replace one another. Objective inquiry removes or evaluates uncertainty; faith exists as the individual’s response to the fact that no finite evidence can compel an infinite commitment. The philologist can establish the text but not its inspiration. Historical research can assess testimony but not decide what the testimony means for one’s eternal happiness.

They are not, however, incommensurable in the sense of being mutually hostile or incapable of interaction. Historical inquiry can prepare, challenge, discipline, and enrich faith. It can prevent faith from becoming indifferent to reality. Faith can also motivate inquiry by making the historical question personally significant. What cannot be achieved is a simple conversion of one into the other. Evidence may occasion faith, but it cannot perform the act of faith. Uncertainty may make faith a venture, but uncertainty alone does not make a commitment true.

## Conclusion

Climacus’s *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* offers a profound account of why Christian faith cannot be reduced to objective knowledge. The passion-venture argument shows that faith requires an absolute personal commitment under conditions in which objective certainty is unavailable. If inquiry eliminated all uncertainty and compelled assent as a detached conclusion, it would change the character of the relation from faith to knowledge. The inspiration argument shows that historical and philological methods, however exact, cannot prove that Scripture is divine. They can reconstruct a text and assess its witnesses, but they cannot establish the theological relation between the text and God.

The thought experiment of the philologist and the two biblical verification scenarios demonstrate that neither perfect textual certainty nor perfect historical testimony would automatically create Christian faith. Even an eyewitness would still have to interpret and appropriate what was seen. Even a scholar who reconstructed the original Bible would still have to decide whether to stand before it as a divine demand. Eternal happiness cannot be obtained through empirical proof alone because it is not an external object to be possessed by observation. It must be appropriated in the individual’s inward existence.

Nevertheless, increased historical certainty does not inherently destroy authentic Christian commitment. It may support or deepen faith, and it may protect faith from arbitrary fantasy. What it cannot do is replace the existential act by which a person commits himself or herself to God. Climacus is most convincing when he argues that no amount of objective research can absolve the individual from this responsibility. He is less convincing if his thesis is taken to mean that evidence and faith must always diminish one another.

The ultimate lesson is not that Christians should reject inquiry or cultivate ignorance. It is that objective inquiry and faith answer different questions. Inquiry asks what can be established about texts, events, and evidence. Faith asks how the individual will exist in relation to the truth. Historical certainty may tell us more about Christianity, but it cannot become Christian existence for us. The venture remains: not because evidence is worthless, but because no evidence can make the decision on behalf of the person who must live it.
