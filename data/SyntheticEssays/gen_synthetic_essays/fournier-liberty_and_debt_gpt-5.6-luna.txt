# Negative Freedom, Social Birth, and the Moral Limits of the Minimal State

The claim that libertarian non-interference has moral primacy over positive freedom, while nevertheless remaining morally incomplete because every individual owes an unacknowledged debt to society, combines two intuitions that are often treated as incompatible. The first is the libertarian insight that persons are not raw materials for collective projects. They possess a sphere of authority over their bodies, labor, associations, and voluntary exchanges, and this sphere should not be invaded merely because others believe that an alternative use of their freedom would be socially beneficial. The second is the communitarian and social-democratic insight that no person becomes an autonomous agent in isolation. Language, law, education, public health, infrastructure, trust, and inherited knowledge are conditions of individual agency. The apparently self-made individual is always enabled by institutions and relationships that precede her.

A defensible position must therefore resist two symmetrical errors. It must reject the idea that freedom consists principally in receiving whatever goods or capacities a political authority deems necessary for an approved form of life. But it must also reject the fantasy that non-interference alone exhausts the moral obligations of political society. Negative freedom is foundational because without protected choice there is no genuine agency, responsibility, experimentation, or personal meaning. Yet it is morally incomplete because the social world that makes such choice possible is not the product of the individual’s effort alone. The resulting “debt” does not automatically authorize unlimited taxation, paternalism, or egalitarian leveling. It does, however, provide grounds for a political order more substantial than Nozick’s strictly minimal state.

Robert Nozick’s experience machine helps illuminate why negative freedom deserves this foundational status. The machine does not prove libertarianism in its entirety, and it does not by itself establish a right to unrestricted private property. Its importance lies elsewhere: it reveals that human flourishing is not exhausted by pleasurable mental states or by the mere possession of desirable outcomes. We care about acting, choosing, accomplishing, relating to actual people, and confronting a world that is not manufactured to gratify us. Meaning arises partly through resistance. But resistance is intelligible as resistance only where an agent is free to encounter it rather than having her world and choices prearranged by another. The experience machine thus supports the primacy of a domain of non-interference, even while the social conditions of that domain point beyond the minimal state.

## I. Negative and Positive Freedom

The distinction between negative and positive freedom is often presented too simply. Negative freedom generally means the absence of coercive interference: one is free insofar as other persons or institutions do not prevent one from acting according to one’s choices. Positive freedom, by contrast, concerns the possession of effective capacities or resources for action: education, health, material security, political participation, and perhaps the psychological development necessary for self-direction.

The distinction matters because positive freedom can be interpreted in two very different ways. In its modest sense, it refers to the real conditions under which individuals can exercise agency. A person who is formally free to pursue education but lacks any minimally adequate access to it may possess legal liberty without meaningful opportunity. In its more ambitious sense, positive freedom becomes a substantive ideal of self-realization, often defined by political authorities or cultural elites. On this interpretation, people are not genuinely free unless they live rationally, healthily, productively, or in accordance with some conception of human fulfillment. The danger is that coercion becomes permissible in the name of liberating people from their own ignorance, weakness, or preferences.

Libertarian non-interference has moral primacy because it protects the person as an author rather than an object. It establishes a presumption against coercion and recognizes that individuals possess distinct perspectives on what makes life worthwhile. A state may know that a citizen needs food, education, or medical care in a general sense, but it does not thereby know the meaning those goods should have in that person’s life. Nor does it follow that the state may force individuals to pursue the life it regards as best. Non-interference protects pluralism: religious, artistic, familial, vocational, and philosophical differences can coexist without a centralized authority having to rank them.

This priority is not merely procedural. It is connected to moral responsibility. A person’s choices can be attributed to her as choices only if she has some protected space in which to make them. If every major decision is engineered by public officials, employers, experts, or majorities, success may be achieved, but it is less clearly hers. Negative freedom therefore guards the conditions of authorship. It makes possible not only doing what one wants, but being accountable for what one does.

Still, non-interference should not be confused with social indifference. The absence of direct coercion does not imply the absence of dependence or power. A person may be legally free while living under conditions of extreme vulnerability, economic domination, or exclusion from the institutions through which social opportunities are distributed. Moreover, positive capacities are often not external additions to freedom but its enabling infrastructure. Literacy, physical security, and access to public space can be prerequisites for exercising choice. The challenge is to acknowledge this without transforming every desirable condition into an enforceable claim against others.

The best formulation is therefore hierarchical rather than absolute. Negative freedom is foundational: coercive interference requires special justification, and no political program may simply treat individual liberty as one good among others to be maximized. Positive freedom is derivative but morally real: society may establish conditions that allow people to exercise their agency, especially where those conditions arise from shared institutions or from obligations of reciprocity. The task is to distinguish enabling freedom from imposed perfection.

## II. The Experience Machine and the Reality of Agency

Nozick’s experience machine asks us to imagine a device capable of producing any experiences we desire. Once connected, a person could enjoy the subjective sensation of writing a great novel, forming deep relationships, overcoming adversity, or achieving moral greatness. The machine would ensure that these experiences felt entirely real. No one outside would know the difference, and the individual could remain within the machine for life.

Nozick expects many people to refuse the offer. That reaction is philosophically significant. If pleasure or desirable experience were the only constituents of value, connecting to the machine would be irrational: it would provide the same or greater quantity of positive experience without the risks, frustrations, and disappointments of ordinary life. Yet many people judge that something important would be lost. They do not merely want to feel as though they are accomplishing things; they want actually to accomplish them. They do not merely want to feel loved by simulated persons; they want relationships with independent others. They do not merely want the experience of choosing; they want to make choices in a world whose structure is not entirely dictated by their own programming.

The thought experiment therefore challenges hedonism and certain forms of welfare consequentialism. It suggests that value includes “being” and “doing,” not merely “seeming.” It also indicates that authenticity is relational and worldly. A meaningful achievement must involve a genuine relation between an agent and a reality that can resist the agent’s wishes. Writing a novel requires confronting language, time, discipline, and the possibility of failure. Friendship requires another person whose response cannot be guaranteed. Moral action requires circumstances in which one can act well or badly. The machine can simulate the phenomenology of these activities, but it cannot supply their full normative reality.

Resistance is central here. If every obstacle disappears because the world has been arranged to make success inevitable, success loses some of its significance. The value of a choice is partly constituted by the fact that alternatives were available, consequences were uncertain, and other realities did not bend automatically to the will of the chooser. Resistance need not mean suffering for its own sake. A world of constant misery is not thereby more meaningful than a comfortable one. But a life entirely insulated from independent reality would lack the friction through which skill, courage, fidelity, creativity, and judgment are formed.

This argument helps establish the foundational status of negative freedom. The experience machine is objectionable not only because it is artificial, but because it places one’s life under the control of an external designer. Even if the machine were entered voluntarily, the resulting world would be one in which the person’s apparent activities were predetermined by a system designed by others. The individual would no longer confront an independent field of possibilities in which her decisions could alter what happens. She would be a consumer of experiences rather than an author of a life.

A critic might respond that the machine is simply a bad technology, not evidence for a general right against interference. After all, some forms of interference—preventing murder, fraud, or exploitation—protect agency rather than destroy it. Likewise, a person may voluntarily choose constraints, such as joining a religious order or submitting to a demanding artistic discipline, without thereby losing freedom. The experience machine does not establish that all external control is wrong.

That objection is correct but not fatal. The relevant principle is not that every limitation is impermissible. It is that individuals require a protected role in determining the conditions under which they act. Legitimate law can prohibit assault because assault eliminates another’s agency; it cannot ordinarily dictate the total shape of a person’s life simply because officials believe that shape would produce superior experiences. The experience machine clarifies why a system that maximizes satisfaction while controlling action can still be deficient. Freedom is not merely the receipt of good states but participation in the authorship of one’s relationship to reality.

The machine also reveals why positive freedom cannot simply replace negative freedom. Suppose a government claimed that its task was to maximize citizens’ substantive well-being. It might provide excellent education, health care, housing, employment, and cultural opportunities. Those goods could be genuinely valuable. But if securing them required extensive surveillance, compulsory participation, ideological education, or the suppression of unpopular choices, citizens might become beneficiaries without being authors. The state would function as a collective experience machine: not necessarily producing pleasure, but arranging the conditions of life according to a comprehensive design.

Yet the thought experiment does not support a romantic fantasy of isolated self-creation. The actual world into which individuals are born is already structured by social institutions. Language, law, roads, schools, markets, and cultural practices are not artificial distractions from authentic agency; they are among the media through which agency becomes possible. The resistance we need is not a purely natural resistance untouched by society. It is resistance within a shared world. We require both freedom from domination and access to the social practices that make meaningful action intelligible.

## III. Against the Myth of the Isolated Contractor

Nozick’s political philosophy begins from individuals with rights and asks what political institutions could arise without violating those rights. His famous account of the minimal state imagines a process in which individuals initially protect themselves and their property, form protective associations, and eventually generate a dominant protective agency. The state is legitimate only if it emerges without imposing uncompensated costs on those excluded from its dominant protection. Its functions are limited to protection against force, theft, fraud, and breach of contract.

This construction has considerable moral power. It emphasizes that political authority cannot be justified merely by claiming superior social outcomes. It asks how institutions may be formed without treating individuals as means. It also exposes the coercive element of taxation: taking resources from someone is not morally neutral simply because the revenue finances a useful program. Nozick’s challenge to patterned theories of justice remains important. If holdings are acquired justly and transferred voluntarily, a constantly enforced pattern may require continuous interference with people’s choices.

But the social ontology underlying a purely contractual or protective account is incomplete. Human beings do not begin as independent contractors who later discover the advantages of cooperation. They begin as dependent children, acquire language from others, inherit norms and knowledge, and develop within networks of care. The basic social order is not created by a pact among already competent adults. It is reproduced across generations through practices of cooperation that individuals did not choose and could not have created alone.

Evolutionary theory supports this point. Human beings evolved under conditions in which survival depended heavily on cooperation, reciprocal exchange, collective childcare, food sharing, coalition-building, and reputation management. Reciprocity was not merely a late rational agreement designed to escape a condition of universal conflict. It was embedded in the developmental and emotional architecture of the species. Humans possess capacities for sympathy, shame, resentment of cheating, gratitude, punishment of defectors, and attachment to groups. These capacities can be distorted, but they also sustain large-scale cooperation.

This does not mean that evolutionary origins straightforwardly determine moral legitimacy. What evolved is not automatically what ought to be. Human beings also evolved violence, domination, tribalism, and exclusion. Evolutionary explanation cannot substitute for normative argument. Nevertheless, it undermines the image of society as an optional arrangement among self-sufficient individuals. Cooperation is not simply a bargain struck to secure mutual defense; it is a developmental condition of becoming the kind of agent who can bargain at all.

The origins of law and governance are therefore better understood as layered forms of reciprocal coordination than as a single contract against a state of nature. Early norms likely emerged from repeated interactions concerning kinship, property, exchange, sexual conduct, retaliation, ritual, and collective danger. Informal expectations became customary rules; customary rules acquired mechanisms of sanction; political authorities later centralized and systematized enforcement. At each stage, governance addressed not only external threats but internal problems of trust, free riding, conflict resolution, and the provision of shared goods.

The social world generated by these practices is a form of inherited capital. A child born today does not choose the language in which she thinks, the legal system that recognizes her claims, the monetary system that enables exchange, the infrastructure that transports goods, or the public knowledge that supports modern medicine and technology. She benefits from a long history of cooperation, including sacrifices made by strangers and ancestors. Even a person who rejects social conventions depends on them to articulate and pursue that rejection. The individual is not thereby morally owned by society. But the individual is not accurately described as owing nothing to it.

This is the source of the “birth debt.” The phrase should not be interpreted as a literal contractual liability. No newborn signed a promissory note, and one cannot owe repayment for every benefit one receives merely because it was beneficial. The debt is instead a moral shorthand for the fact that agency is socially enabled. It expresses a relationship of reciprocity: one inherits conditions of agency and has at least some obligation to sustain fair conditions for others, especially future persons who will also arrive without having chosen the institutional order.

The metaphor is dangerous if used carelessly. If society is said to own everything that contributes to a person’s success, then no stable boundary remains around individual rights. Every achievement depends on social conditions in some broad sense, and an unlimited debt could justify unlimited claims. The birth debt must therefore be constrained by principles of proportionality, equal standing, and respect for personal authorship. It grounds duties to maintain a system of cooperation, not a general license to commandeer lives.

## IV. Does the Birth Debt Justify Taxation?

The most direct political implication of the social debt concerns taxation. If individuals benefit from public institutions that no private person could provide alone, then taxation may be justified as a contribution to the maintenance of a cooperative order. This argument differs from the claim that the state may take resources whenever doing so increases aggregate welfare. It also differs from the claim that taxation is simply the price of services purchased by each citizen. The justification is reciprocal and institutional: people are entitled to the benefits of a social scheme only if they contribute, according to fair rules, to sustaining the scheme on which those benefits depend.

Consider familiar public goods. Courts, contract enforcement, disease control, roads, basic scientific research, environmental protection, and emergency response cannot be supplied adequately through purely voluntary contributions. A person may refuse to pay while still relying on the legal and physical infrastructure that protects her property and enables her business. If everyone behaved this way, the cooperative system would collapse. Taxation can therefore be understood as a rule against free riding, provided that the taxes finance genuinely shared institutions and are imposed through publicly accountable procedures.

This argument gives taxation a stronger basis than Nozick’s strict minimalism allows. If the state’s legitimate role is only to prevent force and fraud, it may still require substantial institutions to perform even those functions effectively. But the argument extends further. Education, public health, and a basic social safety net can be justified not merely as charitable transfers but as investments in the reciprocal conditions of agency. A population unable to read, chronically vulnerable to preventable disease, or exposed to catastrophic deprivation cannot participate fully in the cooperative order. Their exclusion diminishes not only their private welfare but the quality and stability of the system from which all benefit.

The strongest case concerns goods that individuals cannot secure adequately through isolated market choices because their value is systemic. Public education does not simply give one child a private advantage; it sustains a population capable of communication, deliberation, skilled work, and lawful cooperation. Public health protects individuals partly by protecting everyone from contagion. Social insurance addresses risks—serious illness, disability, unemployment, old age—that are difficult to predict and that private contracts may cover imperfectly, especially when people begin life with unequal circumstances.

Still, not every social program follows from the birth debt. The fact that people benefit from society does not establish that the government may equalize all outcomes or guarantee every preferred capability. A distinction must be drawn between maintaining the background conditions of agency and directing the distribution of life prospects toward a predetermined pattern. Taxation that funds universal legal protection, basic education, health safeguards, and insurance against severe risks can be compatible with libertarian primacy. Taxation aimed at ensuring that all citizens possess identical resources, social status, or levels of achievement is much harder to justify on this basis.

The problem of coercion remains. Taxation takes resources under threat of legal penalty. It therefore requires justification proportionate to the purposes served. A legitimate tax system should satisfy several conditions. It should fund public goods or reciprocal institutions rather than ideological projects. It should be transparent and democratically accountable. It should not arbitrarily target unpopular groups. It should preserve a substantial sphere for private choice. And it should avoid imposing burdens that effectively subordinate some citizens to others.

Progressive taxation may nevertheless be defensible. The birth debt is not necessarily a flat fee, because individuals do not benefit equally in identical ways, and a flat contribution may impose radically different sacrifices. Those who accumulate greater resources often do so through the use of public institutions, legal protections, educated labor forces, stable markets, and publicly generated knowledge. A progressive system can ask more from those with greater ability to contribute while still recognizing that the resources remain theirs in an important sense.

The justification should not rest solely on the assertion that wealth is socially produced. That claim, taken without qualification, would erase personal effort, risk, innovation, and voluntary cooperation. Rather, the point is that private production occurs within a jointly maintained framework. Taxation can require contribution to that framework without denying that individuals have legitimate claims over the fruits of their labor. The moral question is not whether society or the individual “really” produced a given resource in isolation. It is whether the rules governing contribution and retention are fair to participants in an interdependent system.

## V. Social Programs Without Egalitarian Homogenization

Recognizing a social debt need not entail egalitarian homogenization. Equality has several meanings, and they should not be conflated. Equality before the law, equal civic status, and equal access to basic institutions are compatible with substantial differences in income, lifestyle, talent, ambition, and achievement. A society can reject both aristocratic privilege and compulsory sameness.

The case for social programs is strongest when they protect a floor of agency rather than enforce an upper pattern of life. A basic income, public health system, disability support, unemployment insurance, or publicly funded education may be justified because without some material security, formal liberty becomes fragile. Such programs can expand the range of meaningful choices while leaving recipients free to decide how to use the support. They provide means without dictating ends.

There are, however, difficulties even here. A social program can become paternalistic if benefits are conditional on approved behavior, family structure, work ethic, medical compliance, or ideological conformity. It can become homogenizing if it assumes that the same service or lifestyle is best for everyone. It can weaken voluntary association by centralizing responsibilities once handled by families, communities, religious groups, or mutual-aid societies. It can also create bureaucratic dependence, in which people must submit to official classification and surveillance to receive what should be a civic entitlement.

The remedy is not to abandon public provision but to design it around agency. Programs should be universal or broadly accessible where universality reduces stigma and administrative intrusion. They should allow plural forms of service delivery, including private, cooperative, religious, and local providers where appropriate. They should be transparent, limited in scope, and subject to democratic revision. They should favor cash or choice-preserving support when recipients can reasonably make their own decisions, while using direct provision where individual choices cannot secure systemic goods such as epidemic control or basic infrastructure.

This approach also resists character perfectionism. The state should not force citizens to become virtuous according to a contested moral ideal. It may prohibit conduct that directly violates others’ rights, and it may regulate institutions to prevent fraud, abuse, or coercive exploitation. But it should not use social policy to manufacture a particular personality: industrious, religious, patriotic, ascetic, competitive, or culturally refined. A person may use publicly supported education to become an artist, entrepreneur, caregiver, scholar, or none of these. The purpose of enabling agency is not to predetermine its content.

Some will argue that social programs necessarily express a substantive conception of the good. Any decision to fund education rather than luxury consumption, or health care rather than entertainment, reflects priorities. This is true, but it does not follow that all political judgments are equally perfectionist. A liberal society inevitably protects certain conditions—bodily security, literacy, equal legal standing—because these are necessary for citizens to pursue their own ends. The relevant distinction is between securing general preconditions of choice and prescribing the final purposes for which choice should be used.

A further issue concerns responsibility. If people receive support regardless of their choices, does the system undermine reciprocity by rewarding free riding? A defensible social order can distinguish between unconditional civic membership and conditional benefits tied to contribution where contribution is feasible. People who are able to work may reasonably be expected to contribute through employment, caregiving, community service, or taxes. But the demand must be calibrated to actual capacity, and it must recognize unpaid forms of socially necessary labor. Reciprocity should not become moralistic punishment of those who are poor, ill, disabled, or unlucky.

The birth debt also runs across generations. A person benefits not only from living contemporaries but from inherited institutions, discoveries, sacrifices, and ecological resources. Repayment cannot be made directly to the dead. It can be made by sustaining and improving the institutional conditions handed to us, particularly for those who arrive later. This supports investments in education, environmental preservation, infrastructure, and public knowledge. Such expenditures are not merely transfers from one group to another; they are contributions to the continuity of the cooperative order itself.

## VI. The Tension with Nozickian Entitlement

Nozick’s entitlement theory maintains that a distribution is just if it arises through just acquisition, just transfer, and rectification of past injustice. It rejects the idea that justice requires a patterned distribution measured by equality, need, or merit. The famous Wilt Chamberlain example illustrates the point: even if an initially equal distribution is just, voluntary exchanges will quickly produce inequality. Preventing those exchanges in order to preserve the pattern would violate people’s rights.

This argument remains a powerful warning against treating distribution as morally primary. People do have claims generated by consent, labor, gift, exchange, and legitimate expectation. A society that continually confiscates the results of voluntary activity may destroy the very freedom it claims to promote. Moreover, the existence of a social debt does not mean that every unequal outcome is unjust. Diversity of outcome may reflect differing choices, risks, talents, and priorities. Liberty necessarily produces variation.

The difficulty is that Nozick’s framework tends to treat the social background as morally external to the process of acquisition and transfer. Yet the initial conditions under which people acquire and exchange are themselves politically constituted. Property is not merely a physical fact; it depends on rules concerning exclusion, inheritance, corporations, contracts, bankruptcy, intellectual property, land, and enforcement. Markets are not alternatives to governance but institutional forms sustained by law. If the social order that defines and protects holdings is collectively maintained, then participants may owe contributions to it without that contribution being an alien intrusion into a pre-political domain.

This does not refute every Nozickian concern. It does mean that the relevant baseline is contested. The question is not simply whether the state takes what was unquestionably private. It is whether a particular legal regime that defines property and exchange may require participants to support the public conditions of that regime. That question can be answered in a limited way without endorsing unlimited redistribution.

Historical injustice further complicates entitlement. Existing holdings often reflect conquest, enslavement, exclusion, colonial appropriation, discriminatory law, and unequal political power. If rectification is required, some redistribution may be necessary even under an entitlement approach. But rectification should not be used as a vague license for permanent political control. It requires evidence, institutional impartiality, and attention to the rights of current persons who may not be personally responsible for past wrongs. Where exact restoration is impossible, broad measures to repair inherited exclusion may be justified, but they should remain connected to the underlying injustice.

The most plausible synthesis therefore modifies rather than abandons the entitlement insight. Individuals have strong claims against arbitrary interference and against confiscation motivated merely by envy or social engineering. At the same time, those claims arise within a cooperative legal order that individuals did not create and cannot sustain alone. Contributions to public goods, social insurance, and rectification of serious exclusion are compatible with individual rights when they preserve rather than abolish the space of personal direction.

## VII. Is Negative Freedom Really Primary?

A final challenge asks whether the hierarchy between negative and positive freedom can be maintained. If someone lacks food, shelter, or medical care, is it meaningful to say that she is free merely because no one is actively stopping her? Perhaps freedom should be understood as effective power rather than non-interference. On this view, a starving person and a wealthy person may have the same legal liberties but radically unequal freedom.

This criticism identifies a real weakness in purely formal accounts. Freedom requires opportunities, and opportunities require resources. But it does not follow that all inequalities in resources are violations of freedom or that the state must equalize them. The concept of effective freedom must remain sensitive to agency and choice. If the state gives one person more resources by forcibly taking them from another, it must show why the transfer is necessary to sustain a fair system of reciprocal agency rather than simply because the outcome appears more desirable.

Negative freedom has priority in at least three senses. First, it protects a domain of personal judgment against collective overreach. Second, it sets a burden of justification for coercion. Third, it preserves the plurality of ends that positive programs should serve. Without these constraints, positive freedom becomes vulnerable to the claim that individuals are free only when they live according to a politically selected ideal.

But negative freedom is not sufficient in at least three corresponding senses. First, rights require institutions capable of protecting and interpreting them. Second, agency requires social capacities that individuals acquire through education, health, security, and recognition. Third, the benefits of a rights-protecting order create obligations of contribution and maintenance. The foundational status of negative freedom thus concerns its role as the framework within which other goods are pursued, not its ability to satisfy all human needs on its own.

This position can be described as liberal reciprocity. It begins with the individual’s authority over her life, recognizes the social sources of that authority, and asks each person to contribute fairly to the conditions under which everyone can exercise comparable authorship. It is neither classical libertarianism in its strictest form nor egalitarian collectivism. It views society as a cooperative achievement whose institutions must be strong enough to secure agency but restrained enough not to consume it.

## Conclusion

The claim that libertarian non-interference has moral primacy while remaining morally incomplete is ultimately persuasive, provided that both parts are carefully understood. Non-interference is primary because persons are agents, not instruments. They need a protected sphere in which to choose, act, experiment, associate, fail, and revise their lives. Nozick’s experience machine makes this vivid. A life composed of perfectly satisfying experiences would still be deficient if the individual were disconnected from actual achievement, independent relationships, and a resistant world. Meaning arises through action in reality, and action in reality presupposes freedom from domination by a power that would arrange one’s choices and experiences in advance.

The experience machine does not establish an absolute prohibition on all state action. Law can protect agency by preventing violence, fraud, and exploitation. Public institutions can enlarge the practical range of choice. But the thought experiment gives us a reason to distrust political projects that treat welfare, equality, or collective purpose as sufficient grounds for comprehensive control. A society that supplies every good while depriving individuals of authorship would fail to respect the very beings it claims to benefit.

At the same time, the isolated individual presupposed by strict minimalism is a fiction. Human agency develops through cooperation, reciprocal dependence, inherited language, public knowledge, law, infrastructure, care, and social trust. Governance did not simply arise as a defensive pact among fully formed individuals trapped in a state of nature. It grew out of evolved capacities and historical practices of reciprocity, coordination, sanction, and mutual aid. Every person enters a social world she did not create and from which she benefits before she can consent to or contribute to it.

This inherited benefit creates a genuine, though limited, birth debt. It is not a claim that society owns the individual or that every benefit must be repaid in kind. It is an obligation to sustain fair conditions of cooperation for others, including future generations. That obligation can justify taxation for public goods, legal institutions, education, health protection, infrastructure, social insurance, and the repair of serious historical exclusion. It does not justify unlimited redistribution, compulsory sameness, or the perfection of citizens according to an official ideal.

The proper political aim is therefore not to choose between freedom and society. It is to preserve the primacy of individual authorship within a social order that makes authorship possible. Negative freedom guards the space of choice; positive provision can secure the background conditions in which choice becomes effective; reciprocity explains why individuals may owe one another contributions to that background. The resulting state may be more than minimal, but it should never become total. Its task is to enable a world in which persons can confront real resistance, make genuinely their own choices, and share responsibility for the inherited conditions of freedom without being absorbed into a common design.
