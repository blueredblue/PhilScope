# Parmenides and the Modal Fallacy in Fragment B6.1–2

Parmenides’ poem is often presented as the founding gesture of Western ontology. In the Way of Truth, the goddess appears to derive a complete metaphysics from a deceptively simple principle: *what is, is; what is not, is not*. From this beginning she proceeds toward the striking conclusions that what-is is ungenerated, imperishable, indivisible, immovable, and complete. Yet the apparent simplicity of the starting point conceals a serious logical difficulty. In fragment B6.1–2 Parmenides writes:

> χρὴ τὸ λέγειν τε νοεῖν τ’ ἐὸν ἔμμεναι· ἔστι γὰρ εἶναι,  
> μηδὲν δ’ οὐκ ἔστιν· ἐγὼ δέ σε φράζω.

The fragment is commonly translated along the following lines:

> It is necessary to say and to think that Being is; for it is possible for it to be, and nothing is not.

The precise wording varies, but the central issue remains. Does Parmenides move illegitimately from a claim about what must be said or thought to a claim about what must exist? If so, he commits a modal fallacy: he confuses the necessity of a proposition, thought, or linguistic act with the necessity of the thing referred to. Alternatively, perhaps the Greek does not express the de re necessity attributed to it by standard translations. The apparent inference may instead concern the conditions under which something can be identified, spoken of, or made available to thought. On such an interpretation, Parmenides may be defending a semantic or epistemological principle rather than inferring metaphysical existence directly from the rules of discourse.

This essay evaluates the charge of modal fallacy by examining the standard translations associated with John Burnet and G. E. L. Owen, and then considering alternative linguistic frameworks offered by Owen Goldin and T. M. Robinson. The central conclusion will be qualified. If B6.1–2 is interpreted in the strongly de re manner encouraged by the standard translations, Parmenides’ deduction does appear to involve an illicit modal shift. But the Greek also permits, and perhaps invites, interpretations in terms of ascertainment, linguistic obligation, or existential availability. These interpretations do not prove that Parmenides’ entire metaphysics is valid, but they do show that the Way of Truth need not collapse into logical invalidity at its first step.

## I. The modal problem

The modal fallacy can be stated schematically. Suppose that Parmenides’ first claim is understood as:

1. It is necessary that we say and think that Being is.

This is a claim about discourse and cognition. It says that a certain proposition is required, perhaps logically, normatively, or epistemically. It concerns what we must assert or think.

But Parmenides is then often taken to conclude:

2. Being necessarily exists.

The second claim is de re: it says that a particular entity or subject, Being, has necessary existence. The difference between the two claims can be represented using the familiar distinction between de dicto and de re necessity. A de dicto statement has the form:

> Necessarily, “Being is.”

A de re statement has the form:

> Being is such that it necessarily exists.

The first concerns the necessity of a sentence or proposition. The second concerns the modal status of the object referred to. The validity of an inference from the first to the second is not automatic. The necessity of saying that Clark Kent is Superman does not entail that Clark Kent necessarily exists. Nor does the necessity of thinking of an object under a certain description establish the necessary existence of that object.

The difficulty is sharpened by the relation between thinking and being in Parmenides. Fragment B3 is often read as saying:

> For thinking and being are the same.

Fragment B8 then insists that what can be thought and spoken of must be. The danger is that Parmenides treats conceptual or linguistic availability as proof of ontological actuality. If whatever can be thought must be, and if “what-is-not” cannot be thought, then perhaps he has simply built existence into the conditions of reference. The conclusion would be secured by a semantic rule, but then it would not establish the robust metaphysical claims Parmenides wishes to draw from it.

The modal problem therefore concerns not only the word “necessary” but also the transition from the conditions of thought and speech to the structure of reality. Does Parmenides argue:

> We must think and say that what-is is; therefore what-is necessarily is?

Or does he argue something less ambitious:

> Thought and speech can concern only what is available as something; therefore whatever is thought or said must be treated as being?

The first argument is invalid unless additional premises are supplied. The second may be defensible, but it yields a more modest result.

## II. Burnet, Owen, and the de re reading

John Burnet’s influential translation presents the opening as a direct ontological injunction:

> It is necessary to say and to think that Being is; for Being is, and Nothing is not.

In some versions of the tradition, the second clause is rendered modally:

> For it is possible for it to be, and it is impossible for nothing to be.

Burnet’s translation is important because it treats *to eon*—“what is” or “Being”—as a substantial subject. The sentence appears to say that Being exists and that Nothing does not exist. The syntax thereby encourages the reader to understand Parmenides as speaking about two opposed entities or principles: Being and Nothing. Being is affirmed, while Nothing is denied.

This reading can be philosophically powerful. It gives Parmenides a sharply ontological project: he is not merely discussing correct speech but identifying the only genuine object of thought and inquiry. Yet it also makes the modal problem conspicuous. If “it is necessary to say and think that Being is” means that Being necessarily exists, the argument seems to move from a requirement on cognition to an ontological conclusion without justification.

G. E. L. Owen’s translation and interpretation are more technically attentive to the modal language. Owen is particularly important because he emphasizes that Parmenides’ language involves possibility and impossibility. The relevant lines can be understood as saying that what is available for thinking and saying must be, because it is possible for it to be, whereas nothing is not possible. Owen’s reading tends to give *esti* and related forms a modal or existential force. The path of truth is the route on which one says that what-is is and that it cannot not be; the opposite path is impossible because what-is-not cannot be thought or spoken.

Owen’s interpretation helps reconstruct the logic of the poem. Parmenides is not simply repeating the empty tautology “what-is is.” He is contrasting two routes of inquiry:

- the route that says and thinks that what-is is;
- the route that says and thinks that what-is-not is.

The first route is viable because being is available for thought and discourse. The second route fails because non-being is not available in the same way. On this account, Parmenides’ conclusion is connected to a modal principle: what can be thought or said must be possible, while what cannot be thought or said is impossible.

Nevertheless, Owen’s formulation can intensify rather than solve the modal difficulty. Consider the following reconstruction:

1. What can be thought or said must be.
2. What-is can be thought or said.
3. Therefore, what-is must be.
4. What-is must be, and cannot not be.

The first premise may be read de re: anything that can be thought or said must necessarily exist. But why should the possibility of reference entail necessary existence? Even if a fictional character can be thought and discussed, it does not follow that the character exists outside the fiction. Even if one can think “a golden mountain,” the thought does not establish that a golden mountain exists. The distinction between intentional content and actual existence was not formulated in modern terms by Parmenides, but it is precisely the distinction his argument must negotiate.

The problem is not avoided by substituting “possible” for “necessary.” A statement such as “it is possible for Being to be” does not entail “Being necessarily is.” Possibility and necessity are distinct modalities. Nor does the impossibility of thinking Nothing show that nothingness is metaphysically impossible. It may show only that thought requires an object, a content, or a determinate reference. The conclusion that non-being cannot exist is stronger than the conclusion that it cannot be thought.

Owen’s interpretation can therefore be understood in two ways. On a strict de re reading, Parmenides appears to argue from the possibility of an object of thought to the necessary existence of that object. This is invalid. On a more charitable reading, Owen’s emphasis on the logical structure of the two paths suggests that Parmenides is analyzing the conditions of meaningful inquiry. But if so, the metaphysical conclusion must be derived through further arguments rather than directly from B6.1–2.

## III. The syntax of *chre* and the force of the first line

A major issue concerns the Greek word *chre*, conventionally translated “it is necessary.” This translation can be misleading if it suggests an impersonal metaphysical necessity. *Chre* may express obligation, propriety, or what is required in a practical or intellectual context. “One must say and think” need not mean “the object of one’s saying and thinking necessarily exists.” It may mean that a genuine inquiry must proceed by affirming what is rather than attempting to make non-being an object of inquiry.

The grammatical structure is also significant. The first line does not simply say:

> Being necessarily exists.

It says, more literally, something like:

> It is necessary to say and think that what-is is.

The object of *say* and *think* is a proposition or state of affairs. The sentence first-orderly concerns the activity of inquiry. Its subject is not straightforwardly “Being” as an entity but the saying and thinking of what-is. The infinitive *einai* may function as the predicate of the object of thought: one must say and think that what-is is. The statement therefore has a reflexive or methodological character. It tells the inquirer how inquiry must be conducted.

This does not make the sentence trivial. Parmenides’ point may be that inquiry presupposes being in a stronger sense than ordinary linguistic convention does. To ask whether something is, one must already distinguish it from what is not. To identify, describe, or investigate anything, one must treat it as something rather than as sheer nothing. The impossibility of making nothing an object of thought may then be an analysis of the preconditions of intelligibility.

But the existence of such preconditions does not by itself establish that reality consists in a single, eternal, unchanging whole. It establishes only that thought and discourse cannot get started from absolute nothingness. The further conclusions of the Way of Truth require a more elaborate argument. Parmenides may believe that the methodological principle has wider ontological consequences, but those consequences cannot be assumed without argument.

## IV. Goldin and existential availability

Owen Goldin’s alternative framework is significant because it resists treating Parmenides’ language as a straightforward assertion of necessary existence. Goldin emphasizes the relation between being and the availability of an object for thought or discourse. On this kind of reading, the phrase “what-is” does not initially designate a fully determinate metaphysical substance. It designates whatever is available as something to be referred to, considered, or investigated.

The notion of existential availability is useful because it occupies a middle position between mere linguistic reference and robust metaphysical existence. To be existentially available is for something to count as a candidate object of thought or discourse. It must be sufficiently determinate to be picked out as something. Absolute nothingness fails this condition, not necessarily because it is a peculiar object that has been examined and found not to exist, but because it cannot function as an object at all.

This framework alters the logical form of the argument. Instead of:

1. Whatever is thinkable necessarily exists.
2. What-is is thinkable.
3. Therefore, what-is necessarily exists.

we obtain:

1. Whatever can be thought or said must be available as something.
2. What-is is the designation of what is available as something.
3. What-is-not is not available as something.
4. Therefore, genuine thought and speech must concern what-is rather than what-is-not.

The conclusion is not a de re modal claim about an entity called Being. It is a semantic-ontological claim about the minimal conditions under which anything can be an object of thought. The phrase “nothing is not” then does not mean that a special entity called Nothing lacks existence. It means that absolute non-being cannot be introduced as an object in the same way as something can.

Goldin’s framework helps explain why Parmenides can move so quickly between thinking, saying, and being. The connection is not an inference from psychological events to an independent metaphysical realm. Rather, “to be” functions partly as a condition of objecthood. What can be thought must be thinkable *as something*. The relevant necessity is therefore internal to the practice of intelligible reference.

Still, existential availability cannot automatically bear the full weight of Parmenides’ later ontology. From the fact that an object must be available in order to be thought, it does not follow that every genuine object is eternal, indivisible, or spatially complete. Nor does it follow that change is impossible. A changing object may be available for thought at one time and differently available at another. Goldin’s interpretation can exonerate B6 from the charge of a modal fallacy only by restricting what B6 establishes. It shows that the opening principle is not invalid when read as a claim about intelligibility, but it does not by itself establish Parmenidean monism.

This limitation is not necessarily a defect. The poem may proceed by developing implications that Parmenides regards as implicit in the initial principle. For example, if genuine being cannot come from what-is-not, and if what-is cannot become what-is-not, then generation and destruction may be excluded. But those arguments require premises concerning the meaning of coming-to-be, passing-away, and non-being. They should not be attributed solely to the opening line.

## V. Robinson and ascertainment

T. M. Robinson offers another way of weakening the de re modal reading. His approach places emphasis on the linguistic and epistemological role of the verb “to be.” Parmenides’ *einai* need not always function as a simple existential predicate. It may carry a veridical, predicative, or ascertainment-related force. To say that something “is” may mean not merely that it exists but that it is the case, is determinately so, or can be correctly identified.

The notion of ascertainment is especially important. Parmenides’ concern may be with what can be established or made intelligible in inquiry. The Way of Truth is the route on which the inquirer can say how things are. The contrary route fails because “what-is-not” cannot be ascertained. One cannot successfully investigate sheer non-being because there is nothing there to provide a determinate object, fact, or answer.

On Robinson’s framework, the first line might be paraphrased as:

> One must say and think that what is the case is the case.

Or, less tautologically:

> One must say and think of what can be ascertained that it is.

This is not the claim that whatever is mentioned necessarily exists. It is the claim that successful thought and truthful speech must be directed toward something determinate. The word “necessary” then expresses an epistemic or methodological requirement. A thinker must not attempt to make non-being into a positive object of inquiry.

This interpretation also clarifies the relationship between B6 and B2. In B2, Parmenides presents the two ways of inquiry. The first way says “that it is and that it is impossible for it not to be.” The second says “that it is not and that it must not be.” The second route is characterized as wholly unthinkable and nameless. If the issue were merely whether an object exists in an external metaphysical sense, Parmenides would need to explain why a negative proposition cannot be thought. But if the issue is ascertainment, the point is more intelligible: one may think *that something is not*, but one cannot make absolute non-being itself the object of a successful inquiry.

Robinson’s approach therefore distinguishes between:

- thinking that a particular thing is not;
- thinking or speaking of absolute what-is-not as though it were an object.

The first is ordinary and intelligible. We can say that a centaur is not a real animal, that a city no longer exists, or that a proposition is false. The second would require treating non-being as something available for identification. Parmenides denies that this is possible. The claim is not that negative predication is meaningless in every context, but that absolute non-being cannot serve as the positive subject of inquiry.

This distinction removes much of the apparent modal fallacy. Parmenides is not inferring:

> Because we must say that being is, being necessarily exists.

He is saying:

> Because inquiry must concern something determinate, it must affirm what is available for ascertainment and cannot make absolute non-being its object.

The modal force belongs to the rules of inquiry, not directly to the object. “Must” means “must, if inquiry is to be genuine,” rather than “must exist in every possible world.” The modal shift disappears because there is no shift from epistemic necessity to metaphysical necessity.

## VI. Is Parmenides’ argument merely semantic?

A possible objection is that the Goldin and Robinson interpretations save Parmenides only by reducing his metaphysics to a linguistic observation. If the point is merely that we cannot talk about nothing, then Parmenides has not shown that reality is one, ungenerated, imperishable, or changeless. He has shown at most that language and thought require an object.

This objection is forceful. The Way of Truth is not presented as a modest theory of reference. The goddess announces conclusions about the nature of what-is. In B8, she argues that what-is is ungenerated and imperishable because it cannot have come from what-is-not, and it cannot arise from what-is, since it would already exist. She argues that change, division, and motion would require some form of non-being. The poem’s rhetoric is unmistakably metaphysical.

However, the alternative interpretations need not imply that Parmenides is merely making a semantic point. They may instead identify the starting point from which the metaphysical argument proceeds. Parmenides’ ontology is unusual precisely because it treats the conditions of intelligibility as revealing something about being. He may believe that reality cannot violate the conditions under which it is thinkable. If “becoming” requires that something come from nothing, and if nothing cannot be thought as an available entity, then becoming is not merely linguistically awkward; it is ontologically impossible.

The question is whether this further inference is valid. It depends on a principle connecting intelligibility and being. Fragment B3, “for thinking and being are the same,” may supply such a principle. But the principle itself is ambiguous. It may mean:

1. whatever is thought exists;
2. whatever genuinely exists can be thought;
3. thought and being are identical;
4. thought is possible only of what is;
5. the content of thought is always something that is, in the relevant sense.

The first interpretation is implausibly strong and generates obvious counterexamples. The fourth and fifth are more plausible but do not entail that every object of thought exists independently or necessarily. If Parmenides’ argument requires the first principle, it is unsound. If it requires the fourth, it may be coherent but metaphysically limited. If it requires the third, it expresses a radical identification whose meaning must be worked out rather than assumed.

Goldin and Robinson make it possible to understand Parmenides as operating with the fourth or fifth principle. The “same” relation between thought and being is not a causal relation from thoughts to objects, but a structural relation between intelligibility and objecthood. Thought, when successful, is always thought of something determinate. Absolute nothingness cannot be a successful object of thought. This does not prove that every intelligible object is actual, but it explains why Parmenides treats non-being as incapable of serving as the basis of explanation.

## VII. The status of “nothing”

The modal fallacy becomes especially visible in the treatment of “nothing.” Parmenides appears to reason as follows:

1. Nothing is not.
2. What-is-not cannot be thought or said.
3. Therefore, there is no genuine route of inquiry through non-being.

The difficulty is that “nothing” is grammatically presented as though it were a subject. “Nothing is not” can sound like a predication about a thing called Nothing. But absolute nothingness is not an entity with properties. The sentence must therefore be interpreted carefully.

There are at least three possibilities.

First, “nothing is not” may mean that there is no case of non-being. This is a strong ontological claim: reality contains no absence of being whatsoever.

Second, it may mean that “nothing” cannot function as a subject of true predication. The phrase “nothing is not” does not describe an entity; it marks the failure of a purported object.

Third, it may mean that absolute non-being cannot be existentially introduced into discourse. “Nothing” is not available as a possible object, even though negative expressions have ordinary uses.

The second and third readings are compatible with Goldin’s existential availability and Robinson’s ascertainment framework. They also explain why Parmenides can insist that what-is-not is unthinkable without denying every ordinary negative judgment. We can think that a house is not occupied or that a god does not exist. But these thoughts do not make absolute non-being into an object. They concern an existing subject, a possible object, a failed description, or a negated state of affairs.

The importance of this distinction is that Parmenides’ argument may be directed against an ontological use of negation rather than against negation as such. He rejects the attempt to explain reality by appealing to sheer non-being. If something comes to be, it must come either from being or from non-being. If it comes from being, it is already there; if from non-being, there is no source. The argument becomes valid only if “non-being” is understood as absolute nothingness, not as the absence of a particular property or the nonexistence of a particular object.

This qualification is often obscured by translations that capitalize “Being” and “Nothing.” Capitalization makes the opposites appear like two metaphysical substances. But the Greek participle *eon* can function more flexibly, as “what is,” “that which is,” or “being.” The phrase need not name a cosmic entity in the manner of a proper noun. A translation that preserves this flexibility is philosophically preferable because it prevents the argument from acquiring a stronger modal and ontological form than the syntax warrants.

## VIII. Does the argument collapse?

We can now distinguish two versions of Parmenides’ argument.

### The strong de re version

The strong version runs approximately as follows:

1. It is necessary to say and think that Being is.
2. What can be thought and said must exist.
3. Therefore, Being necessarily exists.
4. Nothing cannot exist.
5. Therefore, only Being exists.
6. Since Being cannot arise from or pass into Nothing, it is ungenerated and imperishable.
7. Since division and motion require non-being, Being is indivisible and immovable.

This argument is vulnerable at several points. Premise 2 is too strong if it means that every thinkable object necessarily exists. The move from a necessity concerning saying and thinking to a necessity concerning the object is invalid. The denial of absolute non-being does not by itself establish the unity of all being. And the later conclusions require substantive premises about the meaning of generation, change, plurality, and space.

On this interpretation, Parmenides does commit a modal fallacy. The argument confuses the necessity of a proposition or practice with the necessary existence of its referent. If the standard translations are read in this way, the criticism is justified.

### The weaker ascertainment or availability version

The weaker version runs as follows:

1. Inquiry and intelligible speech must concern something determinate.
2. What is available for thought and discourse must be treated as being, in the relevant sense.
3. Absolute non-being is not available as an object of thought or discourse.
4. Therefore, the genuine route of inquiry must affirm what-is and cannot proceed by positing absolute non-being.
5. Any account of coming-to-be or passing-away that presupposes absolute non-being is defective.

This argument does not commit a modal fallacy. The necessity in the first premise is methodological or epistemic. It governs the activity of inquiry. The conclusion concerns the impossibility of using absolute non-being as an explanatory principle. There is no illicit inference to the necessary existence of a separately identified object.

The weaker argument, however, does not automatically establish Parmenides’ entire metaphysics. To reach the claims of B8, Parmenides must add arguments about the conditions of generation, destruction, division, and motion. Those arguments may themselves be disputed, but they are not simply instances of the initial modal confusion. The accusation of fallacy must therefore be directed at particular transitions, not at the whole poem indiscriminately.

## IX. Evaluating Burnet and Owen in light of the alternatives

Burnet’s translation has the merit of clarity and philosophical force. It makes the opposition between Being and Nothing immediately intelligible. But its capitalization and its rendering of the infinitives can encourage a substantialist interpretation. “Being is” sounds like a metaphysical thesis about a cosmic entity. That may reflect one important dimension of Parmenides, but it risks concealing the methodological and linguistic structure of the Greek.

Owen’s interpretation is more sensitive to the poem’s modal vocabulary and its relation to the two ways of inquiry. Yet precisely because it foregrounds possibility and necessity, it can be read as attributing to Parmenides a de re modal thesis that the text does not straightforwardly express. If “what can be thought and spoken of must be” is understood as “whatever is thinkable necessarily exists,” the resulting argument is invalid. Owen’s reconstruction is philosophically illuminating, but it must not be treated as a transparent statement of the Greek’s logical form.

Goldin’s framework improves the situation by identifying a sense in which thought and being are linked without making all thinkable objects independently existent. Existential availability explains why a candidate for thought must be “something” while preserving the distinction between availability and necessary existence. It also explains why nothing is unavailable without positing Nothing as a deficient object.

Robinson’s emphasis on ascertainment complements Goldin’s account. It clarifies the practical and epistemic force of *chre*. Parmenides may be saying what an inquirer must do to conduct a genuine investigation: affirm what can be established and refuse to treat absolute non-being as an object of inquiry. The relevant necessity is not the metaphysical necessity of a thing but the rational necessity of a method.

The alternatives therefore do not merely offer softer translations. They propose a different theory of the relation between language, thought, and being. Under the Burnet–Owen de re framework, the crucial inference appears to be:

> It is necessary to think of being as existing; therefore being necessarily exists.

Under the Goldin–Robinson framework, the inference is closer to:

> Anything that can serve as an object of thought or ascertainment must be available as something; therefore inquiry cannot make absolute non-being its object.

The second argument is not vulnerable to the same modal criticism because it does not infer an ontological necessity from a linguistic necessity. Its claim is conditional: if thought and inquiry are to be possible, their objects must be existentially available or ascertainable.

## Conclusion

Parmenides does commit a modal fallacy if fragment B6.1–2 is reconstructed according to a strong de re reading. The necessity of saying and thinking that Being is cannot by itself establish that Being necessarily exists. Nor does the impossibility of thinking absolute non-being automatically prove that non-being is metaphysically impossible. The standard translations associated with Burnet and Owen can encourage precisely this confusion by presenting “Being” as a substantial subject and by allowing the modal force of “must” to migrate from the act of thought to its supposed object.
