### The Direct Argument and the Illusion of Autonomy: A Collapse into Indirect Control

The debate between compatibilists and incompatibilists over moral responsibility hinges on whether the truth of determinism would undermine the conditions required for agents to be morally responsible for their actions. Historically, incompatibilists relied heavily on the Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP), which asserts that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if they could have done otherwise. However, the advent of Frankfurt-style counterexamples—which feature counterfactual interveners ensuring that the agent cannot do otherwise while the agent still acts freely—severely weakened the appeal of PAP. In response, philosophers like Peter van Inwagen developed the *Direct Argument* for incompatibilism, which purports to show that determinism is incompatible with moral responsibility without invoking PAP at all. This argument relies on a transfer principle, often called Transfer NR1, which states that if no one is morally responsible for a proposition *p*, and no one is morally responsible for the fact that *p* entails *q*, then no one is morally responsible for *q*. If valid, this principle would allow incompatibilists to conclude that since no one is responsible for the distant past or the laws of nature, and these entail all subsequent events under determinism, no one is responsible for anything.

In this essay, I will argue that the Direct Argument fails to successfully avoid reliance on PAP because its central inference rule, Transfer NR1, is demonstrably invalid. I will substantiate this claim by examining a specific one-path counterexample involving an agent, Bob, whose undetermined choice prevents a brain abnormality from operating, while a lightning strike simultaneously eliminates all alternative possibilities. Through this case, I will show that an agent can be morally responsible for an action even when the conditions of NR1 are met, thereby refuting the principle. Furthermore, I will assess whether incompatibilists can neutralize this counterexample without defending PAP against Frankfurt-type objections. I will conclude that they cannot. Any attempt to salvage NR1—whether by restricting its scope, denying Bob’s responsibility, or appealing to a sourcehood condition—either tacitly reintroduces a control requirement functionally equivalent to PAP or collapses the Direct Argument into an indirect argument that relies on substantive metaphysical conditions for responsibility. Consequently, the Direct Argument does not stand as a distinct, self-sufficient route to incompatibilism; it inevitably collapses into an indirect argument vulnerable to the very traditional control requirements it sought to bypass.

#### I. The Structure of the Direct Argument and the Role of Transfer NR1

To understand the stakes, it is essential to articulate the Direct Argument precisely. The argument is designed to establish that if determinism is true, then no agent is morally responsible for any action, regardless of whether they could have done otherwise. The argument’s power lies in its apparent neutrality: it does not require a positive account of what moral responsibility *requires*; it only requires a negative transfer principle. The standard formulation, derived from van Inwagen’s work, employs a sentential operator *N* such that *Np* reads “No one is, or ever has been, morally responsible for the fact that *p*.” The crucial inference rule, which I will call Transfer NR1 (or Rule B), is:

- (NR1) From *Np* and *N(p ⊃ q)*, infer *Nq*.
