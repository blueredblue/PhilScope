# Faith, Ontology, and the Limits of the Pre-Theological: Heidegger and Andersen on Theology’s Conceptual Task

## Introduction

Martin Heidegger’s philosophy of existence presents theology with both a resource and a threat. It offers theology a vocabulary for describing human existence as finite, situated, historical, and responsible. Yet it also insists that philosophy must remain independent of faith. Philosophy, Heidegger argues, is “atheistic” in a methodological sense: it cannot appeal to revelation, grace, creation, Christ, or God as the basis of its analysis. Theology, by contrast, is an ontic science. It arises from faith and reflects conceptually upon the existence opened by faith. For that reason, Heidegger contends, theology requires a pre-theological ontology. It must borrow from philosophy an account of the being of the human being, of worldhood, temporality, finitude, and responsibility before it can properly articulate its own faith-specific concepts.

Nathan Andersen’s critique challenges this order of dependence. The problem, as Andersen presents it, is not simply that Heidegger’s ontology is incomplete or that theology might prefer another philosophical vocabulary. The deeper problem is that Heidegger’s account of Dasein appears unable to describe precisely those phenomena that theology regards as most fundamental. Heidegger’s analysis of thrown projection and Being-guilty may illuminate human finitude and responsibility, but it does not seem capable of explaining man’s guilt before God. Likewise, an ontology that begins with an explicitly non-faithful account of Dasein appears unable to account for the creative existence of the Word, or for the manner in which faith constitutes rather than merely modifies human existence.

The debate therefore concerns the possibility of theological conceptual understanding. Can theology use philosophy to clarify its concepts while remaining within faith? Or does Heidegger’s demand for a pre-theological foundation require theology to abandon the very relation to God that makes it theology? The issue is not whether theology should think philosophically. Theology has always required conceptual reflection, distinctions, argument, and interpretation. The issue is whether philosophy can supply theology with the ontological ground on which its concepts rest, or whether the attempt to do so distorts the phenomena of faith by treating them as secondary expressions of a more basic human structure.

This essay argues that Andersen’s critique exposes a genuine limit in Heidegger’s claim, but not necessarily a complete defeat of it. Heidegger is right that theology cannot dispense with ontological clarification and that faith cannot simply replace conceptual analysis. Yet he is wrong if he means that theology must receive its basic account of existence from a philosophy methodologically closed to faith. Theological concepts cannot be adequately understood through an ontology that regards faith as an optional or derivative mode of existence. At the same time, theology can employ Heideggerian analysis critically, provided that it treats ontology not as a neutral foundation but as a provisional and revisable account that must be transformed by the content of revelation. Theology can achieve conceptual understanding through philosophy, but only if philosophy is subordinated to, and eventually reoriented by, the faithful relation to God.

## I. Heidegger’s Distinction Between Philosophy and Theology

Heidegger’s account of theology depends upon a distinction between the ontological and the ontic. Ontology asks about Being and about the structures that make beings intelligible. Ontic inquiry concerns particular beings and particular domains of existence. The natural sciences, for example, investigate regions of entities according to determinate methods. Theology, Heidegger argues, is an ontic science because it is concerned with a specific mode of human existence: Christian existence as constituted by faith. It does not begin from a universal analysis of Being as such. It begins from a concrete event or fact of faith, from the revelation of Christ and from the believer’s participation in the life made possible by that revelation.

This does not make theology inferior to philosophy. Heidegger’s point is instead that the two disciplines have different objects and methods. Theology is not a defective philosophy, and philosophy is not a more abstract theology. Theology has its own concepts—sin, grace, revelation, faith, reconciliation, Christ, and the Church—whose meaning arises from the Christian event. But precisely because theology is a science, it must reflect conceptually upon these phenomena. It cannot remain at the level of unexamined religious experience or ecclesial assertion.

Theology’s conceptual task, however, requires some prior understanding of the human being. If theology speaks of sin, repentance, faith, or grace, it must know what sort of being can sin, repent, believe, and receive grace. If it speaks of revelation, it must have some account of the being to whom revelation occurs. If it speaks of salvation, it must understand the structure of the existence that requires salvation. This is where Heidegger’s ontology enters. Fundamental ontology, especially the analytic of Dasein in *Being and Time*, describes the human being as the entity for whom Being is an issue. Dasein is not defined primarily as a rational animal, a substance, or a soul. It is characterized by being-in-the-world, thrownness, projection, attunement, understanding, discourse, temporality, and being-with-others.

The theological significance of this analysis lies in its ability to clarify the formal structures within which religious existence occurs. Faith is not an event happening to an otherwise completed subject. It takes place in a being already situated in a world, shaped by historical inheritance, exposed to others, and projected toward possibilities. Theology can therefore use ontology to avoid vague or purely psychological accounts of faith. A theological doctrine of conversion, for example, would be inadequate if it treated conversion as a private alteration of consciousness without considering the world, practices, language, and community in which it takes shape.

Yet Heidegger insists that philosophy must remain independent of faith. Philosophy is “atheistic,” not necessarily because it denies God, but because it cannot appeal to God within its own method. A philosophical analysis must not presuppose the truth of revelation. It must suspend theological commitments in order to disclose the structures of existence as such. The philosopher may be personally religious, but philosophy itself cannot proceed by faith. In this respect, Heidegger’s philosophy is formally atheistic: it does not have God as an object of its inquiry and does not take divine revelation as an evidentiary source.

This methodological atheism explains why Heidegger believes philosophy can correct theology. Theology is tempted, he suggests, to use concepts inherited from metaphysics, psychology, or ordinary religious language without recognizing their ontological implications. Philosophy can expose the implicit understanding of Being that theology brings to its concepts. It can show, for example, whether theological accounts of the self unconsciously treat the human being as a detached subject or as a timeless substance. Philosophy thus performs an ontological corrective to theology’s ontic concepts.

The essential structure of this proposal is asymmetrical: theology depends upon philosophy for ontological clarification, while philosophy does not depend upon theology for its fundamental task. Theology may contribute information about Christian existence, but the ontological interpretation of existence must be accomplished without faith. This asymmetry is the point at which Andersen’s critique becomes most forceful.

## II. Andersen’s Critique: Faith as the Beginning and End of Theology

Andersen’s central objection is that Heidegger’s distinction between ontology and theology cannot account for the way faith constitutes theological existence. Theology does not merely begin with faith as an empirical fact and then proceed to a philosophical understanding of the structures underlying it. Faith is not an optional layer placed upon a preexisting human existence. It is the relation through which Christian existence receives its meaning. If theology begins and ends in faith, then the concepts it develops cannot be grounded in an ontology that deliberately excludes faith from its account of existence.

This does not mean that every theological claim is self-validating or that theology may ignore philosophy. Rather, it means that theology’s object is not fully available apart from the relation by which it is disclosed. Christian existence cannot be adequately understood from the standpoint of a neutral observer who brackets revelation. To understand faith as Christian faith is already to be concerned with its reference to God, Christ, and the Word. A description that excludes that reference may describe something about the believer, but it cannot yet describe Christian existence in its theological specificity.

The issue can be stated in terms of the difference between a formal structure and a concrete relation. Heidegger’s analysis often presents structures such as thrownness, projection, understanding, and guilt in a formally general way. These structures are intended to apply to Dasein as such, before any particular religious or cultural determination. They identify the conditions under which any human existence can occur. Andersen’s worry is that theology’s phenomena are not merely concrete instances of these formal structures. Faith may not be one possibility among others within Dasein’s preexisting field of possibilities. It may instead transform what it means for Dasein to have possibilities, to be responsible, to understand itself, and to relate to its origin.

If faith is treated as an ontic mode of a more fundamental Dasein, then the relation to God is subordinated to a structure that has already been defined without God. Theologically, however, the human being is not first an autonomous or neutral Dasein who then happens to enter into relation with God. The human being is created, called, judged, and sustained. Human existence is from the beginning dependent upon God, even when that dependence is denied or misunderstood. The theological meaning of existence is therefore not something added to its ontological structure; it concerns the structure’s ultimate source and truth.

Andersen’s critique also targets Heidegger’s account of theology as an ontic science. The designation “ontic” seems to imply that theology describes a particular region or mode of beings within a more universal ontological horizon. But theology’s subject matter—God, creation, sin, revelation, salvation—does not fit neatly within the category of a regional science. Theology does not merely investigate a special type of human behavior called faith. It asks about the relation between the creature and the Creator, and this relation bears upon the meaning of Being, world, history, and existence as a whole. If theology’s concepts are shaped by revelation, then the theological account of the human being may not be ontically regional in the sense Heidegger intends.

The question is therefore whether Heidegger’s “pre-theological ontology” is genuinely pre-theological or whether it secretly imposes a non-theological interpretation of human existence upon theology. If ontology defines the human being before faith as thrown projection, theology must then translate its doctrines into a conceptual framework whose basic categories already exclude creation, revelation, and divine address. Theology might still speak of God, but God would appear as a determination of an existence whose fundamental structure had already been established without God.

## III. Being-Guilty and Guilt Before God

The disagreement becomes especially clear in the contrast between Heidegger’s concept of Being-guilty and the theological concept of guilt before God. In *Being and Time*, Heidegger interprets guilt existentially rather than primarily morally or juridically. Dasein is guilty because it is the ground of a nullity. It is thrown into existence without having chosen its thrownness, yet it must project itself into possibilities. In choosing one possibility, it leaves others unactualized. Its existence is therefore marked by a constitutive indebtedness or lack. Dasein is responsible for its existence, but it never possesses itself as a complete origin. It must be the ground of itself while never having laid that ground.

This analysis has considerable philosophical power. It shows that responsibility is not simply a matter of violating externally imposed rules. Human beings are implicated in their own existence before they make particular moral decisions. They inherit conditions they did not choose, yet they must respond to them. Their freedom is finite because it is situated within facticity. Their projects are exposed to failure, and their self-understanding is haunted by possibilities not realized. Heidegger’s Being-guilty captures a deep dimension of human finitude: the human being is answerable for a life whose ultimate conditions it did not create.

For theology, however, this existential guilt is not identical with sin. Guilt before God is not merely the ontological indebtedness of a finite being. It involves a broken relation to the Creator and a failure to respond to divine command and love. Sin is not simply the fact that human existence is finite, contingent, and non-self-grounding. A creature can be finite without being sinful. The distinction matters because if finitude itself is equated with guilt, then creation becomes intrinsically defective. But Christian theology generally holds that the creature’s dependence upon God is good; the problem is not that the human being is created, but that the human being turns away from God.

Heidegger’s account cannot, by itself, explain this distinction. Being-guilty is built into the structure of Dasein as such. It belongs to every human existence, regardless of whether the individual has heard of God, received revelation, or violated a divine command. The theological concept of guilt, by contrast, is relational and historical. It depends upon the relation between human beings and God, and it is illuminated by the content of divine law, covenant, judgment, and forgiveness. A person may be existentially guilty in Heidegger’s sense simply by being a finite projector of possibilities. But to be guilty before God is to stand under a claim that cannot be derived from the mere structure of projection.

This difference is not erased by saying that Heidegger’s guilt is formal and theology may later give it a concrete interpretation. The question is whether the formal structure is neutral enough to receive the theological meaning without distortion. If guilt is originally defined as the necessity of being the ground of a nullity, then theological sin risks being understood as a symbolic or ontic interpretation of a more basic existential condition. The theological claim that sin is rebellion against God would then become one way of describing a universal structure that is, in itself, indifferent to God.

Andersen is right to resist this reduction. Theological guilt cannot be understood merely as an intensified version of existential finitude. Guilt before God presupposes that existence is fundamentally gift. The human being owes its existence to another and is called to receive that gift in a proper way. Sin is therefore not simply a deficiency in self-grounding; it is a refusal of creaturely dependence. The possibility of sin arises only within a created order in which the human being is addressed by God. The divine relation is not a later interpretation of a neutral existential condition but the horizon within which the meaning of guilt is constituted.

Nevertheless, Heidegger’s analysis can still offer a limited service to theology. It can help theology articulate how guilt is experienced as a burden of existence, how human beings evade responsibility, and how they are tempted to understand themselves as victims of circumstances or as self-sufficient subjects. It can reveal why moral language sometimes fails to reach the depth of human responsibility. But it cannot decide what guilt ultimately is. It clarifies the ontological possibility of responsibility, not the theological source, object, or resolution of guilt. In this respect, Being-guilty may be a useful phenomenological analogue to sin, but it cannot ground the doctrine of sin.

## IV. Thrown Projection and the Creative Existence of the Word

A second difficulty concerns Heidegger’s account of Dasein as thrown projection and theology’s account of creation, especially the creative existence of the Word. Dasein is thrown into a world it did not choose, and it projects itself toward possibilities. Its existence is thus neither pure determinism nor absolute self-creation. It is a finite synthesis of facticity and freedom. Dasein receives a situation and must take it up through projection.

This structure appears, at first, to have theological promise. Human beings are not self-originating; they receive their conditions. They are called to make something of an existence they did not invent. One might even interpret thrownness as a secular analogue of creatureliness. The human being exists from another and must live responsibly within a given world. Similarly, projection may seem to express the dynamism of created existence, which is not static but directed toward fulfillment.

Yet the analogy breaks down at the level of creation itself. Heidegger’s thrownness is a description of Dasein’s condition, not an account of the act by which beings are brought into existence. It tells us that Dasein finds itself already in a world, but it does not tell us why there is a world or why Dasein exists. Thrownness is therefore phenomenological rather than cosmological or theological. It describes the experience of having been delivered into existence without identifying the giver.

The theological concept of creation is not merely the assertion that human beings have a past they did not choose. Creation concerns the dependence of being itself upon God. The creature does not merely begin within an already existing field; it receives its existence at every moment. The world is not simply the context into which Dasein is thrown. It is created, sustained, ordered, and directed by God. The difference is important because Heidegger’s ontology treats worldhood as a structure of Dasein’s disclosedness, whereas theology treats the world as a reality that exists by divine act independently of its being disclosed by human existence.

The issue is even sharper when one considers the Word. In Christian theology, the Word is not simply a human possibility, a meaning articulated within language, or a horizon of intelligibility. The Word is creative. “In the beginning was the Word,” and through the Word all things come to be. The Word is simultaneously divine self-expression, the principle of creation, and, in Christian confession, the person of Christ. An ontology of Dasein may analyze discourse, language, interpretation, and the disclosure of beings, but it cannot thereby account for the Word as the source of being.

Heidegger’s later philosophy gives language a much more fundamental status than does the early analytic of Dasein. His claim that language is the “house of Being” may appear to approach a theological understanding of the Word. Yet the philosophical Word remains distinct from the Christian Logos. Heidegger’s language discloses Being; it does not create beings. It is the site in which Being comes to presence, not the divine act by which the world is called into existence. The creative Word cannot be reduced to a structure of intelligibility available within human language.

If the Word is treated merely as a mode of disclosure, theology loses the distinction between creation and interpretation. Human beings interpret a world; God creates the world. The Word is not simply that through which Dasein understands entities. It is that through which entities are. The ontological difference between creaturely disclosure and divine creation must be maintained. Heidegger’s analysis can describe how the world becomes meaningful for Dasein, but it cannot explain the source of the world’s existence or the divine freedom of creation.

This limitation also affects the theological understanding of faith. Faith is not simply a new projection upon possibilities disclosed by an existing world. It is a response to the Word that creates and recreates. The believer does not merely choose a religious possibility from within the field of Dasein’s projects. Faith is awakened by an address that comes from beyond the subject’s self-projection. It is receptive before it is elective. It is a response to a call that does not originate in Dasein and cannot be reduced to Dasein’s projection of itself.

Heidegger’s account of authenticity may therefore be inadequate to faith. Authenticity involves Dasein’s resolute retrieval of its own possibilities in the face of death and its refusal to lose itself in the anonymous “they.” Faith, by contrast, may require a surrender of self-possession and a reception of an identity that cannot be generated through resolute projection. The believer’s existence is not simply more authentic Dasein. It is existence transformed by a relation to God. The central category is not only authenticity but receptivity, obedience, trust, and participation.

## V. Is Heidegger’s Atheism a Defect or a Necessary Discipline?

The force of Heidegger’s position should not be dismissed. There is a legitimate concern behind his insistence that philosophy remain atheistic. If philosophy begins by affirming God, it may cease to be philosophy in the sense of an inquiry that exposes its claims to rational examination. A philosophy that treats revelation as an unquestionable premise may be unable to distinguish between faith’s internal convictions and conclusions that can be shared across differing modes of existence. Heidegger wishes to preserve philosophy’s critical independence.

Moreover, theology itself can suffer when it uses religious language without examining the ontological assumptions embedded in it. Theological traditions have often borrowed concepts from Greek metaphysics, modern subjectivity, natural science, and psychology. These concepts may illuminate doctrine, but they can also obscure it. Heidegger’s demand for ontological correction is thus a warning against conceptual complacency. Theology should ask what it means to speak of a person, an event, a world, a possibility, a relation, or a history. It should not assume that inherited categories are transparent.

The difficulty is that Heidegger turns a legitimate methodological caution into a substantive hierarchy. He does not merely say that philosophy must not presuppose faith; he suggests that theology must first submit its basic understanding of existence to a philosophy that has intentionally excluded faith. This is much more problematic. Philosophy’s independence does not entail its priority. A method that brackets revelation may be useful for examining certain formal structures, but it cannot claim to offer a complete account of the being at issue in theology.

The term “atheism” is ambiguous here. If it means that philosophy cannot establish its claims by appeal to faith, then theology can accept it as a methodological limitation. Theology itself may recognize that philosophical argument cannot be replaced by scriptural citation in every context. But if atheism means that the structures of existence must be interpreted as though God were absent, then it becomes more than a method. It becomes a substantive account of reality, one that theology cannot simply adopt without contradiction.

An explicitly non-faithful ontology cannot be neutral with respect to theological phenomena. Its exclusion of God already determines how it understands dependence, freedom, guilt, truth, and possibility. Dependence becomes thrownness rather than creation. Calling becomes conscience or resoluteness rather than divine address. Guilt becomes ontological indebtedness rather than sin. Salvation becomes authenticity, freedom, or transformation rather than reconciliation. These are not merely different words for the same phenomena. They locate phenomena within different worlds of meaning.

Heidegger might respond that he is not trying to explain theological meanings, only to describe the formal conditions under which they can appear. But the formal cannot be separated entirely from the material when the material changes the meaning of the form. A call from God is not simply an instance of calling. Guilt before God is not simply an instance of responsibility. Creation is not simply an instance of thrownness. The theological content reconfigures the structures themselves. It changes what it means to exist, to be responsible, to understand, and to project.

Thus philosophy can be atheistic in the limited sense of not proceeding from faith, but it cannot then claim to provide theology with an unalterable ontological foundation. Theology must be able to question the philosophical account in light of revelation. The relation should be reciprocal rather than one-directional: philosophy may clarify theology, while theology may disclose limits and hidden presuppositions in philosophy.

## VI. Can Theology Think Through Philosophy Without Leaving Faith?

The most important question is not whether theology needs philosophy. It does. The question is what kind of philosophical use is compatible with faith. A theology that refuses all philosophical mediation risks collapsing into assertion, sentiment, or private experience. Faith seeks understanding, and understanding requires concepts. Theological doctrines must be distinguished from one another, related coherently, defended against objections, and interpreted in changing historical circumstances. Philosophy contributes to this task by examining the meaning and coherence of theological claims.

But theology’s philosophical use must be governed by the nature of its object. Philosophy cannot be treated as an external foundation upon which theology builds a second story. Theology is not a house erected upon an ontological basement supplied by secular philosophy. Its concepts arise from a distinctive event of revelation, and philosophical reflection must serve the articulation of that event rather than determine in advance what may count as real or intelligible.

This does not mean that theology must develop an entirely separate ontology unrelated to philosophy. It means that theology should be willing to transform philosophical categories. For example, Heidegger’s thrownness can be reinterpreted through creation, but not simply identified with it. Projection can be understood as the finite activity of a creature called toward fulfillment, but not as the ultimate structure of human freedom. Being-guilty can illuminate existential responsibility, but theological guilt must be defined through the relation to God and the possibility of forgiveness. Discourse and disclosure can help describe how revelation is received and interpreted, but they cannot replace the creative and personal Word.

Such appropriation is neither mere rejection nor uncritical acceptance. It is a hermeneutical process in which philosophy provides distinctions and theology determines their ultimate orientation. Theological thinking can recognize in Heidegger a description of the human being as never self-enclosed, always situated, and always responsible. Yet it can also insist that these structures are not intelligible apart from God. Thrownness becomes creaturely givenness. Projection becomes vocation. Conscience becomes capable of bearing divine address. Authenticity is transformed into a form of truthful existence before God rather than self-recovery through resolute choice.

The concept of faith is decisive in this transformation. Faith is not irrational assent opposed to conceptual thought. Nor is it simply an ontic possibility chosen by Dasein. Faith is a mode of knowing and existing in which the human being receives truth through relation to God. It is therefore both cognitive and existential. Faith gives theology its object and orientation, but it does not eliminate the need for reason. Instead, it calls reason to a task that reason could not set for itself. Theology thinks within faith because faith opens the horizon in which theological concepts become meaningful.

To say that theology thinks within faith is also to deny that the theologian occupies a neutral standpoint outside the phenomenon. Heidegger’s model seems to imagine that theology can be objectified by philosophy in the same way that a science studies a regional domain. But theology’s relation to its object is participatory. The theologian does not stand outside God’s revelation as an indifferent observer. The truth theology seeks concerns the theologian’s own existence and demands response. This does not make theology arbitrary; rather, it means that its object cannot be separated from the mode of relation by which it is known.

A parallel may be drawn with ethics. A purely external observer can describe moral behavior, but such description does not exhaust the meaning of obligation. The force of “you ought” is not captured by cataloguing human responses to social norms. Similarly, one may describe religious behavior without understanding the meaning of faith. Theological understanding requires participation in the relation that gives theological phenomena their identity. This is why a non-faithful ontology can describe the conditions around faith but not fully comprehend faith’s essence.

## VII. The Possibility of a Theological Ontology

Andersen’s critique implies that theology may need more than a pre-theological ontology; it may need a theological ontology. Such an ontology would not claim to replace philosophy or to turn revelation into a deductive system. It would instead ask how the fundamental structures of existence appear when interpreted through creation, fall, incarnation, and redemption.

A theological ontology would begin with givenness, but it would distinguish several forms of givenness. The human being is given to itself through creation, through historical embodiment, through relations with others, and through the divine call. Thrownness would not be the final word about givenness because the creature’s being-thrown is itself dependent upon being-created. The world would not be merely the field of Dasein’s practical dealings but the created order in which beings possess a goodness that exceeds their usefulness for human projects.

Likewise, freedom would be interpreted neither as arbitrary choice nor solely as projection. Created freedom is responsive. It is capable of receiving and returning the good. Its fulfillment is not self-grounding but communion. Sin would then appear not as a necessary feature of finitude but as a deformation of freedom’s relation to its source. This allows theology to preserve the goodness of creation while explaining human alienation.

A theological ontology would also give priority to relation over isolated subjectivity. Heidegger’s Dasein is already being-in-the-world and being-with-others, but the theological account deepens this relationality by grounding it in divine love. The human being is not merely open to beings or disclosed to a world; it is addressed by God and called into communion. The personal character of this address matters. The Word is not an impersonal clearing in which beings appear. It is divine self-communication. Theologically understood, truth is not only disclosure but gift and invitation.

This ontology would remain philosophical in the sense that it would seek conceptual clarity and rational coherence. But it would not be pre-theological in the strong Heideggerian sense. It would be generated from theology’s reflection upon revelation and would remain answerable to faith. Its categories would be neither imposed by a neutral philosophy nor insulated from philosophical criticism. They would be open to dialogue with metaphysics, phenomenology, hermeneutics, and the sciences while refusing to treat any of these disciplines as the final judge of theological meaning.

The notion of the Word illustrates the need for such an ontology. If the Word is creative, then being is fundamentally communicative and received. Things are not simply present objects; they are creatures called into existence. Human language is not the source of being, but it can participate in the response to divine speech. Theology can therefore use Heidegger’s concern with language and disclosure while redirecting it toward creation and revelation. The question is not only how beings are disclosed to Dasein, but how Dasein itself is addressed and constituted by the Word.

## VIII. A Qualified Resolution

The dispute between Heidegger and Andersen can now be resolved through a distinction between formal dependence and substantive dependence. Theology may depend upon philosophy for formal clarification. It needs help in examining concepts such as existence, temporality, freedom, relation, responsibility, and interpretation. In that sense, Heidegger is right: theology should not assume that it already possesses an adequate ontology simply because it possesses faith.

But theology does not depend upon a non-faithful philosophy for the substantive meaning of its fundamental concepts. Philosophy cannot tell theology what sin is, what creation is, what revelation is, or who Christ is. It can identify structures within which these realities are experienced and understood, but it cannot supply their theological content. The relation between philosophy and theology is therefore not one of foundation and application. It is one of critical cooperation in which philosophy clarifies, while revelation determines the horizon and content of theological thought.

This qualification also answers the question of whether an explicitly non-faithful ontology can account for guilt before God and the creative existence of the Word. It cannot account for them in their theological specificity. It can describe finite responsibility, dependence, disclosure, and linguistic meaning. It can show why human existence is never self-transparent or self-grounding. But it cannot derive the relation to God from Dasein’s structure. God does not emerge as the conclusion of thrown projection. Divine address is not a possibility generated by Dasein. Creation is not explained by the fact that Dasein finds itself in a world. The Word is not exhausted by language’s function of disclosure.

Still, the failure to account for these phenomena does not make Heidegger useless to theology. It shows instead that his ontology is regional in a different sense: it is an analysis of finite existence under conditions in which revelation is not methodologically assumed. It can illuminate the human side of theological existence, but it cannot disclose the divine side. Theology must therefore refuse both total dependence and total independence. It should neither reduce revelation to existential structures nor reject existential analysis as irrelevant.

The deepest issue concerns whether faith and conceptual understanding are opposed. Heidegger’s atheism seems to preserve philosophical rigor by excluding faith, while Andersen’s critique insists that excluding faith also excludes the phenomenon theology must understand. The proper conclusion is not that faith makes concepts unnecessary, but that faith changes the task of conceptuality. Theology does not think from a neutral starting point toward God as an external conclusion. It thinks from God’s self-disclosure toward an understanding of what human existence means in relation to God. Its concepts are therefore reflexive: they clarify the content and implications of faith without pretending to stand outside faith.

This is not circularity in a disqualifying sense. Every discipline has a horizon that makes its questions possible. Physics presupposes the intelligibility of nature; ethics presupposes that obligation is meaningful; historical interpretation presupposes that the past can be understood. Theology presupposes revelation and faith, not as arbitrary dogmas but as the event and relation that constitute its object. The task is to think responsibly within that horizon, to test interpretations, expose contradictions, and distinguish authentic understanding from projection or wishful thinking.

## Conclusion

Nathan Andersen’s critique successfully identifies the central weakness in Heidegger’s demand that theology ground its conceptual understanding in a pre-theological ontology. Heidegger’s analysis of Dasein as thrown projection can illuminate human finitude, situated freedom, responsibility, and the experience of existential indebtedness. His concept of Being-guilty reveals that responsibility reaches deeper than the violation of external rules. His insistence that theology requires conceptual clarification is also salutary. Faith does not excuse theology from the obligations of thought.

Yet Heidegger’s methodological atheism becomes inadequate when it is treated as the foundation of theology rather than as a limit upon philosophy. An ontology that excludes faith cannot fully explain guilt before God because theological guilt is not merely existential indebtedness; it is a broken relation to the Creator. Nor can it account for the creative existence of the Word, because the Word is not simply the medium through which Dasein discloses beings. It is the divine source through which beings come to be and by which human beings are addressed. Thrownness is not creation, projection is not vocation, Being-guilty is not sin, and disclosure is not revelation.

Theology can therefore achieve conceptual understanding through philosophy, but not by surrendering its foundational relation to faith. Philosophy can supply critical distinctions, expose hidden assumptions, and clarify formal structures of existence. It cannot determine the ultimate meaning of theological phenomena while remaining explicitly non-faithful. Theology must use philosophy without allowing philosophy to dictate the limits of revelation. It must remain open to ontological analysis while also allowing revelation to transform the meaning of ontology.

The proper relationship is consequently reciprocal and asymmetrical in a new way. Philosophy retains its independence as a discipline that need not begin in faith. Theology retains its integrity as reflection that begins and ends in faith. But theology may interrogate and redirect philosophy when philosophical categories prove incapable of containing the realities of creation, sin, grace, Word, and reconciliation. Faith does not abolish thought; it gives thought a new object, a new orientation, and a new account of existence. Theology remains theology not when it refuses philosophy, but when it refuses to let a faithless ontology become the final measure of what can be thought.
