**The Fractured Field: Identity, Hiatus, and the Trilemma of Hasker’s Emergent Dualism**

The philosophy of mind has long grappled with the elusive nature of consciousness, and among the most sophisticated attempts to reconcile the reality of subjective experience with the physical world is William Hasker’s emergent dualism. In Hasker’s framework, consciousness is not reducible to neural activity, yet it arises naturally from the complex physical processes of the brain. The mind, conceived as an emergent field, possesses its own causal powers, interacting with and exerting top-down influence upon the physical substrate that generates it. To illustrate this relationship, Hasker frequently invokes the magnet analogy: just as a magnetic field emerges from the ordered motion of electrons within a magnet, the field of consciousness emerges from the electrochemical activities of the brain. The field is neither reducible to the magnet nor independent of it; it is a distinct ontological entity generated by and dependent upon its physical base.

However, this elegant analogy encounters severe difficulties when confronted with phenomena involving hiatuses in consciousness—periods of complete cessation such as deep coma, cardiac arrest, resuscitation, or cryonic suspension. If the field of consciousness is the criterion for personal identity, what happens to the self when the field dissipates? Does the field survive the cessation of its generating base, and if so, how? If it does not survive, how can the same person resume consciousness after a period of unconsciousness? This essay evaluates the coherence of Hasker’s emergent dualism when faced with hiatuses in consciousness, arguing that the model generates an irresolvable trilemma. Hasker must either abandon the field of consciousness as the criterion for personal identity in favor of physical brain continuity, accept that numerical identity is lost across any hiatus in consciousness, or invoke divine intervention to preserve identity at the cost of collapsing his theory into a Cartesian-style interaction problem.

**The Magnet Analogy and the Mechanics of the Emergent Field**

To understand the crisis of identity that arises during hiatuses, one must first grasp the mechanics of Hasker’s magnet analogy. In a standard magnet, the physical base—the ordered alignment of electron spins—generates a magnetic field. The field is not merely a descriptive tool; it has causal efficacy. It can exert force on other magnetic materials, induce currents, and possess energy. Yet, the field is entirely dependent on the physical base. If the magnet is destroyed or its temperature exceeds the Curie point, disrupting the electron alignment, the magnetic field ceases to exist. The field does not float away or persist in a diminished state; it vanishes entirely, only to reappear if and when the physical base is restored to its ordered state.

Hasker applies this model to the mind-brain relationship. The brain is the physical base; the emergent field of consciousness is the mental field. The field is generated by the brain’s neural activity and, in turn, exerts downward causation, influencing the physical processes that generated it. Crucially, Hasker argues that this emergent field serves as the criterion for personal identity. It is the field—the unified center of consciousness, the locus of thoughts, intentions, and experiences—that constitutes the person, not the physical brain itself. The brain is merely the generating mechanism, the hardware upon which the software of the self runs.

This model relies heavily on the interplay between determinism and indeterminism. The physical base (the brain) operates under the laws of physics, which are largely deterministic at the macro level and indeterministic at the quantum level. The emergent field, however, is not strictly determined by the physical base. Because it possesses downward causation, the field can influence the physical base in ways that are not strictly deducible from the physical state alone. This allows for genuine mental agency and libertarian free will within a naturalistic framework. The mind is a real, causally efficacious entity, yet it remains ontologically dependent on its physical generator.

**The Crisis of Hiatus: The Dissipation of the Field**

The magnet analogy works seamlessly when consciousness is continuous. The magnet generates a field; the field persists as long as the magnet is active. But what happens during a hiatus in consciousness? Consider a patient in a deep coma, their brain activity drastically reduced or altered, or an individual undergoing cryonic suspension, where the brain is cooled to near absolute zero and metabolic processes cease entirely. In both cases, the physical base is either severely degraded or entirely inactive. 

According to the strict mechanics of the magnet analogy, when the generating base is deactivated, the field it produces must cease to exist. The magnetic field does not linger in a state of suspension when the magnet loses its magnetization; it dissipates into the background noise of the universe. Similarly, if the emergent field of consciousness is strictly generated by the active electrochemical processes of the brain, it too must dissipate during a period of unconsciousness. The field does not go dormant; it ceases to be.

This presents a profound problem for Hasker’s criterion of personal identity. If the field of consciousness is the person, and the field ceases to exist during a coma, then the person ceases to exist during the coma. When the individual is resuscitated or thawed from cryonic suspension, and the brain resumes activity, a new field of consciousness is generated. But is this new field numerically identical to the field that existed before the hiatus? 

The tension here is acute. If the field is identical only insofar as it is the same continuous field, then a hiatus in consciousness constitutes a death of the self. The post-hiatus consciousness would be a continuation of the psychological stream, but it would not be numerically identical to the pre-hiatus person. It would be a new emergent field, instantiated by the same physical base, but lacking the numerical identity of the original. This conclusion is deeply counterintuitive to our ordinary conception of personal identity. We do not believe that a person who wakes from a coma is a different numerical entity than the one who fell asleep; we believe it is the same person who has temporarily lost consciousness. Yet Hasker’s emergent dualism, wedded to the magnet analogy, struggles to account for this intuition without modifying its core commitments.

**The Trilemma: Abandoning the Field, Accepting Identity Loss, or Invoking the Divine**

Faced with the inability of the magnet analogy to bridge the gap of hiatus, Hasker’s emergent dualism is forced into a trilemma. To maintain coherence, he must choose one of three paths, each of which entails severe philosophical costs.

**Horn One: Abandoning the Field Criterion in Favor of Physical Brain Continuity**

The first option is to abandon the emergent field as the criterion for personal identity and instead adopt physical brain continuity as the basis for identity. Under this approach, the person is identical to the living human organism, or more specifically, to the continuous physical brain. During a coma or cryonic suspension, the person persists because the brain persists, even if its functional activity is suppressed or the field of consciousness has dissipated. When the brain is reactivated, the same person awakens because the physical base is the same physical base.

However, this option effectively collapses Hasker’s emergent dualism into a form of physicalism or property dualism that is deeply unsatisfying to its own metaphysical commitments. If physical brain continuity is the criterion of identity, then the emergent field is reduced to a mere epiphenomenon or an accidental byproduct of brain activity. The field no longer constitutes the person; it is merely what the person experiences. This undermines the very reason Hasker posits the emergent field: to provide a robust, causally efficacious mental substance that grounds personal identity and free will. By retreating to physical continuity, Hasker concedes the fundamental dualist intuition that the mental is ontologically primary for identity. He is left with a theory where consciousness is real and irreducible, but it is not the self. The magnet analogy becomes merely a causal story about how the brain produces experiences, not a story about what the person *is*.

**Horn Two: Accepting That Numerical Identity Is Lost Across Hiatuses**

The second option is to accept the consequences of the magnet analogy strictly: numerical identity is lost across any hiatus in consciousness. On this view, the person who enters the coma dies, and a numerically distinct emergent field is generated when the brain is resuscitated. The post-coma individual is a new entity, qualitatively identical to the pre-coma individual, sharing all memories, personality traits, and psychological continuities, but lacking numerical identity with the original.

This view, while logically consistent with the magnet analogy, is philosophically radical and practically untenable. It implies that every period of dreamless sleep, every faint, or every general anesthesia constitutes a micro-death of the self. More severely, it suggests that the individual who is resuscitated after clinical death is a replica, not the original person. This creates insurmountable problems for ethics and law. If a person in a persistent vegetative state is a different numerical entity than the one who entered it, then the continuity of moral responsibility and legal accountability is shattered. Furthermore, for Hasker, who is a Christian philosopher, this view creates theological absurdities. If numerical identity is lost at death, then the resurrection of the body cannot be the resurrection of the same person; it would be the creation of a new emergent field in a new physical base, which contradicts the core Christian doctrine of the resurrection of the dead.

**Horn Three: Invoking Divine Intervention and Collapsing into Cartesianism**

The third option is to invoke divine intervention to preserve the field of consciousness across hiatuses. In this scenario, God sustains the emergent field during periods when the physical base is inactive, or God miraculously reconstitutes the exact same field upon the reactivation of the brain. This would allow Hasker to maintain that the field is the criterion of personal identity while explaining how the same field survives the cessation of its physical generator.

However, this option is deeply problematic because it collapses Hasker’s emergent dualism into a form of Cartesian substance dualism, or more precisely, occasionalism. In Cartesian dualism, the immaterial soul is a distinct substance that can exist independently of the body. If Hasker posits that God sustains the emergent field independently of the brain, the field effectively becomes an immaterial soul. The brain ceases to be the *generator* of the field and becomes merely the *occasion* for God to instantiate the field in the physical world.

This move destroys the naturalistic framework that Hasker’s emergent dualism seeks to maintain. Hasker’s model is designed to avoid the interaction problem of Cartesian dualism by making the mind a product of the brain, ensuring that mental causation is ultimately grounded in physical causation. By invoking divine intervention to bridge the gap of hiatus, Hasker reintroduces the very interaction problem he sought to escape. How does the divinely sustained field interact with the physical brain when it is reactivated? If the field is sustained by God and not generated by the brain, then the downward causation of the mind upon the brain is no longer a natural, emergent process but a supernatural one. The brain becomes a passive receiver or a vessel for a field that is sustained by divine fiat, undermining the autonomy of the physical world and the naturalistic closure of physics that Hasker’s model relies upon.

**Determinism, Indeterminism, and the Gap of Re-emergence**

The tension between determinism and indeterminism further illuminates the incoherence of preserving numerical identity across a hiatus. In Hasker’s model, the physical base operates under deterministic and indeterministic physical laws. When the brain is reactivated after a coma, the physical processes resume. If the re-emergence of consciousness is deterministic—meaning the exact same physical state necessarily produces the exact same mental field—then the post-hiatus field is a deterministic repetition of the pre-hiatus field. But determinism only guarantees the same *state*, not the same *entity*. A deterministic replay of a movie is not the same movie being watched continuously; it is a new instance of the same sequence. The numerical identity of the field requires a continuous causal chain, which is broken by the cessation of the generating base.

If, on the other hand, the re-emergence involves indeterminism—as quantum indeterminacy in neural processes might suggest—then the post-hiatus field is not even deterministically guaranteed to be qualitatively identical, let alone numerically identical. Indeterminism introduces genuine novelty and randomness. The field that emerges after cryonic suspension, even if structurally similar, would be a product of novel physical fluctuations, making its identity as the *same* field entirely arbitrary. The magnet analogy fails here because a magnetic field, once dissipated, does not retain a latent identity waiting to be rekindled; it is replaced by a new field with the same properties. Hasker’s emergent dualism lacks a metaphysical mechanism to anchor the numerical identity of the field across a void of non-existence.

**Conclusion**

William Hasker’s emergent dualism offers a compelling account of the mind-brain relationship under conditions of continuous consciousness. The magnet analogy elegantly captures the interplay between a generating physical base and an emergent, causally efficacious field. However, when confronted with the phenomena of hiatuses in consciousness—resuscitation, coma, and cryonic suspension—the coherence of this model fractures. The magnet analogy dictates that when the generating base ceases, the field it produces must also cease. If the field is the criterion of personal identity, as Hasker claims, then a hiatus in consciousness entails a hiatus in personal identity.

This realization traps Hasker in an inescapable trilemma. He must either abandon the field as the criterion of identity, retreating to physical brain continuity and thereby undermining the dualist metaphysics that motivate emergent dualism; or he must accept that numerical identity is lost across any cessation of consciousness, a conclusion that is counterintuitive, ethically devastating, and theologically incompatible with doctrines of resurrection; or he must invoke divine intervention to preserve the field across the gap, a move that collapses emergent dualism into Cartesian substance dualism and occasionalism, destroying the naturalistic framework that gives the model its philosophical appeal. 

The trilemma reveals a fundamental fragility in Hasker’s attempt to construct a naturalistic dualism. By making the emergent field ontologically primary for personal identity, Hasker creates a dependency that cannot survive the natural cessation of the physical base. The emergent field, like the magnetic field, is inextricably bound to its generator. When the generator stops, the field dies, and with it, the numerical identity of the person. Hasker’s emergent dualism, while a powerful tool for explaining the reality of consciousness in the living brain, ultimately fails to provide a coherent account of the self across the boundaries of unconsciousness.
