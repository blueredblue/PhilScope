The ontological argument, in its classical formulation by St. Anselm of Canterbury, has long been a focal point of philosophical debate regarding the nature of existence, necessity, and the limits of conceptual analysis. Anselm’s argument posits that God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived," and that if such a being existed only in the mind, a greater being could be conceived—one that exists in reality. Therefore, God must exist necessarily in reality. However, from the outset, the argument has been plagued by parody objections, most famously Gaunilo’s perfect island and, in the modern era, Michael Martin’s Absolute Evil One. These parodies suggest that the logical form of Anselm’s argument is so broad that it generates mutually exclusive entities or unwanted ontological commitments, thereby undermining its validity.

Charles Hartshorne, a prominent figure in process theology and modal metaphysics, sought to rescue the ontological argument by reformulating it within the framework of modal logic. By shifting the ontological ground from contingent existence to necessary existence, Hartshorne claimed to insulate the argument from the structural vulnerabilities that plague Anselm’s original formulation. Hartshorne deploys modal axioms alongside what is often termed Becker’s postulate—specifically, the principle that existence is a perfection and that a perfect being must possess it necessarily—to construct a "logic of perfection." The central question, however, remains: does moving from contingent existence to necessary existence genuinely insulate the ontological argument from the generation of mutually exclusive entities or unwanted ontological commitments? Or does Hartshorne’s modal framework remain equally vulnerable to structural parity, thereby failing to successfully establish the incoherence of parodic counterparts?

To evaluate Hartshorne’s claim, one must first understand the structural threat posed by parody objections. Anselm’s original argument relies on the conceptual leap from existence in the intellect to existence in reality. Gaunilo’s perfect island objection exploits this leap by arguing that if the logic of "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" is valid, one could similarly conceive of a perfect island—the most excellent island—and logically conclude that it must exist in reality. The parody works because Anselm’s argument treats existence as a property that can be added to any concept to increase its greatness. The logical form is content-neutral, meaning it can be applied to islands, unicorns, or any other concept, thereby generating mutually exclusive entities (a perfect island and a perfect unicorn) and unwanted ontological commitments.

Michael Martin’s formulation of the Absolute Evil One extends this parody into the moral domain. Martin argues that if necessary existence is a great-making property, then a being that embodies maximal evil and necessary existence would be the Absolute Evil One. If the ontological argument proves the existence of a necessary, maximally great being, Martin contends, it equally proves the existence of a necessary, maximally evil being, provided one accepts a symmetrical definition of "greatness" that maximizes evil. This parody threatens the ontological argument with the generation of mutually exclusive entities: a maximally perfect, necessary God and a maximally evil, necessary Absolute Evil One. If both are necessary, they cannot coexist, yet the modal framework would logically generate both, resulting in an absurd ontological commitment.

Hartshorne’s modal reformulation attempts to resolve these parodies by fundamentally altering the ontological substrate of the argument. In Anselm’s original formulation, existence is treated as a contingent property that a being might or might not possess. Hartshorne, however, shifts the argument to necessary existence. In Hartshorne’s system, God is not merely a being that happens to exist; God is a necessary being, a being whose non-existence is impossible. This shift is operationalized through the deployment of modal axioms, specifically those of S5 modal logic, where necessity and possibility are tightly interwoven. In S5, if it is possible that a maximally great being exists (◇∃x Gx), then it exists in all possible worlds (□∃x Gx). Thus, the ontological argument moves from proving that God exists in reality to proving that God exists necessarily.

This move from contingent to necessary existence is central to Hartshorne’s defense against parody objections. Hartshorne deploys modal axioms alongside Becker’s postulate to construct what he calls the logic of perfection. Becker’s postulate, in this context, asserts that existence is a positive, great-making property, and that for a being to be maximal, it must possess existence necessarily. In Hartshorne’s logic of perfection, greatness is defined as the maximal possession of positive, great-making properties. Because necessary existence is the supreme great-making property—since a contingent being is limited by its dependence on other factors—a maximally great being must necessarily exist. By embedding necessary existence into the very definition of maximal greatness, Hartshorne attempts to filter out parodic concepts that do not meet this criterion.

When applied to Gaunilo’s perfect island, Hartshorne’s modal framework successfully establishes the incoherence of the parody. The perfect island, by its very nature, is a contingent, finite object. Its concept does not include necessary existence as a constituent of its greatness. An island cannot be maximal if it is contingent; a perfect island that might have failed to exist is not as great as a perfect island that must exist. Therefore, the modal axioms do not apply to the perfect island in the same way they apply to God. The perfect island fails to instantiate maximal greatness because it lacks the property of necessary existence. Hartshorne’s framework successfully insulates the ontological argument from Gaunilo’s parody by demonstrating that the parody concept is structurally incoherent—it attempts to apply the logic of maximal greatness to a concept that cannot sustain it.
