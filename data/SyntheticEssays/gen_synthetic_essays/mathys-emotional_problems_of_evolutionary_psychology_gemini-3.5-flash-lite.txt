# The Architecture of Interiority: Evolutionary Psychology, Expressivism, and the Transformation of the Emotional Object

## Introduction

At the heart of contemporary evolutionary psychology and socio-biology lies a methodological imperative: the reduction of human experiential life to the algorithmic calculus of inclusive fitness. Operating on the neo-Darwinian synthesis—or what its critics term "ultra-Darwinism"—this framework treats human emotions not as self-authenticating qualitative states of an experiencing subject, but as design-features, ancestral heuristics, and neuro-hormonal signaling mechanisms selected for their efficacy in maximizing genetic replication across geological time. In this optic, anger is a strategic bargaining stance designed to calibrate resource distribution; romantic love is a commitment device engineered to secure biparental investment; grief is a measure of mammalian attachment disruption that optimizes future reproductive expenditure.

Yet, this explanatory matrix faces a profound philosophical crisis that strikes at the very root of its scientific validity. Does the ultra-Darwinian translation of human emotion merely *explain* its target phenomenon, or does it *annihilate* and *transform* it? By rendering the qualitative interiority of emotional life in the designative, instrumentalist register of gene selection, ultra-Darwinian frameworks do not simply decode a pre-existing reality; they substitute it with a functionalist artifact. 

To evaluate this transformation, we must stage a dialectical confrontation between the designative-instrumentalist philosophy of language implicit in socio-biology and the expressivist framework developed by Charles Taylor. Taylor argues that human emotions are not raw internal sensations arbitrarily linked to external stimuli, but are constitutively bound to *subject-referring import-ascriptions*—articulations of what matters significantly to an agent within a horizon of shared meaning. Crucially, Taylor contends that these imports require language not merely as a transparent medium of designation, but as a constitutive matrix that brings them into reflective reality. 

This essay will investigate whether translating everyday emotional experience into neo-Darwinian gene language alters the qualitative nature of feelings such as anger or love. It will argue that ultra-Darwinian explanations undermine their own explanatory targets by stripping emotions of their constitutive self-understandings, reducing normative horizons to adaptive strategies, and replacing the subject of experience with the sub-personal vehicle of genetic dissemination. Finally, it will address the dialectical paradox at the core of this enterprise: can an explanatory framework that evacuates the phenomenal and normative reality of the emotion it seeks to elucidate successfully account for human emotional life, or does its reductionism render the phenomenon unintelligible?

## Part I: The Ultra-Darwinian Paradigm and the Designative-Instrumentalist View of Language

To understand the friction between evolutionary psychology and phenomenological accounts of emotion, one must first map the epistemological architecture of the ultra-Darwinian paradigm. Rooted in George Williams’s *Adaptation and Natural Selection* (1966), Richard Dawkins’s *The Selfish Gene* (1976), and expanded by evolutionary psychologists such as Leda Cosmides and John Tooby, ultra-Darwinism asserts that natural selection operates primarily at the level of the gene. Organisms, including human beings, are conceptualized as "survival machines" or phenotypic vehicles constructed by genes to facilitate their own replication.

Within this framework, human psychological capacities are analyzed as a collection of domain-specific cognitive and affective modules shaped during the Pleistocene epoch—the Environment of Evolutionary Adaptedness (EEA). Emotions are defined as complex functional programs that coordinate physiological, cognitive, behavioral, and motivational outputs to solve recurrent adaptive problems. Cosmides and Tooby famously characterize emotions as "supra-ordinate computational programs" that orchestrate other psychological mechanisms (such as perception, attention, and memory) to maximize reproductive success under ancestral conditions.

This scientific project is underpinned by a distinct philosophy of language: the *designative-instrumentalist* view. As Taylor articulates in *Human Agency and Language* (1985), the designative tradition—descending from seventeenth-century empiricism (Locke, Hobbes) and finding contemporary expression in formal semantics and functionalist cognitive science—treats language as a collection of instruments or designators used to map, classify, and manipulate a pre-existing reality. In this view, words are labels affixed to discrete mental or physical items. A term like "anger" designates a specific neuro-chemical state, behavioral disposition, or computational subroutine that exists independently of the linguistic act of naming it.

The designative-instrumentalist approach assumes a clean bifurcation between *fact* and *value*, or between *mechanism* and *meaning*. In the socio-biological lexicon, an emotion is a mechanistic fact; its "meaning" is nothing more than its functional utility in reproductive calculus. Language functions here as a neutral medium of tracking causal relations. When an evolutionary psychologist writes that "jealousy is a sex-specific adaptation designed to mitigate paternity uncertainty in males and loss of resource investment in females," the language deployed is strictly instrumental. It aims to bypass the messy, self-interpreting disclosures of the jealous subject and home in on the underlying evolutionary circuitry.

However, this instrumentalization of language entails a specific ontological reduction. By treating emotional terms as designators of sub-personal, adaptationist mechanisms, ultra-Darwinism implicitly adopts a mechanical ontology. The emotional episode is atomized into inputs (environmental triggers), internal processing (modular computation), and outputs (fight, flight, mate-selection). The qualitative feel of the emotion—what philosophers of mind call *qualia*—along with its normative claims upon the agent, are relegated to epiphenomena: delightful or distressing cognitive illusions thrown off by the machinery of natural selection to ensure we do its bidding. 

As we shall see, this semantic strategy creates an immediate tension with the lived reality of human emotional experience, where emotions are experienced not as levers pulled by ancestral genes, but as disclosures of value that demand articulation, justification, and moral accountability.

## Part II: Taylor’s Expressivist Framework and Subject-Referring Import-Ascriptions

To grasp what is lost in the ultra-Darwinian translation, we must turn to Charles Taylor’s expressivist alternative, heavily indebted to Herder, Humboldt, Merleau-Ponty, and Heidegger. In his seminal essays "Theories of Meaning" and "Self-Interpreting Animals," Taylor argues against the designative-instrumentalist model of language and the atomistic psychology that accompanies it. 

Taylor posits that human emotions are *subject-referring import-ascriptions*. An "import" is a property of a situation that makes sense only in relation to a subject for whom things matter. Unlike physical properties (such as mass or velocity), which can be designated by purely observer-independent, third-person descriptions, emotional imports are irreducibly dependent on the agent's horizon of significance. For example, to feel *indignation* is not merely to experience a spike in autonomic arousal coupled with a behavioral disposition to retaliate; it is to perceive a situation as *unjust*. The injustice is an import: it is a feature of the world that exists only for a creature capable of moral reflection, social norms, and mutual recognition.

Crucially, Taylor insists that emotions are *self-interpreting*. Human beings do not first have raw, non-linguistic feelings (like internal itches or bodily pains) and then subsequently apply labels to them. Rather, our emotions are partially constituted by our interpretations of them. To feel shame is to interpret oneself as falling short of a standard of worth; to alter that interpretation—to realize, say, that the standard was tyrannical or irrelevant—is often to alter the emotion itself. The emotion is not a fixed mechanical subroutine waiting to be decoded by an evolutionary psychologist; it is an ongoing, fluid articulation of our stance toward the good.

This brings us to Taylor’s constitutive theory of language. Against the designative view, Taylor argues that language is the medium through which these imports are first brought to articulation and clarity. Language is not merely a tool for labeling pre-existing mental objects; it is the constitutive matrix that allows those objects to exist in a distinctively human way. Before we articulate our feelings through linguistic and cultural forms, they are often inchoate, murky, and indeterminate. By naming our grief, our longing, or our resentment, we do not merely report on an internal state; we *shape* it, give it definition, and place it within a normative space of reasons.

Taylor’s framework reveals that human emotions are inextricably bound up with *strong evaluations*. Unlike weak evaluations, which merely calculate instrumental preferences (preferring chocolate over vanilla), strong evaluations involve discriminations of qualitative worth—distinctions between noble and base, authentic and degraded, fulfilling and empty. Love, anger, guilt, and pride are saturated with strong evaluations. They are ways we matter to ourselves and to others within a shared cultural world. 

When ultra-Darwinian psychology steps into this domain, it attempts to bypass the agent's self-interpretation and strong evaluations, translating the rich, normative vocabulary of import-ascription into the amoral, non-teleological (in the intentional sense) language of natural selection. It is precisely this translation that we must now interrogate.

## Part III: The Translation of Experience into Gene Language: Anger and Love

To evaluate whether translating everyday emotional experience into neo-Darwinian gene language alters the qualitative nature of feelings, we must examine specific emotional phenomena. Let us take two foundational human emotions: *anger* and *romantic love*.

### The Case of Anger

In everyday phenomenology, anger is experienced as a passionate response to a perceived transgression, insult, or injustice. It carries a normative claim: *you have crossed a boundary, you have wronged me, and this wrong demands redress.* Anger is felt in the chest, the clenched jaw, and the focused intensity of attention, but its semantic core is moral and relational. It implicates a shared world of mutual expectations, rights, and recognition.

In the ultra-Darwinian framework, however, anger undergoes a radical ontological metamorphosis. According to evolutionary psychologists like Aaron Sell, John Tooby, and Leda Cosmides, anger is conceptualized as the "Calibrated Dispute-Resolution (CDR) system." The function of anger is understood as a bargaining tactic designed to navigate conflicts of interest in ancestral social groups. Natural selection, we are told, fashioned anger to inflict or threaten costs on others when they fail to value the actor's welfare sufficiently, thereby coercing them into recalibrating their internal welfare trade-off ratios in the actor's favor.

Let us trace what happens to anger in this translation:
1. **From Normative Claim to Evolutionary Bargaining:** The phenomenological experience of injustice ("You have wronged me") is translated into a sub-personal fitness calculation ("Your valuation of my reproductive interests is sub-optimal; increasing aggressive posture will extract resource concessions").
2. **From Subject to Vehicle:** The angry subject—a person standing up for their dignity—is dissolved into an algorithmic vehicle executing a genetically encoded game-theoretic strategy (Hawk-Dove dynamics).
3. **From Meaning to Mechanism:** The moral gravity of anger, its intelligibility within a space of reasons and mutual accountability, is evacuated. Anger is no longer a response to a moral failure, but a biological mechanism for behavioral coercion.

### The Case of Love

Consider next romantic love. Phenomenologically, love is experienced as a transcendent opening toward another person, an experience of profound attunement, vulnerability, and valuation of the other *for their own sake*. It involves a sense of irreplaceability ("There is no one else in the world like you") and an unconditional commitment that defies purely instrumental calculation.

In the socio-biological and evolutionary psychological lexicon, romantic love is analyzed through the lenses of parental investment theory, mate-selection heuristics, and commitment devices. As formulated by researchers like David Buss and Robert Frank, romantic love is a psychological adaptation designed to solve the adaptive problems of securing reliable biparental investment, defending offspring against infanticide, and signaling long-term reliability in mating markets. The passionate feeling of being "in love" is a neuro-hormonal cocktail (dominated by oxytocin, vasopressin, dopamine, and endorphins) selected to bind reproductive partners together long enough to rear altricial human infants. Furthermore, the feeling of irreplaceability is viewed as an evolutionary trick—a cognitive bias that prevents defection when a temporarily more fertile or resourced mate appears on the horizon.

Here, the transformation is even more vertiginous:
1. **From Altruistic Valuation to Selfish Replication:** The qualitative experience of valuing the other *for their own sake* is reframed as a sophisticated vehicle for gene dissemination. The lover’s gaze is unmasked as an optical illusion generated by selfish genes manipulating their host to secure cooperative child-rearing.
2. **From Irreplaceability to Interchangeability:** The phenomenological sense of absolute uniqueness ("You are irreplaceable") is contradicted by the ultra-Darwinian premise that mate choice is governed by standardized algorithmic parameters (e.g., female preference for status and resources; male preference for youth and physical symmetry as markers of reproductive value). Underneath the patina of romance lies an economic market of biological commodities.
3. **From Transcendent Bond to Chemical Shackle:** The passionate devotion of love is reduced to an adaptive neuro-chemical binding agent, no more intrinsically noble than the glue that holds a termite mound together.

Does this translation alter the qualitative nature of these feelings? To the ultra-Darwinian, the translation does not *alter* the emotion; it *reveals* its true, hidden essence. The phenomenological experience is dismissed as a functional "user illusion" (to borrow a term from computer science)—a subjective interface that masks the underlying machine code from the user, because conscious awareness of gene-maximization would be evolutionarily maladaptive.

However, from Taylor’s expressivist standpoint, this ultra-Darwinian maneuver is not a revelation of truth, but a categorical error. By substituting the functional description for the phenomenological reality, ultra-Darwinism commits what Alfred North Whitehead called the "Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness." It takes an abstract, theoretical model (gene propagation) and treats it as more concrete and real than the lived reality of the experiencing subject. 

More fundamentally, the translation alters the emotion because *emotions are partly constituted by their self-understandings*. If an agent truly came to internalize the ultra-Darwinian account—if every time they felt a surge of romantic devotion, they experienced it strictly as a neuro-chemical commitment device engineered by selfish genes to ensure infant survival—the phenomenology of love would disintegrate. The open-ended, transcendent vulnerability of love cannot survive the meta-belief that one is merely being manipulated by a genetic algorithm. The ultra-Darwinian explanation, if fully digested by the subject, would erode the very object it seeks to explain, transforming love from a shared existential project into a biological entrapment.

## Part IV: The Dialectical Problem of Explanatory Annihilation

This brings us to the core dialectical problem: **Can an explanatory framework that annihilates its original target phenomenon successfully account for human emotional life?**

To pose this question is to enter the domain of meta-critique. Science frequently proceeds by reduction: thermodynamics reduces to statistical mechanics; biology reduces to chemistry; mental states allegedly reduce to neurobiology. In many domains of inquiry, reduction does not destroy the phenomenon; it explains *how* it works. Water is experienced as a wet, flowing, quenching liquid; science reveals it to be $H_2O$ molecules bound by hydrogen bonds. The scientific explanation does not annihilate water; it explains its macro-properties via micro-structures.

Why, then, does the ultra-Darwinian reduction of emotion feel qualitatively different—more like an annihilation than an explanation? 

The reason lies in the unique nature of *intentionality* and *normativity* constitutive of mental and emotional life. Water does not have a point of view; water does not interpret itself; water does not exist within a horizon of strong evaluation and normative significance. Physical science can successfully explain water precisely because water's macroscopic properties are fully exhausted by physical interactions.

Emotions, by contrast, are inherently intentional and self-interpreting. They are ways of *making sense* of the world. As Taylor argues, human agency is constitutively dialogical and normative; we are self-defining creatures who live in a space of questions about what is good, worthy, admirable, and contemptible. An explanation of emotion that strips away intentionality, normative import, and subjective self-interpretation does not reduce the phenomenon; it changes the subject. It replaces the *human subject living in a world of meaning* with an *organism responding to adaptive pressures in an environment*.

When socio-biology and evolutionary psychology claim to explain human emotion, they engage in a strategic substitution. They take the rich, messy, norm-saturated phenomenological field of emotional life, declare it to be a surface epiphenomenon, and substitute in its place a functional-algorithmic model. 

This generates a profound internal contradiction within the ultra-Darwinian research program itself: *the methodology used to explain the phenomenon presupposes the validity of the very realm of meaning it seeks to explain away.* Consider the very practice of evolutionary psychology. Evolutionary psychologists write books, engage in peer review, construct arguments, appeal to evidence, evaluate truth claims, and debate ethical implications. All of these activities—arguing, reasoning, evaluating, seeking truth—presuppose the space of reasons, normative assessment, and linguistic articulation that Taylor defends. 

If all human thought, emotion, and behavior are ultimately nothing more than genetic replication strategies designed to maximize inclusive fitness, then evolutionary psychology itself is not an objective pursuit of truth; it is merely another phenotypic behavior—a clever meme-spreading or gene-maximizing tactic deployed by the researchers' brains. By reducing all mental life to sub-personal adaptationist mechanisms, ultra-Darwinism saws off the branch upon which its own scientific rationality sits. If reasons are merely causes disguised as justifications, then the arguments put forward by evolutionary psychologists carry no epistemic weight; they are just biological noises emitted by primate vocal cords.

Thus, the dialectical problem reveals a fatal limit to ultra-Darwinian reductionism. An explanatory framework that annihilates its target phenomenon—by rendering the subject a mechanical vehicle, the import an adaptive illusion, and the meaning a gene-maximizing ruse—destructively collapses the very horizon of intelligibility required for explanation to take place at all.

## Part V: Reconciling Nature and Meaning: Toward a Non-Reductive Synthesis

Does recognizing the limitations of ultra-Darwinian reductionism require us to retreat into a dualistic anti-naturalism that severs human emotion from our evolutionary history? Not necessarily. The dialectical tension between evolutionary biology and expressivist phenomenology points toward the need for a non-reductive, stratified ontology of human emotional life—one that bridges the chasm between *mechanism* and *meaning* without reducing one to the other.

To achieve this reconciliation, we can draw upon the philosophical frameworks of thinkers like Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Paul Ricoeur, and contemporary non-reductive naturalists who reject ultra-Darwinian imperialism while acknowledging biological embodiment.

First, we must recognize that human beings are simultaneously *organisms* subject to biological evolution and *self-interpreting agents* inhabiting a cultural space of reasons. These are not two competing metaphysical substances (Cartesian dualism), but two irreducible dimensions of a single, unified mode of existence. As Merleau-Ponty demonstrated in *The Phenomenology of Perception*, our biological body (*corps propre*) is the very vehicle of our engagement with the world. Our physiological drives, neuro-hormonal surges, and evolutionary heritage do not stand *outside* our meaningful emotional life as cold mechanisms pulling strings; rather, they form the visceral, affective *ground* upon which our cultural interpretations and strong evaluations are built.

Evolutionary history provides the structural preconditions—the bodily capacities, the affective potentials, the neuro-chemical baselines—out of which human emotional life is forged. Natural selection gave us the capacity for fear, anger, love, and grief. But in human beings, these biological endowments are radically transfigured by language, history, and culture. 

To use an Aristotelian or phenomenological distinction, evolution provides the *material cause* (the neuro-biological machinery and ancestral predispositions), but culture and self-interpretation provide the *formal and final causes* (the normative structure, the intentional object, and the existential meaning). Anger in a chimpanzee and anger in a constitutional lawyer arguing for civil rights share a common phylogenetic ancestor, but phenomenologically, structurally, and normatively, they are worlds apart. To collapse the latter into the former is to commit a category mistake that blinds us to the emergence of novel ontological levels of reality.

Second, we must embrace what Paul Ricoeur termed "the long detour" of structural and scientific explanation. Psychoanalysis, sociology, and evolutionary psychology perform a vital hermeneutic service. They remind us that we are not fully transparent to ourselves. We are often driven by subterranean motives, biological imperatives, and historical forces of which our conscious ego is blissfully unaware. The ultra-Darwinian insight—that our emotional lives bear the deep structural scars of our evolutionary past—is a profound corrective to naive Cartesian transparency.

However, this scientific explanation must be subordinated to a larger hermeneutic circle. A valid account of human emotion cannot terminate in the sub-personal mechanisms of gene selection; it must return to the lived experience of the subject and integrate those biological insights into a coherent narrative of human agency and moral aspiration. When we understand anger not merely as a "Calibrated Dispute-Resolution system," but as an embodied, culturally shaped response to a perceived injustice, we preserve both our biological rootedness and our moral humanity.

---

## Conclusion

The ultra-Darwinian claim to explain human emotion through the exclusive lens of gene-maximization and adaptive design represents one of the most powerful—and most reductionist—intellectual projects of modernity. Operating on a designative-instrumentalist view of language, evolutionary psychology attempts to decode human emotional life by translating its messy, norm-saturated phenomenology into the cold algorithmic calculus of inclusive fitness.

Yet, as Charles Taylor’s expressivist framework demonstrates, this explanatory translation comes at a devastating cost. By treating emotions as sub-personal mechanisms rather than subject-referring import-ascriptions embedded in a horizon of strong evaluation, ultra-Darwinism performs an ontological substitution. It replaces the human subject—who suffers, loves, judges, and seeks justice—with a genetic vehicle executing ancestral survival strategies. 

In doing so, ultra-Darwinian explanations undermine the very objects they attempt to explain. They evacuate the intentionality, the normative gravity, and the self-interpreting agency that constitute the qualitative essence of human emotions like anger and love. Furthermore, by reducing all mental life to fitness-maximizing computations, the paradigm undermines the epistemic validity of its own scientific claims, collapsing the space of reasons into a theater of biological blind forces.

Ultimately, human emotional life cannot be fully comprehended either by ignoring our deep evolutionary heritage or by reducing it to genetic determinism. Resolving the dialectical crisis requires moving beyond ultra-Darwinian imperialism toward a stratified, non-reductive synthesis. We must honor the biological embodiment that links us to the natural world while fiercely guarding the irreducible dignity of human meaning, linguistic articulation, and moral freedom. Only by recognizing that we are at once biological creatures shaped by millions of years of evolution *and* self-interpreting agents navigating an open horizon of significance can we account for the profound, luminous, and terrifying reality of human emotion.
