Immanuel Kant's Critique of Judgment (1790) introduces the concept of subjective universality in aesthetic judgments, which appears to be an oxymoron at first glance. On one hand, aesthetic pleasure is inherently subjective and person-relative, as individuals have unique preferences and tastes. On the other hand, Kant argues that aesthetic judgments can claim universal validity, implying that they can be shared and agreed upon by all individuals. This tension between personal pleasure and universal validity is the central concern of this essay, as we delve into Kant's resolution of this apparent paradox through the requirements of disinterest and the non-conceptual free play between the faculties of understanding and imagination.

Kant's concept of subjective universality is rooted in his distinction between aesthetic and cognitive judgments. Aesthetic judgments are not concerned with the objective properties of an object, but rather with the subjective experience of pleasure or displeasure it evokes. This pleasure is not based on personal interests or desires, but rather on the free play between the faculties of understanding and imagination. When we encounter an object, our understanding attempts to categorize and conceptualize it, while our imagination seeks to create a mental image of the object. In the case of aesthetic experience, this free play occurs without the constraints of conceptualization, allowing the faculties to engage in a harmonious and dynamic interaction.

The requirement of disinterest is crucial in Kant's account of subjective universality. Disinterest refers to the ability to appreciate an object without being influenced by personal interests, desires, or biases. When we approach an object with disinterest, we are able to focus on its formal properties, such as its shape, color, and composition, rather than its functional or practical uses. This disinterested contemplation enables us to experience the object in a way that is not tainted by personal preferences or agendas, allowing us to access a more universal and shared aspect of human experience.

Kant's notion of disinterest is often misunderstood as a call for emotional detachment or a lack of engagement with the object. However, this is not the case. Disinterest does not imply a lack of passion or enthusiasm, but rather a willingness to set aside personal interests and biases in order to engage with the object on its own terms. In this sense, disinterest is not a negation of subjective experience, but rather a refinement of it, allowing us to access a more nuanced and subtle aspect of human emotion.

The non-conceptual free play between the faculties of understanding and imagination is also essential to Kant's account of subjective universality. When we encounter an object, our understanding attempts to categorize and conceptualize it, while our imagination seeks to create a mental image of the object. In the case of aesthetic experience, this free play occurs without the constraints of conceptualization, allowing the faculties to engage in a harmonious and dynamic interaction. This free play is non-conceptual, meaning that it does not rely on pre-existing concepts or categories, but rather creates a new and unique experience in each instance.

One of the primary objections to Kant's account of subjective universality is that conflicting aesthetic judgments undermine its claims to universal validity. For example, two individuals may have differing evaluations of a Picasso painting, with one person finding it beautiful and the other finding it ugly. This apparent disagreement seems to challenge the idea that aesthetic judgments can claim universal validity. However, Kant would argue that this disagreement is not necessarily a challenge to his account, but rather a reflection of the fact that individuals have not yet achieved the level of disinterest and non-conceptual free play required for aesthetic experience.

Kant would argue that the disagreement between the two individuals is not a reflection of the object itself, but rather a reflection of their own personal biases and interests. The individual who finds the painting beautiful may be responding to its formal properties, such as its composition and color, while the individual who finds it ugly may be responding to its content or message. In this sense, the disagreement is not a challenge to the universal validity of aesthetic judgments, but rather a reflection of the fact that individuals have not yet achieved the level of disinterest and non-conceptual free play required for aesthetic experience.

Defending subjective universality relies on distinguishing between actual psychological disagreement and the normative requirements of disinterested contemplation. While individuals may have differing evaluations of an object, this does not necessarily undermine the idea that aesthetic judgments can claim universal validity. Rather, it highlights the need for individuals to engage in disinterested contemplation, setting aside personal biases and interests in order to access a more universal and shared aspect of human experience.

The broader implication of Kant's account of subjective universality is that aesthetic judgments can theoretically apply to any object. This means that any object, regardless of its functional or practical uses, can be experienced as beautiful or sublime. This challenges the traditional view of aesthetics, which often limited the scope of aesthetic experience to a specific set of objects, such as works of art or natural landscapes. Kant's account, on the other hand, suggests that aesthetic experience is not limited to a specific set of objects, but rather can occur in response to any object that is experienced with disinterest and non-conceptual free play.
