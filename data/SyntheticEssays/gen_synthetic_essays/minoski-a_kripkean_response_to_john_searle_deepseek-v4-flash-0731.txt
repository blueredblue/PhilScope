# The Object, Not the Description: Defending Kripke's Rigid Designation against Searle's Descriptivist Challenge

## Introduction

The philosophy of language has long been preoccupied with a deceptively simple question: how does a proper name like "Aristotle" or "Barack Obama" manage to refer to a specific individual? For over a century, the dominant answer was descriptivism, a view traceable to Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell, which holds that a name's meaning is equivalent to a cluster of descriptions uniquely identifying its bearer. According to this view, "Aristotle" means something like "the pupil of Plato and teacher of Alexander the Great," and the name refers to whoever satisfies these descriptions. In 1970, Saul Kripke delivered a series of lectures—later published as *Naming and Necessity*—that fundamentally challenged this orthodoxy. Kripke proposed a causal-historical theory of reference, arguing that names are rigid designators: they pick out the same object in all possible worlds, not through descriptive content, but through an initial baptism and a chain of communication linking speakers to that original act.

However, a persistent objection, most forcefully articulated by John Searle, contends that Kripke's theory is not as far from descriptivism as it appears. Searle argues that even the initial baptism—the ceremony or act by which a name is first attached to an object—must rely on descriptive content to fix the referent. Furthermore, the causal chain that transmits the name from speaker to speaker cannot function without descriptive content, as each speaker must have some cognitive grip on the referent. If this is correct, Kripke's theory collapses into a sophisticated form of descriptivism, where descriptions play an ineliminable role in determining reference.

This essay will evaluate this claim. I will argue that Searle's critique, while insightful, ultimately fails to establish that Kripke's theory is fundamentally descriptivist. The key distinction lies between *referring through* descriptive content—where the description constitutes the name's meaning—and descriptive content *merely fixing the reference* of a rigid designator—where the description is a contingent tool used to identify an object, but does not become part of the name's semantic content. By examining Searle's arguments concerning baptism and causal chains, the hospital twin thought experiment, and cases of mistaken identity, I will demonstrate that the object itself, rather than descriptive content, is the true driving force behind reference. Ultimately, I will defend Kripke's view of rigid designation, arguing that it provides a more accurate account of how names actually function in language, thought, and modal discourse.

## Section 1: The Landscape of the Debate

To understand the stakes of this debate, we must first articulate the two competing frameworks with precision. Classical descriptivism, as formulated by Frege and Russell, treats proper names as abbreviated definite descriptions. Russell's theory of descriptions holds that "Aristotle" is logically equivalent to a conjunction of predicates: "there exists an x such that x was a pupil of Plato, x taught Alexander, x wrote the *Nicomachean Ethics*, and for any y, if y satisfies these conditions, y is identical to x." The name's meaning is thus exhausted by this description. If the description is false—if no one satisfies it—the name fails to refer. If it is incomplete—if multiple people satisfy it—the name is ambiguous.

Frege added a crucial refinement: the sense of a name (*Sinn*) is the mode of presentation of its referent (*Bedeutung*). The sense is a descriptive content that determines the referent. Two names can have the same referent but different senses (e.g., "the morning star" and "the evening star" both refer to Venus, but present it differently). For Frege, the sense is what we grasp when we understand a name, and it is the sense that fixes the referent.

Kripke's attack on this view was devastating. He argued that names are *rigid designators*: they refer to the same object in every possible world where that object exists. Consider the counterfactual scenario where Aristotle never became a philosopher, but instead became a fisherman. In that world, "Aristotle" still refers to Aristotle—the same individual—even though he lacks the property of being a philosopher. However, the description "the teacher of Alexander the Great" would refer to someone else in that world (perhaps a different person, or no one at all). Descriptions, Kripke argued, are *non-rigid* designators: they pick out different objects in different possible worlds depending on which object satisfies the description. Since "Aristotle" and "the teacher of Alexander" diverge in their modal behavior, they cannot have the same meaning.

Kripke's positive account posits an initial baptism. A name is introduced in a ceremony—literal or figurative—whereby a speaker points to an object and says, "Let's call this 'Aristotle'." This act establishes a causal link between the name and the object. Subsequently, the name is transmitted through a chain of communication. Each speaker acquires the name from a previous speaker, intending to refer to the same object. This chain is causal, not descriptive: speakers need not have any accurate beliefs about the referent; they merely need to be part of the causal network originating in the baptism.

Searle's cluster theory, developed in his 1958 paper "Proper Names," was an early attempt to salvage descriptivism from some of its problems. Searle argued that a name is not associated with a single description, but with a *cluster* of descriptions. The name refers to the object that satisfies a sufficient number—though not necessarily all—of these descriptions. This allows for some flexibility: if one description is false (e.g., "Aristotle taught Alexander" is false in some counterfactual), the name still refers because other descriptions (e.g., "the author of the *Metaphysics*") are true. However, Searle maintained that descriptions are *constitutive* of the name's meaning. The name does not refer independently of these descriptions; rather, it refers *in virtue of* them.

Searle's later critique of Kripke, articulated in *Intentionality* and various essays, focuses on the role of descriptions in the causal-historical chain. He argues that the chain cannot be purely causal because the initial baptism requires a descriptive identification of the object. How does the baptizer know which object to name? They must use some descriptive content, even if it is merely a demonstrative like "this" or "that." Furthermore, when a speaker passes the name along, they must have some intention to refer to the same object, and this intention is typically guided by descriptive beliefs. Searle concludes that the causal chain is parasitic on descriptive content, making Kripke's theory a disguised form of descriptivism.

## Section 2: Searle's Challenge—Baptism and the Necessity of Descriptive Content

Searle's most potent objection targets the initial baptism. Kripke claims that reference is fixed by an act of naming, where the speaker points to an object and assigns a name. But what makes the pointing successful? Consider a scenario where a nurse in a hospital is asked to baptize a newborn baby. She looks at a crib, points to the infant, and says, "I name this child 'John'." What does "this" refer to? It refers to the baby in the crib. But how does the nurse distinguish this baby from any other? She might rely on spatial location ("the baby in the left crib"), visual appearance ("the baby with the red blanket"), or a prior description ("the baby born to Mrs. Smith"). In each case, the nurse uses descriptive content to fix the referent.

Searle argues that this is unavoidable. Pointing alone is insufficient because it is ambiguous. If there are multiple babies in the room, pointing to a crib does not uniquely identify a specific baby unless there is some descriptive content—even if implicit—that distinguishes that baby from others. The demonstrative "this" is itself a kind of description, carrying indexical content that refers to whatever is salient in the context. Without such content, the baptism would be blind, and the name would fail to refer to any determinate object.

Moreover, Searle contends that the causal chain itself is replete with descriptive content. When I tell you, "Aristotle was wise," you understand the name "Aristotle" because you associate it with descriptions like "the famous Greek philosopher" or "the teacher of Alexander." If you had no descriptive content whatsoever, you would not be able to use the name meaningfully. The chain of communication is not a mere causal relay; it is a chain of speakers who each possess a set of beliefs about the referent. These beliefs are transmitted along with the name, and they are what enable the name to function in discourse.

Does this require *unique* descriptive content? Searle's argument suggests that it does. If the nurse baptizes a baby using only the description "the baby in the crib," and there are two babies in the crib, the baptism fails. She must add content—"the baby on the left" or "the baby with the birthmark"—to uniquely identify the referent. Similarly, in the causal chain, if a speaker has only the description "some Greek philosopher," they might fail to refer to Aristotle specifically, as the description could apply to Plato or Socrates. Unique descriptive content seems necessary for successful reference.

This is a powerful objection. Kripke's response, as we shall see, is to concede that descriptions are *epistemically* necessary for the *act* of baptism, but to deny that they are *semantically* constitutive of the name's meaning. The distinction between fixing reference and giving meaning is the crux of the debate.

## Section 3: Fixing Reference versus Meaning

Kripke explicitly addresses the distinction between fixing reference and giving meaning in *Naming and Necessity*. He uses the example of "Neptune." The name "Neptune" was introduced by the astronomer Urbain Le Verrier as a name for "the planet causing perturbations in the orbit of Uranus." This description fixed the reference of "Neptune" to a specific celestial body. However, "Neptune" does not *mean* "the planet causing perturbations in the orbit of Uranus." If astronomers had later discovered that the perturbations were caused by a different planet (or by some other phenomenon), they would not have said, "Neptune does not exist." They would have said, "The description we used to fix the reference of 'Neptune' was false, but 'Neptune' still refers to the planet we were tracking."

This illustrates a crucial distinction. A description can be used as a *tool* to pick out an object, but once the object is identified, the description can be discarded. The name becomes a rigid designator, referring directly to the object in all possible worlds. The description, by contrast, is a non-rigid designator. In a possible world where the perturbations of Uranus were caused by a different planet, the description "the planet causing perturbations in the orbit of Uranus" would refer to that other planet, while "Neptune" would still refer to the actual planet Neptune.

Searle's mistake, Kripke argues, is to conflate the *epistemic* process of identifying a referent with the *metaphysical* nature of reference. The description is a ladder we use to climb to the object; once we reach the object, we can kick the ladder away. The name's meaning is not the description; it is the object itself, as picked out by the causal-historical chain.

To further clarify, consider the modal argument. If "Aristotle" meant "the teacher of Alexander," then the proposition "Aristotle might not have taught Alexander" would be a contradiction. But it is clearly a meaningful, true statement. We can easily imagine a world where Aristotle died young and never taught anyone. In that world, "Aristotle" still refers to Aristotle, but the description "the teacher of Alexander" refers to someone else (or no one). Therefore, the name and the description have different modal profiles, proving they cannot be synonymous.

Searle might respond that his cluster theory avoids this problem. The name "Aristotle" is associated with a cluster of descriptions: "the teacher of Alexander," "the student of Plato," "the author of the *Metaphysics*," "the founder of the Lyceum," and so on. The name refers to whoever satisfies a sufficient number of these descriptions. In a world where Aristotle never taught Alexander, he still satisfies many other descriptions (e.g., "the author of the *Metaphysics*"), so the name still refers to him. The cluster theory allows for flexibility.

However, Kripke's modal argument works against the cluster theory as well. Consider the disjunction of all the descriptions in the cluster. If "Aristotle" meant "the teacher of Alexander OR the student of Plato OR the author of the *Metaphysics* OR...", then it would be necessary that Aristotle satisfies at least one of these descriptions. But it is clearly contingent. We can imagine a world where Aristotle was a completely different person—a fisherman with no philosophical achievements—yet he is still Aristotle. The cluster theory cannot account for this, because it ties the name's reference to a set of properties that could have been different.

The distinction between fixing and meaning is thus decisive. Kripke allows that descriptions are used in the initial baptism to *fix* the reference, but he denies that they become part of the name's *meaning*. The name is a rigid designator, and its reference is determined by the object itself, not by any description.

## Section 4: The Hospital Twin Thought Experiment and Cases of Mistaken Identity

To test the competing theories, we can construct a thought experiment that isolates the role of descriptive content versus the object itself. Consider the following scenario, which I shall call the "hospital twin case."

Two babies are born in a hospital on the same day. Baby A is the biological child of Mr. and Mrs. Smith. Baby B is the biological child of Mr. and Mrs. Jones. Due to a negligent nurse, the babies are accidentally switched. Baby A is placed in the crib labeled "Jones," and Baby B is placed in the crib labeled "Smith." A different nurse, unaware of the mix-up, is tasked with baptizing the baby in the crib labeled "Smith." She looks at the infant, points to Baby B, and says, "I name this child 'John'."

Now, what does "John" refer to? According to Kripke's causal-historical theory, "John" refers to Baby B, because the baptism was performed on Baby B. The nurse pointed at Baby B, and the causal link was established between the name "John" and Baby B. The fact that the crib was labeled "Smith" is irrelevant; the label was a false description. The name "John" is rigidly attached to Baby B.

According to Searle's descriptivism, however, "John" might refer to Baby A. The cluster of descriptions associated with "John" includes "the baby born to the Smiths," "the baby in the crib labeled Smith," "the child of Mr. and Mrs. Smith," and so on. Baby A satisfies these descriptions (biologically and legally, assuming the switch is discovered later), while Baby B does not. Therefore, a descriptivist might argue that "John" refers to Baby A, even though the baptism was performed on Baby B.

This thought experiment reveals a fundamental divergence. Kripke's theory prioritizes the object of the baptism—the actual physical entity that was named—while Searle's theory prioritizes the descriptive content that the speakers associate with the name. Which account is more intuitive? Let us consider how the speakers would use the name.

Suppose the Smiths take Baby B home, believing he is their biological son. They call him "John." They describe him as "our son," "the baby we brought home from the hospital," and so on. Years later, the mix-up is discovered. The Smiths learn that Baby B is not their biological child; their biological child (Baby A) is living with the Joneses. What should they say?

If we follow Kripke, the Smiths would say, "John is not our biological son, but he is still the boy we raised. The name 'John' refers to him." The name continues to refer to Baby B, despite the discovery that the description "our biological son" is false. The object—Baby B—is the referent, and the description was merely a mistaken belief.

If we follow Searle, the Smiths might say, "We were wrong. The person we called 'John' was never really John. The real John is Baby A, living with the Joneses." This seems deeply counterintuitive. The boy they raised, the boy they named, the boy they loved—surely he is John. The fact that he is not biologically related to them does not change his identity. The name was attached to him at baptism, and that attachment is what matters.

This thought experiment demonstrates that the object itself, not descriptive content, drives reference. The descriptive content ("our biological son") turned out to be false, yet the name "John" still referred to Baby B. If descriptivism were true, the name would have switched its referent when the description was discovered to be false. But it does not. The name remains rigidly attached to the object that was originally baptized.
