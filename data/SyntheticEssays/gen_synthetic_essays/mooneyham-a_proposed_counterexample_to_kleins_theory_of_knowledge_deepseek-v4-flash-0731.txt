### The Illusion of the Felicitous Coincidence: A Critique of Klein’s Fourth Condition

The Gettier problem, first articulated by Edmund Gettier in 1963, stands as one of the most devastating and generative challenges in the history of epistemology. By presenting counterexamples where a subject holds a justified true belief (JTB) that nonetheless fails to constitute knowledge, Gettier demonstrated that the classical tripartite analysis is insufficient. The central issue is that of epistemic luck: in Gettier cases, the truth of the belief is not appropriately connected to the subject’s justification; rather, it is a fortunate accident that the belief is true. In response, epistemologists have proposed numerous "fourth conditions" designed to bridge this gap. Among the most sophisticated of these attempts is Peter Klein’s "no-defeater" condition, specifically his refinement involving the concept of an *undefeated defeater*. Klein argues that a subject knows a proposition *p* if and only if (1) *p* is true, (2) S believes *p*, (3) S is justified in believing *p*, and (4) there is no *undefeated defeater* of S’s justification for *p*. A defeater is a true proposition that, if known by S, would undermine S’s justification. Klein’s innovation, however, is the notion that a defeater can itself be defeated by a further true proposition, rendering the original defeater "misleading" and thus preserving knowledge. He calls this the "felicitous-coincidence" principle, arguing that the epistemic situation is felicitous because the initial defeater is neutralized by a further truth, even if the agent is entirely unaware of this chain.

While Klein’s condition is a brilliant formal solution that handles many standard Gettier cases, it ultimately fails to preserve genuine knowledge. The primary reason for this failure is that Klein’s rejection of counterexamples—such as those involving hidden background circumstances like an unexecuted threat to the State Capitol Building or a delayed visit from a sister-in-law—relies on an overly strong, externalist definition of knowledge that either renders ordinary knowledge claims virtually impossible or, conversely, makes knowledge too easy by relocating epistemic luck to a higher level. The existence of unknown defeaters and subsequent defeaters for those defeaters does not rescue the necessity of Klein’s requirement; rather, it exposes a fundamental tension between the objective truth of the epistemic situation and the subjective justification of the believer. Ultimately, the presence of a defeater for a defeater does not preserve genuine knowledge in the absence of agent awareness, because it fails to establish the non-accidental connection between justification and truth that is the very essence of knowledge.

#### The Gettier Problem and the Search for a Fourth Condition

To appreciate Klein’s contribution, one must first understand the precise nature of the problem he addresses. In Gettier’s classic cases, the subject’s justification is defective in a specific way. In the first case, Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get the job and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith thereby infers that "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket." Unbeknownst to Smith, he himself will get the job, and he also has ten coins in his pocket. Smith’s belief is true, and it is justified (by the evidence about Jones), yet intuitively Smith does not *know* that the man who will get the job has ten coins. The truth of his belief is entirely accidental relative to his justification. In the second case, Smith has strong evidence that Jones owns a Ford and infers that "either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona." It turns out that Jones does not own a Ford, but Brown is indeed in Barcelona. Again, Smith’s belief is true and justified, but he does not know it.

The common thread in these cases is that the subject’s justification is based on a false premise, and the truth of the conclusion is secured by a fact entirely disconnected from the justification. The belief is true, but the justification does not *track* the truth. Consequently, the JTB analysis is insufficient because it allows for epistemic luck—specifically, the luck that the false premise happens to lead to a true conclusion.

One natural response to Gettier is to introduce a defeater condition. A defeater is a true proposition that, if known to the subject, would undermine or defeat the justification for the belief. For example, in the first Gettier case, a defeater would be the true proposition "Smith will get the job," which, if known, would undermine Smith’s justification for believing that Jones will get the job. The idea is that a subject knows *p* only if there are no defeaters for the justification of *p*. However, this simple defeater condition is too strong. Consider a case where S has a justified true belief that "there is a sheep in the field" based on seeing a sheep-shaped object. Unbeknownst to S, the object is actually a dog disguised as a sheep, but there is a real sheep hiding behind the dog. The proposition "the object S sees is a dog" is a true defeater—if S knew it, S would no longer be justified in believing there is a sheep. Yet, intuitively, S *does* know there is a sheep in the field, because the belief is true and justified in a non-accidental way (there is a real sheep there). Thus, a simple no-defeater condition would incorrectly rule out this case as knowledge.
