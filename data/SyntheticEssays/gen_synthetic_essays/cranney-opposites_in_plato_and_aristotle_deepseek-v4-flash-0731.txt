## The Persistence of the One: Aristotle’s *Categories* and the Resolution of Change Against Plato’s *Phaedo*

The problem of change is the primordial anxiety of Western metaphysics. How can a thing become what it is not, without ceasing to be what it is? Parmenides declared change an illusion, a logical scandal, for being cannot arise from non-being. Heraclitus, conversely, dissolved being into flux, making identity a fleeting fiction. Plato and Aristotle both inherited this dilemma, and both sought to reconcile the permanence required for knowledge with the evident mutability of the sensible world. Yet their solutions diverge radically. Plato, in the *Phaedo*, posits a realm of eternal, immutable Forms, separate from particulars, which ground predication through the mysterious mechanism of participation. Aristotle, in the *Categories* and the *Physics*, grounds change within the sensible substance itself, employing the immanent mechanisms of potentiality and actuality. When evaluated strictly on the criteria of explanatory cogency—specifically, the ability to account for how a numerically one particular can transition between contrary states, such as growing taller, and the capacity to resolve the problem of an underlying substratum—Aristotle’s ontological framework proves demonstrably superior. By rejecting the separation of Forms and grounding change in the actualization of a potential inherent in a persisting substance, Aristotle not only avoids the metaphysical burdens and inconsistencies that plague Plato’s treatment of opposite properties but provides a logically rigorous and empirically adequate account of predication and change.

### Part I: The Platonic Framework of the *Phaedo* and the Paradox of Opposites

To understand Aristotle’s triumph, one must first grasp the specific difficulties inherent in Plato’s solution. In the *Phaedo*, Plato’s primary concern is the immortality of the soul, but his argument rests upon a broader metaphysical commitment: the theory of Forms. The Forms are eternal, immutable, intelligible essences—absolute Tallness, absolute Justice, absolute Equality. They are the true objects of knowledge, while particulars are merely their fleeting, imperfect copies. The relationship between a particular and its Form is described as *methexis* (participation) or *koinonia* (communion). A thing is beautiful insofar as it participates in the Form of Beauty; a thing is tall insofar as it participates in the Form of Tallness.

This framework immediately encounters the problem of contrary properties. In the *Phaedo* (102-103), Socrates presents the famous example of Simmias. Simmias is both taller than Socrates and shorter than Phaedo. Thus, Simmias possesses both the property of tallness and the property of shortness simultaneously. Plato’s solution is to distinguish between the Form itself and the particular’s relation to it. The Form of Tallness is never short; it is essentially and unqualifiedly tall. Simmias, however, can be tall *in relation to* Socrates and short *in relation to* Phaedo. This relational predication avoids a direct contradiction, but it does not solve the deeper problem of *change*.

The prompt asks specifically about the transition between opposite states—how does an individual *grow* taller? For Plato, this transition is not a change in the Form, which is static. It is a change in the particular’s participation. Simmias ceases to participate in the Form of Shortness and begins to participate in the Form of Tallness. But what is the mechanism of this transition? Plato offers two answers in the *Phaedo*. The first is the “safe” answer: a thing is tall because it participates in Tallness. This is safe because it avoids causal speculation, but it is also utterly vacuous—it merely restates the phenomenon. The second is the “clever” answer, which Socrates introduces to explain the soul’s relationship to life. The soul is the cause of life; it brings life to the body. Similarly, one might say that the presence of a specific Form causes the corresponding property. However, this still does not explain *how* the Form acts upon the particular, nor *why* the particular’s participation changes over time.

The core metaphysical burden for Plato is the problem of the substratum. What is it that undergoes the change from short to tall? In the *Phaedo*, the soul is the true self, the principle of life and cognition. Yet the soul, in its essential nature, is simple and akin to the Forms. It is unchanging in its essence. If the soul is the substratum that admits the predicate “tall,” then the soul itself would have to become tall, which seems to violate its simplicity. Alternatively, the substratum is the body, which is a composite and constantly changing. But the body is not the true self; it is a prison or a tomb. Thus, Plato’s ontology bifurcates reality into two realms—the intelligible and the sensible—but fails to provide a coherent account of how the two interact. The transition from short to tall is a change in the sensible realm, but the cause of that change lies in the intelligible realm. This separation (*chorismos*) creates an unbridgeable gap. How can a non-spatial, eternal Form cause a change in a spatial, temporal particular? Plato’s participation is a metaphor, not a mechanism. It explains *that* a thing has a property, but not *how* it comes to have it, nor *why* it loses it.

Furthermore, the doctrine of recollection (*anamnesis*) is introduced as the epistemic bridge to the Forms. We recognize the Form of Equality because we recollect it from a previous life. But this does not solve the ontological problem of change; it merely explains how we *know* the Forms. The transition of a particular from one opposite state to another remains a brute fact, an unexplained correlation between the sensible and the intelligible. The substratum problem remains unresolved: if the substratum is the soul, it cannot admit contrary qualities without compromising its simplicity; if it is the body, it is not the subject of true predication (since the body is not the self). Plato’s account of growing taller is thus ontologically impoverished. It reduces change to a change in relational participation, but offers no account of the underlying continuity that allows us to say that the *same* Simmias is now tall and was previously short. The Form of Tallness is eternal, but the particular’s relation to it is inscrutable.

### Part II: Aristotle’s *Categories* and the Architecture of Predication

Aristotle’s response begins with a radical reorientation of ontology. In the *Categories*, he establishes a comprehensive framework for predication based on the distinction between primary and secondary substances. A primary substance is a particular, such as “this man” or “this horse.” It is the ultimate subject of predication—that which underlies all properties and cannot itself be predicated of anything else. Secondary substances are the species and genera under which primary substances fall (e.g., “man,” “animal”). All other things—qualities, quantities, relations, actions, affections—are *said of* or *present in* a primary substance. This logical and ontological priority of the primary substance is the cornerstone of Aristotle’s solution to the problem of change.

The *Categories* directly addresses the problem of contraries in Chapter 5 (4a10-4b20). Aristotle states that a primary substance is capable of receiving contrary qualities while remaining numerically one and the same. He gives the example of an individual man: he can be pale at one time and dark at another, or hot and cold, or good and bad. The man himself does not change in his essence; he remains the same primary substance. What changes is the *quality* that is present in him. This is the crucial insight. For Plato, the particular is defined by its participation in a Form, which makes the particular’s identity contingent upon its properties. For Aristotle, the primary substance is ontologically prior to its properties. The properties are *in* the substance, but they do not constitute its essence. The substance is the *hupokeimenon*—the underlying subject—that persists through change.

This framework elegantly resolves the paradox of contrary predication. The same primary substance can be both pale and dark, but not *at the same time* and *in the same respect*. The principle of non-contradiction is preserved because the substance is not identical to its properties. The man is a substance; paleness is a quality. The quality is present in the substance, but the substance is not the quality. Thus, when the man changes from pale to dark, it is not the *man* that changes (in his essential nature), but the *quality* that is present in him. The substratum remains numerically one, providing the continuity necessary for identity through change.

However, the *Categories* is primarily a work of logical classification, not a complete account of physical change. It tells us *what* changes (a primary substance) and *what* the change is (a change in a category such as quality or quantity), but it does not yet explain the *mechanism* of change. For this, we must turn to the *Physics* and the *Metaphysics*, where Aristotle introduces the crucial distinction between potentiality (*dunamis*) and actuality (*energeia* or *entelecheia*). While the prompt specifically mentions the *Categories*, it also explicitly asks for an examination of “Aristotle’s mechanisms of potentiality and actuality.” These mechanisms are the dynamic complement to the static categories. The *Categories* provides the ontological grammar, while the *Physics* provides the physical process.

Aristotle’s account of change in *Physics* I.7-9 posits three principles: the substratum, the privation, and the form. The substratum is that which persists; the privation is the absence of the form; the form is the new actuality. Change is the actualization of a potential that exists in the substratum. For example, consider a seed growing into a tree. The seed is potentially a tree. It has within it the capacity to become a tree, but it is not yet actually a tree. The change from seed to tree is the actualization of this potential. The substratum—the matter—persists, but it undergoes a transformation from privation (not being a tree) to form (being a tree).

Applying this to the specific example of an individual growing taller: The individual is a primary substance. He has a certain height, which is a quantity (a category of being). At time *t1*, he is 5 feet tall. At time *t2*, he is 6 feet tall. For Aristotle, the individual is potentially 6 feet tall at *t1*. This potentiality is not a mere logical possibility; it is a real capacity grounded in his biological nature—his genetic makeup, his nutrition, his growth mechanisms. When he grows, this potentiality is actualized. The form of “6 feet tall” is realized in the substratum. The substratum—the individual—remains numerically one throughout this process. He is the same person before and after the growth. What has changed is the quantity that is present in him. The privation (not being 6 feet tall) is replaced by the form (being 6 feet tall). Crucially, the individual does not cease to exist, nor does he become a different substance. He simply actualizes a potential that was always latent within him.

This mechanism is profoundly different from Plato’s. For Plato, the individual grows taller because his participation in the Form of Tallness increases, or because he ceases to participate in Shortness and begins to participate in Tallness. But this leaves the individual’s own nature untouched; he is merely a passive recipient of a relation to an external Form. For Aristotle, the individual is an active agent of his own change. The potentiality is *in* him. He grows taller because his own intrinsic nature—his *phusis*—drives him toward the actualization of his mature height. The substratum is not a passive mirror reflecting Forms; it is an active principle of change.

### Part III: The Substratum Problem and the Cogency of Predication

The central test posed by the prompt is how each thinker resolves the problem of the underlying substratum remaining numerically one while admitting contrary qualities. Plato’s failure here is decisive. In the *Phaedo*, the substratum is never clearly identified. If we take the soul as the substratum, we face the problem that the soul is supposed to be simple and unchanging. How can a simple, unchanging entity admit the contrary qualities of tall and short? Plato might respond that the soul *has* a body, and it is the body that is tall or short. But then the substratum is the body, not the soul. This leads to a dualism where the true self (the soul) is not the subject of physical predicates, and the physical subject (the body) is not the true self. The unity of the person is shattered. The “I” that grows taller is not the “I” that knows the Forms. This is a metaphysical inconsistency that undermines the coherence of predication. When we say “Simmias is tall,” we are predicating tallness of *Simmias*, not of his body or his soul. But Plato’s ontology cannot account for this unified subject. He must either reduce Simmias to a bundle of relations to Forms, or posit a mysterious third entity that is neither soul nor body.

Aristotle’s solution is elegantly simple and robust. The primary substance is the unified subject of predication. It is numerically one, and it admits contrary qualities *in different respects* or *at different times*. The man is pale at time *t1* and dark at time *t2*. He is the same man. The quality of paleness is present in him at *t1*, and the quality of darkness is present in him at *t2*. The substance itself does not change; it is the *accidents* that change. This is the distinction between essential and accidental predication. The man’s essence—his being a rational animal—remains constant. His accidental properties—his height, his color, his location—are subject to change. The substratum is the primary substance, which is ontologically prior to its accidents. This priority ensures that the substance can persist through change without being annihilated or becoming a different substance.

Furthermore, Aristotle’s account of the substratum in the *Physics* is more nuanced than a simple substance/accident distinction. In substantial change (generation and corruption), the substratum is prime matter, which has no form of its own. But in accidental change (such as growing taller), the substratum is the primary substance itself. This distinction is crucial. When a man grows taller, he is not ceasing to be a man; he is merely acquiring a new quantity. The substratum is the man, who remains numerically one. The change is a change in the category of quantity, not a change in substance. This avoids the problem of the Third Man, which plagues Plato.

The Third Man argument, famously articulated in the *Parmenides*, demonstrates an infinite regress in the theory of Forms. If a particular man is a man because he participates in the Form of Man, then the Form of Man and the particular man share a common property—being a man. This common property requires a third Form (the Third Man) to explain their similarity. This leads to an infinite regress of Forms. Aristotle avoids this entirely by denying the separate existence of Forms. Universals exist *in* particulars, not apart from them. The species “man” is predicated of the individual man, but it does not exist as a separate entity. There is no need for a Form of Man to explain why Socrates is a man; Socrates is a man because he has the essence of humanity, which is instantiated in him. This avoids the regress and provides a more parsimonious ontology.

In terms of predication, Aristotle’s framework is also more cogent. Plato’s predication is ultimately relational and ambiguous. When Simmias is tall, he is tall *relative to* Socrates. But what does it mean to be tall *simpliciter*? Plato struggles with this. Aristotle, by categorizing height as a quantity, allows for absolute predication. We can say “Simmias is 5 feet 10 inches tall” without any reference to another individual. This is a precise, non-relational predication. The quality or quantity is present in the substance, and the substance is the subject of the predication. This avoids the paradox of opposites because the opposites are not predicated of the same subject in the same respect at the same time. Simmias is tall relative to Socrates, but short relative to Phaedo. These are two different relations, and they do not contradict each other. But Aristotle goes further: he allows for the predication of a specific quantity, which is not relative at all. This is a significant advance in logical clarity.

### Part IV: The Burdens of Participation and the Success of Actuality

Plato’s doctrine of participation is arguably the weakest link in his metaphysical system. The *Phaedo* offers no clear explanation of what participation *is*. Is it a causal relation? A resemblance? A mere linguistic convention? Plato himself acknowledges the difficulty in the *Parmenides*, where he questions whether a Form can be wholly in many particulars or only partially in each. If the Form is wholly in each particular, then it is separated from itself; if it is partially in each, then it is divisible and no longer simple. This is a devastating critique. Aristotle seizes on this in his own criticism of the Forms, arguing that participation is a mere metaphor, an empty phrase. He writes in the *Metaphysics* (I.9, 991a20-22) that “to say that the Forms are patterns and that other things participate in them is to use empty words and poetical metaphors.”

Aristotle’s potentiality/actuality distinction provides a substantive, non-metaphorical mechanism for change. It explains *how* a thing changes, not just *that* it changes. The potential is a real feature of the substance; it is the capacity to be actualized. The actuality is the realization of that capacity. This is not a mysterious relation to an external entity; it is an internal process of development. The seed is potentially a tree; the tree is the actuality of the seed. The child is potentially a grown adult; the adult is the actuality of the child. This teleological framework is immanent, not transcendent. The end (*telos*) of the change is not a separate Form but the full actualization of the substance’s own nature.

Moreover, Aristotle’s account avoids the problem of recollection (*anamnesis*). Plato’s doctrine of recollection is necessary because the Forms are separate from the sensible world, and knowledge of them cannot be derived from sense experience. We must have known them before birth. This is a heavy metaphysical burden, requiring a pre-natal existence of the soul and a doctrine of reincarnation. Aristotle, by contrast, grounds knowledge in the sensible world. We come to know universals through abstraction from particulars. We see many individual men and abstract the universal “man.” This is a more empirical, scientifically viable epistemology. It does not require a prior life or a separate realm of Forms.

The prompt asks whether Aristotle successfully avoids the metaphysical burdens and inconsistencies that plague Plato’s treatment of opposite properties. The answer is unequivocally yes. Let us enumerate the burdens that Plato faces and show how Aristotle avoids them:

1.  **Separation (*Chorismos*)**: Plato separates the Forms from particulars, creating an unbridgeable gap. Aristotle denies this separation. Universals exist in particulars. This avoids the problem of explaining how a transcendent Form can causally interact with an immanent particular.
2.  **The Third Man**: Plato’s theory leads to an infinite regress of Forms. Aristotle’s immanent universals do not generate this regress because there is no need for a third entity to explain the similarity between a particular and its universal.
3.  **The Mechanism of Participation**: Plato’s participation is a metaphor without explanatory power. Aristotle’s potentiality/actuality is a real mechanism grounded in the nature of the substance.
4.  **The Substratum**: Plato fails to identify a coherent substratum that can admit contrary qualities. Aristotle’s primary substance is the perfect substratum—numerically one, ontologically prior to its accidents, and capable of receiving contraries at different times.
5.  **The Paradox of Opposites**: Plato’s relational predication avoids contradiction but fails to explain absolute predication.
