# The Chasm and the Field: William Hasker’s Emergent Dualism, Hiatuses in Consciousness, and the Paradox of Numerical Identity

## Introduction

In *The Emergent Self* (2001), William Hasker mounts a sophisticated defense of substance dualism designed to navigate the Scylla of Cartesian immaterialism—which struggles to account for how a wholly non-physical mind can interact with a physical brain—and the Charybdis of reductive physicalism, which struggles to account for the qualitative, unquantifiable interiority of conscious experience. Hasker’s solution is **emergent dualism**: the thesis that consciousness is a genuine substance-property, an emergent field of phenomenal experience generated by the complex neurological organization of the brain, yet possessing novel causal powers irreducible to its physical base. 

Central to Hasker’s ontological architecture is the **magnet analogy**, modeled on the electrostatic or magnetic field, wherein a physical configuration produces a field that, once generated, exerts top-down causal influence upon the very substrate that gave rise to it. Furthermore, Hasker anchors personal identity not in physical continuity alone, but in the numerical identity of this emergent field of consciousness across time.

However, emergent dualism faces a profound metaphysical stress test when confronted with phenomena involving **hiatuses in consciousness**—interruptions of subjective awareness such as deep coma, general anesthesia, clinical death followed by successful resuscitation, and speculative states like cryonic suspension. If an emergent field of consciousness is strictly dependent upon its neurological generating base for its ongoing existence, what happens to the *numerical identity* of that field when the generating base ceases to support conscious activity, or when the activity is completely suspended? 

This essay will evaluate the coherence of Hasker’s emergent dualism when brought into direct confrontation with these hiatuses. It will argue that Hasker’s framework is caught in a rigid **trilemma**: 
1. He can abandon the emergent field as the criterion for personal identity in favor of physical brain continuity, thereby collapsing his dualism into a functionalist physicalism;
2. He can accept that numerical identity is irretrievably lost across any hiatus in consciousness, making the resurrected or revived subject a distinct entity and rendering personal survival illusory; or
3. He can invoke divine intervention to bridge the ontological gap, thereby sacrificing the emergentist credentials of his theory and collapsing back into traditional Cartesian-style interaction problems and occasionalism. 

Through an analytical dissection of the magnet analogy, the mechanics of emergence under determinism and indeterminism, and the criterion of personal identity, this essay will demonstrate that emergent dualism cannot satisfactorily reconcile the generation-dependence of the field with the preservation of numerical identity through sustained periods of unconsciousness.

---

## Part I: The Architecture of Emergent Dualism and the Magnet Analogy

To understand why hiatuses in consciousness pose such a lethal threat to Hasker’s theory, one must first map the metaphysical contours of emergent dualism. Hasker rejects standard physicalism because it cannot bridge the "explanatory gap" between objective neurophysiological processes and subjective qualia. Yet he equally rejects traditional Cartesian dualism, which treats the mind as an uncreated or independently existing spiritual substance that happens to inhabit a body, creating an insoluble interactionist mystery: How can the entirely non-spatial and non-physical casually nudge a physical molecule?

Hasker’s emergent dualism posits that when matter attains a sufficiently high degree of organizational complexity—specifically, the human brain—a new *substance* (or a substantial emergent entity, the "self") comes into existence. This self is not merely a bundle of fleeting mental states; it is an enduring subject of experience. Crucially, this emergent self is generated *by* the brain, but it is not *identical to* the brain. Once brought into being, the emergent self possesses a degree of autonomy and exerts top-down causal efficacy on the brain, guiding behavior, making decisions, and shaping neuroplastic pathways.

To clarify this ontological novelty, Hasker employs the **magnet analogy**. Consider a bar magnet. The magnet is a physical object composed of an iron lattice. Because of the alignment of electron spins within this physical base, a magnetic field is generated surrounding the magnet. This field is real; it has causal powers (it can deflect compass needles, attract iron filings, and interact with other magnetic fields). Yet, the magnetic field is not itself a collection of iron atoms. It is an emergent property—or field—generated by the physical arrangement of the underlying substrate. 

Within Hasker’s framework, the brain acts as the permanent physical magnet, and the field of consciousness acts as the emergent magnetic field. The field depends utterly upon the physical base for its initial generation and its ongoing maintenance. If you smash the magnet or heat it past the Curie point, the magnetic field dissipates entirely. 

However, the magnet analogy carries heavy ontological baggage that Hasker must manage carefully. A physical magnetic field is co-extensive with and strictly dependent upon the continuous state of its generating base. If the electric currents or atomic alignments fluctuate, the field fluctuates in direct, lawful correspondence. When the current is cut off, the magnetic field instantly ceases to exist; it does not "hover" in empty space waiting for the current to be restored. 

This introduces the first major tension in Hasker’s model when applied to human consciousness: **The asymmetry of dependence.** Hasker wants the emergent field to be powerful enough to exercise top-down causation, maintaining a unified sense of self that can resist or redirect physical impulses. Yet, because the field is *generated* by the physical brain, its ontological status is parasitic upon that brain. If the brain's activity stops, or drops below the threshold required to sustain the field, what happens to the field? In a standard magnetic field, cessation of the base equals cessation of the field. If consciousness is structurally analogous, then any complete interruption of brain activity—any hiatus in consciousness—must result in the absolute destruction of the emergent field.

## Part II: The Criterion of Personal Identity and the Threat of the Hiatus

Personal identity over time requires answering John Locke’s famous question: What makes a conscious subject at time $t_2$ the *same person* as the conscious subject who existed at time $t_1$? 

For Hasker, the answer is unequivocally bound to the emergent field of consciousness. The self *is* the emergent field of experience. Therefore, numerical identity over time consists in the continuous, uninterrupted existence of this singular emergent field. Physical continuity of the brain is necessary for *generating* and *sustaining* the field, but physical continuity is merely the causal condition; the *identity* itself inheres in the mental substance—the field of consciousness. 

This creates a critical juncture when we examine cases of **hiatuses in consciousness**:
* **Clinical Death and Resuscitation:** A patient suffers cardiac arrest. Brain activity ceases entirely (flat EEG, absent brainstem reflexes). After ten minutes, spontaneous circulation is restored, and brain activity resumes.
* **Coma and Anesthesia:** A patient is placed in a medically induced coma or undergoes deep general anesthesia. Cortical integration collapses; phenomenological awareness drops to absolute zero. Hours or days later, the brain recovers its integrative capacity, and consciousness "returns."
* **Cryonic Suspension:** A speculative future scenario where a brain is vitrified, halting all biochemical processes indefinitely, only to be warmed and revived centuries later.

In all these cases, we witness a temporal gap: 
* $T_1$: Subject $S$ is conscious.
* $T_2$: The physical generating base (the brain) suffers a structural or functional collapse such that the generation of the emergent field is entirely suspended. The field ceases to exist.
* $T_3$: The physical brain is repaired or recovers, and a new state of consciousness begins.

The central philosophical question is: **Is the field of consciousness at $T_3$ numerically identical to the field of consciousness that existed at $T_1$?**

According to the strict mechanics of the magnet analogy, the answer must be **no**. When a magnet is demagnetized, the original field is destroyed. If you subsequently remagnetize the iron bar, you do not restore the *same* numerical field; you generate a *new* field that is qualitatively similar (or identical in structure) to the old one, but numerically distinct. 

If the emergent field of consciousness is strictly analogous, then during a hiatus where brain activity drops below the threshold of generation, the original emergent self ceases to exist. When the brain recovers at $T_3$, it does not "reawaken" the old self; rather, the resurrected brain generates a *brand-new* emergent self. This new self may inherit memories encoded in the physical synaptic pathways of the brain, leading it to *believe* it is the same person, but ontologically, it is a psychological impostor—a newly minted soul generated by a rebooted biological machine.

For a philosopher like Hasker, who wishes to defend personal survival (and implicitly, theological frameworks involving resurrection or personal endurance through bodily trauma), this conclusion is devastating. If numerical identity is severed by every deep coma or surgical anesthesia, then personal identity is vastly more fragile than common sense or religious metaphysics demands. We die a metaphysical death every time we undergo general anesthesia, replaced instantaneously by a successor ego that merely inherits our biological archives.

## Part III: Hasker’s Trilemma

Faced with this crisis of numerical identity across hiatuses, emergent dualism is thrust onto the horns of a severe philosophical **trilemma**. Hasker must choose one of three paths, each of which undermines the internal coherence or the distinctiveness of his emergentist project.

### Horn 1: Abandoning the Field as the Criterion for Identity in Favor of Physical Brain Continuity

To avoid the collapse of personal identity during a hiatus, Hasker could modify his criterion of identity. He could argue that the emergent field of consciousness is *not* the primary bearer of numerical identity; rather, numerical identity is anchored in the continuous physical-functional organization of the brain, and the emergent field is merely a dependent epiphenomenon or transient state of that physical substrate.

**The Critique of Horn 1:** 
If Hasker takes this route, he effectively abandons emergent *dualism* and slides into a form of **emergent physicalism** or biological functionalism. If the numerical identity of the person is carried entirely by the physical brain (its synaptic architecture, structural continuity, and functional organization), then the emergent field of consciousness loses its status as an independent substance-property. It becomes merely what the physical brain *does* when it is active. 

Worse yet, this evacuates the core motivation of Hasker’s dualism. The entire point of positing an emergent *self* distinct from the physical brain was to secure robust mental autonomy, genuine top-down agency, and an ontology that transcends physical reductionism. If the self's continued existence across a coma is sustained solely by the physical brain holding a structural pattern (much like a computer hard drive retaining files while powered down), then the "field of consciousness" is nothing more than a flickering shadow cast by physical mechanisms. It can no longer serve as an independent metaphysical anchor for personal identity.

### Horn 2: Accepting That Numerical Identity is Lost Across Any Hiatus in Consciousness

Hasker could bite the bullet and accept the strict entailment of his own analogy: numerical identity *is* indeed lost across any absolute hiatus in consciousness. On this view, deep sleep (without dreaming), general anesthesia, coma, and clinical death are literal interruptions of the self's existence. The subject who goes under anesthesia at 9:00 AM does not wake up at 11:00 AM; rather, a new subject is created whose mental life begins with the contents of the waking brain's memory traces.

**The Critique of Horn 2:** 
Accepting this horn requires a radical revision of our commonsense intuitions about personal identity and moral responsibility, and it introduces deep metaphysical incoherence regarding the nature of memory. 

First, consider the moral and legal implications. If numerical identity is severed by a medically induced coma, then holding a patient morally or legally accountable for actions committed before a severe brain trauma—or even punishing someone who "woke up" after a criminal act rendered them temporarily unconscious—becomes ontologically problematic. We would be punishing a successor entity for the crimes of its predecessor. 

Second, and more fundamentally, this position generates a profound epistemological and metaphysical absurdity regarding memory. How can a newly generated emergent field possess apparent first-person memories of an event experienced by a *completely different* emergent field that ceased to exist three hours prior? 

In Hasker’s ontology, memories are presumably stored as physical traces (engrams) in the brain's neural networks. When the brain reactivates, these physical traces cause the newly emergent field of consciousness to experience "memories." But notice the sleight of hand: the new field is experiencing *false direct memories*. It feels as though *I* experienced walking in the park yesterday, but ontologically, the "I" who walked in the park was annihilated during the surgical coma, and the current "I" is a fresh creation reading the physical diary left behind in the neural wiring. This turns personal history into a grand deception, where every recovery from unconsciousness involves the instantaneous manufacturing of a stolen autobiographical past. 

### Horn 3: Invoking Divine Intervention to Bridge the Ontological Gap

To preserve both emergent dualism and numerical identity across hiatuses, Hasker might appeal to a supernatural mechanism. He could argue that during a hiatus (such as clinical death or cryonic suspension), when the physical generating base fails, God steps in to miraculously preserve the emergent field of consciousness in a disembodied state (or hold its numerical identity in divine memory/power) and miraculously re-anchors it to the brain when resuscitation occurs.

**The Critique of Horn 3:** 
This is the most perilous path for an emergentist. By invoking divine intervention to rescue numerical identity, Hasker commits ontological treason against his own theory. 

Emergentism is designed to be a naturalistic metaphysics of mind. It claims that consciousness emerges *naturally* from complex physical organization, operating according to lawful, emergentist principles analogous to magnetic fields or chemical properties. The moment God is required to step in and miraculously maintain or transplant an emergent field across a biological blackout, the theory ceases to be *emergentism* in any meaningful sense. It collapses back into a form of **occasionalism** or Cartesian dualism infused with divine stewardship. 

If God has to intervene to keep the soul/field in existence when the brain dies, then the brain is no longer doing the generating and sustaining work. The theory is no longer explaining consciousness through the upward causation of physical complexity; it is explaining consciousness through divine preservation. Furthermore, this reintroduces the classic Cartesian interaction problem with a vengeance: How does a divine agent interact with and sustain an emergent field of consciousness independently of its physical base? Hasker has traded the explanatory gaps of neurobiology for the unfalsifiable mysteries of theology.

## Part IV: A Deeper Mechanics of the Magnet Analogy: Determinism, Indeterminism, and Field-State Persistence

To appreciate the structural rigidity of Hasker’s trilemma, we must examine the mechanics of the magnet analogy under both deterministic and indeterministic frameworks. Hasker is a libertarian about free will; he believes the emergent self exerts indeterministic top-down causation upon the brain. How does this complicate the fate of the field during a hiatus?

In physics, a magnetic field does not possess a memory of its own history. If you demagnetize a piece of iron by heating it past the Curie point (agitating the magnetic domains into complete randomness) and then cool it in the absence of an external magnetic field, the new magnetic state bears no directional relation to the old one. The history of the field is erased because the field is an instantaneous function of the current physical state of the substrate.

Let us apply this to Hasker’s emergent field of consciousness. The field at any given moment $T_2$ is a function of:
1. The immediate physical state of the brain at $T_2$.
2. The past history of the field acting *downward* upon the brain to shape its current physical state (top-down feedback loops).

During a hiatus (e.g., general anesthesia), the physical substrate undergoes a radical disruption. The synchronized neural oscillations required for conscious integration collapse into localized, incoherent firings or complete quiescence. 

If consciousness is strictly an emergent property of *active, integrated* neural processing, then when integration ceases, the emergence condition is violated. Just as water ceases to be "wet" when the molecules are separated into isolated hydrogen and oxygen gases at high temperatures, the emergent field ceases to exist when neural integration dissolves.

Can an emergent field possess **hysteresis**—the dependence of the state of a system on its history, such that it could "survive" a temporary disruption of its base? In physical systems, hysteresis occurs in ferromagnetic materials where a memory of magnetization persists due to domain alignment. However, this persistence requires the physical substrate *itself* to retain its internal structural alignment. 

In a biological brain undergoing a hiatus (like deep coma or clinical death), the physical substrate *does not* retain its precise functional alignment. Neuronal membranes depolarize, metabolic gradients collapse (ATP depletion), and synaptic transmission halts. If the physical substrate loses its structural and functional organization during the hiatus, then even if hysteresis were a feature of emergent fields, the physical base required to anchor that hysteresis has been structurally obliterated or temporarily disabled.

Therefore, whether the underlying brain dynamics are deterministic or indeterministic, the result for the emergent field is the same:
* **Under Determinism:** The physical state dictates the field state. If the physical state supporting consciousness drops to zero, the field state drops to zero. When the physical state restarts, it generates a new field state determined by the *new* physical configuration, but the continuity of the ontological subject is broken.
* **Under Indeterminism:** The field exerts libertarian free will upon the brain. But libertarian agency requires an active, functioning conscious subject to deliberate and choose. During a coma, there is no conscious subject to exercise indeterministic agency. When the brain spontaneously or artificially restarts, the new indeterministic choices made by the "revived" subject spring from a newly initiated causal nexus, not from a continuously existing emergent self.

## Part V: Evaluating the Viability of Emergent Dualism Under the Hiatus Challenge

Having mapped the trilemma and the mechanics of the magnet analogy, we must now render a final philosophical evaluation of Hasker’s emergent dualism when confronted with hiatuses in consciousness. 

The core vulnerability of Hasker’s project lies in an unresolved tension between **ontological dualism** and **functional dependence**. Hasker wants the best of both worlds:
1. He wants the mind to be a distinct *substance* (an emergent self) so that it can possess robust unity, qualitative interiority, and libertarian free will.
2. He wants the mind to be strictly *dependent* upon the brain for its generation and physical grounding, avoiding the spooky placelessness of Cartesian souls.

This dual desire creates an unstable metaphysical hybrid. If the emergent self is a distinct substance, it ought to possess a degree of ontological robustness that transcends transient fluctuations in its generating base—much as a nation-state possesses an identity distinct from any single citizen, or an ecosystem possesses properties distinct from individual organisms. Yet, Hasker’s strict reliance on the magnet analogy ties the emergent field inextricably to the continuous operation of the physical substrate. 

When a physical substrate undergoes a hiatus—when the brain flatlines, goes cold, or enters deep anesthesia—the emergentist who maintains substance dualism is trapped. If the substance is real, where does it go during the hiatus? 
* If it **persists** without a physical base, Hasker has abandoned emergentism and lapsed into standard Cartesian dualism (since an emergent field that can exist uncoupled from matter is no longer strictly emergent from that matter). 
* If it **ceases to exist**, then numerical identity is destroyed, and the doctrine of personal survival through medical resuscitation or bodily continuity is reduced to an illusion.

Hasker’s failure to provide a coherent account of personal identity across hiatuses exposes a fatal structural flaw in emergent dualism: **The Generation-Identity Paradox.** An emergent entity cannot simultaneously be *generated* by a physical base in real-time dependence and maintain *numerical identity* independently of the continuous operation of that base. If generation equals ongoing ontological dependence, then every interruption of the base is an annihilation of the entity.

---

## Conclusion

William Hasker’s emergent dualism represents a valiant and intellectually rich attempt to forge a middle path between the blind alleys of reductive physicalism and Cartesian dualism. By utilizing the magnet analogy, he attempts to make intelligible how physical complexity can give rise to a non-reductionist field of conscious experience equipped with top-down causal powers.

However, when confronted with the realities of hiatuses in consciousness—clinical death, deep coma, anesthesia, and cryonic suspension—emergent dualism shatters against the horns of a trilemma. Hasker must either sacrifice the emergent field as the criterion of personal identity (collapsing into physicalism), accept the continuous annihilation and recreation of the self across every blackout (undermining personal survival and turning memory into a cruel epistemological deception), or invoke miraculous divine intervention (abandoning naturalistic emergence for occasionalism). 

Because none of these horns can be grasped without betraying the core commitments of Hasker’s philosophical system, we must conclude that emergent dualism is profoundly incoherent when applied to the temporal discontinuities of human existence. The magnet analogy, illuminating though it is for synchronous generation, ultimately exposes the fatal fragility of the emergent self: an emergent field of consciousness, bound as it is to the flickering currents of a fragile biological machine, cannot survive the darkness when the machine goes quiet.
