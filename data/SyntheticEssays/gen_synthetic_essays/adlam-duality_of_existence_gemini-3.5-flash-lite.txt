### Introduction

The question of whether existence is univocal—whether the verb "to exist" means one and the same thing when predicated of any entity whatsoever—stands as one of the most persistent fault lines in metaphysics and ontology. From Duns Scotus’s *univocatio entis*, which broke with the analogical predication of being characteristic of high scholasticism, to Willard Van Orman Quine’s famous dictum that "to be is to be the value of a variable," modern analytical ontology has largely favored the univocal view. On this picture, existence is a flat, indiscriminate domain. A number, a physical particle, a property, and a social institution all *exist* in precisely the same sense, even if they differ radically in their categorial profiles, causal powers, and modal properties.

Yet this flattened landscape has faced deep and enduring resistance. The most intuitive, yet philosophically treacherous, resistance arises from the stark ontological divergence between concrete entities—particulars bound by the coordinates of spacetime, possessing causal efficacy and material constitution—and abstract entities, such as numbers, propositions, and sets, which are traditionally held to be outside spacetime, causally inert, and necessarily existent. When we assert that an electron exists, and when we assert that the number 7 exists, are we deploying the existential quantifier in a single, semantically stable sense? Or does the invocation of "existence" across these domains trade on an equivocation, masking a profound ontological rupture?

This essay evaluates the claim that existence is not univocal by examining whether concrete and abstract entities require distinct existence criteria anchored in the concept of mind-independence. To test this, we will traverse a rigorous philosophical terrain. We will analyze Rudolf Carnap’s dissolution of traditional ontology through the machinery of linguistic frameworks, contrasting it with Peter van Inwagen’s robust, deflationary defenses of univocity. We will directly confront the spatialized intuition: does spatiotemporal location versus location in relational abstract spaces generate two separate senses of existence? We will scrutinize Quine’s criterion of ontological commitment to see whether formal logic adequately unifies these domains or merely papered over a semantically heterogeneous reality. Finally, we will test the limits of univocity through the Problem of the Many, asking whether the paradoxes of vagueness reveal an irreducible duality—or outright incoherence—in the very grammar of existence.

### I. The Scotist Legacy and the Analytic Consensus of Univocity

To appreciate the weight of the claim that existence is not univocal, we must first reconstruct the ontological stakes of univocity. Duns Scotus introduced the univocity of being (*univocatio entis*) to ensure that human beings could predicate existence and attributes of God and creatures in the same literal sense, without lapsing into complete equivocation or pure analogy. For Scotus, being is the most abstract, indeterminate concept available to the intellect; it is a notion prior to all division, including the division between finite and infinite, or substance and accident. 

In contemporary analytic philosophy, this Scotist inheritance was secularized and formalized, most notably by Quine. Quine’s ontological program strips existence of its traditional, thick metaphysical garb—its connection to essences, primary substances, or creative grounds—and reduces it to the austere mechanics of quantification. Famously summarizing his view in "On What There Is" (1948), Quine argued that ontological questions are settled not by consulting a private realm of intuitions or carving nature at its joints, but by looking at our best scientific theories and asking what bound variables must range over for those theories to be true. 

The Quinean consensus relies on a powerful intuition: if we abandon univocity, language fractures. If "exists" means one thing when applied to a dog, another when applied to a number, and yet another when applied to a hole or a proposition, then existential statements lose their combinatorial semantic force. How could we understand disjunctions like "Either the physical universe exists or the number 2 exists" if the disjuncts employ radically incommensurable notions of existence? Univocity, on this view, is a precondition for meaningful ontological debate. If disputants do not share a single meaning of "exists," then realism and nominalism, Platonism and physicalism, are merely talking past one another in distinct languages.

Yet, critics of univocity argue that this linguistic hygiene purchases formal tidiness at the price of ontological blindness. By treating existence as a mere predicate-modifier or quantifier-value, the univocal view elides the yawning chasm between entities that can kick back (causally interact with us in the laboratory) and entities that merely anchor the truth of our mathematical theorems. To declare that both quarks and the number 7 *exist* in the exact same sense is, critics charge, to render the word "existence" so thin as to be virtually contentless.

### II. Carnap’s Linguistic Frameworks and the Rejection of External Questions

To understand how the univocity of existence can be challenged without falling into naive, pre-critical metaphysics, we must turn to Rudolf Carnap’s seminal 1950 paper, "Empiricism, Ontology, and Linguistic Frameworks." Carnap sought to exorcise traditional metaphysics by drawing a sharp distinction between two types of questions concerning the existence of entities: *internal* questions and *external* questions.

Internal questions are formulated within a chosen linguistic framework. Once we adopt a framework—say, the framework of physical things, or the framework of numbers (arithmetic)—we can ask internal questions like, "Is there a physical object in my room?" or "Is there a prime number greater than one hundred?" These questions are answered by empirical investigation (in the case of physical things) or by logical and mathematical deduction (in the case of numbers). To answer "yes" to an internal question is to assert that the entity in question exists *within* that framework.

External questions, by contrast, are questions posed *outside* of any linguistic framework. They ask whether the system of entities as a whole—or the framework itself—objectively *exists* in some mind-independent, absolute reality. Does the physical world *really* exist? Do numbers *really* exist in the realm of Platonic heaven? 

Carnap’s diagnosis is devastating to traditional ontologists: external questions are not theoretical questions at all, but practical decisions concerning the adoption of a linguistic framework. To ask whether numbers "really" exist, stripped of the rules of arithmetic and our linguistic practices, is to pose a pseudo-question. It lacks cognitive content because there is no external standpoint from which to evaluate it. 

When applied to our present inquiry, Carnap’s framework analysis suggests that the question of whether existence is univocal is itself a pseudo-question or, at best, a pragmatic linguistic choice. Within the physical framework, "exists" has a specific set of verification conditions (spatiotemporal localization, causal interaction). Within the abstract mathematical framework, "exists" has entirely different verification conditions (derivability from axioms, proof-theoretic consistency). 

Carnap would argue that asking whether these two uses of "exists" denote a *single* underlying property of "existence" is a category mistake. There is no such thing as "existence" apart from the rules of the languages we use to speak of things. Therefore, the claim that existence is univocal assumes a non-sensical, externalist standpoint that treats frameworks as if they were objects floating in an absolute ontological space.

However, Carnap’s anti-metaphysical instrumentalism has not gone unchallenged. Philosophers like Quine vigorously pushed back against the internal/external distinction, arguing that the boundary between framework adoption (pragmatic choice) and empirical theory-building (internal assertion) is thoroughly porous. Our choice of linguistic frameworks is itself subject to empirical and ontological revision; we choose frameworks because we believe they map onto a mind-independent reality better than their rivals. If Carnap is right, we cannot ask whether abstract entities *really* exist independently of our language. But for the realist, that is precisely the question that matters most.

### III. Peter van Inwagen and the Defense of Univocotion

Standing diametrically opposed to any fragmentation of existence is Peter van Inwagen, one of the most vociferous contemporary defenders of the univocity of being and the Quinean reduction of ontology to quantification. In works such as *Ontological Demurral* and *Being, Existence, and Ontological Commitment*, van Inwagen mounts a rigorous defense of the thesis that "existence is univocal" and that "to be is to be the value of a bound variable."

Van Inwagen argues that ordinary language and rigorous philosophy alike employ "exists" in precisely one sense. When we say that lions exist, that holes exist, that numbers exist, and that God exists, we are deploying the existential quantifier ($\exists x$) in a uniform logical sense. The variations do not lie in the meaning of the word "exists," but entirely in the nature of the *entities* being quantified over. 

To illustrate van Inwagen’s point, consider an analogy: the verb "to kick." When a horse kicks a wall, a child kicks a ball, and a comedian kicks a bucket, the verb "to kick" does not change its meaning; rather, the *objects* and the physical actions involved differ, but the semantic content of the relational predicate remains constant. Similarly, for van Inwagen, existence is not a property that entities possess in different ways or to different degrees; it is simply the feature of being included in the domain of quantification.

Van Inwagen confronts head-on the intuition that abstract and concrete entities must have different modes of existence. He argues that this intuition rests on a conflation between *existence* and *being concrete*. It is true that concrete entities have spatiotemporal coordinates and causal powers, whereas abstract entities do not. But these are differences in their *properties*, not in their *existence*. To say that an abstract entity exists is simply to say that there is such a thing as the number 7—that the open sentence "$x = 7$" is satisfied by at least one object in our domain.

Crucially, van Inwagen defends this univocity by arguing that any attempt to posit "analogical," "tiered," or "graded" existence inevitably lapses into incoherence. If "exists" means one thing for concrete objects and another for abstract objects, what does "exists" mean when we assert a mixed claim, such as "There are both prime numbers and physical planets"? If we cannot provide a univocal semantics for the quantifier in such sentences, logic collapses into a fragmented series of local dialects, making cross-categorial truth and falsehood impossible to formulate.

Yet, van Inwagen’s defense relies heavily on a deflationary conception of ontology. By reducing ontological commitment to the austere mechanics of first-order logic, he bypasses the traditional philosophical anxiety: *How* can an entity that is causally inert, non-spatiotemporal, and mind-independent be said to "be" in the same robust sense as a flesh-and-blood human being? For many critics, declaring that the difference is merely one of properties, while keeping existence univocal, feels like a verbal sleight-of-hand that papers over a genuine metaphysical abyss.

### IV. The Spatialized Intuition: Spacetime vs. Relational Abstract Spaces

To press further into the heart of this debate, we must confront the spatialized intuition that drives the non-univocal view: the idea that location—specifically, spatiotemporal location versus location in relational abstract spaces—generates two separate senses of existence.

For the commonsense realist and the hard-nosed physicalist, to exist is to be *somewhere* and *sometime*. Spacetime provides the ultimate arena of being. To say that a table, a star, or a neuro-chemical state exists is to locate it within the four-dimensional manifold of relativistic physics. In this framework, existence is intimately tied to causal interaction; to exist is to occupy a position in the causal nexus, capable of exerting forces and undergoing changes.

Abstract entities, by definition, fail this test. The number 7 has no mass, emits no radiation, occupies no spatial region, and has no temporal duration. It cannot be localized in the universe. Instead, Platonists often speak of abstract entities as occupying "relational abstract spaces"—semantic, mathematical, or conceptual structures where entities are individuated entirely by their structural relations to one another. For example, the number 7 exists not in the Milky Way, but as a node in the network of the natural number sequence, defined by the Peano axioms.

Does this divergence in the *type of location* (or lack of spatiotemporal location) dictate a divergence in the *meaning of existence*?

Proponents of non-univocity argue that it does. They contend that "existence" without spatiotemporal localization is an entirely different conceptual animal. When we say a physical object exists, existence is tied to *presence* and *actuality*. When we say an abstract object exists, existence is tied to *necessity*, *consistency*, or *subsistence*. To elide this difference is to treat the word "existence" as an equivocal placeholder. Just as the word "bank" denotes both a financial institution and the side of a river—homonyms with distinct semantic entries—so too, the argument goes, "existence" as applied to stones is a homonym of "existence" as applied to sets.

Against this, defenders of univocity respond that spatial location is merely one specific type of relation that an entity can bear to a framework, but it is not a defining characteristic of existence itself. Spacetime is just a four-dimensional relational structure among physical events. If abstract spaces are likewise relational structures among concepts or mathematical objects, then "location" in an abstract space is structurally analogous to location in spacetime, even if it lacks causal and metric properties. 

Furthermore, defenders of univocity ask: What would a "non-spatiotemporal sense of existence" even look like? If we cannot give a positive semantic analysis of what "existence" means when stripped of quantification, then positing a "second sense of existence" is not an explanation, but a mystery masquerading as a distinction. It introduces a proliferation of ontological modes without semantic necessity.

### V. Quine’s Criterion and the Limits of Ontological Commitment

To evaluate whether Quine’s criterion and the existential quantifier adequately capture ontological commitments across both concrete and abstract domains, we must subject Quine’s methodology to severe philosophical stress testing.

Quine’s famous criterion states: *To be is to be the value of a variable*. In formal logic, an interpreted first-order theory is ontologically committed to those and only those entities that must be included in the range of the theory’s bound variables if the statements affirmed in the theory are to be true. 

On the surface, this criterion treats concrete and abstract entities with absolute egalitarianism. If our best physical theory requires quantification over spacetime points and gravitational fields, we are committed to physical entities. If our best mathematical theory requires quantification over sets and functions (e.g., in real analysis), we are equally committed to abstract entities. The logical machinery does not blink; the quantifier $\exists x$ ranges indifferently over the domain $\mathcal{D}$, whether $\mathcal{D}$ contains electrons or equivalence classes.

However, critics have long argued that Quine’s criterion is profoundly insensitive to the internal heterogeneity of ontological commitments—a failure that strongly suggests existence cannot be neatly captured by a single quantifier.

Consider the phenomenon of *ineffability* or *ontological deflation*. Mathematical nominalists (such as Hartry Field in *Science Without Numbers*) argue that while our scientific theories *appear* to quantify over abstract mathematical entities, this is merely a convenient, instrumental façon de parler. Field demonstrated that Newtonian gravitational theory can be nominalized—reformulated entirely without quantification over numbers or functions, using second-order quantification over regions of space and spatio-temporal relations instead. If mathematical discourse can be paraphrased away, does the Quinean quantifier reveal a genuine ontological commitment to abstract objects, or does it merely reveal the limitations of our current linguistic packaging?

More fundamentally, critics point to the *indispensability argument* (associated with W.V.O. Quine and Hilary Putnam). This argument asserts that we have just as much reason to believe in the existence of abstract mathematical entities as we do in unobservable physical entities (like quarks or strings), because both are indispensable to our best scientific theories. 

Yet, this assimilation reveals the deep tension in Quine’s criterion. Physical entities are bound up with causal explanation, empirical testability, and spatiotemporal localization. Abstract entities are known a priori, are causally inert, and are supposedly necessary across all possible worlds. To declare that both types of entities are ontologically on a par simply because they both occupy a slot after an existential quantifier $\exists$ is, critics argue, an instance of logical imperialism. It uses the austere syntax of mathematical logic to legislate away genuine metaphysical differences. 

If logic forces us to treat numbers and physical particles as existing in the identical sense, perhaps the fault lies not in reality, but in the flattening, reductive nature of first-order logic. Perhaps standard predicate calculus is simply too blunt an instrument to capture the rich, stratified architecture of being.

### VI. The Problem of the Many and the Breakdown of Univocity

To test whether existence is truly univocal or whether it fractures under philosophical scrutiny, we can examine one of the most stubborn paradoxes in contemporary metaphysics: **The Problem of the Many**, originally formulated by Peter Geach.

Consider a physical cloud floating in the sky, or a cat sitting on a mat. At the boundary where the cloud meets the surrounding air, or where the cat’s fur meets the ambient space, there is physical vagueness. Molecules of water vapor or loose hairs are in a borderline state—neither determinutely inside the object nor determinutely outside it. 

Because of this microscopic indeterminacy, there are not one, but *vastly many* distinct aggregates of matter (let us call them $C_1, C_2, C_3 \dots C_n$), each differing by the inclusion or exclusion of one or a few borderline molecules, any one of which has an equal claim to be "the" cloud. Each of these aggregates satisfies our ordinary criteria for being a cloud. Yet, our ordinary intuition and linguistic practice insist that there is precisely *one* cloud in the sky, not ten thousand overlapping, micro-indiscernible clouds.

How does the univocity thesis handle the Problem of the Many?

If existence is univocal, then every well-formed aggregate of matter that satisfies our predicates either *exists* or *does not exist*. But if we say that all $n$ aggregates exist, we are forced into an ontological explosion: we must accept that every ordinary macroscopic object is accompanied by a swarm of near-duplicates, multiplying entities beyond necessity and violating our ordinary discourse. If we say that none of them uniquely exists, or that "cloud" fails to denote, we plunge into error theory or eliminativism.

Various solutions have been proposed within a univocal framework—such as supervaluationism, epistemicism, or brute-fact composition. But each solution strains our understanding of what it means for an object to "exist." 

When we introduce abstract entities into the mix, the Problem of the Many takes on an even more acute form. Consider properties, propositions, or sets. Are there distinct properties corresponding to "is red" and "is colored and red"? Are there distinct sets generated by iterative set-theoretical axioms that crowd our ontological landscape with redundant entities?

Crucially, the Problem of the Many reveals that our criteria for existence are profoundly sensitive to *category-specific constraints*. The criteria we use to count and individuate physical clouds (spatiotemporal boundaries, causal cohesion) are radically different from the criteria we use to count and individuate abstract numbers (axiomatic generation, logical consistency). 

When we attempt to force both domains under a single univocal quantifier, we encounter paradoxes not because reality is simple, but because the grammar of "existence" is being stretched across fundamentally incommensurable ontological domains. The paradoxes of vagueness do not merely show that our language is imprecise; they suggest that the very concept of an "object" and its "existence" shifts its meaning depending on whether we are navigating the messy, fuzzy world of physical space or the pristine, sharp boundaries of abstract relational structures.

### VII. Synthesis: Toward an Explanatory Pluralism of Being

Having traversed Carnap’s frameworks, van Inwagen’s univocity, spatialized intuitions, Quinean commitment, and the Problem of the Many, where does this leave us? Is existence univocal, or is it riven by an irreducible duality between concrete and abstract entities?

To resolve this, we must distinguish between two levels of inquiry: *syntactic-logical univocity* and *metaphysical-criteria pluralism*.

At the level of formal logic and semantics, **existence is and must remain univocal**. If we abandon the univocity of the existential quantifier, we forfeit the power of deductive reasoning across mixed domains. We lose the ability to state cleanly that "There are prime numbers, and there are physical planets, and some planets orbit stars." A fragmented logic is no logic at all; it is a tower of Babel where reasoning grinds to a halt at every categorial boundary. In this narrow, deflationary sense, Quine and van Inwagen are correct: to exist is to be values of bound variables within a unified logical framework.

However—and this is the crucial pivot—**acknowledging syntactic univocity does not entail endorsing ontological homogeneity.** 

To say that the existential quantifier ranges uniformly over both numbers and electrons is to make a formal claim about our language and logic. It does *not* mean that numbers and electrons share a common nature, mode of being, or set of existence criteria. 

Concrete entities and abstract entities *do* require distinct existence criteria anchored in the concept of mind-independence, causal efficacy, and spatiotemporal localization. 

*   For **concrete entities**, existence is indexed to the causal nexus, spatiotemporal coordinates, and empirical resistance. A concrete entity exists if and only if it occupies spacetime and participates in causal interactions that can constrain our empirical theories.
*   For **abstract entities**, existence is indexed to structural necessity, axiomatic coherence, and explanatory indispensability within formal or conceptual frameworks. An abstract entity exists if and only if it is an inescapable node in the relational architecture of our best systematic theories.

To conflate these distinct criteria—to demand that numbers possess mass, or that electrons exist necessarily outside of spacetime—is to commit a category mistake of the highest order. But to conclude from this difference that "existence" has multiple semantic meanings is to confuse the *criteria* for an entity’s instantiation with the *logical operator* through which we state that it is instantiated.

Carnap was right to insist that existence questions are framework-dependent, even if he went too far in dismissing external questions as mere linguistic preferences. Our frameworks are constrained by what the world forces upon us (in the concrete domain) and what our rational structures demand of us (in the abstract domain). 

Van Inwagen and Quine are right to insist on the formal univocity of the quantifier, even if their deflationary approach sometimes sanitizes the profound metaphysical differences between the types of things we quantify over.

---

### Conclusion

Existence is syntactically univocal, but ontologically pluralistic. 

The claim that existence is not univocal captures a deep and legitimate intuition: that a number and a star do not exist in the same *way*, understood in terms of their grounding, their localization, and their interaction with the mind and the cosmos. Abstract entities and concrete entities do indeed demand entirely distinct criteria of existence, rooted in the divergence between relational structural necessity and spatiotemporal causal efficacy.

Yet, recognizing this divergence does not require us to shatter the logical meaning of the existential quantifier into a swarm of equivocal homonyms. We can maintain a unified, Quinean logical syntax while embracing a robustly stratified metaphysics. By driving a wedge between the *formal grammar of quantification* and the *metaphysical criteria of instantiation*, we preserve the rigor of deductive logic without erasing the rich, heterogeneous tapestry of being. In the end, the universe—both physical and abstract—is complex enough to require a single logical voice to state what there is, and an infinitely varied ontological chorus to explain what it is that we have named.
