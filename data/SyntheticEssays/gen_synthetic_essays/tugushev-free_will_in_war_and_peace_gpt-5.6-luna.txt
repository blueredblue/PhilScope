# Freedom After the Rejection of Totality: Tolstoy, Tugushev, and the Problem of Determinism

Leo Tolstoy’s reflections on free will and historical necessity in *War and Peace* are often treated as an uneasy mixture of literary intuition, metaphysical speculation, and proto-scientific determinism. In the epilogue, Tolstoy argues that the apparent freedom of human action becomes increasingly difficult to defend as one considers the innumerable causes that condition every individual decision. A person experiences himself as free because he is conscious of his own activity from within, but an observer who considers the same activity in relation to heredity, social circumstance, bodily condition, historical forces, and preceding events sees it as necessary. Tolstoy’s most powerful move is to extend this contrast from individual psychology to the totality of history. If it were possible to know every circumstance relevant to an action, then the action would be predictable; and if it were predictable, it would not be free in the ordinary sense.

Nikolay Tugushev’s critique challenges this argument by questioning one of its most important but least examined assumptions: the presupposition that being forms a complete totality within which every event can, at least in principle, be placed and explained. If one rejects this metaphysical totality, Tugushev suggests, Tolstoy’s reductio does not compel us to accept determinism. The human act may not be exhaustively contained within a closed system of antecedent causes. Yet this proposal immediately encounters a serious difficulty. If the rejection of totality merely introduces an unexplained gap into the causal order, then it seems to preserve free will only by transforming it into an arbitrary, spontaneous force that arises from nothingness. A decision that is not determined by prior conditions is not automatically a free decision. It may be random, accidental, or unintelligible.

The central question, therefore, is whether Tugushev’s criticism successfully circumvents Tolstoy’s deterministic conclusion without reducing freedom to an ex nihilo spontaneity. The answer is qualified. Tugushev is right to expose a metaphysical assumption in Tolstoy’s argument and to show that the determinist conclusion does not follow merely from the existence of causes. He is also right that the totality of being cannot simply be treated as an innocent methodological premise. But rejecting totality, by itself, is insufficient. It blocks one version of Tolstoy’s reductio without yet providing an account of agency. To become philosophically persuasive, the critique must be supplemented by a positive conception of freedom in which human action is neither uncaused nor mechanically necessitated: an account of freedom as self-formation, rational responsiveness, and participation in the production of reasons. On such a view, the agent does not create himself or his action out of nothing. Rather, the agent acts through a historically formed, embodied, and socially situated identity that is nevertheless capable of reflection, revision, and genuine origination.

## I. Tolstoy’s Argument from the Individual to History

Tolstoy’s treatment of free will in *War and Peace* begins from a distinction between two standpoints. From the first-person standpoint, a person experiences himself as free. He deliberates, chooses, hesitates, and acts. From the standpoint of an external observer, however, the same person appears to be situated within a network of causes. His character has been shaped by education and habit; his desires reflect bodily and social conditions; his opportunities are structured by institutions; and the consequences of his action depend on an enormous range of circumstances outside his control.

Tolstoy does not simply claim that human beings are influenced by causes. That claim is trivial. The more radical claim is that all human actions are necessary when considered in relation to the totality of their causes. The individual’s sense of freedom arises because he does not know all of the conditions that determine him. Freedom is therefore associated with an internal limitation of knowledge. Necessity, by contrast, appears from the perspective of universal explanation.

This argument has an intuitive force. Suppose one knew a person’s complete psychological history, genetic inheritance, bodily state, social environment, and all relevant features of the surrounding world. If one also possessed laws sufficient to connect those conditions with subsequent behavior, then one could predict what the person would do. The person might still feel free, but that feeling would be comparable to an illusion generated by ignorance. His action would be the inevitable result of preceding conditions.

Tolstoy extends this logic to history. Historians commonly attribute great events to individuals: Napoleon, Alexander, Kutuzov, or some other leader appears to make a decisive choice. Yet the historical process is not generated by the intentions of famous individuals alone. Armies, institutions, economic pressures, popular expectations, communications, weather, geography, and countless small decisions converge in ways no individual controls. The more historians seek to explain events comprehensively, the less plausible it becomes to regard any individual as the absolute author of historical outcomes. Human beings believe they are directing history, while in fact they participate in processes whose complete causal structure exceeds their consciousness.

Tolstoy’s argument is thus a kind of reductio. He begins with the ordinary belief that individuals are free. He then asks what that belief would entail if taken seriously. Would each act have to be independent of all preceding conditions? If so, the act would have no intelligible relation to the person’s character, motives, reasons, or circumstances. But if the act is related to these things, then it is conditioned by them. The more fully we explain it, the less free it seems. Conversely, if an act is not explainable through prior conditions, then it appears arbitrary rather than free. Tolstoy concludes that ordinary free will is conceptually unstable. It cannot survive both demands for causal intelligibility and independence from necessity.

The force of this reductio depends upon a crucial conception of reality. Tolstoy’s argument assumes that the causal conditions of an action belong to a single comprehensive order. The action may be extraordinarily complex, and the causes may be practically unknowable, but they are nevertheless part of one totality of being. Nothing genuinely new can enter the order from outside. If the whole of reality is closed and every event is fixed by what precedes it, then freedom is at best an appearance produced by partial knowledge.

This totality is not merely the sum of all known empirical causes. It is a metaphysical totality: the assumption that there is no ontological remainder beyond the system of conditions that constitutes the world. Tolstoy’s historical determinism therefore rests upon more than the observation that people are constrained. It rests upon the belief that reality is exhaustively structured by necessity and that human action is one event within this complete structure.

## II. Tugushev’s Challenge to the Metaphysics of Closure

Tugushev’s criticism is directed at precisely this point. The assumption that being forms a complete, closed totality cannot simply be inferred from the fact that events have causes. Nor can it be established merely by imagining an ideally omniscient observer. The possibility of a complete description is not the same as the existence of a complete causal determination.

The distinction is important. Tolstoy moves from the claim that every action can be related to conditions to the stronger claim that every action is fixed by those conditions. He also moves from the fact that an action belongs to the world to the conclusion that it is exhaustively contained within a totality of antecedent causes. Tugushev’s objection is that these transitions smuggle in a metaphysical picture of reality as closed, finished, and fully self-identical. If reality is not a completed totality, then the future may not be reducible to a determinate rearrangement of what already exists.

This need not mean that the world contains supernatural interruptions or violations of natural regularity. The rejection of totality can instead mean that being is open-ended, internally productive, and capable of generating genuinely new structures. On such a view, the future is not already present as a hidden object waiting to be disclosed by a sufficiently powerful observer. It is produced through processes of development, interaction, and decision. Human action belongs to this productive process rather than standing outside nature.

Tugushev’s point can be formulated in several related ways.

First, the totality of being may be epistemically inaccessible. We cannot assume that because an action has conditions, those conditions form a complete and determinately knowable system. The inability to predict an action would then not merely reflect human ignorance. It might reflect an openness in reality itself.

Second, the totality may be ontologically incomplete. Reality may not be a finished whole but a process in which possibilities become actual through the activity of beings. If so, the future is not simply determined by the past, even though it is constrained by the past.

Third, the human agent may not be merely one item within the causal order. A person is a center of organization, interpretation, and evaluation. The agent receives conditions from the world but also transforms them by forming intentions, adopting principles, and selecting among possibilities. The action is neither independent of the world nor reducible to a linear chain of external causes.

These possibilities expose a weakness in Tolstoy’s reasoning. His argument appears to establish determinism only if all forms of causation are understood as efficient causation operating from prior states of affairs to later states of affairs. But human agency may involve a different kind of causality. A person acts for reasons. The reason is not necessarily a prior event that pushes the agent into action. It is a normatively intelligible consideration that the agent recognizes as counting in favor of an action. To say that an action was caused by a reason is not the same as saying that the action was mechanically forced by a prior state.

Tolstoy might respond that the person’s recognition of a reason is itself caused by prior conditions. The person’s values, desires, beliefs, and deliberative habits have histories. But this response does not settle the matter. It shows that agency is conditioned, not necessarily that it is absent. The determinist must establish that being conditioned by reasons is equivalent to being determined in the sense relevant to the denial of freedom. That equivalence is not self-evident.

## III. Is Tolstoy’s Reductio Defeated?

Tugushev’s rejection of totality does undermine Tolstoy’s reductio in one specific sense. Tolstoy’s conclusion is not forced upon us if we deny that the causal order is closed and exhaustive. If the future is genuinely open, then the fact that an action has antecedent conditions does not entail that only one action was possible. A person may deliberate among alternatives whose realization depends partly upon his own activity. The act of deliberation is not merely a passive registration of a pre-existing outcome; it contributes to determining which possibility becomes actual.

This point resembles the distinction between determinism and causal explanation. Determinism says that a complete state of the world at one time, together with the laws of nature, entails one and only one later state. Causal explanation is weaker. It says that events arise in orderly dependence upon prior conditions. A world may contain causal regularities without being deterministic in the strict sense. It may also contain indeterminacy without containing freedom. The rejection of totality therefore creates logical space for freedom, but it does not establish freedom.

This qualification is essential. Suppose an agent’s decision is not determined by prior conditions because some quantum fluctuation, unconscious impulse, or ontologically unconditioned event intervenes. The decision would then escape Tolstoyan necessity, but it would not thereby become free. If the agent had no control over the fluctuation or impulse, the event would be accidental. The choice would be undetermined but not self-determined.

Here lies the principal danger in a simple appeal to an open totality. One can avoid determinism by making human action inexplicable, but inexplicability is not agency. One can say that the act comes from beyond the totality of prior conditions, but unless one explains how the agent relates to this “beyond,” one has merely moved the problem. The mysterious source may be called freedom, but the word does not yet identify a capacity belonging to the person.

Tolstoy’s argument is strongest against a particular model of libertarian freedom: the idea that at the moment of choice the agent must be able to initiate an action independently of all causal and motivational conditions. If this is what freedom means, then the determinist can reasonably ask how such an action differs from a random occurrence. A choice wholly detached from the agent’s character, reasons, commitments, and situation would not express the agent. It would be no more genuinely hers than an involuntary spasm.

Tugushev need not accept this model. Indeed, his critique is most promising when it treats the rejection of totality not as a license for uncaused choice but as a rejection of the assumption that all causation must be external, linear, and completed in advance. The agent can be an origin without being an absolute beginning. A person can initiate action from within a causal history without being the cause of himself in the impossible sense of causa sui.

## IV. The False Alternatives of Necessity and Ex Nihilo Freedom

The debate often becomes trapped between two false alternatives. On one side stands mechanical determinism: every action is the inevitable effect of prior causes, and the conscious sense of choosing is merely an aftereffect. On the other side stands an absolute voluntarism: the free act must arise spontaneously from the agent without being determined by anything, including the agent’s own character and reasons.

The first view threatens to eliminate agency. The second threatens to make agency unintelligible. If a decision is wholly determined by antecedent conditions, then the agent appears to be a conduit through which forces operate. If it is wholly independent of antecedent conditions, then it appears to be a disconnected event. Neither model captures the structure of ordinary deliberation.

A more adequate conception begins by distinguishing causal determination from causal dependence. An action can depend upon an agent’s beliefs, values, and reasons without being necessitated by them in the manner of a physical mechanism. The agent’s reasons are not merely external inputs. They are incorporated into a self-reflective process through which the agent evaluates possibilities and commits herself to one. The action is caused by the agent because it flows from the agent’s practical identity, but that identity is not a fixed substance standing outside history. It is an evolving organization of dispositions, commitments, memories, and aspirations.

This model does not require the agent to create herself from nothing. No one chooses the conditions of their birth, language, body, family, historical period, or initial psychological formation. But freedom need not mean independence from all conditions. It may mean the capacity to take up conditions, interpret them, contest them, and transform their significance. The person does not choose the starting point of her life, but she can participate in determining what that starting point means for the continuation of her life.

Such participation is not a magical addition to the causal order. It is a distinctive form of causality. A human being can act upon a desire by asking whether the desire should be endorsed. He can treat a fear as a reason to withdraw or as a weakness to overcome. He can inherit a social norm and either reproduce it or reject it. These capacities are themselves historically formed, but their historical formation does not make their exercise unreal. Indeed, reflection is possible only because the agent is situated in a world of inherited meanings and practices.

The concept of self-causation should therefore be handled carefully. If it means that the self is the ultimate cause of its own existence, it is incoherent. No finite agent can be the complete cause of itself, because it would have to exist before it existed. If it means that the self can be the source of actions through its reasons, commitments, and deliberative activity, it is coherent and does not require ex nihilo creation. The agent is not self-created; she is self-directing within conditions she did not choose.

This distinction allows one to accept Tugushev’s rejection of totality without embracing metaphysical spontaneity. The openness of being need not be located in an inexplicable rupture. It can be located in the capacity of finite beings to reorganize inherited conditions through meaningful activity. The new does not arise from nothing. It arises from the transformation of what already exists.

## V. The Importance of Reasons

The most promising way to supplement Tugushev’s critique is through a theory of reasons. Tolstoy’s causal vocabulary tends to flatten the distinction between a reason and a cause. If an agent chooses to rescue someone because she judges the person to be in danger, the judgment is both causally relevant and normatively intelligible. It explains the action not merely by describing a preceding mental event but by showing what made the action seem worth doing.

This distinction matters for freedom. An action is free, in an important sense, when it is responsive to the agent’s reasons and when those reasons are integrated into the agent’s reflective commitments. Coercion, compulsion, manipulation, and addiction can interfere with this responsiveness. The issue is not whether the action has causes; it is whether the action expresses the agent’s evaluative standpoint.

Consider two people who perform the same act. One acts because she judges it right. The other acts because a gun is pointed at his head. Both actions have causes. Yet the first is more fully free because it issues from the agent’s own reasons, while the second is produced by an external threat that bypasses or overwhelms his practical judgment. A deterministic description of both cases might identify sufficient conditions for each action, but it would fail to capture the normative difference between them.

The compatibilist might argue that this is enough: freedom consists in acting according to one’s desires and reasons, regardless of whether those desires and reasons are determined. Tugushev’s critique, however, appears to seek something stronger than mere absence of external coercion. It seeks to preserve the sense that the agent genuinely contributes to what happens. For that purpose, a purely compatibilist account may be insufficient if it treats the agent’s reasons as ultimately passive products of prior forces.

A richer account can combine reasons-responsiveness with openness. The agent’s reasons do not mechanically determine action because their significance must be interpreted, weighed, and integrated. Deliberation is a process in which several possibilities remain practically live. The outcome is not fixed independently of the deliberation; it is settled through it. Yet the process is not random, because the agent’s judgment is guided by reasons, character, and commitments. The action is therefore both caused and authored.

This does not imply that, in precisely the same circumstances, the agent could have chosen otherwise in an absolute sense. The more modest and defensible claim is that the agent’s deliberation makes a difference to what occurs. If the agent had judged differently, valued differently, or attended to different reasons, the action would have been different. The counterfactual dependence of action upon rational activity is sufficient for a meaningful form of authorship, even if the activity itself has a history of causes.

Tugushev’s rejection of totality helps here because it prevents us from treating the entire deliberative process as a mere epiphenomenon of a pre-existing total state. It allows us to regard deliberation as a site where the future is formed. But the content and structure of deliberation must do the real philosophical work. Without them, openness is only indeterminacy.

## VI. Emergence, Temporality, and the Production of the New

Another way to develop Tugushev’s insight is through an emergent conception of agency. Human consciousness is not plausibly explained as a separate substance floating above the physical world. Nor is it adequately described as nothing more than a simple sum of neural events. Conscious agency emerges from biological, social, linguistic, and historical organization. The emergent property is dependent upon its conditions but not reducible to a description of those conditions at a lower level.

This kind of emergence does not violate causality. A living organism depends upon chemical processes, but biological explanations cannot be replaced entirely by chemical descriptions. A language depends upon individual sounds and physical signs, but the meaning of a sentence is not identical with its acoustic pattern. Similarly, practical agency depends upon neural and bodily processes, but an agent’s decision cannot be fully understood without reference to reasons, norms, and self-conceptions.

The totality presupposed by Tolstoy tends to treat lower-level causal explanation as if it exhausted higher-level agency. If every event has a physical antecedent, then the personal explanation is regarded as dispensable. But the existence of a lower-level explanation does not eliminate higher-level causation. A person’s decision can be explained simultaneously in neural, psychological, social, and normative terms. These explanations are not competitors. They identify different structures within the same event.

The emergence of agency also clarifies how novelty can enter the world without arising from nothing. A new plan, institution, work of art, or moral commitment emerges from prior materials. Shakespeare does not create language from nothing, but his use of language produces meanings that did not previously exist. A political movement does not emerge without historical conditions, but it can reconfigure those conditions and create a new field of possibilities. Likewise, a person’s decision is formed from inherited motives and circumstances, yet it can establish a new commitment that changes the practical meaning of subsequent events.

The temporal character of action is central. Determinism imagines the future as already fixed by the past, while agency is experienced as a process of bringing a possibility into actuality. This experience should not be accepted uncritically, but neither should it be dismissed as mere ignorance. In deliberation, the future is not present as a completed object. It exists as a field of possibilities evaluated from the standpoint of the agent. The agent’s commitment closes some possibilities and opens others. The decision is not an event inserted into time from outside; it is a temporal transition in which the agent’s relation to the future changes.

Tugushev’s rejection of totality is therefore most plausible as a rejection of a static ontology. If being is fundamentally processual, then an action can be conditioned by the past without being already contained in it as a finished fact. The agent’s freedom lies not in escaping time and causality but in participating in the temporal production of actuality.

## VII. Objections and Limits

There are, nevertheless, powerful objections to this interpretation. The first is that openness at the level of the world does not imply openness at the level of human choice. The universe might be indeterministic while every human decision is determined by psychological and social processes. Randomness in physics would not automatically yield freedom in ethics.

This objection is correct. Tugushev cannot infer human freedom from the mere failure of universal determinism. An indeterministic event becomes an exercise of agency only when it is organized by the agent’s capacities for judgment, intention, and self-regulation. The argument must therefore proceed through a theory of personal causation rather than through indeterminism alone.

A second objection is that an emergent agent may still be determined by the conditions at the emergent level. If an agent’s character and reasons are fixed by prior development, then the fact that the action is explained in personal terms does not show that it is free. The higher-level description may be useful without altering the metaphysical determination of the event.

This objection challenges strong claims about alternative possibilities, but it does not eliminate all forms of agency. The relevant question is whether agency requires absolute origination or whether it requires a structured capacity to respond to reasons, revise commitments, and act without coercion. If the latter, then higher-level determination does not necessarily negate freedom. Yet those who seek a more robust libertarian freedom will find the emergent account incomplete unless it can show how deliberation genuinely settles among open alternatives.

A third objection concerns responsibility. If a person’s character is shaped by forces she did not choose, why should she be praised or blamed? Tolstoy’s determinism has an ethically attractive side: it encourages compassion by revealing how deeply every person is conditioned. To condemn someone absolutely may be unjust when that person’s conduct reflects deprivation, trauma, social pressure, or inherited dispositions.

The rejection of totality should not lead to a simplistic restoration of moral blame. A non-deterministic account of agency must acknowledge degrees of freedom. Responsibility varies with self-knowledge, available alternatives, social conditions, coercion, and psychological capacity. The agent is not an unconditioned origin, but neither is she merely an object. Responsibility can be understood as answerability: the capacity to give reasons for one’s conduct and to respond to criticism by revising oneself. This capacity is compatible with profound dependence upon others and upon circumstances.

Finally, one might object that Tugushev’s notion of an incomplete totality is itself obscure. What would it mean for being not to be a totality? If “totality” means simply everything that exists, then rejection appears contradictory. If it means a closed system governed by complete antecedent determination, then the target is clearer, but the critique is no longer a rejection of totality in the broad metaphysical sense. It is a rejection of closure.

This terminological issue is significant. The philosophically useful claim is not that there is something outside all being. Such an outside could not be intelligibly described. The useful claim is that being is not a finished, self-enclosed system whose future is already fixed. The world can be total in the sense that nothing lies outside it while remaining open in the sense that its processes generate new forms and possibilities. The rejection of totality should therefore be reformulated as a rejection of completed totalization rather than a postulation of an external realm.

## VIII. Tolstoy Reconsidered

Tolstoy’s argument should not be dismissed simply because its metaphysical presupposition is contestable. His account identifies genuine features of human existence. We are not isolated sources of action. We are formed by history, language, institutions, bodies, and relationships. Great events cannot be adequately explained by individual intention alone. The belief that one is the sole author of one’s life is indeed an illusion if it means independence from all conditions.

Moreover, Tolstoy is right that a crude libertarian theory faces a dilemma. If freedom means that an act must be independent of reasons and causes, then the act threatens to become arbitrary. A decision that expresses nothing about the agent is not obviously more free than one determined by external forces. Tolstoy’s reductio therefore remains decisive against the fantasy of an absolutely self-grounding will.

What Tugushev exposes is not that Tolstoy has shown determinism to be false, but that Tolstoy has shown only that a certain definition of freedom is incompatible with a certain definition of totality. Once the closure of being is denied, the contradiction no longer follows. But the positive content of freedom must be supplied by an account of how agents act within an open world.

The best synthesis sees human beings as finite origins. They are origins because their practical activity makes a difference to what occurs. They are finite because they do not originate themselves, their world, or the conditions of their agency. Their freedom is therefore neither absolute independence nor random spontaneity. It is situated origination.

Situated origination includes at least four elements. First, the agent possesses a degree of reflective distance from immediate impulses. She can ask whether a desire should govern her. Second, she is responsive to reasons that are not reducible to coercive forces. Third, she can form and revise commitments that organize future conduct. Fourth, her activity can transform the social and material conditions from which it arose. None of these capacities requires an ex nihilo self. Each depends upon education, language, embodiment, and relationship. But dependence does not cancel agency when the dependent being actively reorganizes what it receives.

On this account, the openness of being is not a metaphysical gap through which an inexplicable will enters. It is the capacity of reality to produce forms capable of self-relation and self-transformation. The human being is one such form. The agent’s act is new because it is not merely a repetition of the past; it is not unconditioned because it arises through the agent’s concrete history. Freedom is located in the mediation between inheritance and transformation.

## Conclusion

Tugushev’s critique of Tolstoy succeeds in identifying a vulnerable point in the deterministic argument of *War and Peace*. Tolstoy’s conclusion depends not only on the existence of causes but on the assumption that all causes belong to a completed and closed totality of being. If reality is open, processual, and capable of generating genuinely new determinations, then Tolstoy’s passage from causal conditioning to absolute necessity is not compulsory. Human deliberation may be a real contribution to the formation of the future rather than a merely subjective accompaniment to an already fixed sequence.

Yet this critique is not sufficient on its own. The rejection of totality can easily collapse into a disguised appeal to randomness. An act that simply erupts from beyond the causal order is not thereby an act of freedom. It is free only if it belongs to the agent as an intelligible exercise of judgment, commitment, and self-direction. Freedom cannot mean that the agent is the cause of herself in an absolute sense. Nor can it mean that her choices are disconnected from her character, reasons, or history.

The most defensible position lies between Tolstoyan determinism and ex nihilo voluntarism. Human action is conditioned but not necessarily necessitated; caused but not merely pushed; historically formed but capable of historical transformation. The agent does not stand outside being or create herself from nothing. She is a finite center within being through which possibilities are interpreted, evaluated, and made actual. Her freedom consists in participating in the formation of what she becomes and what the world becomes through her action.

Thus, rejecting Tolstoy’s metaphysical presupposition successfully circumvents his deterministic conclusion only when the rejection of closure is joined to a substantive account of agency. Tugushev opens the door, but he does not by himself explain what lies beyond it. The philosophical task is to show that the alternative to necessity is not arbitrariness, and that the alternative to self-causation from nothingness is a situated, reason-responsive power of self-formation. Only then can freedom be preserved without denying the depth of our dependence upon the world and the history that make us possible.
