**The Argument from Queerness: An Evaluation of Mackie’s Challenge to Moral Realism**

The question of whether objective moral values exist has long been a central preoccupation of philosophy. For the moral realist, moral facts are as objective and mind-independent as the physical facts studied by the natural sciences. For the anti-realist, however, such a claim is not merely false but metaphysically absurd. The most formidable articulation of the anti-realist position is J.L. Mackie’s argument from queerness, presented in his seminal 1977 work, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*. Mackie contends that objective moral values would be entities of a "very strange sort," utterly unlike anything else in the universe, and that our supposed knowledge of them would require a faculty of perception entirely alien to our ordinary cognitive faculties. By labeling objective moral values as metaphysically and epistemically queer, Mackie aims to dismantle moral realism and establish his error theory—the view that all moral statements are false because they presuppose the existence of objective values that do not exist.

However, an evaluation of Mackie’s argument reveals a critical flaw at its foundation: the presumption against queerness rests on an unwarranted standard of plausibility. Dismissing properties simply on the grounds of strangeness is a heuristic, not a logical proof. When we analyze the parallel Mackie draws between the alleged strangeness of moral properties and scientifically accepted phenomena, we find that the argument fails to successfully eliminate the possibility of objective moral facts. Instead, the argument from queerness exposes the limits of naturalistic metaphysical frameworks rather than the limits of moral reality itself.

**I. The Anatomy of Mackie’s Argument from Queerness**

Mackie’s argument operates on two distinct but complementary fronts: metaphysical queerness and epistemic queerness. Together, they form a pincer movement designed to make the acceptance of objective moral values rationally untenable.

The argument from metaphysical queerness begins with the observation that moral values, if objective, would be entities of a very strange sort. Mackie argues that for moral values to be objective, they must be non-natural. They cannot be reducible to natural properties like pleasure, evolutionary fitness, or social cohesion, because such reductions inevitably fail to capture the prescriptive, "ought"-engendering nature of moral claims. Yet, non-natural entities are radically unlike anything else in our experience. They are not spatial, temporal, or causal. They do not interact with the physical world in the way that physical objects do. Mackie finds this deeply problematic, particularly when coupled with the Humean theory of motivation, which posits that beliefs alone cannot motivate action; only desires can. If moral values are objective, non-natural entities, how do they motivate moral action? Mackie argues that they cannot, because non-natural entities cannot enter into causal relations with human beings. Thus, objective moral values are metaphysically queer: they are entities of a kind utterly different from anything else in the universe, yet they are supposed to have a direct, motivating influence on human behavior.

The argument from epistemic queerness follows naturally from this metaphysical strangeness. If moral values exist as non-natural, non-causal entities, how could we possibly come to know them? Our ordinary knowledge of the world is mediated by causal interaction. We perceive a tree because light reflects off it and strikes our eyes; we perceive heat because molecules collide with our skin. If moral facts are non-natural and non-causal, they cannot enter into the causal chain that leads to our beliefs. Therefore, if we were to know objective moral facts, we would require a special, non-causal faculty of perception—a "moral sense"—entirely unlike any of our ordinary cognitive faculties. Mackie argues that this is an incredibly implausible requirement. Our moral beliefs are clearly influenced by our emotions, cultural upbringing, and evolutionary history, which are all causal, natural processes. The idea that we possess a separate, non-causal faculty for detecting non-natural moral truths is epistemically queer. It posits a radical disjunction between how we know the moral world and how we know the physical world, a disjunction for which there is no independent evidence.

Mackie concludes that because objective moral values are both metaphysically and epistemically queer, the rational presumption is against their existence. He argues that it is far more plausible to accept that there are no objective moral values—only subjective attitudes and culturally constructed norms—than to accept the existence of such strange entities and faculties. This leads him to his error theory: moral discourse is systematically mistaken because it presupposes objective values that do not exist.

**II. The Unwarranted Standard of Plausibility**

While Mackie’s argument is elegantly constructed, its fundamental weakness lies in its reliance on an unwarranted standard of plausibility. By labeling objective moral values as "queer," Mackie is not demonstrating that they are impossible; he is merely asserting that they are unfamiliar and counterintuitive to our naturalistic preconceptions. Dismissing properties simply on the grounds of strangeness is a fallacy of irrelevance. The plausibility of a metaphysical posits cannot be judged solely by its conformity to our existing intuitive frameworks, especially when those frameworks are themselves contested and historically contingent.

Mackie’s argument implicitly assumes a prior commitment to a specific metaphysical paradigm—varieties of naturalism or physicalism—within which non-natural entities are inherently implausible. However, this assumption is precisely what the moral realist disputes. The realist is not obligated to accept the naturalist's definition of what counts as plausible. If the universe contains non-natural facts, then non-natural entities are simply the furniture of that universe, not inherently "queer." The accusation of queerness is therefore circular: it assumes the very framework (naturalism) that the moral realist rejects. 

Furthermore, the standard of plausibility Mackie employs is historically parochial. What counts as "strange" or "queer" is a function of the prevailing scientific and philosophical paradigm at a given time. Entities that were once considered metaphysically absurd—such as atoms, electromagnetic fields, or curved spacetime—were eventually accepted by science once the explanatory and predictive evidence demanded it. The strangeness of an entity is not a reliable indicator of its non-existence; it is often merely an indicator of the limits of our current conceptual resources. By dismissing objective moral values on the grounds of their strangeness, Mackie imposes an anachronistic and dogmatic standard of plausibility that privileges the familiar over the potentially real.

**III. The Parallel with Scientifically Accepted Phenomena**

Mackie draws a parallel between the alleged strangeness of moral properties and scientifically accepted phenomena, arguing that while science has embraced certain queer entities (like fields or particles), it has done so only because they are causally efficacious and empirically verifiable. Objective moral values, by contrast, are allegedly non-causal and non-empirical, and thus cannot be accommodated within a scientific worldview. It is on this basis that Mackie claims science provides the ultimate standard of plausibility, and moral realism fails to meet it.

However, a critical analysis of this parallel reveals that it actually undermines Mackie's position rather than strengthening it. The parallel between the strangeness of moral properties and scientifically accepted phenomena demonstrates that queerness is not a disqualifying feature of a belief system; rather, it is often a precursor to paradigm shifts. 

Consider the history of electromagnetism. James Clerk Maxwell’s equations predicted the existence of electromagnetic fields—entities that were not composed of matter, were not spatially located in the way objects are, and could transmit energy across a vacuum. To a mechanistic physicist of the seventeenth century, the idea of an invisible, immaterial field permeating all of space would have been profoundly "queer." It would have violated the deeply held assumption that all causal interactions required direct physical contact. Yet, the scientific community ultimately accepted fields because they provided the best explanation for observable phenomena. The strangeness of the entity did not prevent its acceptance; the explanatory power of the theory did. 

Similarly, quantum mechanics introduced entities like superposition and entanglement that defy classical intuition and appear "queer" to our macroscopic, everyday experience. If we had applied Mackie's presumption against queerness to the history of physics, we would have rejected the very foundations of modern science. We would have said that invisible, non-mechanical, non-contact fields are too strange to be believed, and that the universe must operate strictly according to billiard-ball mechanics. 

Mackie’s parallel attempts to use science as a cudgel to beat moral realism, but it inadvertently highlights the fact that science itself is no stranger to ontological queerness. The presumption against queerness is not an absolute rule within science, but a methodological heuristic used to avoid ad hoc hypotheses. When a theory predicts a strange entity and that entity successfully explains empirical data, science accepts the strangeness. By failing to apply this same openness to moral philosophy, Mackie reveals that his standard of plausibility is not genuinely scientific but rather dogmatically naturalistic. He demands that moral facts conform to the current scientific paradigm, but he cannot explain why the paradigm itself is immune to revision.

**IV. Resolving the Presumption Against Queerness**

If the presumption against queerness is merely a reflection of our current metaphysical biases rather than a logical constraint on reality, then it cannot successfully eliminate the possibility of objective moral facts. The resolution of this presumption requires recognizing that the "queerness" of moral properties is a function of the framework used to describe them, not an inherent feature of the properties themselves.

First, Mackie’s argument from metaphysical queerness rests on a false dichotomy between naturalism and non-naturalism. Mackie assumes that if moral values are objective, they must be non-natural entities, and if they are non-natural, they are metaphysically queer. However, this ignores the robust tradition of naturalized moral realism. Aristotle, for instance, argued that moral virtues are natural properties of human beings, grounded in our biological and social nature. Contemporary neo-Aristotelian philosophers like Philippa Foot and Alasdair MacIntyre argue that moral facts are entirely natural facts—facts about human flourishing, health, and the proper functioning of a species. On this view, moral properties are no more metaphysically queer than the properties of a healthy heart or a balanced ecosystem. They are as natural and empirically accessible as any other biological or psychological trait. By dismissing objective moral values as queer, Mackie is actually dismissing a specific, narrow form of non-natural moral realism, not moral realism as a whole.

Second, the epistemic queerness of moral properties is not unique to moral realism; it is shared by other widely accepted metaphysical doctrines, most notably mathematical Platonism. Mathematical Platonists hold that numbers, sets, and functions are objective, abstract entities that exist outside of space and time and have no causal powers. How do we know them? We do not perceive them causally; we apprehend them through rational intuition. If Mackie's standard of epistemic plausibility were applied consistently, it would force us to reject mathematical realism as equally "queer" and irrational. Yet, few philosophers are willing to deny the existence of mathematical objects or the objective truth of mathematical statements. The fact that we accept the epistemic queerness of mathematics while rejecting the epistemic queerness of morals suggests that our resistance to moral realism is not based on a principled epistemic standard, but rather on a deep-seated prejudice against the prescriptive nature of moral discourse.

Third, the moral realist can respond to the Humean challenge—the claim that non-causal entities cannot motivate action—by rejecting the Humean theory of motivation itself. If moral facts are objective, they might constitute the very standards by which we evaluate our desires. On this view, moral facts do not need to cause motivation in the same way a physical object causes a billiard ball to move; rather, moral facts provide the rational basis for motivation. When we recognize that murder is wrong, we do not need a causal push from a non-natural entity to refrain from murder; the recognition of the wrongness itself is sufficient to motivate us, provided we are rational. The apparent queerness of moral motivation dissolves if we abandon the narrow, causal conception of motivation that Mackie imports from Humean psychology.

**V. Conclusion**

J.L. Mackie’s argument from queerness remains one of the most influential challenges to moral realism. By highlighting the metaphysical and epistemic strangeness of objective moral values, he forces the realist to provide a robust account of how non-natural entities can exist and be known. However, the argument ultimately fails to provide a sound basis for rejecting moral realism. It rests on an unwarranted standard of plausibility that conflates unfamiliarity with impossibility and that illegitimately privileges naturalism as the default metaphysical framework.

The parallel drawn between the strangeness of moral properties and scientifically accepted phenomena reveals that queerness is not a reliable indicator of falsity. Science has historically embraced the metaphysically queer when it provided the best explanation for the phenomena, demonstrating that our standards of plausibility must be flexible enough to accommodate reality, however strange it may be. The presumption against queerness does not successfully eliminate the possibility of objective moral facts; it merely eliminates a narrow, caricatured version of moral realism that is overly dependent on a dualistic metaphysics of natural and non-natural kinds.

Objective moral values may indeed be metaphysically and epistemically demanding. They may require us to expand our conception of reality beyond the causally closed physical world, or to accept that our moral cognition involves a rational intuition rather than a sensory perception. But demandingness is not the same as impossibility. The argument from queerness is a powerful rhetorical tool, but it is not a logical proof. It exposes the strangeness of our current metaphysical commitments, but it fails to prove that the universe itself is not stranger still. As long as the possibility of objective moral facts remains coherently explicable—whether through naturalized Aristotelianism, rational intuitionism, or a broader conception of causality—the presumption against queerness cannot serve as the final word against moral realism.
