# Noema, Text, and the Possibility of Non-Arbitrary Interpretation

## Introduction

The problem of textual interpretation appears, at first glance, to oscillate between two equally unsatisfactory extremes. On one side stands the idea that meaning is located entirely within the text. According to this view, the text possesses a determinate meaning independently of every act of reading, and interpretation is successful insofar as it reproduces or discovers that meaning. On the other side stands the idea that meaning is produced wholly by readers. If interpretation consists only in the projection of subjective interests, historical situations, or psychological responses onto an otherwise indeterminate textual object, then there seems to be no principled way to distinguish a defensible interpretation from an arbitrary one. The text becomes merely an occasion for meaning rather than a constraint upon meaning.

Edmund Husserl’s phenomenology offers a way of reformulating this opposition. His concept of the *noema* makes it possible to understand meaning neither as a self-subsisting property hidden inside an object nor as an invention imposed upon an inert material. Meaning arises within the intentional relation between consciousness and its object. Yet this claim does not entail that every interpretation is equally legitimate. Intentionality is not an unstructured act of subjective creation. It is a directed, organized, and normatively assessable relation in which an object is apprehended under a sense, through a determinate mode of givenness, and against a horizon of possible fulfillment. Applied to textual interpretation, this structure permits a theory of meaning that is historically and perspectivally open without collapsing into radical relativism.

The noema may be understood as the object as intended, or more precisely as the articulated sense through which an object is given in an intentional act. In the case of reading, the text is not simply a physical sequence of marks, nor is its meaning reducible to the reader’s private associations. It is encountered as an intentional object through a noematic structure comprising at least two interrelated dimensions. First, there is a shared or ideal core-stratum of *Sinn*: the intelligible content that makes the text this text and allows it to be recognized, discussed, cited, translated, criticized, and understood by different readers. Second, there are individualizing thetic characters of *Gegebenheitsweise*, or modes of givenness: the ways in which that content is posited, emphasized, anticipated, remembered, imagined, questioned, doubted, affirmed, or otherwise presented in a particular act of consciousness.

This distinction enables us to explain both stability and variation in interpretation. Different readers may grasp the same textual sense while encountering it through different modes of givenness. One reader may read a passage historically, another aesthetically, another suspiciously, and another devotionally. Their interpretations can differ without thereby concerning wholly different objects. At the same time, noematic analysis shows why differences cannot be unlimited. Interpretations remain answerable to the text insofar as the text functions as a structured intentional object with its own articulated sense, internal relations, horizon of implications, and possible modes of fulfillment. The noematic relationship therefore does not eliminate plurality; rather, it distinguishes legitimate plurality from arbitrary projection.

The central question is whether this phenomenological grounding is sufficient to prevent arbitrariness. Husserl’s framework is powerful, but it must be supplemented by attention to intersubjectivity, language, historical tradition, textual materiality, and interpretive norms. The noema does not provide an automatic algorithm for adjudicating every disagreement. It does, however, establish the ontological and epistemological conditions under which interpretation can be constrained without being reduced to the recovery of a single fixed intention. It makes room for an open-ended multiplicity of valid and potential interpretations while preserving the idea that some interpretations fail because they cannot be fulfilled by the text, cannot integrate its semantic structure, or depend upon distinctions that the text does not support.

## I. Intentionality and the Noematic Constitution of Meaning

Husserl’s analysis begins with intentionality, the claim that consciousness is always consciousness *of* something. Perceiving is perceiving an object; judging is judging a state of affairs; desiring is desiring something; remembering is remembering an event; imagining is imagining an object or scene. Consciousness is not a sealed interior container filled with representations. It is essentially directed beyond itself toward an intentional object.

This directedness must be distinguished from a merely causal relation. A physical object may cause a visual experience, but the object as intended is not identical with the causal stimulus. A reader may see ink marks on a page, but reading involves more than receiving optical impressions. The marks are apprehended as letters, words, sentences, arguments, images, commands, questions, or narrative events. They are organized within a meaningful act. The text is therefore not simply present as a physical thing; it is given as something that says, signifies, describes, invokes, or performs something.

Husserl’s noematic analysis addresses this “as” structure of intentionality. The noema is not a second object located inside consciousness. It is not an internal copy or mental picture standing between the mind and the world. Nor is it simply identical with the real object independently of its being intended. It is the object insofar as it is meant, presented, or constituted in a particular intentional act. When I perceive a tree, remember a tree, imagine a tree, and judge that the tree is diseased, the intentional object may remain “the same” in one sense, while the noematic sense and mode of givenness differ. The tree is perceived as bodily present, remembered as past, imagined as absent, or judged under a determinate predication.

The noema consequently possesses a complex structure. Husserl often distinguishes the noematic sense, or *Sinn*, from the object-pole toward which the sense is directed. The sense is what makes the object intelligible as the object it is intended to be. The object-pole provides a unity across changing appearances and acts. In textual interpretation, this distinction is crucial. The physical book, manuscript, inscription, or digital file is not identical with the text as meaningfully intended. Yet the text as intended is not an arbitrary construction. It has an identity that persists across acts of reading, quotation, translation, and criticism.

The distinction between noematic sense and mode of givenness can be stated as follows. A noema includes a content or core that answers the question, “What is intended?” It also includes a manner in which that content is given, answering the question, “How is it intended or presented?” The same proposition may be asserted, imagined, questioned, remembered, or doubted. “The city is burning” has a certain sense, but the noematic character differs when the statement is encountered as a historical report, a fictional sentence, a hypothesis, a warning, or an ironic remark. The core-stratum of sense is not wholly separable from its mode of givenness, because meaning is always meaning in a mode. Nevertheless, the distinction allows us to describe the identity of content across differing acts and the variation introduced by the reader’s situation and orientation.

In textual interpretation, the reader’s consciousness does not first encounter a meaningless object and then freely add significance. The text is already encountered within a historically sedimented linguistic and cultural field. Words have habitual meanings; grammatical forms establish expectations; genres provide interpretive anticipations; literary conventions guide the constitution of narrative, voice, metaphor, and argument. The reader’s act is therefore intentional, but it is not unconditioned. It operates through established signs, inherited linguistic capacities, and a horizon of relevance.

The noema is thus neither a subjective image nor an objective meaning existing independently of consciousness. It is the sense-bearing pole of an intentional relation. This middle position is what makes it philosophically relevant to hermeneutics. It avoids the naïve realism according to which the meaning of a text is simply a thing waiting to be extracted, while also avoiding the idealism according to which the reader fabricates meaning out of nothing.

## II. Textual Meaning and the Shared Core-Stratum of *Sinn*

A theory of interpretation requires some account of what remains identical across different readings. If every act of reading constituted a wholly new object, communication would be impossible. We could not say that two readers interpreted the same poem, argument, or legal provision. We could only say that they produced unrelated private experiences prompted by similar marks. Husserl’s notion of an ideal or shared sense offers a basis for preserving textual identity across acts.

The core-stratum of *Sinn* is not necessarily a fully determinate proposition that can be stated once and for all. It is better understood as the articulated semantic identity that makes the text recognizable as a meaningful unity. In a simple sentence, this may include lexical meanings, grammatical relations, and a referential or propositional structure. In a complex literary work, it includes patterns of imagery, narrative sequence, rhetorical form, thematic tensions, tonal possibilities, and relations among parts. The core is not a bare atom of meaning but a structured field.

This core possesses a kind of ideality. The sentence “The door is open” can be read by many people, at different times, in different circumstances, without becoming numerically different in each act. The sentence’s sense is repeatable. It can be cited, taught, translated, affirmed, denied, or placed within a larger argument. Its repeatability does not mean that every reader experiences it identically or that every context activates precisely the same implications. It means that the text has a communicable identity that exceeds any single subjective event.

The ideality of textual sense should not be confused with a Platonic meaning existing in a separate realm. Husserl’s point is phenomenological: meaning is available as the same meaning through potentially many acts. It is an ideal object in the sense that it can be reactivated and recognized across individual experiences. The identity of the sense is constituted and maintained through possible repetition, fulfillment, correction, and intersubjective confirmation.

This shared core-stratum explains why the text can constrain interpretation. An interpretation must be able to connect its claims to features of the text’s sense. It must be possible to show how its assertions arise from lexical, syntactic, rhetorical, generic, or thematic structures that are available in the textual object. An interpretation of a tragic drama that simply describes it as a recipe book would not be false merely because it differs from conventional readings; it would fail because the intentional object constituted through the text does not support that sense. The marks, words, organization, and genre do not offer the relevant modes of fulfillment.

Yet the core-stratum is not identical with a single exhaustive interpretation. The sense of a text can exceed what is thematically explicit. Texts have implications, presuppositions, ambiguities, and horizons. A poem may support different accounts of its tone without reducing those accounts to arbitrary inventions. A philosophical work may be read through different conceptual frameworks while retaining identifiable theses, distinctions, and argumentative pressures. A novel may generate ethical, political, psychoanalytic, or formal interpretations because its noematic structure contains multiple dimensions capable of being thematized.

The possibility of multiple interpretations therefore follows from the richness of textual sense, not from its absence. If a text were semantically empty, any interpretation would be possible, but none would be genuinely an interpretation. Interpretive plurality becomes meaningful only where there is a common object capable of being disclosed in more than one way.

## III. The Individualizing Characters of *Gegebenheitsweise*

If *Sinn* accounts for the repeatable identity of what is intended, *Gegebenheitsweise* accounts for the way in which it is given. This dimension is essential for understanding the role of the reader. The same text may be encountered as authoritative or dubious, intimate or alien, transparent or obscure, literal or ironic, historically distant or urgently contemporary. These are not merely emotional reactions added after comprehension. They can shape what aspects of the text become salient and what interpretive possibilities are opened.

Thetic character refers broadly to the position or positing involved in an intentional act. A text may be apprehended assertorically, as making a claim; hypothetically, as entertaining a possibility; interrogatively, as posing a question; normatively, as issuing a demand; fictively, as presenting an imagined world; or ironically, as saying one thing while intending another. A reader’s act may also suspend, modify, or contest the text’s apparent positing. A sentence within a novel is not necessarily treated as an assertion about the actual world. A rhetorical question is not necessarily taken as a request for information. A quotation may be understood as cited rather than endorsed.

The mode of givenness individualizes the noema because no reader encounters a text from nowhere. Reading occurs from a temporal, bodily, linguistic, cultural, and practical situation. The same passage may be given differently to someone who knows the historical context and someone who does not; to someone familiar with a genre and someone encountering it for the first time; to someone reading for legal interpretation and someone reading for aesthetic pleasure. This does not mean that the text becomes whatever the reader wants. It means that the text’s possibilities are disclosed selectively and perspectivally.

Husserl’s account of intentionality allows us to distinguish the text’s sense from its current appearance without separating them absolutely. Perception is always perspectival: an object is given through profiles, and no single profile exhausts it. Likewise, a text can be disclosed through different interpretive profiles. A political reading, a theological reading, and a formalist reading may foreground distinct features. Each can be legitimate if it remains directed toward the same textual object and if its claims can be fulfilled by the text’s structure.

The idea of perspectival givenness also explains why interpretation is open-ended. A text possesses an internal horizon of possibilities that may not be actualized in any one reading. New historical circumstances can make previously unnoticed dimensions salient. Concepts unavailable to earlier readers may reveal patterns of exclusion or domination. Subsequent readers may identify implications that were not explicit in the original reception of the work. The noematic object is not thereby replaced by a new object; rather, its horizon is more fully articulated.

This horizon is not infinite in the sense of permitting anything whatsoever. It is structured by the text’s own anticipations and by the possibilities of fulfillment that belong to it. A new interpretation is not justified simply because it is novel. It is justified when it discloses a possibility that can be integrated into the textual whole, confirmed through attentive reading, and sustained in relation to relevant evidence. The mode of givenness changes, but it must remain answerable to the core-stratum of sense.

## IV. Against Meaning Located Strictly Within the Text

The first extreme in textual theory is the claim that meaning is strictly inside the text. This position may take several forms. It might assert that the author’s intention fixes the text’s meaning; that the linguistic structure itself contains a determinate signification; or that interpretation should reproduce an objective semantic content without reference to the reader’s standpoint. What these views share is the effort to protect textual meaning from subjective interference.

Husserl’s phenomenology challenges this position by showing that meaning is not accessible apart from acts of consciousness. A text is meaningful only insofar as it can be taken up in intentional acts of reading, understanding, judging, imagining, or interpretation. There is no “meaning inside the text” in the sense of a self-interpreting entity. Physical marks do not mean by themselves. Their significance depends upon linguistic intentionality, habitual signification, and the possibility of being recognized and fulfilled by consciousness.

This does not imply that the text has no identity apart from a particular reader. The text’s ideal sense is not reducible to an individual psychological event. But its ideality is inseparable from the possibility of repeated intentional actualization. To say that meaning is noematic is precisely to say that it belongs to the object as intended, not to the object considered as a merely physical thing. Meaning is relational through and through.

The strict internalist view also tends to underestimate ambiguity and contextual dependence. Words can have several senses; sentences can be ironic; genres modify the force of statements; passages can acquire significance through their relation to other passages. Meaning is not always a fixed deposit that interpretation merely removes from the text. It is constituted through a play of anticipation and fulfillment. The reader anticipates a syntactic completion, a narrative development, or a rhetorical aim; subsequent textual features confirm, modify, or disappoint those anticipations.

Furthermore, a purely internal account cannot adequately explain why the same textual object can be understood differently without becoming meaningless. If the text contained a complete and self-sufficient meaning independent of all modes of givenness, then differences among interpretations would appear to be merely failures to reproduce it. But this would obscure the fact that interpretation can disclose genuine possibilities that belong to the text’s horizon but are not exhausted by a single formulation.

The noematic approach therefore rejects the idea of an entirely reader-independent textual meaning. Yet it does not abandon textual objectivity. It relocates objectivity from an imagined meaning hidden inside the text to the structured relation between the text and possible acts of fulfillment. The text is objective insofar as it offers stable and repeatable constraints to a community of interpreters. Its identity is not a private product, but neither is it a thing that exists as meaningful without any intentional relation.

## V. Against Meaning as Pure Subjective Projection

The opposite extreme is the view that readers create meaning entirely through their own acts. This position can be associated with certain forms of subjectivism, constructivism, or radical reader-response theory. If the text is treated as an empty signifier whose meaning is supplied by the reader, interpretation seems to become unlimited. Any response could be called an interpretation, and disagreement could be resolved only by power, preference, or rhetorical force.

Husserl’s account of intentionality resists this conclusion because intentional acts are not arbitrary acts of production. Consciousness is directed toward an object under a sense. The reader intends *this* text, not an indeterminate anything. The object pole imposes unity upon the manifold of experiences, while the sense specifies the kind of object toward which the act is directed. To interpret a text is to seek fulfillment in the text’s structures, not merely to attach one’s private associations to its physical presence.

The difference between interpretation and projection can be expressed in terms of fulfillment. An anticipatory interpretation projects possibilities, but these possibilities are tested against the text. Does the text support the proposed meaning through its language, context, form, and internal relations? Do further readings confirm the initial anticipation, or do they force revision? Can the interpretation explain passages that initially seemed resistant? Does it preserve distinctions that the text itself establishes? A projection that cannot undergo this process of confirmation and correction lacks phenomenological legitimacy.

Projection is not entirely avoidable. Every act of understanding begins from a horizon of prior experience. Readers bring expectations, concepts, memories, and interests. The task is not to purify interpretation of all presuppositions, which would be impossible, but to make them available for reflection and correction. A reader may initially understand a text through a familiar category and later discover that the text resists that category. In such a case, the noematic object exercises a corrective force. It compels the reader to alter the mode in which the text is given.

This corrective force is central to Husserl’s conception of evidence. Evidence is not infallible certainty in every context. It is the fulfillment of an intention in a manner that gives the object itself a role in confirming or disconfirming what was anticipated. In reading, evidence can take the form of textual coherence, semantic compatibility, argumentative necessity, narrative development, or convergence with independent historical information. The text cannot speak outside interpretation, but it can resist interpretations that fail to achieve fulfillment.

Thus, the reader is neither a passive receiver nor an unrestricted creator. The reader is an active participant in the constitution of meaning, but the activity is governed by an intentional object whose sense and horizon constrain what can be legitimately said.

## VI. How the Noematic Relation Constrains Interpretation

The noematic relation prevents arbitrary interpretation through several interconnected mechanisms.

First, there is semantic constraint. Words and grammatical structures establish a field of possible meanings. Although language is flexible and historically changing, not every use is equally available in every textual context. An interpretation that ignores ordinary lexical meaning must provide a reason for doing so, such as irony, metaphor, technical terminology, or deliberate defamiliarization.

Second, there is syntactic and propositional constraint. Sentences relate terms in determinate ways. An interpretation that reverses subject and object, confuses affirmation with negation, or treats a question as a straightforward assertion requires strong textual evidence. The text’s semantic core is structured, and interpretations must preserve enough of that structure to remain interpretations of the same object.

Third, there is generic and rhetorical constraint. A poem, legal statute, philosophical treatise, satire, and fictional narrative disclose their contents through different modes of givenness. Genre does not mechanically determine meaning, but it shapes expectations about reference, assertion, voice, and significance. To read a satirical statement as a direct endorsement may be possible in some cases, but it cannot be justified without considering the rhetorical structure that modifies the statement’s thetic character.

Fourth, there is holistic constraint. The meaning of a part is conditioned by its relation to the whole, while the whole is progressively revised through the interpretation of its parts. This is the phenomenological basis for a hermeneutic circle. A proposed interpretation must account for a range of textual features rather than selecting isolated phrases. An interpretation that explains one sentence by ignoring the rest of the work may be locally plausible but globally defective.

Fifth, there is horizon constraint. Every text opens a field of implicit references and possible developments. Words evoke related concepts; narratives establish expectations; arguments imply consequences; metaphors invite extensions. An interpretation can legitimately develop these implications, but it must remain connected to the horizon generated by the text. A claim that cannot be motivated by any textual anticipation or relation is not an interpretation of the text but a separate discourse prompted by it.

Sixth, there is intersubjective constraint. The noematic sense is not merely private because textual meaning is available to a plurality of subjects. A reading may be initially idiosyncratic, but it can be tested through communication. Can another competent reader follow the path from the textual object to the interpretation? Can the relevant evidence be displayed publicly? Can objections be answered? Intersubjectivity does not guarantee unanimity, but it transforms private conviction into an accountable claim.

Finally, there is the possibility of correction. Interpretive claims are not fixed by the first act in which they arise. They can be revised through rereading, comparison with other passages, attention to linguistic detail, historical research, and dialogue with alternative interpretations. The text’s resistance becomes visible over time. A theory that makes interpretation wholly subjective has difficulty explaining why readers experience surprise, error, or correction. Husserl’s intentionality explains these phenomena by treating consciousness as answerable to an object whose givenness can fulfill or disappoint its anticipations.

These constraints do not yield one final meaning in every case. They establish a space of admissible interpretation. Within that space, interpretations can differ in emphasis, scope, and explanatory purpose. The fact that the space is plural does not make it arbitrary.

## VII. Plurality Without Radical Relativism

The possibility of multiple valid interpretations is not an accidental complication; it follows from the structure of noematic givenness. Because a text can be presented through different profiles and horizons, no single reading necessarily exhausts its sense. The same work may disclose philosophical, ethical, political, psychological, historical, and formal dimensions. These interpretations can be mutually illuminating even when they cannot be reduced to one another.

Radical relativism, however, is the stronger claim that there is no criterion by which interpretations can be distinguished. If truth is entirely relative to the reader, then no interpretation can be criticized except from another subjective standpoint. Husserl’s framework rejects this by preserving the distinction between the object’s ideal sense and the individual mode in which it is given. The reader’s standpoint influences what is disclosed, but it does not determine without remainder what can be disclosed.

The core-stratum of *Sinn* provides continuity across perspectives. The individualizing character of *Gegebenheitsweise* explains variation. The relation between the two yields a model of situated objectivity. Every interpretation is situated because it occurs through a particular act, but it is objective to the extent that it can be referred back to the shared sense and validated through possible fulfillment.

This model resembles the difference between a horizon and an unlimited void. A horizon marks the boundary of what is currently visible, but it also implies further possibilities of movement and disclosure. A text’s horizon can expand as readers approach it from new situations. Yet the horizon belongs to the object’s structure; it is not generated from nothing by each interpreter. Interpretive history can therefore be progressive without being a simple accumulation of subjective reactions. Later readings may disclose possibilities that earlier readings overlooked, while still being answerable to the same textual object.

An open-ended multiplicity of interpretations does not mean that there are infinitely many equally valid interpretations. It means that the text’s sense can support more than one complete and coherent articulation, and that future acts may reveal aspects not yet thematized. Some readings are stronger than others because they achieve more comprehensive fulfillment, integrate more textual evidence, explain tensions rather than suppressing them, and remain more responsive to the text’s modes of givenness.

Plurality also exists at the level of interpretive purpose. A legal interpretation may seek normative applicability; a historical interpretation may reconstruct social context; a literary interpretation may analyze form and affect; a philosophical interpretation may trace conceptual implications. These purposes disclose different aspects of the noematic object. Their diversity does not necessarily indicate disagreement about the text’s core sense. It may instead reflect different legitimate acts directed toward the same object under different thematic interests.

Nevertheless, plurality can become conflictual. Interpretations may make incompatible claims about what the text affirms, denies, or presupposes. In such cases, the noematic model does not promise immediate resolution. It directs inquiry toward the structure of the acts involved: Are the interpreters positing the same object? Are they using the same textual unit? Are they assigning different thetic characters to the same sentence? Are their conflicts semantic, historical, rhetorical, or normative? Clarifying these differences can reveal that apparent disagreement concerns different layers of the noema. Where genuine conflict remains, interpretations can be compared according to evidential adequacy and fulfillment.

## VIII. The Role of Intersubjectivity and Historical Tradition

A possible objection is that Husserl’s emphasis on consciousness risks returning textual meaning to individual subjectivity. If the noema is constituted in an act of consciousness, why should the reader’s noema have any authority beyond that reader’s experience? The answer requires Husserl’s broader account of intersubjectivity and the lifeworld.

Meaning is not constituted by an isolated ego. Language is a social institution, and textual sense is sedimented through historical practices of communication. A reader inherits linguistic meanings, genres, interpretive conventions, and cultural distinctions. Even a highly original interpretation depends upon capacities that are socially acquired. The shared core-stratum of sense is therefore intersubjective: it is available to multiple subjects who can recognize and reidentify it.

Intersubjectivity does not mean that a community’s current consensus automatically determines truth. Communities can misunderstand texts, exclude perspectives, or stabilize ideological distortions. But communal validity provides a necessary dimension of objectivity. An interpretation must be capable of being taken up, examined, and either confirmed or criticized by others. It must enter a space of reasons rather than remain a private association.

Historical tradition plays a similar role. Texts are not encountered in a vacuum but within a sequence of prior interpretations. These traditions can obscure as well as reveal. A conventional interpretation may make certain features visible while rendering others invisible. A new reading may challenge inherited assumptions and disclose an overlooked horizon. Yet even the critical reading remains historically situated. It does not escape tradition; it enters into a reflective relation with it.

This historical dimension further complicates the notion of a fixed textual meaning. The sense of a text may remain identically available while its significance changes. “Meaning” here should be distinguished from “significance.” The core sense concerns what the text says or presents; significance concerns what that sense comes to mean for a particular historical community or reader. A text may acquire new ethical or political significance without its semantic identity being wholly transformed. Conversely, a shift in historical context may alter the mode of givenness through which its sense is apprehended.

The noematic framework can accommodate this distinction. The ideal sense remains the pole of reidentification, while the modes of givenness and horizons of relevance vary across historical acts. Interpretation is thus neither pure recovery nor pure invention. It is a continuing reactivation of an ideal object under changing conditions.

## IX. Objections to a Noematic Theory of Interpretation

Despite its strengths, a noematic theory faces several objections.

The first concerns the ambiguity of Husserl’s concept of the noema. Some interpreters treat the noema as an ideal sense distinct from the object; others understand it as the object as intended; still others emphasize its role as a mediating structure within consciousness. If the concept is too indeterminate, it may seem unable to support a precise theory of textual meaning.

This objection can be answered by recognizing that the noema is structurally complex rather than conceptually defective. The noema must account for both the identity of the object intended and the sense through which it is intended. In textual interpretation, this means distinguishing but not separating the text’s repeatable semantic core from its modes of presentation. The concept is useful precisely because it resists the reduction of meaning to either an object alone or a subjective experience alone.

A second objection is that the “shared core” may be so thin that it cannot constrain interpretation. If the core consists only in minimal lexical or grammatical identity, then radically different readings may remain possible. If it consists in a complete interpretation, then the theory simply presupposes what it aims to establish.

The appropriate response is to treat the core as structured but revisable. It is not a minimal residue, nor is it a final reading. It includes the textual relations that make interpretations answerable: semantic, syntactic, rhetorical, generic, and formal structures. These structures can be articulated differently, but they cannot be ignored without cost. The core functions as a regulative identity that readings seek to disclose more adequately.

A third objection is that readers often project meanings that are not consciously intended by the author or historically available in the original context. Can such meanings genuinely belong to the text? The noematic answer is that authorial intention is one important but not exhaustive dimension of textual sense. Once produced and circulated, a text can sustain implications beyond the author’s explicit awareness. However, the possibility of such implications must be demonstrated through the text’s language and structure. Unconscious or unintended meaning is not the same as unrestricted meaning.

A fourth objection concerns power. What counts as “fulfillment” may be determined by dominant interpretive communities. Appeals to shared sense can conceal social exclusion by treating established readings as objectively correct. This is a serious danger. Intersubjectivity is not equivalent to consensus, and phenomenological evidence must remain open to critique. The noematic theory should therefore distinguish between actual communal acceptance and the ideal possibility of rational accountability. A marginalized reading may be more adequate than a dominant one if it reveals textual structures that convention has ignored. The criterion is not conformity to prevailing opinion but responsiveness to the object under conditions of open examination.

A fifth objection is that texts, especially literary texts, may deliberately resist stable identity. Experimental writing, fragmentation, ambiguity, and undecidability can undermine the assumption that there is a coherent noematic core. Yet resistance to closure does not necessarily entail absence of sense. A text may have indeterminacy as part of its sense. Its core can include formal disruption, contradiction, or unresolved plurality. In such cases, the legitimate interpretation is not one that forces unity but one that accurately describes the structured failure of unity. The noematic framework can therefore preserve ambiguity without turning it into arbitrary meaning.

## X. Does the Noematic Relation Successfully Prevent Arbitrariness?

The decisive question is whether grounding interpretation in the noematic relationship between consciousness and text genuinely prevents arbitrary claims. The answer should be qualified. The noematic relation is a necessary condition for non-arbitrary interpretation, but it is not by itself a sufficient method for resolving every dispute.

It is necessary because it locates meaning in a relation governed by intentional structure. The reader’s act is directed toward an object that possesses an ideal identity, a sense, and a horizon of possible fulfillment. This rules out the idea that any response to a text automatically constitutes an interpretation. A personal memory triggered by a poem may be meaningful for the reader without being a claim about what the poem means. The distinction between response and interpretation is preserved by reference to the intentional object.

The noematic relation is also necessary because it explains how the text can resist the reader. The reader’s initial anticipation may be disappointed; a proposed interpretation may fail to account for a passage; a supposed metaphor may be contradicted by syntax or context. These experiences of resistance are not merely psychological frustrations. They indicate that the object has not fulfilled the intention in the anticipated way. Interpretation thus has a norm internal to intentionality itself.

However, the noematic relation is not sufficient if understood as a purely private correspondence between an individual consciousness and a text. A reader could insist that a private intuition is the text’s meaning. To prevent this, noematic analysis must be connected to linguistic competence, historical inquiry, intersubjective criticism, and explicit argumentative justification. The interpreter must make the path of disclosure communicable. The claim “this is what the text means” requires more than “this is how it appears to me.” It requires showing how the text can be given in that way and why the proposed mode of givenness is more adequate than alternatives.

The noematic model therefore secures a middle position. It rejects textual absolutism, because meaning is constituted through intentional acts and can be disclosed perspectivally. It rejects subjective relativism, because intentional acts are directed toward an object whose sense constrains and corrects them. It allows multiplicity because no object is exhausted by one profile. It prevents arbitrariness because not every profile belongs to the object’s horizon.

The success of this position depends on maintaining the distinction between “possible interpretation” and “valid interpretation.” A possible interpretation may be imaginatively conceivable or historically interesting. A valid interpretation must satisfy stronger conditions: it must be textually motivated, semantically coherent, attentive to the text’s thetic characters, compatible with its broader structure, open to intersubjective examination, and capable of surviving correction. Even then, validity may be graded rather than binary. Interpretations can be more or less adequate, illuminating, comprehensive, or persuasive.

This graded model is preferable to both absolutism and relativism. Absolute textual meaning would make interpretation a matter of duplication, while radical relativism would eliminate error. Phenomenological interpretation preserves error because the object can be misapprehended; it preserves discovery because the object can be disclosed anew; and it preserves plurality because different acts can fulfill different possibilities within the same horizon.

## XI. The Open-Endedness of Meaning

The noematic theory also clarifies why textual interpretation can remain open-ended without becoming indeterminate. Open-endedness arises from the inexhaustibility of intentional objects. A text can be reread under new questions, in new historical circumstances, and through new conceptual resources. Each reading may bring certain aspects into prominence while leaving others in the background. The text’s sense is not exhausted by the sum of already completed interpretations.

This inexhaustibility is not evidence that the text has no determinate identity. On the contrary, only a relatively stable object can support repeated disclosure. If each reading produced a wholly new text, there would be no reason to call rereading a return to the same work. The very possibility of discovering something new depends upon the persistence of something that can be discovered.

The open-ended horizon also permits interpretations that were not anticipated by the author or original audience. A text may contain structural possibilities that historical circumstances prevent from becoming visible. For example, later readers may identify gendered assumptions, colonial presuppositions, or ecological implications that were not thematized at the time of composition. Such interpretations need not be dismissed as anachronistic projections. They can be legitimate if they disclose consequences or structures genuinely operative in the text, even if those structures were not consciously recognized.

Still, historical difference imposes responsibilities. A contemporary reader should distinguish between claiming that a text explicitly meant a modern concept and claiming that the text can be illuminatingly interpreted through that concept. The first claim may be historically false; the second may be philosophically or ethically fruitful. Noematic analysis encourages this distinction by separating the text’s core sense from the reader’s individualizing mode of givenness and horizon of significance.

Open-endedness therefore concerns the future of possible fulfillment. The text can continue to generate interpretations because its noematic object has more possibilities than any single act actualizes. But those possibilities are not unlimited. They are bounded by the text’s semantic identity and relational structure.

## Conclusion

Husserl’s concept of the noema provides a compelling foundation for a theory of textual interpretation that avoids both objectivist rigidity and radical relativism. The noema identifies the text not as a meaningless physical artifact and not as a private mental representation, but as an intentional object constituted through a structured relation between consciousness and what is meant. Within this relation, the noema includes a shared core-stratum of *Sinn*, through which the text possesses a repeatable and intersubjectively available identity, and individualizing thetic characters of *Gegebenheitsweise*, through which that identity is presented under particular historical, rhetorical, affective, and interpretive conditions.

The distinction between sense and mode of givenness explains how the same text can support multiple readings without becoming a different object for every reader. It also explains why readers cannot simply impose any meaning they wish. The text is encountered through linguistic, syntactic, generic, rhetorical, and holistic structures that establish a horizon of possible fulfillment. Interpretations are non-arbitrary when they remain responsive to this horizon, when their claims can be motivated by the textual object, and when they are open to intersubjective testing and correction.

The noematic relation does not guarantee one final interpretation. Nor should it. Textual meaning is perspectival, historically situated, and inexhaustible. A text may disclose different dimensions to different readers and may acquire new significance under changing conditions. Such plurality is not a weakness of interpretation but a consequence of the richness of intentional objects. What phenomenology denies is not multiplicity but the claim that multiplicity eliminates all norms.
