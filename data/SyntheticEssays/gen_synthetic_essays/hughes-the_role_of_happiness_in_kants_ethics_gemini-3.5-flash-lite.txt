### Introduction

In the history of moral philosophy, few figures have executed a conceptual rupture as radical or as systematic as Immanuel Kant. For antiquity and the early modern period, the foundational question of ethics was almost invariably eudaimonistic: *What is the good life for a human being, and how do we attain happiness?* From Aristotle to the British sentimentalists, morality and happiness were conceptualized in an intrinsic, albeit sometimes complex, alignment. To act morally was, ultimately, to realize one’s true nature, achieve eudaimonia, or maximize pleasure and minimize pain. Kant shatters this continuity. In his critical philosophy, happiness is systematically dethroned from the foundational seat of moral motivation, justification, and obligation. 

Kant’s rejection of eudaimonism is not merely a rejection of a specific hedonistic or perfectionist calculus; it is a profound epistemological and metaphysical critique of the very architecture of practical reason. To ground morality in happiness is, for Kant, to commit a category mistake that conflates empirical contingency with a priori necessity, reduces freedom to natural causality, and reduces the majestic unconditional demand of duty to a conditional, hypothetical prudence. 

Yet, to understand Kant solely as an ascetic adversary of happiness is to misread the architectonic of the *Critique of Practical Reason*. Kant does not banish happiness from his moral universe; rather, he relocates it. By redefining morality not as the pursuit of happiness, but as becoming *worthy* of happiness, Kant preserves a place for human flourishing without compromising the purity of the moral law. Furthermore, through his solution to the antinomy of practical reason—articulated via the concept of the *highest good* (*summum bonum*) and the postulates of practical reason—he re-establishes a harmonious synthesis of virtue and happiness. This synthesis, however, is achieved without ever allowing happiness to usurp the legislative throne of the categorical imperative. 

This essay will evaluate the role of happiness in Kant’s ethics, focusing primarily on why he rejects the eudaimonist doctrine that happiness can serve as the basis of morality. It will explore the deep philosophical tension between the indeterminate, empirical nature of happiness and the *a priori* necessity of the categorical imperative, analyze Kant's redefinition of morality as worthiness, and finally resolve the antinomy of practical reason to demonstrate how virtue and happiness cohere within the highest good while fully safeguarding the autonomy of the will.

---

### I. The Eudaimonist Baseline and Kant’s Decisive Rupture

To appreciate the radical nature of Kant’s intervention, one must briefly recall the eudaimonist tradition against which he reacts. For Aristotle, ethics is an inquiry into the chief good (*summum bonum*), which all human beings agree is happiness (*eudaimonia*), even if they dispute its precise contents. For the ancient eudaimonists, moral virtue is the indispensable instrument or constitutive component of this happiness. To be virtuous is to actualize human capacities in accordance with reason, which in turn yields a life of flourishing. Similarly, even in modern empirical systems—such as the moral sense theories of Hutcheson or the utilitarian calculus of later thinkers—morality remains tethered to the promotion of happiness, whether conceived as pleasure, satisfaction of desires, or the maximization of general welfare.

Kant’s critique of these systems is unsparing. In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* and the *Critique of Practical Reason*, Kant argues that all previous moral philosophies suffer from a fatal structural flaw: they are grounded in *heteronomy*. Heteronomy occurs when the will does not give *itself* the law, but rather seeks the law in the character of some object of its desire—namely, happiness. 

Kant’s rejection of eudaimonism rests on several pillars. First, happiness is radically indeterminate. In the *Groundwork*, Kant defines happiness not as a determinate concept of reason, but as an *idea of the imagination*—an aggregate of empirical ends, desires, inclinations, and physical well-being. Because human beings are finite sensuous creatures subject to changing needs, empirical environments, and constitutions, happiness presents a shifting target. One person finds happiness in wealth, another in ascetic contemplation, another in power, and another in sensual indulgence. 

Because happiness is a manifold of empirical inclinations, it can never yield universal and necessary laws. As Kant famously notes in the *Critique of Practical Reason*: 
> "The concept of happiness is not one that a human being can abstract from his instincts and so derive from himself... it is an unhappy, merely empirical concept of the sufficiency of all conditions in life... upon which an unconditional practical law can never be based."

Second, eudaimonism destroys the very concept of *obligation*. If an action is commanded only because it leads to happiness, the necessity of the command is entirely conditional. It is a *hypothetical imperative*: *If* you wish to be happy, *then* you must do X. But a conditional command possesses no moral force; it possesses only the prudential force of counsel or technical skill. If an agent rejects happiness (or defines it in a way that bypasses traditional virtue), the hypothetical imperative loses its grip. Morality, however, presents itself to human consciousness as an *unconditional* demand—a categorical imperative that commands absolutely, regardless of inclination, consequence, or personal satisfaction. To base morality on happiness is thus to reduce the categorical imperative to a counsel of prudence, thereby annihilating its status as a moral law.

### II. The Epistemological and Metaphysical Chasm: Empirical Indeterminacy vs. *A Priori* Necessity

The fundamental tension between happiness and morality in Kant’s philosophy is rooted in an epistemological and metaphysical dualism that runs throughout his critical system: the division between the empirical world of appearances (*phenomena*) and the intelligible world of noumenal freedom.

#### The Empirical Contingency of Happiness
Happiness belongs entirely to the phenomenal realm. It is bound up with the causality of nature, sensuous inclinations, and subjective feelings of pleasure and pain. Because it is derived from sensibility (*receptivity*), its contents cannot be known *a priori*. We cannot deduce what will make a human being happy purely through rational conceptual analysis; we must consult experience, observation, and psychological constitution. 

Consequently, the pursuit of happiness is governed by the principles of self-love (*Selbstliebe*) and pragmatism. It relies on empirical generalizations about what generally satisfies human desires. But empirical generalizations can never provide the ground for necessity. A rule derived from experience may hold *for the most part*, but it can never hold *necessarily* and *universally*. Yet, universality and necessity are the indispensable marks of genuine moral laws. A maxim of conduct can only qualify as a moral law if it holds for all rational beings at all times, without exception. Therefore, an empirical concept like happiness is structurally incapable of serving as the ground of a moral law.

#### The *A Priori* Necessity of the Categorical Imperative
In sharp contrast to the empirical flux of happiness, the moral law must originate *a priori* in pure practical reason. In the *Groundwork*, Kant insists that if a single moral law is to possess genuine moral worth, it cannot borrow anything from human nature or empirical anthropology. It must be derived entirely from the concept of a rational being as such.

The categorical imperative commands unconditionally: *Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.* This imperative does not instruct us on how to achieve our desires or secure our well-being; it scrutinizes the formal structure of our maxims to test their universalizability and consistency with rational agency. The authority of the categorical imperative is entirely independent of whether its fulfillment makes us happy or miserable. Indeed, Kant argues that the moral law often demands the outright frustration of our deepest inclinations and happiness—as in cases where duty requires truth-telling even to a murderous pursuer, or self-preservation when life has become an unbearable burden.

To allow happiness to dictate moral obligation would be to subordinate the pure, self-legislating faculty of reason to the heteronomous promptings of animal nature. It would be, in Kant's view, a debasement of human dignity. For Kant, human dignity (*Würde*) consists precisely in our capacity to act independently of sensuous determination—to be free agents capable of framing our lives according to laws we give ourselves *a priori*. Happiness has a *price*, and can be traded off or compromised; morality has an intrinsic, unexchangeable *dignity*.

### III. Redefining Morality: From Achieving Happiness to Worthiness

If happiness is banished from the foundation of morality, what role does it play in the moral life? Does Kant advocate an asceticism that views human happiness with hostility? 

The answer is nuanced. Kant does not deny that human beings naturally desire happiness; indeed, he acknowledges that to desire happiness is an unavoidable necessity for any finite, needy rational creature. However, he radically reconfigures the relationship between morality and happiness by introducing the concept of *worthiness* (*Würdigkeit*).

#### The Separation of Motive and Consequence
In the opening pages of the *Groundwork*, Kant famously declares that a good will is not good because of what it effects or accomplishes, nor because of its adequacy to attain some proposed end; it is good only through its willing—that is, it is good in itself. This entails a strict separation between the *moral worth* of an action and its *consequences*, including the generation of happiness.

When an individual acts purely from duty—respect for the moral law (*Achtung fürs Gesetz*)—their action possesses genuine moral worth. If an individual acts from the desire for happiness (even if the action happens to coincide with what duty requires, such as being honest to maintain a good reputation), the action may be praise-worthy or prudent, but it lacks genuine *moral* worth. It is grounded in a hypothetical imperative of self-love.

#### Worthiness as the Moral Telos
While we cannot make happiness the *motive* of moral action without destroying its morality, Kant recognizes that human agents naturally seek happiness as their ultimate subjective end. How does practical reason accommodate this inescapable aspect of human nature? 

Kant introduces the concept of being *worthy* of happiness. He writes in the *Critique of Practical Reason*:
> "Virtue and happiness are the two specific elements of the highest good possible in a world... virtue as the condition is the supreme good, and happiness is the second element, though in such a way that it is only morally conditioned, viz., as dependent upon the former."

To be worthy of happiness means to possess a disposition of will (virtue) that *deserves* happiness. Virtue is the supreme condition of all our pursuit of happiness; it is the worthiness to be happy. Kant argues that while reason does not command us to make our own happiness our direct duty (because we desire it naturally), it *does* command us to preserve our moral integrity—to act virtuously—so that if happiness were distributed in proportion to morality, we would be worthy of receiving it.

Thus, morality is redefined: instead of striving directly to *achieve* happiness through prudential calculation, the moral agent strives to become *worthy* of happiness through unconditional obedience to the moral law. The shift is monumental. Eudaimonism says: *Be virtuous so that you may be happy.* Kantianism says: *Be virtuous unconditionally, and let the worthiness to be happy constitute the only rational link between virtue and happiness.*

### IV. The Antinomy of Practical Reason and the Highest Good

This sharp separation between virtue and happiness, however, generates a profound philosophical difficulty that threatens to undermine the coherence of practical reason: the *Antinomy of Practical Reason*.

#### The Antinomy Defined
In the second *Critique*, Kant formulates an antinomy regarding the object of pure practical reason—the *summum bonum* (highest good). The highest good requires two elements: virtue and happiness. Moreover, these two elements must be connected analytically or synthetically. 

1. **The Thesis (Eudaimonist claim):** The desire for happiness is the motive to maxims of virtue, or the striving for virtue is the efficient cause of happiness. Kant quickly dismisses this as analytically and empirically false. Experience shows that virtue and happiness do not naturally coincide in this world; the righteous often suffer, while the wicked flourish.
2. **The Antithesis (Kantian purity claim):** The maxim of virtue cannot possibly be the cause of the presence of happiness, and the desire for happiness cannot be the motive to maxims of virtue. If virtue causes happiness, or vice versa, the purity of the moral motive is compromised, because virtue is then practiced for the sake of happiness (heteronomy).

Yet, practical reason commands us to seek the highest good. If the highest good is impossible, then the moral law that commands it must be fantastic and directed to empty, imaginary ends—which is absurd. Reason is thus caught in an antinomy: the connection between virtue and happiness cannot be the *basis* of moral action (without destroying autonomy), yet practical reason demands that virtue and happiness must be connected in the highest good.

#### Resolution through the Postulates of Practical Reason
Kant resolves this antinomy by making a crucial distinction between the *determining ground* of the moral will and the *necessary object* of that will. 

*   **The Determining Ground:** The determining ground of the will must be *purely formal*—the moral law itself, completely abstracted from any consideration of happiness.
*   **The Object:** The necessary object of a moral will is the *highest good*—a world in which virtue and happiness are harmoniously proportioned.

To resolve the impossibility of this union in the phenomenal world, Kant introduces his famous *postulates of practical reason*: the immortality of the soul and the existence of God.

1. **Immortality:** Because complete conformity of the will to the moral law (holiness) is required for the highest good, and this perfection can never be attained by a finite sensuous creature in earthly existence, we must postulate an endless progression toward holiness, which requires an immortal soul.
2. **God:** The natural world operates according to mechanical physical laws where virtue does not systematically guarantee happiness. Therefore, to ensure that virtue and happiness are proportionally united in the highest good, we must postulate the existence of a moral author and ruler of the universe—God—who possesses the power to distribute happiness in exact proportion to moral worth in a future state.

Crucially, Kant insists that these postulates do not serve as *motives* for moral action. If an agent acts out of hope for heavenly reward (divine happiness) or fear of divine punishment, the action is heteronomous and devoid of moral worth. The moral agent must act *as if* God and immortality were not postulated; the postulation follows *from* the objective demand of practical reason for the highest good, not as the psychological engine driving duty.

### V. Autonomy, Freedom, and the Preservation of Moral Purity

At the heart of Kant’s rejection of eudaimonism and his resolution of the practical antinomy lies the supreme principle of his ethics: **Autonomy** (*Autonomie des Willens*).

#### Autonomy vs. Heteronomy
For Kant, true freedom is not merely negative—the absence of external physical coercion—but positive: the property of the will to be a law to itself. When a will seeks its law in the character of its objects (such as happiness), it falls into heteronomy. The will becomes a slave to its own empirical inclinations and the natural causes that produce them. 

If happiness were the basis of morality, the human will would be fundamentally reactive, determined by external stimuli (pleasure and pain) rather than self-legislation. The agent would be no different in kind from a sophisticated animal calculating how best to satisfy its biological drives. Kant writes:
> "If the will seeks the law that is to determine it anywhere else than in the fitness of its maxims for its own universal lawgiving... heteronomy always results. The will does not give itself the law, but the object does so by means of its relation to the will."

#### Safeguarding Freedom within the Highest Good
By carefully structuring the relationship between morality, happiness, and the postulates, Kant ensures that the autonomy of the will is never compromised. 

1. **Primacy of Virtue:** Virtue always retains unconditional priority over happiness. Happiness is never the *reason* for being virtuous; it is merely the *consequent reward* warranted by virtue, distributed by a moral deity.
2. **Motivational Purity:** The moral agent acts solely from respect for the law (*Pflicht aus Pflicht*). The thought of the highest good does not provide the *incentive* (*Triebfeder*) for moral action; rather, respect for the moral law is the sole incentive. The highest good is merely the *horizon* or systemic goal that reason must necessarily project as the completion of its practical endeavors.

Through this exquisite architectonic, Kant achieves a synthesis that honors both the dignity of moral freedom and the deeply human yearning for happiness. Morality does not make us happy in this life—indeed, it often demands sacrifice and suffering—but it makes us *worthy* of happiness, and reason demands that a rational, moral universe must ultimately bring these two elements into harmony.

---

### Conclusion

Immanuel Kant’s evaluation of happiness in ethics represents one of the most uncompromising revolutions in the history of philosophy. By rejecting the ancient and modern eudaimonist doctrines that anchor morality in the pursuit of happiness, Kant rescued ethics from empirical contingency, psychological subjectivism, and heteronomous manipulation. 

He demonstrated that because happiness is an indeterminate empirical concept rooted in sensuous inclinations, it can never provide the universality and necessity required of a genuine moral law. To base morality on happiness is to reduce the categorical imperative to a conditional counsel of prudence, thereby stripping duty of its unshakeable majesty and human agency of its true autonomy.

Yet, Kant’s philosophy is far from a sterile, life-denying asceticism. By redefining morality as becoming *worthy* of happiness, he preserves a coherent and elevated place for human flourishing. Through his brilliant resolution of the antinomy of practical reason via the postulates of the soul's immortality and the existence of God, Kant secures the highest good—a harmonious synthesis of virtue and happiness—without ever sacrificing the absolute purity of the moral motive. In Kant’s moral universe, happiness is neither the foundation of duty nor its primary driver; rather, it is the rightful, rational crown bestowed upon a will that has proven itself worthy of freedom through unconditional obedience to the moral law.
