# The Cost of Duration: Kantian Dignity, Successive Selves, and the Morality of Imprudence

### Introduction

The modern moral landscape is characterized by an uneasy tension between personal autonomy and public health paternalism. Nowhere is this tension sharper than in the normative evaluation of everyday, self-affecting choices that carry long-term health risks—such as cigarette smoking, extreme sports, dietary imprudence, or chronic sleep deprivation. To the contemporary liberal intuition, these acts are paradigmatic instances of self-regarding behavior. Provided an agent is fully informed and acts without coercing others, the decision to trade longevity for immediate pleasure or stress relief is seen as an expression of sovereignty over one's own body and life. Yet, certain rigorous philosophical frameworks resist this conclusion. They argue that such choices are not merely unwise, but morally impermissible. 

This essay evaluates two of the most sophisticated philosophical attempts to establish the moral wrongness of self-affecting imprudence: J. David Velleman’s Kantian framework of personhood and dignity, and Derek Parfit’s reductionist, successive-selves model of personal identity. Velleman argues that trading the structural integrity of one’s person for external or hedonic benefits constitutes a desecration of the moral standing inherent in agency itself. Parfit, conversely, dissolves the unified subject across time, treating the relationship between the present self and the future self as morally continuous with the relationship between distinct persons. By recasting intra-personal trade-offs as inter-personal harms, Parfit seeks to subject imprudence to the demands of distributive justice.

Despite their radically different metaphysical starting points—one anchored in the inviolable sanctity of the Kantian noumenal subject, the other in the deflationary Humean bundle of mental states—both Velleman and Parfit arrive at conclusions that demand an aggressive moralization of private life. This essay argues that both frameworks ultimately fail to successfully establish the immorality of ordinary self-affecting practices like smoking. In their zeal to condemn life-shortening practices, both theories collapse the crucial moral distinction between acting immorally toward others and living poorly for oneself. By inflating the normative demands of personal continuity and rational agency, both Velleman and Parfit commit a form of moral overreach. They undermine the rightful authority of the present self, effectively disenfranchising the living agent in the service of an abstraction: either the abstract dignity of the rational will or the distributive balance of a psychological ledger.

### Part I: Velleman and the Kantian Sanctity of the Person

To understand J. David Velleman’s critique of imprudence, one must first locate it within his broader project of grounding ethics in the nature of human agency. In essays such as "A Right of Self-Termination?" and "The Guise of the Good," Velleman departs from standard contemporary preference-satisfaction models of practical reason. He argues that human actions are not merely driven by antecedent desires that agents passively discover within themselves. Instead, agents are fundamentally constituted by a motive to act *in accordance with reasons*, which requires the capacity for self-governance and reflective endorsement. 

For Velleman, human beings are not merely sites of experiential welfare whose utility is to be maximized or balanced across a lifetime. We are Kantian *persons*: entities capable of stepping back from our desires, evaluating them, and determining our courses of action through practical reason. This capacity confers upon us a specific kind of value that Immanuel Kant famously distinguished from price: *dignity* (*Würde*). As Kant argues in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, that which has a price can be replaced by something else as its equivalent; whereas that which is elevated above all price, and admits of no equivalent, has dignity. 

Velleman extends this Kantian insight into the domain of intra-personal trade-offs. The core of his argument against imprudence—and specifically against practices that trade the structural integrity of one's person for external or hedonic benefits—relies on the mechanics of valuation. When an agent engages in a life-shortening, health-destroying habit like smoking for the sake of pleasure, relaxation, or social conformity, what is the structure of the transaction? 

According to Velleman, the agent is treating the very capacity for value-conferring agency as a mere instrument or trade-good to secure experiential goods. But this reverses the proper order of normative dependence. Experiential goods—pleasures, satisfactions, and subjective states—only have value *for* a person, and a person only has the capacity to evaluate and confer value because of their rational agency. To trade the structural conditions of agency (such as cognitive clarity, health, and physiological longevity) for the sake of transient experiences generated within that agency is to commit a category mistake of valuation. It is to sacrifice the condition of all value for the sake of a derivative product of that condition.

Velleman formulates this as a violation of the categorical imperative to treat humanity—whether in one's own person or in that of another—always as an end in itself, never merely as a means. While traditional moral philosophy applies this injunction almost exclusively to our interactions with *other* people (prohibiting exploitation, manipulation, and physical assault), Velleman insists that the injunction is reflexive. The phrase "in one's own person" is not merely a rhetorical flourish; it designates the agent's body and mind as the immediate locus of moral duties.

When an individual smokes, knowing that the practice will systematically degrade their physical constitution, shorten their life, and potentially dull their cognitive faculties, Velleman argues that they are using their own person as a mere instrument. The present self uses the biological and psychological substrate of the future self as a smoke-generator, a dopamine-delivery system, or a stress-relief valve. In doing so, the agent subordinates the objective value of personhood to the subjective valence of an experience. For Velleman, this is not merely an imprudent miscalculation of utility; it is a degradation of moral status. It is a failure of self-respect. Just as one cannot sell oneself into slavery without violating one’s dignity (because doing so uses one's capacity for agency as a tool for a master), one cannot systematically degrade one’s bodily and mental integrity for minor pleasures without committing an analogous form of self-enslavement to appetite.

### Part II: Parfit, Reductionism, and the Successive-Selves Model

While Velleman seeks to protect the metaphysical sanctity of the unified Kantian subject, Derek Parfit approaches personal identity from the opposite direction. In *Reasons and Persons*, Parfit defends a radical reductionist view of personal identity. Drawing on David Hume and Buddhist philosophy, Parfit argues that what we call a "person" or a "self" does not refer to a separately existing, indivisible entity—such as a Cartesian ego or a Kantian noumenal subject—that persists over time. Instead, a person is simply a series of interconnected mental states and experiences linked by relations of psychological continuity and connectedness.

Psychological connectedness refers to the direct chains of memories, desires, intentions, and character traits that link one moment of consciousness to another. Psychological continuity is the overlapping chain of such connections over longer stretches of time. Parfit famously argues that *identity is not what matters*. When we ask whether the person who will exist in twenty years is "the same person" who exists today, we are asking a question that may have no determinate answer, or an answer that lacks deep moral significance. Over time, psychological connectedness fades. The person I will be in forty years may share very few memories, desires, or values with the person I am today.

This metaphysical shift has revolutionary implications for practical reason and ethics, particularly regarding our obligations to our future selves. In standard non-reductionist views, an agent has a special, prudential reason to care for their future self because that future self is strictly identical to the present self. The future self is *me*. Therefore, harming my future self is just harming myself, and any imbalance between present sacrifice and future benefit is merely an internal negotiation within a single economic unit.

Parfit disrupts this picture by observing that the degree of psychological connectedness between the present self and a future self can vary wildly, and often diminishes over time. The relationship between my current self and my self in thirty years may exhibit less psychological connectedness than the relationship between me and a close stranger. If personal identity is not an all-or-nothing fact, but a matter of degree, then the boundaries of the "self" become porous. 

From this reductionist premise, Parfit constructs his critique of imprudence. If the psychological ties between the present self and the future self weaken over time, then the present self’s imposition of costs on the future self begins to look structurally indistinguishable from the imposition of costs on *another person*. When a twenty-year-old takes up smoking, knowing that at age sixty they will suffer from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or lung cancer, the twenty-year-old is not merely harming "themselves." They are acting as a distinct psychological agent who is offloading a massive health burden onto a temporally distant successor—a successor who will have to bear the pain, disability, and medical costs without having given informed consent to the initial transaction.

Parfit thus translates traditional prudential problems into the language of inter-personal morality. Imprudence is recast as a failure of distributive justice or interpersonal benevolence across time. Just as it is wrong for a privileged generation to deplete environmental resources and saddle future generations with ecological collapse, it is morally wrong for the present self to deplete bodily resources and saddle the future self with physical decay. By breaking down the barrier of strict personal identity, Parfit’s successive-selves model brings self-regarding acts under the direct jurisdiction of moral evaluation. Imprudence is no longer just irrational; it is unjust.

### Part III: The Central Tension: Condemning Life-Shortening vs. Preserving Autonomy

Both Velleman’s Kantian framework and Parfit’s reductionist framework are designed to secure a robust moral condemnation of self-affecting imprudence. However, both theories generate an acute structural tension when placed alongside our deepest commitments to individual autonomy. This tension manifests as an overbreadth problem: in their eagerness to condemn extreme self-destruction or reckless self-harm, both theoretical frameworks entail overly restrictive conclusions regarding everyday human activities that carry minor, moderate, or even statistically significant health risks.

Consider the vast spectrum of human behavior that modern science identifies as life-shortening or health-compromising. It is not merely cigarette smoking that diminishes life expectancy and degrades physiological integrity. The list includes:
* Consuming alcohol, red meat, or ultra-processed foods.
* Engaging in sedentary desk work that strains the cardiovascular and musculoskeletal systems.
* Practicing high-risk sports such as skiing, mountaineering, cycling in urban traffic, or martial arts.
* Working in high-stress professions that elevate cortisol levels and induce chronic fatigue.
* Choosing to live in densely populated urban environments with poor air quality for the sake of cultural vibrancy or career opportunities.
* Even choosing to have children, an endeavor statistically associated with significant physical toll, sleep deprivation, and psychological stress.

If we apply Velleman’s criterion strictly—that any act which systematically degrades the structural integrity of one's person for the sake of experiential or external benefits is a violation of Kantian dignity—the scope of permissible human agency collapses. Velleman’s framework appears to commit us to a stringent asceticism. If the body and mind are the non-tradeable substrate of agency, then *any* calculated risk taken for pleasure or convenience risks being classified as a desecration of that substrate. When a person eats a decadent dessert, drinks a glass of wine, stays up late to finish a novel rather than sleeping, or drives to a vacation spot rather than staying safely at home, they are trading bodily preservation for hedonic or experiential goods. 

Velleman might attempt to draw a principled line between minor risks and major self-destruction, arguing that smoking or severe addiction involves a systemic, structural dismantling of agency, whereas enjoying a glass of wine does not. Yet, this boundary is notoriously difficult to maintain within a Kantian framework. Kant himself was notoriously austere, famously arguing that masturbation is a degradation of humanity in one's person worse than suicide, and that lying is a total forfeiture of moral worth. Velleman’s modernized Kantianism inherits this puritanical streak. If the foundational value of the agent is tied to the preservation of the physical and mental apparatus of practical reason, then *any* voluntary trade of health for pleasure is conceptually continuous with smoking or self-mutilation. It differs only in magnitude, not in kind. This turns ethics into a calculus of biological preservation, where the highest virtue is the maximization of physiological longevity—a strange outcome for a philosophy ostensibly dedicated to the transcendence of mere biological life in favor of rational freedom.

The tension is equally acute, though differently configured, in Parfit’s successive-selves model. Parfit seeks to protect future selves from the depredations of present selves by treating inter-temporal conflicts as inter-personal ones. But if the present self stands in a relation of moral responsibility to future selves analogous to our responsibility to strangers, the normative implications quickly become paralyzing.

In interpersonal ethics, we generally have strict duties not to impose unconsented-to risks of serious harm on others. If I drive recklessly through a neighborhood, I wrong my neighbors by exposing them to a statistical risk of injury, even if I successfully avoid hitting anyone. If Parfit’s model applies this logic intra-personally, then *any* action performed by the present self that imposes a statistical risk of future harm on a successor self stands in need of strong moral justification. 

When a person chooses a sedentary lifestyle because they prefer comfort to exercise, they are imposing a high statistical probability of cardiovascular disease on their fifty-year-old successor. When a young adult works eighty-hour weeks in a high-stress investment bank to achieve early financial independence, they are trading the psychological and physical health of their future self for the wealth of their present/intermediate self. Under Parfit's view, these are not merely private trade-offs; they are acts of inter-temporal exploitation. 

Yet, treating these choices as moral wrongs radically undermines the scope of legitimate personal projects. Human life is inherently project-driven, and meaningful projects almost invariably involve trade-offs between present effort, immediate pleasure, and future well-being. A life dedicated entirely to minimizing risk and maximizing the welfare of one's future selves would be a life drained of spontaneity, passion, and risk-taking. More paradoxically, Parfit’s model threatens to paralyze the present self entirely. Since virtually *every* choice the present self makes alters the identity, character, and well-being of future selves, the present self is cast into a perpetual state of moral indebtedness. The agent becomes an ethical accountant, forced to balance the ledger of psychological connectedness against every sip of whiskey, every skipped workout, and every stressful career ambition.

### Part IV: Reconstructing the Mechanisms of Moralization

To evaluate whether Velleman and Parfit successfully establish the immorality of practices like smoking, we must meticulously reconstruct the exact mechanisms by which they bridge the gap between self-affecting behavior and moral transgression.

#### Velleman’s Mechanism: The Inversion of Value and Kantian Reflexivity
Velleman’s mechanism operates through a structural critique of practical rationality. The core of his argument relies on the asymmetry between *instrumental value* and *constitutive value*. 
1. **The Axiological Ground:** Rational agency is the source of all value. Without a rational agent to confer value upon objects, states of affairs are merely brute facts devoid of normative significance.
2. **The Reflexive Imperative:** Kantian morality commands that agents be treated as ends in themselves, never merely as means. Because this command is reflexive (it applies to "humanity in one's own person"), the agent stands in a fiduciary moral relationship to their own capacity for agency.
3. **The Transactional Fallacy:** When an agent smokes for pleasure or stress relief, they use their physical and cognitive capacities (the physical substrate of agency) as an instrument to generate a subjective state (pleasure or relief). 
4. **The Moral Conclusion:** This transaction inverts the proper order of value. It subordinates the condition of all value (the agent) to a product of that condition (an experience). Because this involves treating the higher capacity as a mere instrument for the lower, it constitutes a structural violation of self-respect, rendering the act morally impermissible.

#### Parfit’s Mechanism: Reductionist Dissolution and Inter-Temporal Justice
Parfit’s mechanism operates through a metaphysical deflation of personal identity followed by an analogical extension of interpersonal morality.
1. **The Metaphysical Premise:** There is no indivisible "self" that persists over time. There are only successive psychological states linked by varying degrees of connectedness and continuity.
2. **The Graduated Moral Weight:** Because psychological connectedness fades over time, the moral reasons we have to care about our future selves diminish in direct proportion to the reduction in connectedness.
3. **The Interpersonal Analogy:** When psychological connectedness between the present self and the future self is low, the ethical distance between them approximates the ethical distance between two distinct persons.
4. **The Distributive Principle:** Imposing unconsented-to, severe, and avoidable harms or heavy risks on another person is a paradigm violation of interpersonal morality (harm and distributive injustice).
5. **The Moral Conclusion:** Therefore, when a present self imposes severe, avoidable health burdens (such as lung cancer from smoking) on a future self with low psychological connectedness, the present self commits an act of inter-temporal injustice, rendering the imprudent act morally wrong.

### Part V: Critique: Do the Theories Undermine the Rightful Role of the Present Self?

Do these meticulously reconstructed mechanisms successfully establish the immorality of smoking and similar imprudent acts? And do their normative demands undermine the rightful role of the present self? I argue that both frameworks fail on both counts. They rely on untenable metaphysical and normative overextensions that distort the actual phenomenology of human life and agency.

#### The Flaw in Velleman: Conflating Instrumentalization with Embodied Vulnerability
Velleman’s argument hinges on the claim that trading bodily integrity for experiential pleasure is an act of treating oneself as a mere means. But this mischaracterizes the nature of human embodiment. Human beings are not disembodied rational wills that happen to inhabit biological shells like drivers in a car. We are psycho-biological unities where mind and body are inextricably intertwined.

When a person smokes, eats rich food, or engages in dangerous sports, they are not typically viewing their body as an external tool or a slave to be exploited. They are experiencing their embodiment *directly*. The pleasure derived from smoking or drinking is not an abstract mental state divorced from the physical vehicle; it is a somatic experience felt *by* the embodied agent. Velleman’s Kantian framework forces a radical dualism back into a non-dualistic reality by treating the physical substrate as if it were distinct from the agent utilizing it. 

Furthermore, Velleman’s prohibition against trading integrity for experience ignores the reality of human trade-offs. Human existence is finite, and all choices involve trade-offs between longevity and quality of life. Is a person who chooses to work a high-stress job to support their family—thereby shortening their life through chronic stress—violating their Kantian dignity? Most moral agents would say no; they are exercising their agency to fulfill commitments they deem valuable. Velleman’s view pathologizes all forms of self-sacrifice, risk-taking, and hedonism by branding them as moral failures. In doing so, it strips the present self of the authority to define what makes a life worth living. If dignity requires the absolute preservation of the physical substrate of agency at all costs, then dignity becomes a prison, turning the agent into a caretaker of a biological museum rather than an author of their own life.

#### The Flaw in Parfit: The Moral Absurdity of Inter-Temporal Justice
Parfit’s reductionist model fares no better when evaluated against the realities of practical agency. The fatal flaw in Parfit’s attempt to equate intra-personal imprudence with inter-personal injustice lies in what we might call the *Consent Paradox* and the *Symmetry Problem*.

First, consider consent. In interpersonal ethics, it is wrong for me to impose risks on another person *because* that person is an independent moral agent who has not consented to bear those risks. But in the intra-personal case, who is the agent granting or withholding consent? The twenty-year-old smoker *is* the psychological ancestor of the sixty-year-old patient. While psychological connectedness has faded, the later self is literally constituted by the developmental trajectory initiated and sustained by the earlier self's psychological states. 

More importantly, Parfit’s model relies on a symmetrical fiction. Interpersonal harms are bidirectional: I can wrong you, and you can wrong me. But inter-temporal relations are strictly asymmetrical. The present self can affect the future self, but the future self can *never* affect the present self. Time’s arrow ensures that the causal flow is unidirectional. Because of this asymmetry, treating the future self as an "other" creates a grotesque moral servitude. The present self is burdened with absolute duties of care toward a succession of future strangers, while those future strangers owe nothing in return—indeed, they cannot even interact with the present self to release it from its duties.

This undermines the rightful role of the present self by rendering present agency impossible. If every action must be vetted against the welfare of future successor-selves, and if all risks must be minimized to avoid charges of inter-temporal injustice, the present moment is entirely hollowed out. Human agency is inherently forward-looking, but it is also inescapably *present-centered*. We live, deliberate, and act in the present. While prudence dictates that we take reasonable care of our futures, elevating prudence to the status of strict moral duty—and transforming imprudence into a moral crime against a successor—destroys the psychological conditions under which projects, passions, and self-creation can occur.

#### The Categorical Distinction Between Morality and Prudence
Ultimately, both Velleman and Parfit commit a fundamental category mistake by collapsing the distinction between *morality* (how we treat others) and *prudence* (how we treat ourselves). 

To say that an act is imprudent is to say that it fails to promote the agent’s own long-term well-being or that it rests on poor practical reasoning. To say that an act is *immoral* is to say that it violates the rights or legitimate interests of others, breaching the demands of fairness, justice, or benevolence in a way that makes the agent liable to moral blame, guilt, and social sanction. 

When a person smokes and contracts lung cancer, they have harmed *themselves*. They may have acted foolishly, shortsightedly, or self-destructively. But they have not violated anyone else’s rights. They have not exploited an innocent party who lacked the capacity to consent. To introduce moral categories like "injustice," "exploitation," or "desecration" into this domain is to indulge in moral hyperbole. It psychologizes and moralizes the inherent tragedy of human vulnerability and finitude. 

We do not need a moral theory to tell us that smoking is bad for our health; medical science and common sense accomplish that quite effectively. What we do not need—and what both Velleman and Parfit inadvertently provide—is a moralistic framework that treats personal vice as a species of wickedness. By inflating the normative stakes of self-regarding choices, these theories encroach upon the legitimate sphere of personal sovereignty. They replace the autonomous agent—with all their flaws, frailties, and desires for immediate gratification—with an idealized, risk-averse automaton whose sole moral duty is to hand over an unbroken, unblemished biological machine to their future self at the end of a sterile, impeccably managed life.

---

### Conclusion

J. David Velleman and Derek Parfit offer two of the most intellectually formidable challenges to the liberal intuition that self-regarding imprudence is a purely private affair. Velleman seeks to rescue the sanctity of the agent through a Kantian defense of structural integrity and dignity, while Parfit seeks to dissolve the unified self into a chain of successive subjects, transforming self-harm into inter-temporal injustice. 

However, as this essay has demonstrated, neither framework successfully establishes the moral wrongness of everyday self-affecting acts like smoking without collapsing into implausible and overly restrictive conclusions. Velleman’s Kantianism mischaracterizes embodied human agency as a form of dualistic exploitation, pathologizing all forms of risk-taking and self-sacrifice. Parfit’s reductionism creates an impossible moral burden, subjecting the present self to the tyranny of future successor-selves through an asymmetrical and fictional interpersonal analogy.

In their zeal to condemn life-shortening practices, both theories undermine the rightful role of the present self as the author of its own existence. They erase the crucial boundary between failing to be prudent and failing to be moral. A philosophically adequate account of human agency must preserve the space for human beings to make mistakes, take risks, and even act foolishly—not because self-harm is desirable, but because the freedom to author one’s life necessarily entails the freedom to ruin it. To strip agents of that freedom in the name of Kantian dignity or Parfitian distributive justice is not to elevate personhood, but to abolish it.
