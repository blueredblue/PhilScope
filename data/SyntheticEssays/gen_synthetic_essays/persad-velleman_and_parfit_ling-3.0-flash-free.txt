**The Moral Cost of the Present Self: Velleman, Parfit, and the Paradox of Prudence**

The question of why imprudent self-affecting acts—such as smoking, excessive drinking, or reckless endangerment of one’s own life—are morally wrong has long troubled moral philosophy. On the surface, such acts appear to be quintessentially self-regarding; they harm the agent and no one else. Yet, two of the most influential frameworks in contemporary philosophy, J. David Velleman’s Kantian account of personhood and Derek Parfit’s successive-selves model of personal identity, both attempt to establish that such acts are indeed morally impermissible. Their approaches, however, are radically different. Velleman grounds the immorality of self-harm in the violation of the agent’s own dignity as a rational being, arguing that trading one’s person for benefits degrades the very source of moral worth. Parfit, conversely, dissolves the strict boundary of the self, arguing that risks to one’s future self are equivalent to harms imposed upon a distinct future person. 

Despite their divergent metaphysical foundations, both theories converge on a troubling normative conclusion: that the present self is constrained by duties that severely restrict its autonomy, particularly regarding activities that carry health risks. This essay evaluates whether either Velleman or Parfit successfully establishes the immorality of imprudent self-affecting acts like smoking. It will reconstruct the specific mechanisms by which each philosopher derives their respective conclusions, analyze the central tension between condemning life-shortening practices and preserving individual autonomy, and assess whether the resulting normative demands undermine the rightful role of the present self. Ultimately, it will be argued that while both theories offer profound insights into the moral status of the self, their rigorous mechanisms lead to overly restrictive conclusions regarding everyday activities, thereby failing to adequately respect the legitimate agency of the present self.

**I. Velleman’s Kantian Dignity and the Immorality of Trading One’s Person**

J. David Velleman’s framework is rooted in a robust Kantian conception of human dignity. For Velleman, the dignity of a person consists in the unconditioned worth of a rational agent—the capacity to set ends and to legislate the moral law for oneself. Because dignity is intrinsic and unconditional, it cannot be traded, sold, or surrendered. It is not a commodity that can be exchanged for benefits, however desirable. Velleman’s central mechanism for deriving the immorality of self-harm rests on the argument that when an agent engages in an imprudent act like smoking, it is effectively trading its person—its future capacity for rational agency, its health, and its life—for immediate benefits, such as the relief of nicotine cravings or the social pleasures of the act. 

To understand Velleman’s derivation, one must grasp the Kantian prohibition against treating a rational being merely as a means. When the present self smokes, it instrumentalizes its future self. It treats the future person—the bearer of the consequences of the present act—as a mere vehicle for present gratification. The present self consumes the future self’s health and longevity as a resource to be spent on the altar of immediate pleasure. Velleman argues that this constitutes a violation of the categorical imperative, specifically the formula of humanity, which demands that we treat humanity, whether in our own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means. By trading one’s person for benefits, the agent degrades the very dignity that constitutes their moral standing. The agent becomes the owner and the commodity simultaneously, engaging in a form of self-alienation.

The immorality of smoking, under Velleman’s framework, is thus not merely a matter of health or prudence, but a fundamental violation of the agent’s status as a person. The act of smoking diminishes the agent’s capacity to exercise rational agency in the future, thereby diminishing the very thing that gives the agent moral worth. The present self, in pursuing a fleeting benefit, commits a moral wrong against the person it will become. This mechanism is powerful because it does not rely on a subjective calculus of harms and benefits; rather, it relies on the objective degradation of personhood.

However, this mechanism immediately raises the specter of over-restrictiveness. If any act that trades future personhood for present benefit is immoral, then the scope of prohibited actions becomes vast. Does accepting a dangerous job to feed one’s family constitute an immoral trade of one’s person for benefits? Is skydiving wrong because it risks the future capacity for rational agency for the present benefit of thrill? Velleman’s framework struggles with proportionality and the distinction between imprudent self-affecting acts and legitimate self-sacrifice. If the mechanism is that any risk to future personhood for present benefit is a degradation of dignity, then the theory demands a life of sterile caution, stripping the agent of the right to take reasonable risks. The present self is denied the ability to negotiate its own ends, bound by a rigid deontological prohibition that fails to account for the complexities of human flourishing.

**II. Parfit’s Successive-Selves Model and the Harm to Future Persons**

Derek Parfit’s successive-selves model offers a starkly different metaphysical picture, yet arrives at a similarly restrictive normative conclusion. Parfit’s theory of personal identity, articulated in *Reasons and Persons*, rejects the notion of a deep, numerically identical self. Instead, he argues that personal identity is a matter of psychological connectedness and continuity, which exists in degrees. The "person" we are today is not a simple, indivisible entity, but a successive series of selves linked by overlapping chains of psychological continuity. The present self and the future self are not numerically identical in any robust sense; rather, the future self is a distinct person, albeit one who is intimately connected to the present self by psychological continuity.

Parfit’s mechanism for deriving the immorality of self-harm relies on this dissociation of the self. If the future self is a distinct person, then risks to that future self are harms imposed upon another person. When the present self smokes, it is not merely harming its own future body; it is imposing a risk of severe harm—such as lung cancer, emphysema, and premature death—upon a distinct future individual. The present self, in Parfit’s framework, has no more right to risk the life and health of this future person than it has the right to risk the life and health of a stranger. Therefore, smoking is morally wrong because it is a form of recklessness toward another person, a violation of the duty not to harm.

This mechanism elegantly solves the problem of why we should care about our future selves at all. In a framework where personal identity is not what matters, the pull of prudence—that feeling that "it is me who will suffer"—is explained by psychological connectedness, but the moral weight of the future self is grounded in the fact that it is a separate person with its own rights. By equating risks to future selves with harms imposed on other people, Parfit transforms self-harm into interpersonal harm. The present self is morally constrained not by a duty to its own dignity, but by a duty of justice and non-maleficence toward the distinct person it will become.

Yet, this mechanism is equally susceptible to the objection of over-restrictiveness. If the future self is a distinct person, then any risk imposed on that future self is a moral wrong. Driving a car, eating unhealthy food, or even failing to exercise all impose risks on the distinct future person. If the present self must never harm another person, and the future self is another person, then the present self is morally required to eliminate all risks to the future self. This demands an impossible level of caution, rendering everyday life practically immoral. The theory transforms the present self into a perpetual guardian of a future stranger, burdened by an absolute duty of care that leaves no room for the normal, acceptable risks of human existence.

**III. The Central Tension: Condemning Life-Shortening Practices vs. Preserving Autonomy**

The central tension in both Velleman’s and Parfit’s frameworks lies in the collision between the condemnation of life-shortening practices and the preservation of individual autonomy. Autonomy, in its classical sense, is the capacity of the present self to author its own life, to make choices, and to accept the risks that come with those choices. Both theories, however, fundamentally constrain this capacity.

Velleman’s Kantian framework subordinates autonomy to dignity. While autonomy is the capacity for self-legislation, it is not the right to self-destruct. To autonomously choose to smoke is to autonomously choose to degrade one’s own personhood, to treat oneself as a means. For Velleman, true autonomy is not the liberty to destroy the conditions of one’s rational agency; it is the capacity to legislate in accordance with the dignity of the person. Therefore, the present self’s autonomy to take risks is overridden by the present self’s duty to preserve the dignity of the person. The tension here is acute: the very act of exercising autonomy to smoke is, by Velleman’s lights, an act of self-instrumentalization that violates the dignity required for autonomy in the first place. Autonomy becomes a closed loop: one can only be autonomous insofar as one does not act in ways that destroy the rational agency that makes autonomy possible.

Parfit’s framework resolves the tension differently, but no less destructively for the present self. By dissolving the identity of the present self into a chain of successive persons, Parfit eliminates the intuitive claim the present self has over the future self. The present self does not "own" the future self; the future self is a distinct entity with its own rights. The tension here is between the present self’s desires and the rights of the future person. The present self’s autonomy to pursue its immediate gratification is constrained by the autonomy and rights of the future self. The present self has no more right to impose risks on the future self than it does on anyone else. Thus, autonomy is not an absolute right of the present agent; it is bounded by the moral claims of the future persons it creates through its actions. The present self is merely one temporal phase in a chain, lacking the ultimate authority over the body it inhabits.

In both frameworks, the present self is stripped of its sovereign right to take risks with its own life. Velleman achieves this by asserting the unalienable dignity of the person, which forbids the present self from trading its future capacities for present pleasures. Parfit achieves this by asserting the distinct personhood of the future self, which forbids the present self from imposing risks on another. Both theories demand that the present self act as a steward or a guardian, but they do so at the cost of alienating the present self from its own agency.

**IV. The Overly Restrictive Objection: Everyday Activities and Health Risks**

The objection that both theoretical positions entail overly restrictive conclusions regarding everyday activities that carry health risks is perhaps the most devastating critique of both frameworks. If smoking is morally wrong because it trades person for benefits or harms a distinct future self, then by parity of reasoning, a vast array of ordinary, accepted behaviors must also be morally wrong.

Under Velleman’s mechanism, any activity that risks future personhood for present benefit is an immoral trade. Consuming alcohol, eating rich foods, failing to exercise, or even driving a car all carry health risks that diminish future personhood for present gratification or convenience. If the present self is forbidden from trading its person for benefits, then the present self is forbidden from engaging in virtually any activity that is not strictly risk-free. This leads to a paradoxical conclusion: the very acts that make life worth living—enjoying a meal, socializing over drinks, driving to work—become morally suspect. The theory demands a life of ascetic rigidity, which contradicts our deeply held intuitions about the value of human life and the legitimacy of enjoying it. The present self is reduced to a mere custodian of a future personhood, unable to live in the present without committing a moral wrong.

Under Parfit’s mechanism, the over-restrictiveness is equally stark. If the future self is a distinct person, then any risk imposed on that person is a harm. The present self must therefore act as a perfect guardian of the future self’s interests. Yet, the present self cannot eliminate all risks; it cannot guarantee the future self’s health or survival. The mere fact of existing and acting in the world imposes risks on the future self. Parfit’s framework thus demands that the present self act with an impossible degree of caution, or else accept the moral culpability of harming a distinct person. Everyday activities become moral hazards. The present self is burdened with an infinite duty of care toward a future stranger, a duty that cannot be fulfilled without renouncing all agency in the present.

Both theories fail to draw a principled line between the egregious wrong of smoking (which significantly shortens life and causes immense suffering) and the mundane risks of everyday life. By grounding the immorality of self-harm in absolute metaphysical claims—either the absolute unalienability of dignity or the absolute distinctness of the future self—both theories collapse into hyper-restrictive moral systems. They fail to account for the proportionality of risk, the context of the agent’s choices, and the legitimate role of risk-taking in human life.

**V. Assessment: Do the Normative Demands Undermine the Rightful Role of the Present Self?**

The resulting normative demands of both Velleman’s and Parfit’s theories do indeed undermine the rightful role of the present self. To understand why, we must first articulate what that rightful role is. The present self is the locus of agency, the author of action, and the conscious bearer of intentions. It is the self that makes choices, weighs trade-offs, and navigates the world. The rightful role of the present self includes the capacity to take reasonable risks, to pursue its own ends, and to accept the uncertainties of the future as part of the human condition.

Velleman’s normative demands undermine this role by transforming the present self into a mere executor of a moral law that forbids self-instrumentalization. The present self is denied the right to negotiate its own ends. When the present self desires a cigarette, it is not merely acting on a weakness; it is committing a violation of its own dignity. The present self is alienated from its own desires, forced to view its own gratification as a moral failing. By forbidding the present self from trading its person for benefits, Velleman strips the present self of its teleological agency. The present self becomes a mere vessel for preserving a metaphysical concept of personhood, unable to live a fully human life that includes the acceptance of risk and the pursuit of pleasure. The present self’s role as an agent is usurped by the moral law, which demands a sterile, risk-free existence that is incompatible with the flourishing of a rational being.

Parfit’s normative demands undermine the rightful role of the present self in a different but equally profound way. By equating the present self with a mere link in a chain of successive persons, Parfit dissolves the unity of the agent. The present self is no longer the author of its life; it is merely the temporary custodian of a body that belongs to a future distinct person. The present self’s desires, projects, and interests are subordinated to the rights of a future stranger. The present self loses its claim to the future, becoming a mere stepping stone in a psychological chain. This fragmentation of the self means that the present self cannot legitimately claim ownership over its own life. It is perpetually overshadowed by the moral claims of its future successors. The present self’s role as an agent is hollowed out, replaced by an infinite burden of care for entities that are, in Parfit’s view, not truly "it." The present self becomes a moral hostage to the rights of the future, unable to act in its own interests without committing a wrong against a distinct person.

In both cases, the present self is stripped of its moral primacy. Velleman’s present self is subordinated to the abstract ideal of dignity, while Parfit’s present self is subordinated to the concrete rights of a future distinct person. Neither framework allows the present self to be the ultimate arbiter of its own risk-taking, to weigh the costs and benefits of its actions in a way that respects both its present desires and its future continuity. The rightful role of the present self—the role of a unified agent capable of self-governance and reasonable risk-taking—is destroyed by the rigid normative demands of both theories.
