# Hylomorphic Partism, the Problem of the Many, and the Statue and the Clay

## Introduction

The problem of the many and the puzzle of the statue and the clay are often treated as problems for any metaphysics that permits distinct objects to occupy the same region. If a piece of clay is shaped into a statue, it seems that there are at least two objects present: the clay and the statue. Yet they appear to have the same spatial boundaries, the same matter, and, at least for a time, the same history. If ordinary mereology is governed by extensionality, however, distinct objects cannot have exactly the same proper parts. The clay and the statue therefore appear either to be identical, or to differ in a way that ordinary parthood cannot represent.

The problem of the many generalizes this difficulty. A collection of simples or a region of matter may seem capable of composing indefinitely many coincident wholes: a statue, a lump, a work of art, a physical object, a temporary aggregate, and perhaps countless other entities. If all of these objects occupy the same region and possess the same material parts, what prevents there from being an intolerable proliferation of objects? Conversely, if there is only one object, why does it seem to have different persistence conditions depending on whether it is described as a statue or as a lump of clay?

Hud Hudson’s three-dimensionalist Partist View attempts to address such issues by giving priority to parthood rather than to composition or identity. Its central strategy is to preserve a rich ontology of overlapping and coincident objects while distinguishing different relations of proper and improper parthood. Yet this approach faces a dialectical tension. If parthood is understood in a sufficiently liberal way, too many objects become parts of one another, and the distinction between proper and improper parthood collapses. If it is understood strictly, then the view cannot adequately represent coincident objects or the way a material object can support several different identities.

Hylomorphic Partism offers a promising modification. It retains the part-theoretic insight that objects are grounded in material constituents, but adds form as a principle of unity and identity. Its five-place constitution relation is intended to describe not merely that some matter constitutes an object, but that a particular material basis constitutes a particular compound in virtue of a particular form, under an appropriate mode or respect and at a time. The associated *in-F* relation is meant to capture the way something can be a constituent “in” a compound relative to a form without thereby being an ordinary proper part of that compound.

This essay evaluates whether that strategy succeeds. I argue that Hylomorphic Partism improves substantially upon Hudson’s three-dimensionalist Partist View. In particular, the *in-F* relation can resolve the immediate tension between proper and improper parthood by separating material constitution from ordinary parthood. Form can also function as a genuine principle of unity rather than as a mere label for a region or a mereological simple. Nevertheless, the proposal succeeds only if form is given substantive metaphysical constraints. If every possible description or arrangement counts as a form, then Hylomorphic Partism merely redescribes the problem of the many while allowing coincident objects to proliferate without limit. Moreover, the claim that multiple hylomorphic compounds may occupy exactly the same spacetime region is coherent only when the compounds differ in genuine formal organization or identity conditions. Mere appeal to form cannot by itself explain why a given form is present, why it unifies some matter rather than other matter, or why certain forms are ontologically privileged.

## I. The difficulties for three-dimensionalist Partism

A three-dimensionalist ontology treats objects as wholly present at each time at which they exist. It therefore does not explain persistence by appeal to temporal parts. Instead, an object persists by continuing to exist as the very same three-dimensional entity, perhaps because it retains a certain material constitution, structure, or causal organization.

Hudson’s Partist View is attractive because it attempts to avoid the familiar choice between universalism and nihilism. Universalism says that every plurality of objects composes an object; nihilism says that only simples exist, or that composition almost never occurs. Partism occupies a more permissive middle position. It takes parthood to be metaphysically basic and allows objects to be related through complex patterns of overlap and inclusion. The result is a picture in which ordinary objects, material aggregates, and perhaps coincident entities can all be accommodated.

The difficulty concerns the status of proper parthood. Suppose the clay constitutes a statue. If the clay and the statue occupy the same region and have the same material parts, then the statue cannot be a proper part of the clay in the ordinary extensional sense. A proper part must be distinct from its whole and must not be identical with it. But if the statue has no material part that the clay lacks, it is unclear what could make it a proper part rather than the whole clay itself.

One response is to say that the statue is an improper part of the clay: it is a part in the broad sense, but not a proper part. Yet this response does not identify the relevant relation. If both the statue and the clay are parts of one another, then either they are identical or parthood has become non-antisymmetric. If they are not parts of one another, then the broad notion of part is too weak to explain the apparent material dependence between them. The statue is not unrelated to the clay; it is made of it. But “made of” cannot simply be equated with ordinary parthood.

This produces the dialectical tension at the heart of the Partist strategy. The theory needs a generous notion of parthood to represent the intimate relation between coincident objects. At the same time, it needs a strict notion of proper parthood to preserve the distinction between an object and something that merely constitutes it. If the distinction is drawn too sharply, the theory cannot explain constitution. If it is drawn too loosely, the theory loses the difference between a whole and itself.

The problem of the many sharpens this tension. Consider a region filled by a quantity of matter. It seems possible to describe that matter as a lump, a statue, a temporary aggregate, and perhaps an indefinitely large number of objects corresponding to different descriptions. If the Partist View treats all of these as objects because they are related to the same material parts, then its ontology becomes dangerously abundant. But if it recognizes only one object, it must explain why the object has the persistence conditions of a statue in one context and of a lump in another. The ordinary difference between “the same statue” and “the same clay” cannot be reduced to a difference in spatial extension alone.

A purely three-dimensionalist Partism thus faces a dilemma. It can regard the statue and the clay as distinct, but then it must explain how distinct objects can share all their parts and occupy the same region. Or it can deny their distinctness, but then it must explain why destruction of the statue need not be destruction of the clay, and why reshaping the clay can destroy the statue while preserving the material object. The theory requires more than a bare relation of material inclusion.

## II. The five-place constitution relation

Hylomorphic Partism introduces a constitution relation designed to occupy this explanatory space. Since formulations differ, the relation may be represented schematically as:

\[
C(m, b, c, F, t).
\]

Here \(m\) is a material constituent or plurality of material constituents; \(b\) is the relevant material basis or body; \(c\) is the hylomorphic compound; \(F\) is the form in virtue of which the compound is unified; and \(t\) is the relevant time or temporal interval. The details of the notation are less important than the five-fold structure. Constitution is not simply a two-place relation between matter and object. It is matter’s constituting a compound through a form, in a specified material or constitutional mode, at a time.

The role of the material-basis place is important. A hylomorphic compound is not constituted by an arbitrary collection of material objects merely because they happen to occupy a region. The matter must stand in the appropriate relation to the form. Nor is the form itself simply another object placed alongside the matter. Form is a principle of organization, unity, and activity. It accounts for why the matter constitutes one thing rather than remaining a mere plurality.

The five-place structure therefore blocks a crude inference:

\[
\text{same matter} + \text{same region} \Rightarrow \text{same object}.
\]

Two compounds can share a material basis and a region while differing in form. They will then differ in identity conditions, capacities, and persistence conditions even if their spatial and material profiles temporarily coincide. The statue and the clay can be distinct because the statue is constituted under the form of the statue, whereas the clay is constituted under a more elementary or materially individuated form.

The relation also permits a more precise account of change. When a sculptor reshapes the clay, the clay may survive while the statue ceases to exist. What changes is not necessarily the entire material basis but the organization that makes that basis the matter of the statue. Conversely, if a statue acquires a small quantity of additional material through repair, the statue may survive even though its matter changes. The relevant continuity is formal and functional, not merely numerical identity of particles.

This does not mean that matter is irrelevant. Form cannot be instantiated in any material whatsoever. A bronze statue requires matter capable of supporting the relevant structure; a living organism requires matter organized in ways that sustain its characteristic activities. Constitution is therefore constrained both by material suitability and by form. The five-place relation is useful precisely because it prevents the constitution of an object from being determined by matter alone.

## III. The *in-F* relation and the distinction between constitution and parthood

The distinctive innovation is the *in-F* relation. In a simplified formulation, \(x\) is *in-F* \(y\) when \(x\) is a constituent of \(y\) under form \(F\). The relation may be read as: \(x\) is in \(y\) as a material constituent in virtue of \(F\). It is not identical with ordinary parthood. Rather, it expresses a form-relative mode of inclusion.

This distinction is philosophically significant. If the clay is *in-F* the statue, the clay need not be a proper part of the statue in the same sense in which a piece of clay is a proper part of a larger quantity of clay. The relation tracks constitution, not spatial inclusion. Similarly, a heart may be *in-F* an organism as an organ contributing to the organism’s life, while not being an object that simply stands alongside the organism within a common mereological hierarchy. The form determines the role that the constituent plays.

The *in-F* relation can therefore resolve the tension between proper and improper parthood in three ways.

First, it prevents the theory from treating constitution as ordinary parthood. The clay is not required to be a proper part of the statue, and the statue is not required to be an improper part of the clay. The fact that the statue is constituted by the clay is represented by a distinct relation.

Second, it preserves ordinary mereological principles within their proper domain. Extensionality, antisymmetry, and the distinction between proper and improper parts can continue to govern ordinary parthood without forcing the statue and clay to collapse. The statue and clay share a material basis but need not share a mereological structure in every respect.

Third, it gives a principled explanation of why the relation between the statue and the clay is asymmetric in one respect and symmetric in another. The statue depends on the clay as its matter. The clay, however, does not depend on the statue as its form, since the clay can survive the destruction of the statue. Thus the clay is constitutionally prior to the statue in one sense. But the statue is not simply a proper part of the clay, because its identity is determined by a different form. The *in-F* relation captures dependence without collapsing dependence into parthood.

Whether this genuinely resolves the dialectical tension depends on the logical behavior assigned to *in-F*. It should not be reflexive in the same way as ordinary parthood, and it should not be freely transitive. If \(x\) is *in-F* \(y\), and \(y\) is *in-G* \(z\), it does not follow that \(x\) is *in-H* \(z\) for some automatically determined form \(H\). The reason is that a constituent may contribute to a compound in one formal role without contributing in the same way to a larger compound. A carbon atom is part of a molecule, and the molecule may be a constituent of an organism, but the atom is not necessarily *in* the organism under the same form under which it is *in* the molecule.

The relation must also be distinguished from relative identity. It is tempting to say that the clay and statue are “the same object under different descriptions.” But this risks obscuring rather than explaining the difference. Relative identity can state that the clay and statue are the same lump but not the same statue, yet it does not explain why one set of identity conditions applies rather than another. The *in-F* relation, combined with the constitution relation, offers a metaphysical account: the same material basis enters into different compounds under different forms.

There remains a danger of trivialization. If every case of constitution can be represented by inventing an appropriate form, then *in-F* merely replaces the unexplained phrase “in virtue of” with a new symbol. A successful theory must therefore explain what forms are and what constrains them. Otherwise, the relation solves the logical problem only by postponing the metaphysical problem.

## IV. Form as a principle of unity

Form must do more than distinguish objects by name. It must explain why a plurality of material constituents counts as one compound. In this respect, form differs from a mereological simple and from region relativization.

A mereological simple is unified by lacking proper parts. Its unity is negative or structural: nothing more basic is present as a part. But a hylomorphic compound is not unified by simplicity. It is unified despite having many material constituents. Its unity consists in the way those constituents are organized and directed toward characteristic activities or capacities. A statue’s bronze is arranged so as to instantiate a determinate shape; an organism’s organs are coordinated so as to sustain life; a tool’s components are organized so as to perform a function. Form accounts for the unity of the many without reducing the compound to a simple.

This is not merely a matter of spatial arrangement. Two collections of matter may occupy the same shape while differing in organization. A corpse can have nearly the same spatial boundaries as a living organism, yet it is not alive in the same way. A disassembled machine may contain all the parts of an assembled machine but fail to be that machine. Conversely, a machine can undergo replacement of parts while preserving its functional organization. Form is therefore a principle of unity that includes structural, functional, causal, and modal dimensions.

Form also differs from region relativization. One might attempt to solve the problem of coincident objects by saying that there is one object relative to one region, another object relative to another conceptual scheme, or one object under one sortal and another under another sortal. But a region is not an organizing principle. It tells us where something is, not what makes it one thing, what it can do, or what changes it can survive.

Region relativization is especially weak in the statue and clay case because the statue and clay occupy the same region. Relativizing to the region therefore fails to distinguish them. Nor can it explain why the statue ceases to exist when the clay is flattened while the clay continues to exist. That difference concerns form and persistence, not location.

Form may also provide a basis for sortal-relative identity without reducing identity to language. The form of a statue determines that certain changes—minor scratches, repairs, or movements—are compatible with its continued existence, while others—flattening or melting—are not. The form of a lump of clay determines a different range of permissible changes. The difference is not simply that we use two words. It is that the material has entered into two distinct modes of unity.

The Aristotelian language of actuality and potentiality is useful here. Matter is capable of receiving different forms; form actualizes a particular organization and set of capacities. The clay is potentially a statue, but it is not actually a statue until it has the relevant organization. Once it is a statue, it retains the potential to become a lump again, but that potential does not imply that the statue and the lump are identical. The same material can support successive or simultaneous formal determinations.

Still, the appeal to form must avoid two opposite errors. It must not reduce form to an abstract universal that exists independently of its material instantiations. Nor should it treat form as a mysterious entity whose only function is to distinguish whatever objects the theory already wishes to recognize. Form should be individuated by its explanatory role: it organizes matter, grounds characteristic capacities, determines relevant persistence conditions, and supports a unified causal history.

## V. The problem of the many

Hylomorphic Partism promises to solve the problem of the many by restricting which material pluralities constitute genuine compounds. Not every arbitrary grouping of particles has a form. A statue form is not instantiated merely because one can draw a boundary around some particles and describe them as statue-like. There must be an appropriate organization capable of grounding the relevant identity and activities.

This restriction is a major advantage over unrestricted composition. A heap of sand may occupy a region, but its particles need not instantiate countless distinct hylomorphic forms. A form is not simply the set of boundaries associated with a description. It is an organizing principle. The existence of a compound therefore depends on more than the possibility of referring to it.

The theory can distinguish a natural unity from an arbitrary aggregate. A living organism, a functioning artifact, and perhaps a work of art have internal principles of organization. A random collection of pebbles generally does not. This distinction is not absolute or easy to apply, but it is at least metaphysically intelligible. The theory need not claim that every plurality composes, nor that composition occurs only in the case of simples.

However, the problem of the many reappears if forms are multiplied too freely. Suppose a region of clay is shaped into a statue. In addition to the statue and the clay, why not posit a statue-minus-one-atom, a statue-plus-one-atom, the object consisting of the statue and the air immediately surrounding it, or the temporary aggregate consisting of the statue and the sculptor’s hand? If each such plurality can be associated with a form, the ontology again becomes bloated.

The hylomorphic response must be that these alleged forms are not genuine principles of unity. A form must do explanatory work. It must organize the matter into a relatively stable, causally integrated, and identity-bearing whole. The statue-minus-one-atom may be a possible object of thought, but it need not be a genuine hylomorphic compound. Likewise, the region of air surrounding a statue does not normally contribute to the statue’s unity or characteristic activity.

Yet this response introduces vagueness. How much causal integration is enough? Are ships, clouds, heaps, and social institutions hylomorphic compounds? Is a pile of coins unified by a form, or only by an external relation? The theory may need a pluralism of forms, allowing different kinds of unity rather than forcing every object into a single model. But pluralism makes the theory more vulnerable to arbitrary proliferation.

There is also a deeper concern. If form determines identity conditions, then the theory must explain why a particular form is present in a material basis. Is form an immanent structure, a causal power, a universal, or a mode of organization? If it is a universal, the theory must explain how one universal can be instantiated in numerically distinct compounds. If it is a particular structure, it risks collapsing into a description of the matter. If it is a causal power, not every statue or artifact may have enough intrinsic causal unity to qualify. Each option has consequences.

Thus, Hylomorphic Partism does not solve the problem of the many simply by adding a fifth argument to the constitution relation. It solves it only if the form-place is constrained by a substantive account of unity. The five-place relation is a framework for solving the problem, not a complete solution by itself.

## VI. Can multiple hylomorphic compounds occupy the exact same region?

The proposal that multiple hylomorphic compounds can occupy exactly the same spacetime region is initially plausible. The statue and clay appear to be the paradigm. A single material basis can support more than one form, and distinct forms can determine distinct objects without requiring spatial separation.

Exact coincidence should therefore not be rejected merely because it violates an intuitive picture of physical objects. Spatial location is not the only criterion of individuation. Two objects can be co-located if they differ in structure, organization, causal powers, or persistence conditions. The relation between a statue and its clay is not like the relation between two ordinary pieces of matter occupying different places. It is a case of formal coincidence.

Nevertheless, the proposal is philosophically costly. If two objects have the same matter, same region, and same temporal history, what exactly distinguishes them? Hylomorphic Partism answers: their forms. But this answer must be more than a formal stipulation. The forms must generate different modal and causal profiles. The statue cannot survive flattening, while the clay can. The statue has aesthetic and artistic properties that the clay lacks, or at least has in a different way. The statue’s unity is determined by shape and artistic organization; the clay’s unity is determined by its material continuity. These differences justify counting two compounds.

There are cases where exact coincidence is less convincing. Consider a lump of clay and an arbitrary object consisting of the lump plus a microscopic quantity of surrounding dust. If both are said to be hylomorphic compounds, the theory must explain the difference in forms. The addition of dust may not create a new principle of unity. It may create a new plurality describable by language but not a new object. Coincidence is therefore acceptable only where distinct forms are genuinely instantiated.

A second concern is causal overdetermination. If the statue and clay occupy the same place and consist of the same matter, do both have causal powers? When the object is moved, are two objects moved? When it breaks, are two objects broken? If the answer is yes, there may be harmless redundancy, since the same event can be described at different formal levels. But if the statue and clay have independent causal effects, the theory risks multiplying causes without necessity.

The best response is to distinguish causal powers by formal role. The clay can be moldable, divisible, and capable of surviving changes of shape. The statue can be aesthetically valuable, representational, and vulnerable to destruction through loss of shape. These powers are not two independent physical forces. They are two profiles of one material reality under different forms. The coincident objects are metaphysically distinct but causally coordinated.

A third concern is whether exact coincidence is compatible with a robust account of identity. If the statue and clay can occupy the same region, then spatial boundaries do not determine identity. This is not itself a defect, but the theory must provide positive identity conditions. The statue is identical over time through continuity of statue-form; the clay through continuity of material substance. The forms determine different answers to counterfactual questions. If the matter had been flattened, would the object still exist? The answer is yes for the clay and no for the statue. These modal differences ground nonidentity.

The proposal is therefore defensible but not cheap. It requires the metaphysics of objects to be richer than the metaphysics of regions. Exact coincidence is not a license for arbitrary object multiplication. It is a consequence of the fact that material being can be organized in more than one way.

## VII. Does *in-F* fully defeat the Partist problem?

The *in-F* relation successfully addresses the specific problem that ordinary parthood cannot simultaneously express material dependence and preserve the distinction between proper and improper parts. It allows the theory to say that matter is constitutive of a compound without saying that the matter is a proper part in the ordinary extensional sense. This is a genuine theoretical improvement over a Partist View that tries to force all relevant relations into one parthood relation.

It also explains why the statue is not simply a proper part of the clay. The statue is constituted by the clay under a statue-form; it is not a smaller spatial or mereological segment of the clay. Likewise, the clay is not a proper part of the statue in the ordinary sense if the two coincide exactly. The relation between them is instead one of form-relative inclusion and dependence.

Yet *in-F* does not by itself resolve every dialectical difficulty. It must be supplemented by principles governing:

1. the identity of forms;
2. the conditions under which a form is instantiated;
3. the relation between form and material suitability;
4. the interaction between *in-F* and ordinary parthood;
5. the criteria for excluding arbitrary compounds.

Without these principles, the theory faces a familiar regress. If the statue is distinct from the clay because it has a statue-form, what makes the statue-form one form rather than a collection of formal aspects? Perhaps another form unifies the statue-form and the matter into a compound. If so, a further form may be needed to unify that compound, and so on. Hylomorphic theories traditionally answer that form is not an additional material component requiring further composition. It is a principle of unity, not another object that must itself be assembled. This response is plausible, but it depends on treating form as metaphysically fundamental in a way that mereological theories may resist.

There is also a question about whether *in-F* is too powerful. If an object can be *in-F* another without being a part, then what prevents all dependence relations from being translated into *in-F* relations? The theory requires a clear distinction between constitutional dependence and other forms of dependence. A cause may be necessary for an object without being *in-F* it. A sculptor may be necessary for a statue’s existence as an artifact, but the sculptor is not a material constituent of the statue. The *in-F* relation must therefore be restricted to constituents that participate in the compound’s internal unity.

The relation also needs to be sensitive to levels of organization. A cell may be *in-F* an organism, while molecules are *in-G* the cell. The same molecule may therefore be related to the organism through a different form from the one under which it is related to the cell. This layered structure is a strength, since it reflects the hierarchical organization of many real entities. But it makes the theory more complex than a simple part-whole ontology.

The final verdict is therefore conditional. The *in-F* relation resolves the logical tension only if it is treated as a genuine primitive or theoretically irreducible relation with a determinate domain and logical profile. If it is merely a new name for constitution, it does not explain anything. If, however, it expresses form-relative material inclusion while ordinary parthood remains extensional and antisymmetric, then it gives Hylomorphic Partism the conceptual resources that Hudson’s three-dimensionalist Partism lacks.

## Conclusion

The central insight of Hylomorphic Partism is that material constitution and mereological parthood should not be identified. The statue and the clay are related, but their relation is not adequately described by saying that one is a proper or improper part of the other. The clay constitutes the statue under a statue-form, and the statue exists as a hylomorphic compound whose identity and persistence conditions differ from those of the clay. The five-place constitution relation captures this complexity by relating material basis, compound, form, mode of constitution, and time. The *in-F* relation then expresses the form-relative inclusion of a constituent without forcing it into the ordinary hierarchy of proper and improper parts.

This strategy improves upon Hudson’s three-dimensionalist Partist View because it prevents a single parthood relation from doing incompatible jobs. It preserves the distinction between a whole and its proper parts while recognizing that objects may be materially coincident and constitutionally dependent. It also explains why the statue can cease to exist while the clay remains: the relevant form has been lost even though the material basis survives.

Form is indispensable to this account. It is not a mereological simple, because it unifies a plurality of material constituents. It is not a region, because it determines organization, capacities, and persistence rather than merely location. It is a principle of unity that makes matter into a determinate kind of object. In the statue and clay case, different forms explain how one material basis can support distinct compounds with different identity conditions.

The proposal that multiple hylomorphic compounds can occupy exactly the same spacetime region is consequently coherent, but only in a restricted sense. Coincidence is justified when distinct forms generate genuinely different modes of unity and different modal or causal profiles. It is not justified merely because several descriptions can be applied to the same region. Hylomorphic Partism must therefore resist the temptation to treat every conceptual partition as a form. Its success depends on a substantive theory of formal unity capable of distinguishing natural or institutionally grounded compounds from arbitrary aggregates.

The introduction of *in-F* does not, by itself, solve every problem. It does not eliminate the need to explain what forms are, how they are instantiated, or why they do not proliferate without limit. But it does resolve the most immediate structural defect in a purely Partist treatment: the inability of a single parthood relation to express both material constitution and strict proper parthood. Hylomorphic Partism is thus best understood not as a rejection of mereology, but as a supplementation of mereology. It recognizes that the unity of an object is not exhausted by the collection of things spatially or materially contained within it. Matter requires form if it is to be one thing rather than merely many, and constitution requires a relation richer than parthood if coincident objects are to be understood without contradiction.
