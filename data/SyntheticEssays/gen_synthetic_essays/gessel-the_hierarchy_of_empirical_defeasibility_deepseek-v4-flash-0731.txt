**Title: The Fragile Certainty: Sensory Modalities and the Hierarchy of Empirical Defeasibility**

**Introduction**

The Cartesian quest for an indubitable foundation upon which to build the edifice of human knowledge remains one of philosophy’s most persistent and haunting ambitions. In its empirical guise, this ambition manifests as the thesis of *conclusive empirical indefeasibility*: the claim that certain beliefs, derived directly from sensory experience, are so securely justified that no further empirical evidence could possibly overturn them. According to this view, when I see a red apple in broad daylight, or feel the hard surface of a table beneath my hand, I possess a certainty that is immune to revision. The belief is not merely *justified*; it is *conclusive*, and its justification is *indefeasible*—meaning that no overriding or undermining evidence can strip it of its epistemic warrant.

In this essay, I will argue that this thesis is fundamentally mistaken. No empirical belief is absolutely indefeasible. However, a rigorous examination of the structure of sensory modalities reveals that empirical beliefs are not all equally vulnerable to defeat. Rather, the distinct *experience kinds* and *information types* generated by different sensory modalities—and their various combinations—ground a strict *hierarchy of empirical defeasibility*. This hierarchy demonstrates that while some beliefs are extraordinarily robust, their robustness is a matter of degree, contingent upon the complexity and integration of the perceptual sources from which they arise, rather than an absolute property of empirical certainty.

To substantiate this claim, I will proceed in five stages. First, I will establish a precise logical framework for understanding empirical defeaters, distinguishing between overriding and undermining forms. Second, I will examine Bryce Gessell’s crucial distinction between “flash vision” and “spherical vision,” demonstrating how the informational richness of the visual field determines its resistance to defeat. Third, I will analyze how *compound perceptually justified beliefs*—those integrating multiple sensory modalities—achieve a remarkable resilience against testimonial defeaters, though not absolute immunity. Fourth, I will critically assess the traditional claim that tactile confirmation provides an unassailable guarantee of material existence, ultimately showing that even this most fundamental of sensory anchors is vulnerable to radical skeptical scenarios. Finally, I will synthesize these findings into a coherent hierarchy of defeasibility, concluding that while empirical beliefs can be *practically* conclusive, they remain *logically* defeasible, and the structure of our sensory apparatus dictates precisely where they fall on this spectrum.

**Section 1: The Logic of Empirical Defeasibility**

To evaluate the thesis of conclusive indefeasibility, we must first clarify what constitutes a *defeater*. In contemporary epistemology, following the influential work of John Pollock, defeaters are typically divided into two categories: *overriding* defeaters and *undermining* defeaters.

An *overriding* defeater provides positive evidence that the original belief is *false*. For example, suppose I look at a straight stick partially submerged in water and believe that it is bent. My visual experience justifies this belief. However, if a trusted physics teacher explains the principles of refraction, I acquire an overriding defeater. The new evidence does not merely cast doubt on my perceptual process; it directly contradicts the content of my belief, providing a superior explanation for the appearance of the stick. The belief that the stick is bent is defeated because a competing, more coherent belief (that it is straight but appears bent) takes its place.

An *undermining* defeater, conversely, does not attack the truth of the belief directly but rather attacks the *reliability* or *justificatory status* of the source from which the belief arose. Suppose I believe I see a red wall, but then I am informed that I have unknowingly taken a drug that causes me to see red objects as blue and vice versa. This information does not tell me the wall is not red; it tells me that my visual experience is not a reliable guide to color in this instance. The justification for my belief is *undermined* because the epistemic trustworthiness of the perceptual faculty itself is called into question.

The thesis of conclusive empirical indefeasibility claims that there exists a class of beliefs, B, such that for any belief b in B, there is no possible empirical defeater—neither overriding nor undermining—that can successfully eliminate the justification for b. This is an extremely strong claim. It entails that the evidence for b is so perfect, so complete, and so self-validating that no future experience, no matter how bizarre or systematic, could render it unjustified.

This thesis faces an immediate structural problem. Empirical beliefs are *contingent*; they are about the state of the external world. The connection between a sensory experience and the external state of affairs it purports to represent is mediated by a complex causal chain—light waves, neural firings, cognitive processing. Any link in this chain is theoretically fallible. The mere *possibility* of a malfunction, a deception, or an alternative causal explanation introduces a potential undermining defeater. Therefore, for an empirical belief to be *absolutely* indefeasible, it would have to be the case that the experience itself somehow guarantees its own veridicality, which seems to require a kind of infallible access to the world that empirical beings do not possess. However, this does not mean all empirical beliefs are equally fragile. The *degree* to which a belief resists defeat is determined by the *quality* and *quantity* of the informational support it receives from the sensory modalities.

**Section 2: Gessell’s Distinction: Flash Vision and Spherical Vision**

The philosopher Bryce Gessell, in his recent work on the epistemology of perception, has offered a powerful framework for understanding how the *structure* of visual experience determines its epistemic weight. Gessell distinguishes between what he calls *flash vision* and *spherical vision*. This distinction is not merely phenomenological; it has profound implications for the defeasibility of the beliefs formed on their basis.

*Flash vision* refers to a momentary, isolated, and decontextualized visual snapshot. Imagine a single frame of a film, stripped of its temporal neighbors, or a brief glimpse of a shape in a dark room before the light is extinguished. Flash vision is characterized by a paucity of *information types*. It provides data about local luminance, basic shapes, and perhaps rudimentary colors, but it lacks crucial relational properties. It does not include depth cues derived from motion parallax, it does not integrate with proprioceptive feedback about the position of one's head or body, and it is not embedded in a continuous temporal stream that would allow for object tracking and persistence.

The epistemic status of beliefs derived from flash vision is exceedingly fragile. Suppose I experience a flash of red in my peripheral vision. I form the belief, "There is a red object over there." This belief is highly susceptible to both overriding and undermining defeaters. An overriding defeater might be the subsequent turning on of the lights, which reveals that the "red object" was merely a reflection of a passing car's taillight on a glossy surface. An undermining defeater is even easier to construct: "You blinked at that exact moment, and the afterimage created the flash," or "You have a retinal detachment that is causing photopsia." Because flash vision provides so little *informational redundancy*—because there is no cross-checking with other visual cues or other modalities—the justificatory chain is thin. A single, modestly plausible alternative explanation is sufficient to topple the belief.

*Spherical vision*, on the other hand, refers to the immersive, embodied, panoramic visual field that characterizes ordinary human perception. It is not a snapshot but a continuous, flowing stream of experience. It includes not only the foveal focus but also the peripheral awareness of the environment. It is rich with depth cues—binocular disparity, motion parallax, occlusion, and shading. Crucially, spherical vision is *egocentrically anchored*; it is experienced from a specific, bodily perspective, and it constantly updates as the subject moves through space. The information type is not discrete and isolated but *relational* and *holistic*. When I see a table in spherical vision, I do not merely see a brown rectangle. I see a three-dimensional object with a top, sides, and legs. I see its shadow cast on the floor. I see how it occludes the wall behind it. I see that as I move my head, the perspective shifts in a lawful manner.

This richness has a direct impact on defeasibility. The belief "There is a table in front of me" formed on the basis of spherical vision is vastly more resistant to defeat than the belief formed on the basis of flash vision. Consider an undermining defeater: "You are hallucinating." To successfully undermine the spherical vision belief, the defeater must explain away the *entire* coherent, structured visual field. It must account for the fact that the table's perspective shifts consistently with my bodily movements, that its shading is consistent with the ambient lighting, and that it maintains its shape as I walk around it. A mere "hallucination" is too vague to serve this purpose. The internal coherence of spherical vision provides a kind of *prima facie* validation; the belief is justified not by a single data point, but by a vast, interlocking network of visual information that collectively points to the existence of a stable, external object.

However, Gessell's distinction does not rescue the thesis of conclusive indefeasibility. While spherical vision is more robust than flash vision, it is not *invulnerable*. A sufficiently sophisticated deceiver—a Cartesian demon or a hyper-advanced virtual reality simulator—could generate a perfectly coherent spherical visual experience that is entirely false. The coherence of the visual field only proves that the *experience* is internally consistent, not that it corresponds to an external reality. To defeat the belief derived from spherical vision, one would need a *global* undermining defeater, such as "You are a brain in a vat being fed this exact visual input." Such a defeater is logically possible, and therefore the belief remains *empirically defeasible*, albeit at a much higher threshold. Gessell’s insight is that the *information type* matters: relational, holistic information is harder to fake than discrete, isolated information, but it is not impossible to fake.

**Section 3: Compound Perceptually Justified Beliefs and Testimonial Defeaters**

The hierarchy of defeasibility becomes even more pronounced when we move from single-modality perceptions to *compound perceptually justified beliefs*. These are beliefs formed not from a single sensory modality, but from the integrated, simultaneous input of multiple modalities. Consider my belief that I am holding a hot cup of coffee. This belief is not solely visual. I see the brown liquid, the white ceramic, and the rising steam. I feel the warmth radiating from the cup against my hand, and I feel the pressure of its weight against my palm. I might hear the faint sloshing of the liquid as I move it. Each of these sensory inputs provides a distinct *information type*. Vision provides spatial layout and color; touch provides thermal and textural data; proprioception provides weight and resistance; audition provides acoustic confirmation of liquid state.

The integration of these diverse information types generates a *compound justification* that is qualitatively stronger than the sum of its parts. This is because the inputs are *cross-modal* and *mutually constraining*. If my vision tells me the cup is full, but my proprioception tells me it is surprisingly light, I have a conflict that requires resolution. The fact that all modalities agree—that the visual fullness, the tactile heat, the proprioceptive weight, and the auditory slosh all cohere—provides a powerful form of epistemic triangulation. The likelihood that all these independent sensory channels would malfunction in a coordinated way to produce a consistent, false picture of a coffee cup is astronomically lower than the likelihood that a single channel would malfunction.

This robustness is particularly evident when we consider *testimonial defeaters*. A testimonial defeater is a challenge to my belief that comes from the testimony of another person. For example, a friend tells me, "That isn't a cup of coffee; it's a cleverly designed prop that looks and feels exactly like a cup of coffee, but it's actually made of solid plastic and contains no liquid."

How does my compound belief fare against this challenge? It fares remarkably well. The testimonial defeater asks me to believe that my visual perception of steam is illusory, that my tactile sensation of heat is false, that my proprioceptive sense of the sloshing weight is mistaken, and that my auditory perception of liquid movement is a trick. To accept the testimony, I must posit a massive, systematic failure across *multiple* independent sensory modalities.

The epistemic asymmetry here is crucial. The testimonial defeater is a *single* source of information (the friend's assertion) that must override *multiple* sources of information (my five senses). In the absence of any reason to distrust my senses, the testimony is simply overwhelmed. My compound belief is *resistant* to the testimonial defeater not because it is logically impossible for the friend to be right, but because the *burden of proof* has shifted so heavily. To successfully defeat the compound belief, the testimony must be accompanied by a higher-order explanation of *why* my senses are all failing. For instance, if my friend is a neuroscientist who shows me a brain scan indicating that I am in a simulated reality, the testimony gains the status of a *global undermining defeater*. But note: even then, the defeater does not rely on the testimony *alone*; it relies on testimony *plus* a new set of empirical evidence (the brain scan) that itself must be processed and trusted.

This analysis reveals a critical point about the hierarchy of defeasibility. Compound perceptually justified beliefs are not merely *more* justified; they are *structurally* more resistant to certain classes of defeaters, particularly those that rely on single-source challenges like ordinary testimony. The information types are so deeply integrated that a defeater must attack the entire network of perceptual coherence to be successful. This is why we are pragmatically certain about the existence of the coffee cup, and why we would dismiss a friend's casual assertion that it is a hologram. However, this pragmatic certainty is not the same as logical indefeasibility. The possibility of a global simulation, a Cartesian demon, or a radical skeptical scenario remains. The compound belief is *empirically* indefeasible only in the sense that no *ordinary* empirical defeater can touch it; it is not indefeasible in the face of *extraordinary* or *global* defeaters. The key takeaway is that the *integration* of modalities creates a higher rung on the hierarchy, but it does not leave the ladder entirely.

**Section 4: The Tactile Guarantee? A Critical Assessment of Material Existence**

If any empirical belief has a claim to being conclusive and indefeasible, it is the belief in the existence of a material object based on *tactile confirmation*. The philosophical tradition, from Aristotle to G.E. Moore, has often privileged touch as the most fundamental and trustworthy of the senses regarding external existence. Vision can be deceived by mirages and refraction; audition by echoes; olfaction and gustation by chemical mimics. But touch, it is argued, involves a direct physical confrontation with the world. When I press my hand against the rough bark of a tree, I feel its resistance. This resistance is not a mere appearance; it is a physical force exerted against my body. The experience of *pressure* and *solidity* seems to provide an unassailable guarantee that something exists outside my mind to exert that pressure.

This intuition is powerful. The phenomenology of touch is distinct from vision. In vision, the object is experienced as distant, mediated by light. In touch, the object is experienced as *co-present* with the body, in a state of mutual interaction. The information type generated by touch is primarily *kinesthetic* and *proprioceptive*—it involves the sensation of muscular effort, joint position, and the deformation of the skin. This type of information is tightly coupled with the experience of an *external* cause. When I push against a wall, I feel the wall pushing back. This reciprocal resistance is the very definition of materiality.

However, a critical assessment reveals that tactile confirmation does **not** provide an unassailable guarantee. There are several lines of attack.

First, consider the phenomenon of *phantom limbs*. Amputees frequently report vivid, detailed tactile sensations in the missing limb, including feelings of pressure, heat, and even pain. These sensations are entirely generated by the brain and do not correspond to any external object. The subject feels the "resistance" of a phantom object, yet no such object exists. This demonstrates that the neural machinery responsible for tactile experience can generate the *feeling* of material contact without any actual material contact. The experience of resistance is not a reliable indicator of an external cause, because it can be produced endogenously.

Second, consider *tactile hallucinations*. These are less common than visual or auditory hallucinations but are well-documented. Patients with certain neurological conditions or under the influence of certain drugs report feeling insects crawling on their skin, or feeling a hand gripping their arm, when no such stimulus is present. Again, the tactile information type—the sensation of pressure, movement, and contact—is generated without a corresponding external object. If tactile experience can be entirely hallucinated, then the mere occurrence of a tactile sensation cannot serve as a *logically* conclusive proof of material existence.

Third, and most devastatingly, we must consider the *Brain-in-a-Vat (BIV)* scenario. Imagine a brain suspended in a vat of nutrients, connected to a supercomputer that perfectly simulates all sensory input, including tactile input. The computer can simulate the exact neural firing patterns that would occur if a real hand were pressing against a real table. The BIV would have a tactile experience that is *phenomenally identical* to my own experience of touching a table. It would feel the same resistance, the same texture, the same temperature. Yet, the BIV's belief that "there is a table in front of me" would be false. There is no table; there is only a computer simulation.

The BIV scenario is a *global undermining defeater*. It does not merely attack the reliability of a single sensory channel; it attacks the reliability of *all* empirical channels simultaneously. It posits that the entire causal chain from the external world to my subjective experience is systematically distorted. In this scenario, my tactile belief is false, but it is *empirically* indistinguishable from a true belief. The information type provided by touch, no matter how rich or compelling, is ultimately just a pattern of neural activity. The brain cannot distinguish between a pattern caused by a real external object and a pattern caused by a simulation that mimics that object perfectly.

Therefore, tactile confirmation fails to provide an unassailable guarantee of material existence. It provides the *strongest possible empirical justification* for such a belief, because it involves a direct, embodied, and reciprocal interaction that is far more complex than passive visual observation. But the possibility of a global simulation or a systematic neural malfunction introduces a logical gap between the experience of resistance and the existence of a resisting object. The belief in material existence based on touch is *empirically conclusive* in the sense that no *ordinary* empirical evidence could ever override it—I cannot imagine a mundane experience that would convince me that the table I am touching is not real. However, it is *not* *metaphysically* or *logically* indefeasible, because the possibility of a radical skeptical scenario remains a coherent, albeit fantastical, hypothesis.

**Section 5: Toward a Hierarchy of Empirical Defeasibility**

The preceding analysis allows us to construct a nuanced hierarchy of empirical defeasibility, grounded in the distinct experience kinds and information types generated by our sensory modalities. The hierarchy is determined by two primary factors: *informational richness* (the amount and complexity of data provided by a single modality) and *cross-modal integration* (the degree to which multiple modalities constrain and confirm each other).

**Level 1: Flash Vision (Highest Defeasibility).** Beliefs derived from isolated, momentary, single-modal snapshots. Information type: sparse, discrete, lacking relational context. Defeaters: easily overridden by subsequent evidence (e.g., turning on the lights) or undermined by minor physiological explanations (e.g., "you blinked," "it was an afterimage"). The justificatory chain is so thin that a single plausible alternative explanation suffices to break it.

**Level 2: Spherical Vision (Moderate Defeasibility).** Beliefs derived from the immersive, continuous, embodied visual field. Information type: rich, relational, holistic, egocentrically anchored. Defeaters: resistant to *ordinary* undermining defeaters (e.g., "you are hallucinating") because the internal coherence of the visual field provides a high degree of self-validation. However, vulnerable to *global* undermining defeaters (e.g., "you are in a sophisticated simulation") and to *cross-modal* overriding defeaters (e.g., if I see a solid object but my hand passes through it). The belief is more robust, but not immune.

**Level 3: Compound Perceptual Beliefs (Low Defeasibility).** Beliefs derived from the integration of multiple sensory modalities (e.g., seeing, feeling, hearing, and smelling a coffee cup). Information type: cross-modal, mutually constraining, highly redundant. Defeaters: highly resistant to *testimonial* defeaters and to *single-source* undermining defeaters. To defeat such a belief, one must posit a coordinated failure across multiple independent sensory channels, which requires a high-order explanatory theory (e.g., a brain scan showing a simulation). The burden of proof on the challenger is immense. The belief is *practically* conclusive.

**Level 4: Tactile-Material Beliefs (Lowest Defeasibility).** Beliefs derived from the direct, embodied, and reciprocal interaction of the body with a resisting object (e.g., "I am touching a solid table"). Information type: kinesthetic, proprioceptive, involving pressure and resistance. Defeaters: resistant to *all* ordinary empirical defeaters. No mundane experience can override the feeling of solidity. However, vulnerable to *global skeptical* defeaters (e.g., the BIV scenario, the Cartesian demon). The belief is the *apex* of empirical justification, but it is not *absolute*.

This hierarchy demonstrates that the thesis of conclusive empirical indefeasibility is false. There is no empirical belief that is *absolutely* immune to defeat, because the connection between experience and the external world is always contingent and fallible. However, the hierarchy shows that the *
