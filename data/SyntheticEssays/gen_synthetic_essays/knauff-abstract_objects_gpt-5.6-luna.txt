# Descartes, D-minus, and the Alleged Failure of Van Inwagen’s Reductio

Peter van Inwagen’s argument against the doctrine of arbitrary undetached parts (DAUP) is one of the most influential contemporary challenges to unrestricted mereological composition. DAUP is the thesis that, whenever some objects exist, there also exists an object composed of any arbitrary selection of their parts. If a human body has a head, torso, arms, and legs, then DAUP appears to imply that there is an object consisting of the torso and one arm, or the left half of the body, or every part except the right leg. Such objects are often called arbitrary undetached parts because they are not necessarily physically detached from the larger objects whose parts they are. They may overlap the larger object while nevertheless being distinct from it.

Van Inwagen’s reductio attempts to show that DAUP has absurd consequences. His central example concerns Descartes and an object called “D-minus,” which consists of Descartes minus one of his legs. Before Descartes loses the leg, Descartes and D-minus are apparently distinct: Descartes has the leg, whereas D-minus does not. After Descartes loses the leg, however, D-minus appears to be identical with Descartes, since both then consist of exactly the same matter or parts. Yet if D-minus is identical with Descartes after the loss, and D-minus existed before the loss, transitivity of identity seems to imply that D-minus was identical with Descartes before the loss as well. That conclusion conflicts with their apparent distinctness before the loss.

Some critics have argued that the reductio depends upon an ambiguity in the term “Descartes.” On one interpretation, “Descartes” denotes the material human organism located in space and composed of bodily parts. On another, it denotes an abstract entity, such as a person, a biography, a mind, a soul, or perhaps a temporally extended individual. If the argument shifts between these interpretations, then the alleged contradiction may be manufactured rather than discovered. The argument would seem to establish a violation of the transitivity of identity only because the relata of the identity claims have silently changed.

The question, then, is whether this ambiguity genuinely defeats van Inwagen’s reductio. Does the argument illicitly move between material and abstract interpretations of “Descartes”? Do the four formal identity claims generated by the argument involve different entities at different stages? And does a strict distinction between material objects and abstract objects successfully insulate DAUP from van Inwagen’s rejection?

The answer requires separating several issues that are often run together. First, there is the question of what “Descartes” denotes. Second, there is the question of whether the temporal expressions “before the loss” and “after the loss” introduce different objects or merely different temporal stages of one object. Third, there is the distinction between numerical identity and coincidence. Finally, there is the question of what sort of entities DAUP quantifies over. Once these issues are made explicit, it becomes clear that ambiguity can expose an illegitimate version of the reductio, but it does not by itself refute the reductio when its terms are consistently interpreted.

## I. The Structure of the Reductio

Let \(D\) denote Descartes and let \(D^{-}\) denote D-minus, the object consisting of all of Descartes’s parts except one leg. The doctrine of arbitrary undetached parts entails, or at least appears to entail, that \(D^{-}\) exists even before Descartes loses the leg. DAUP does not permit us to say merely that there could be such an object after the leg is removed. It says that an arbitrary collection of parts already composes an object. Thus, while Descartes still has both legs, there is supposedly an object consisting of Descartes minus one leg.

The argument then considers a later time at which Descartes actually loses that leg. At that later time, Descartes consists of exactly those parts that constituted D-minus before the loss. The two objects therefore coincide after the loss. If one assumes that an object is wholly determined by its parts, or that coincidence of all parts is sufficient for identity, then D-minus and Descartes are identical after the loss.

The intuitive conflict can be represented as follows:

1. Before the loss, Descartes has a leg.
2. Before the loss, D-minus lacks that leg.
3. Therefore, before the loss, Descartes and D-minus are distinct.
4. D-minus exists before the loss because DAUP says that the arbitrary collection of parts composes an object.
5. After the loss, Descartes has exactly the parts that D-minus has.
6. Therefore, after the loss, Descartes and D-minus are identical.
7. If D-minus is identical with Descartes after the loss, and Descartes before the loss is identical with Descartes after the loss, then D-minus was identical with Descartes before the loss.
8. But they were distinct before the loss.
9. Therefore, DAUP generates a contradiction.

The key premise is not simply that D-minus and Descartes overlap. They overlap before the loss as well as after it. The crucial claim is that they become identical after the loss while having been distinct before it. Van Inwagen’s argument therefore relies on the assumption that identity persists through time: if an object at one time is identical with an object at another time, then the object does not acquire a new identity merely by changing its parts. Descartes before the loss and Descartes after the loss are stages, or time-indexed manifestations, of one persisting object.

The reductio is powerful because it appears to exploit the ordinary logic of identity. Identity is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. If \(a=b\), and \(b=c\), then \(a=c\). Identity is also not normally regarded as temporary. An object cannot be identical with another object only after a certain event but not before it, unless the relevant expressions refer to different entities at the different times.

## II. The Four Formal Identity Claims

The argument can be made more precise by introducing temporal subscripts. Let:

- \(D_{B}\) be Descartes before the leg loss;
- \(D_{A}\) be Descartes after the leg loss;
- \(M_{B}\) be D-minus before the leg loss;
- \(M_{A}\) be D-minus after the leg loss.

The four central claims may then be represented as follows:

\[
(1)\quad D_{B}=D_{A}
\]

\[
(2)\quad M_{B}=M_{A}
\]

\[
(3)\quad M_{B}\neq D_{B}
\]

\[
(4)\quad M_{A}=D_{A}
\]

These are the four relations needed to produce the apparent contradiction.

The first identity says that Descartes survives the loss of the leg. The Descartes who exists before the accident is the same individual as the Descartes who exists afterward. This is a standard persistence claim. It does not say that Descartes has exactly the same parts at both times. It says that one object can survive a change in its parts.

The second identity says that D-minus also persists through the event. D-minus is the object composed of the relevant collection of parts, excluding the leg. Those parts remain together when Descartes loses the leg. Accordingly, the object existing before the loss as D-minus is the same object existing afterward as D-minus.

The third claim says that D-minus and Descartes are distinct before the loss. This follows from their different parts. If Descartes has the leg and D-minus does not, then they cannot be the same object, at least under the ordinary assumption that distinctness of parts entails distinctness of objects.

The fourth claim says that D-minus and Descartes are identical after the loss. Once the leg is gone, both appear to have the same parts. If the parts of an object determine its identity, they are one and the same object at that later time.

The contradiction follows immediately. From (2) and (4), we obtain:

\[
M_{B}=M_{A}=D_{A}
\]

From (1), we have:

\[
D_{A}=D_{B}
\]

Therefore:

\[
M_{B}=D_{B}
\]

But this contradicts (3), which says:

\[
M_{B}\neq D_{B}
\]

The argument can also be formulated without temporal subscripts, although doing so tends to conceal the source of the apparent paradox:

\[
D^{-}=D^{-}
\]

\[
D=D
\]

\[
D^{-}\neq D \quad \text{before the loss}
\]

\[
D^{-}=D \quad \text{after the loss}
\]

The temporal subscripts make clear that the argument is not simply asserting both \(D^{-}=D\) and \(D^{-}\neq D\ without qualification. It is asserting those relations at different times and then using persistence to show that the difference cannot be maintained.

The crucial philosophical question is whether the symbols \(D_{B}\), \(D_{A}\), \(M_{B}\), and \(M_{A}\) refer to the same kinds of entities. If they do not, then the displayed argument is not a genuine identity argument. It merely resembles one.

## III. The Ambiguity in “Descartes”

The name “Descartes” can plausibly be used in several ways. It may denote:

1. a material human organism;
2. a person;
3. a mind or soul;
4. an embodied subject;
5. an abstract individual;
6. a biography or historical figure;
7. a temporally extended four-dimensional object.

These interpretations are not equivalent. A material organism has spatial parts. A biography does not. A soul, if one believes in souls, may not have legs. A person may be understood as a biological organism, a psychological subject, or an abstract continuant. A historical figure may be an individual but not a material object in the relevant mereological sense.

This matters because van Inwagen’s argument needs “Descartes” to denote a material object that has a leg before the accident, loses the leg, and survives afterward. If “Descartes” denotes an abstract object, then the argument cannot straightforwardly say that Descartes has a leg, loses it, or is composed of a certain arrangement of bodily parts. Abstract objects are not located in space, are not vulnerable to physical injury, and do not undergo material change in the relevant way.

Suppose, for example, that in the first part of the argument “Descartes” denotes the material body of Descartes. Then \(D_{B}\) has a leg and \(D_{A}\) lacks it. But suppose that in the claim “D-minus is identical with Descartes after the loss,” “Descartes” denotes the abstract person or mind of Descartes. Then the identity claim is not between two material objects. D-minus is a concrete material object, whereas the abstract Descartes is not. The two relata do not even belong to the same ontological category. The apparent identity \(M_{A}=D_{A}\) is therefore false or ill-typed, not paradoxical.

Similarly, suppose “Descartes” before the loss denotes the living human organism, while “Descartes” after the loss denotes the person or subject who persists through bodily change. Then the argument may seem to establish:

\[
M_{B}\neq D_{B}
\]

and

\[
M_{A}=D_{A}
\]

But this does not create a contradiction unless \(D_{B}\) and \(D_{A}\) are stages of the same sort of entity. If \(D_{B}\) is a body and \(D_{A}\) is a person, then the identity \(D_{B}=D_{A}\) is itself unavailable. The chain of identity has been interrupted at its first step.

This is the basis of the ambiguity objection. The reductio may seem to use “Descartes” in two different senses: first as the material organism that includes the leg, and later as an abstract or non-material individual who remains the same despite the loss. If so, the conclusion that identity is non-transitive is an artifact of equivocation. The terms in the apparent chain are not stable. There is no single \(D\) standing in all the relevant relations.

## IV. Does the Ambiguity Manufacture a Violation of Transitivity?

The answer depends upon how the argument is reconstructed.

If the argument is reconstructed with an equivocal use of “Descartes,” then yes, the apparent violation of transitivity is manufactured. Consider the following illicit sequence:

\[
M_{B}\neq D^{\text{body}}_{B}
\]

\[
D^{\text{body}}_{B}=D^{\text{person}}_{A}
\]

\[
M_{A}=D^{\text{person}}_{A}
\]

Here the first “Descartes” is a body and the later “Descartes” is a person. The middle identity is not plausible, and the argument cannot infer that \(M_{B}=D^{\text{body}}_{B}\). The symbol \(D\) has changed reference. What appears to be a chain of identity is actually a chain involving different relations between different entities.

The problem is especially clear if the abstract interpretation is taken seriously. An abstract object does not have a leg as a constituent. It cannot lose a leg. Nor can a concrete material object be identical with an abstract object merely because both are associated with Descartes. A material body may instantiate a person, constitute a person, or be associated with a person, but constitution and instantiation are not identity. If the argument moves from “the body of Descartes” to “the person Descartes,” it has substituted a nonidentity relation for identity.

This point can be generalized. The expression “Descartes” may function as a proper name, a description, or a context-sensitive label. In ordinary language, we can say that Descartes lost a leg, that Descartes wrote the *Meditations*, and that Descartes was a philosopher. These statements need not all select exactly the same ontological object in every metaphysical theory. One may regard “Descartes” in the first statement as a living body, in the second as a person or agent, and in the third as a historical individual. Ordinary language tolerates this flexibility because practical communication does not require an exact theory of identity.

A reductio in metaphysics cannot rely on that flexibility without qualification. It must specify the reference of its terms. If “Descartes” changes referent, then the contradiction is not a contradiction in the doctrine of arbitrary undetached parts. It is a contradiction introduced by imprecise language.

However, it does not follow that van Inwagen’s argument fails once the ambiguity is noticed. The ambiguity objection defeats only an equivocal formulation. Van Inwagen can stipulate that “Descartes” denotes the same material object throughout the argument. Let \(D\) be a particular concrete organism. Let \(D^{-}\) be a particular concrete object composed of some but not all of the organism’s parts. Then all four claims concern material objects:

\[
D_{B}=D_{A}
\]

\[
D^{-}_{B}=D^{-}_{A}
\]

\[
D^{-}_{B}\neq D_{B}
\]

\[
D^{-}_{A}=D_{A}
\]

No shift between abstract and material interpretations is required. The argument can be stated entirely in the language of concrete bodies. If the four claims are jointly inconsistent, the ambiguity of “Descartes” is irrelevant to that version of the reductio.

The objection therefore establishes a conditional result: if van Inwagen’s argument relies on an unannounced shift from a material Descartes to an abstract Descartes, it fails as stated. But if “Descartes” is held fixed as a material continuant, the objection does not dissolve the paradox.

## V. Identity, Coincidence, and the Parts of an Object

A further complication concerns the transition from “having the same parts after the loss” to “being identical after the loss.” This transition is not universally accepted. It presupposes a strong principle of extensionality for material objects: if two objects have exactly the same parts, then they are identical. Many philosophers reject this principle because distinct objects can coincide spatially and share all their matter. A statue and the lump of clay composing it are a standard example. They may have the same matter at a time while differing in persistence conditions. The statue can survive certain changes that the lump cannot, or vice versa.

If extensionality is rejected, then D-minus and Descartes could coincide after the loss without being identical. D-minus might be an object whose essential parts are all of Descartes’s parts except the leg. Descartes might be an organism with a different criterion of persistence. After the loss, the two objects could occupy the same region and have the same current parts while remaining numerically distinct. The fourth identity claim would then be false:

\[
M_{A}\neq D_{A}
\]

rather than:

\[
M_{A}=D_{A}
\]

Van Inwagen’s argument would not generate a contradiction, but not because of ambiguity between material and abstract objects. It would fail because the theory permits coincident material objects.

This response is important, but it changes the target. It does not show that DAUP is harmless in general. It shows that DAUP can be combined with a theory of material coincidence or non-extensionality. Whether that combination is philosophically plausible is a separate question. Van Inwagen’s own argument is designed to pressure those who accept a sparse ontology of material objects and who believe that sameness of parts is sufficient for identity.

There is also a distinction between temporary coincidence and identity through time. Even if D-minus and Descartes are distinct before and after the accident, they might coincide after the accident. The fact that two objects have the same parts at one time does not by itself entail that they are identical. If the argument assumes that they are identical after the loss, it must defend that assumption independently.

Nevertheless, many versions of DAUP are naturally associated with extensionalism. If arbitrary sums are generated solely by their parts, then it is tempting to say that any two sums with the same parts are the same sum. On that view, the identity claim after the loss is not arbitrary. It follows from the theory’s treatment of composition. The challenge is then to explain why D-minus was a distinct object before the loss but becomes identical with Descartes afterward.

## VI. The Role of Temporal Stages

One response to van Inwagen is to deny that \(D_{B}=D_{A}\). A four-dimensionalist may say that Descartes before the loss and Descartes after the loss are different temporal parts of a larger spacetime worm. If so, the first formal identity claim is false. \(D_{B}\) and \(D_{A}\) are not the same object; they are distinct temporal segments.

This move can block the inference from the later identity to the earlier identity. If \(D_{B}\) and \(D_{A}\) are different objects, then from:

\[
M_{A}=D_{A}
\]

and

\[
D_{B}\neq D_{A}
\]

one cannot infer:

\[
M_{B}=D_{B}
\]

The paradox disappears because the ordinary notion of persistence has been replaced by a temporal-parts ontology.

But again, this is not an ambiguity objection. It is a substantive metaphysical response concerning persistence. It rejects the claim that the same material object exists before and after the loss. The four-dimensionalist may preserve a kind of identity between the whole spacetime worm and its temporal parts, but not the identity of the pre-loss and post-loss stages.

An endurantist, by contrast, holds that the same object is wholly present at each time. For the endurantist, \(D_{B}=D_{A}\) is natural, though not mandatory. The question then becomes how an enduring object can survive the loss of one of its parts. If Descartes can survive the loss, the mereological object D-minus appears to survive as well. The difficulty arises because D-minus and Descartes converge after the loss.

Thus, the paradox may be resisted by adopting temporal parts, by allowing coincidence, or by rejecting the claim that arbitrary sums persist. Each response attacks a different premise. None depends merely on distinguishing material and abstract objects.

## VII. What Does DAUP Actually Quantify Over?

The appeal to “abstract Descartes” raises a foundational question about the domain of DAUP. DAUP is generally understood as a thesis about material composition. It says, roughly, that for any collection of material parts, there is a material object composed of them. It does not say that every abstract object has arbitrary abstract parts, nor that every concept, property, number, or person has arbitrary mereological sums.

If DAUP is restricted to material objects, then D-minus is material. The Descartes relevant to the argument should therefore also be material, if D-minus is to be compared with Descartes as a candidate for identity. An abstract Descartes lies outside the domain of the doctrine. Invoking it cannot undermine DAUP, because the doctrine never asserted anything about abstract entities.

This gives force to the strict-distinction strategy. Let the ontology contain two disjoint categories:

- concrete material objects, which occupy space and have material parts;
- abstract objects, which do not occupy space and do not have material parts.

DAUP applies only to the first category. “Descartes,” if understood as a material organism, is within its scope. “Descartes,” if understood as an abstract historical individual or person, is not. Therefore, any argument that identifies D-minus with abstract Descartes is invalid because it crosses an ontological category boundary.

The question is whether this strategy is sufficient to insulate DAUP from van Inwagen’s rejection. It is sufficient against one version of the reductio: the version that relies on ambiguity. But it is not sufficient against the material-object version. Van Inwagen can simply insist that the argument concerns a concrete organism named “Descartes.” Nothing in the reductio requires an abstract entity. The body before the accident and the body afterward are both material objects. D-minus is a material object throughout. The alleged contradiction remains entirely within the domain to which DAUP applies.

The strict distinction therefore blocks an illicit inference but does not settle the material case. It tells us that “Descartes” cannot shift from body to person without argument. It does not show that there is no problem concerning a body and its arbitrary undetached part.

## VIII. Can D-minus Be Material While Descartes Is Abstract?

There is a more specific issue concerning the phrase “D-minus.” If “Descartes” is abstract, then what could it mean to subtract a leg from him? An abstract entity has no leg as a material part. D-minus could not be an arbitrary undetached part of abstract Descartes. The expression would be semantically defective.

One might try to reinterpret D-minus as the material body associated with the abstract person Descartes. But then the relation between D-minus and Descartes is not identity. It might be embodiment, constitution, realization, or possession. The body constitutes or realizes the person, depending on one’s theory, but it is not thereby identical with the person. The identity claims required by the reductio would have to be replaced by nonidentity relations:

\[
D^{-}\ \text{constitutes Descartes}
\]

or:

\[
D^{-}\ \text{is the body of Descartes}
\]

Neither relation is transitive in the same way as identity. The paradox cannot be generated by substituting these relations into an identity argument.

This observation reinforces the ambiguity critique. If the reductio uses D-minus as a material object and Descartes as an abstract object, then it is not a reductio against DAUP but an exposure of a category mistake. A material part cannot be identical with an abstract whole. But the same observation also reveals why the ambiguity critique cannot be the final word. The argument does not need the abstract reading. It can assign “Descartes” a material reference from beginning to end.

## IX. The Strongest Form of the Ambiguity Objection

The strongest version of the objection is not merely that “Descartes” can mean different things in ordinary language. It is that van Inwagen’s argument requires incompatible criteria of identity at different stages.

Before the accident, “Descartes” is identified by his complete bodily structure, including the leg. After the accident, “Descartes” is identified by a looser criterion that allows him to lose the leg and remain the same. Meanwhile, D-minus is identified before the accident by the parts it has and after the accident by the same parts. Thus, the argument may be said to treat Descartes intensionally before the loss and extensionally afterward. Before the loss, Descartes is not D-minus because they have different persistence conditions or histories. After the loss, they are said to be identical merely because their current parts coincide.

This is a serious objection. Numerical identity is not generally determined by current qualitative or mereological similarity. Two objects can have exactly the same properties at a time yet differ in origin, history, or persistence conditions. If so, even within the material category, “same parts now” does not entail “same object.” The reductio may be relying on a hidden shift from a historical or sortal conception of identity to a purely extensional conception.

The response is that the shift is not necessarily hidden. Van Inwagen’s argument can be understood as targeting precisely the conjunction of DAUP with extensionalism. If DAUP says that every arbitrary plurality of parts composes a material object, and if material objects are extensional sums, then the object consisting of the relevant parts after the loss is the same sum as Descartes after the loss. Before the loss, however, the two sums differ. The problem is not that van Inwagen forgets the history of the objects. It is that DAUP appears to generate an object whose identity conditions are difficult to reconcile with the identity conditions of the larger organism.

Whether this is a genuine contradiction depends upon the theory of persistence and composition one accepts. An opponent of DAUP may say that the argument reveals that arbitrary sums cannot be ordinary persisting objects. A defender may say that D-minus is a sum that exists at each time but does not persist in the same way as Descartes. Another defender may deny that material objects have determinate identity through change. These responses are possible, but they have costs.

## X. Does Disambiguation Dissolve the Paradox?

Disambiguation dissolves the paradox only if the paradox depends upon the ambiguity. Once all terms are fixed, there are several possibilities.

### 1. Material Descartes throughout

Suppose “Descartes” consistently denotes a material organism. Then all four formal claims concern concrete objects. No category mistake occurs. If one accepts:

\[
D_{B}=D_{A}
\]

\[
M_{B}=M_{A}
\]

\[
M_{B}\neq D_{B}
\]

\[
M_{A}=D_{A}
\]

then the contradiction follows by transitivity. Disambiguation does not dissolve it.

### 2. Abstract Descartes throughout

Suppose “Descartes” consistently denotes an abstract person, soul, historical individual, or biography. Then the argument cannot begin. Such an entity does not have a leg in the relevant mereological sense and cannot lose one. D-minus cannot be an arbitrary undetached part of it. The reductio is blocked, but only because the example has been removed from the domain of DAUP.

### 3. Material body before, abstract person after

Suppose the argument shifts between material and abstract readings. Then the identity chain is invalid. The apparent contradiction is manufactured by equivocation. But this is not a refutation of DAUP; it is a criticism of an imprecise presentation of the reductio.

### 4. Material objects with non-extensional identity

Suppose both Descartes and D-minus are material, but coincidence does not entail identity. Then \(M_{A}=D_{A}\) is rejected. Again, the contradiction disappears, but the reason is not semantic ambiguity. It is a metaphysical theory of coincident objects.

### 5. Four-dimensional material objects

Suppose both objects are material, but \(D_{B}\) and \(D_{A}\) are temporal parts rather than one enduring object. Then \(D_{B}=D_{A}\) is rejected. The contradiction disappears because persistence is analyzed differently.

These possibilities show that van Inwagen’s reductio is not an argument with only one vulnerable point. The ambiguity objection identifies one possible defect, but the argument’s ultimate success depends on substantive commitments concerning identity, composition, extensionality, and persistence.

## XI. The Philosophical Significance of the Case

The Descartes example illustrates a deeper tension in unrestricted composition. If every arbitrary collection of parts forms an object, then ordinary objects are accompanied by a vast population of overlapping objects. A human body has an object corresponding to almost every selection of its particles. This is not formally contradictory by itself. An ontology may be crowded without being inconsistent.

The difficulty is that these arbitrary objects seem to inherit ordinary persistence conditions in problematic ways. D-minus can survive the loss of a leg. The larger organism can also survive the loss. At the later time, they appear to coincide. If identity is determined by parts, they become one object; if identity is determined by history or kind, they remain distinct. DAUP thus pressures us to choose between extensionalism and a theory of coincident objects, or between endurance and temporal parts.

The ambiguity of “Descartes” matters because ordinary concepts of persons and bodies are layered. We speak of a person losing a limb, of a body persisting after injury, and of a mind surviving bodily change. These descriptions encourage us to treat person, organism, body, and mind as interchangeable in ordinary contexts. They are not interchangeable in a metaphysical argument. The reductio is strongest when those distinctions are controlled, not ignored.

If the target is specifically DAUP as a thesis about material composition, then the relevant Descartes is the material organism. Abstract persons and historical individuals do not enter the argument unless a theory of their relation to bodies is added. This restriction makes the argument more precise but also narrows its conclusion. It does not show that every ontology accepting arbitrary sums is incoherent. It shows that a particular combination of DAUP, ordinary endurance, and extensional identity produces pressure.

## XII. Final Assessment

The claim that van Inwagen’s reductio fails because of ambiguity in “Descartes” is partly correct but ultimately too strong. It is correct that an argument shifting between a material Descartes and an abstract Descartes would be invalid. A material object cannot be identical with an abstract person merely because the latter is associated with it. If “Descartes” denotes a body in the claim that he has a leg, but an abstract subject in the claim that he survives the loss, then the four formal relations do not form a legitimate chain of identity. The alleged violation of transitivity has been manufactured by changing the relata.

The four formal identities make this clear. The relevant claims are:

\[
D_{B}=D_{A}
\]

\[
D^{-}_{B}=D^{-}_{A}
\]

\[
D^{-}_{B}\neq D_{B}
\]

\[
D^{-}_{A}=D_{A}
\]

If \(D_{B}\) and \(D_{A}\) are not temporal stages of the same entity, or if one denotes a material object and the other an abstract object, then the inference to contradiction is blocked. Likewise, if \(D^{-}_{A}\) is merely constituted by or associated with Descartes rather than identical with him, the relevant relation is not identity and transitivity cannot be applied.

Nevertheless, the ambiguity does not defeat the reductio when “Descartes” is consistently understood as a material organism. On that reading, all four identities concern material objects, and the apparent contradiction remains. To escape it, one must reject or revise some substantive premise: one may deny extensionality, allow coincident objects, adopt a four-dimensional theory of temporal parts, deny that D-minus persists, or reject the claim that Descartes survives the loss in the relevant sense. These are genuine metaphysical responses, but they are not consequences of disambiguating “Descartes.”

A strict distinction between material and abstract objects therefore provides only limited protection for DAUP. It successfully prevents category mistakes and exposes equivocal formulations of the reductio. It shows that an abstract Descartes cannot be the material whole from which a leg is subtracted, and that a material D-minus cannot be identical with an abstract person. But it does not insulate DAUP from a version of van Inwagen’s argument conducted entirely within the domain of concrete material objects.

The best conclusion is thus conditional. If van Inwagen’s actual reasoning depends essentially on shifting between material and abstract interpretations of “Descartes,” then that reasoning fails. The resulting paradox is linguistic rather than metaphysical. If, however, the argument is reformulated with a single, stable material referent, then the ambiguity objection no longer applies. The challenge posed by D-minus survives as a problem about whether arbitrary material sums can possess coherent identity and persistence conditions. Disambiguation removes one possible source of confusion, but it does not by itself vindicate DAUP.
