# The Lockean Impasse: A Critical Evaluation of Mohr's Argument Against the Relevance of Locke's Political Theory for Contemporary Individualists

## I. Introduction

The question of whether John Locke's political theory provides a viable philosophical foundation for contemporary individualists and libertarians has become a matter of urgent debate in political philosophy. Tyson Mohr's argument, which contends that Locke's framework fundamentally fails to support modern libertarian aspirations, strikes at the heart of a long-standing tension between seventeenth-century political theory and twenty-first-century political reality. Mohr's critique proceeds from two central observations: first, that Locke's theory depends upon conditions—particularly the existence of unoccupied land (*in vacuis locis*) and the feasibility of exiting political communities—that no longer obtain in a world of densely populated nation-states; and second, that the mechanisms Locke provides for resisting unjust government, including the right of revolution, have been rendered practically incoherent by the sheer scale and intrusiveness of modern governance.

This essay evaluates Mohr's argument with careful attention to both its strengths and its limitations. I argue that while Mohr correctly identifies genuine structural problems in applying Locke's theory to contemporary circumstances, his conclusion—that modern individualists are left with no legitimate recourse other than submission to majority rule or revolution—rests upon an overly narrow interpretation of Locke's framework and an unduly pessimistic assessment of the resources available within Lockean political thought. I will offer an independent objection to Mohr's thesis, maintaining that Locke's theory, properly understood, contains conceptual resources for addressing the concerns of contemporary libertarians without collapsing into either passive acquiescence or violent insurrection.

## II. Locke's Original Framework: Exit, Tacit Consent, and the Right of Revolution

To evaluate Mohr's argument, we must first reconstruct the Lockean framework with precision. Locke's political theory, as articulated in the *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), proceeds from a picture of the state of nature in which individuals possess natural rights to life, liberty, and property. These rights are grounded in each person's labor, which transforms unowned natural resources into private property. However, the state of nature, despite its relative freedom, suffers from inconveniences: the absence of a known and settled law, an impartial judge, and sufficient power to enforce judgments. Individuals therefore consent to form civil governments that protect their pre-existing rights.

Crucially, Locke embeds within his theory two mechanisms that allow individuals to resist or escape unjust political authority. The first is the right of exit, or the freedom to leave a political community. Locke argues that individuals who remain within the borders of a society, enjoying its protections and benefits, give tacit consent to its laws and governance. This tacit consent is what obligates them to obey the political authority that governs them. The second mechanism is the right of revolution: when a government systematically violates the trust placed in it by violating the natural rights of its citizens—particularly by seizing property without consent or imposing tyranny—the people retain the right to dissolve that government and establish a new one.

The right of exit and the doctrine of tacit consent are intimately connected in Locke's theory. If individuals could freely leave at any time, the question of whether their continued presence constituted consent would be less pressing. But Locke's theory acknowledges that physical departure is not always feasible or desirable, and so it posits that remaining within a political society, benefiting from its protections, constitutes a form of implied agreement. This doctrine was designed for a world in which land was relatively abundant, borders were porous or nonexistent, and the costs of emigration were minimal. In such a world, the right of exit functioned as a genuine check on governmental tyranny: if a government became intolerable, one could simply leave.

The right of revolution, meanwhile, was designed as a more dramatic remedy—one reserved for cases of systematic and egregious tyranny. Locke himself was writing in the aftermath of the Glorious Revolution, and his theory was explicitly concerned with justifying resistance against James II. The criteria Locke sets for legitimate rebellion are stringent: the government must have acted beyond the scope of its authority, typically by violating property rights or imposing arbitrary power, and the people must have first attempted to resolve the matter through legal and constitutional channels. Revolution is thus a last resort, not a first option.

## III. The *In Vacuis Locis* Problem: The Absence of Unoccupied Land

Mohr's first major objection concerns the empirical premise that underpins Locke's account of the right of exit. Locke's theory, Mohr argues, presupposes the existence of unoccupied land—*terra nullius*—to which individuals may resort when they find political conditions intolerable. In Locke's world, this was largely accurate. The Americas, Australia, and much of Africa were understood (however problematically) as lands available for appropriation, and European emigrants could indeed establish new societies free from the political arrangements they had left behind. The right of exit was not merely a theoretical possibility but a practical one.

In the contemporary world, however, the situation is radically different. There is no unoccupied land of sufficient size and quality to serve as a refuge for those who wish to escape the governance of their native political communities. Every habitable territory on earth falls under the jurisdiction of some sovereign state, and the remaining unsettled territories—Antarctica, portions of international waters, and remote regions—are either unsuitable for permanent habitation or governed by international treaties that limit sovereignty. The absence of *in vacuis locis* means that the practical feasibility of exit is dramatically diminished.

Mohr draws from this observation the conclusion that the right of exit has been effectively neutralized in the modern world. If one cannot leave, then tacit consent cannot be meaningfully invoked as a mechanism of political obligation. The individual who remains within a state not because they have chosen to do so, but because they have no alternative, is not truly consenting. They are, in Mohr's view, subjects rather than citizens, governed not by their own agreement but by the arbitrary boundaries of history and geography.

This objection has considerable force. Locke's theory of tacit consent was always somewhat strained as a justification for political obligation—few individuals explicitly consent to the laws of their society, and the act of merely living within its borders hardly constitutes a meaningful act of agreement. The doctrine was always more of a pragmatic concession than a rigorous philosophical foundation. In the modern world, where exit is genuinely impossible for most people, the doctrine becomes even more strained. If one cannot leave, then remaining is not a choice at all, and calling it "consent" is a fiction that serves to legitimize coercion.

However, I think Mohr overstates the significance of this problem for Locke's theory as a whole. The right of exit was never the only mechanism Locke provided for limiting governmental power. More importantly, the absence of unoccupied land does not invalidate the conceptual structure of Locke's theory; it merely exposes the contingency of its original empirical assumptions. A Lockean theorist could, in principle, develop an account of political obligation and resistance that does not depend upon the physical possibility of exit. The question is whether Locke himself provides such resources, and whether Mohr's critique is limited to the historical contingency of Locke's theory or extends to its conceptual architecture.

## IV. The Scale Problem: Tacit Consent in Megastates

Mohr's second major objection concerns the scale of modern political communities. Locke's theory was developed with relatively small political entities in mind—the English commonwealth, the Dutch Republic, the American colonies. In such contexts, the relationship between government and governed was relatively intimate, and the possibility of meaningful political participation was more realistic. The right of revolution, too, was conceivable as a practical possibility when states were small enough that coordinated resistance could be organized and sustained.

In the modern world, by contrast, states are vast, populous, and bureaucratically complex. The United States, for instance, comprises over three hundred million people spread across a continent. The idea that such a polity could be dissolved by revolution, or that individuals could meaningfully participate in governance at a level that would make their consent genuine, strains credulity. Mohr argues that the sheer scale of modern states renders both tacit consent and the right of revolution practically meaningless.

The scale problem affects tacit consent in several ways. First, the anonymity of modern governance makes it difficult to speak of individuals "benefiting" from the state in any meaningful sense. Locke's tacit consent doctrine assumes that individuals are aware of the protections they receive and can meaningfully assess whether those protections justify their obedience. In a modern bureaucratic state, this is far from clear. The benefits of governance are diffuse, indirect, and often invisible, while the costs—particularly the tax burden—are concrete and immediate. The asymmetry between visible costs and invisible benefits makes the claim that individuals tacitly consent to taxation and regulation deeply implausible.

Second, the scale problem affects the right of revolution by making coordinated resistance enormously difficult. Locke's right of revolution presupposes a degree of political cohesion and organizational capacity that modern states effectively preclude. When the state possesses a monopoly on violence, a vast surveillance apparatus, and a professional military, the prospect of successful revolution is remote. And even if revolution were successful, the question of what would replace the existing government remains entirely open. Locke's theory offers no guidance for the dissolution and reconstitution of megastates.

These are serious objections, and I do not dismiss them. However, I think they point more toward the need to adapt or supplement Locke's theory than toward its wholesale abandonment. The scale problem is a problem of political technology, not of political principle. If Locke's theory is fundamentally sound in its account of natural rights and the justification of government, then the fact that it was developed for a different technological and demographic context does not invalidate its core insights. What is needed is a contemporary Lockean theory that accounts for the challenges of scale, not a rejection of Lockean principles altogether.

## V. Taxation and Rebellion: Do Modern Policies Meet Locke's Criteria?

Mohr's third major argument concerns the question of whether contemporary taxation and government policies meet Locke's criteria for legitimate resistance. Locke was, of course, writing in the seventeenth century, when taxation was relatively limited and government intervention in economic life was minimal. The question Mohr raises is whether the vast apparatus of modern governance—the welfare state, regulatory regimes, progressive taxation, monetary policy—constitutes the kind of systematic violation of natural rights that would justify revolution under Locke's criteria.

Mohr's answer is essentially negative. He argues that Locke's criteria for revolution were designed for a specific kind of tyranny—the arbitrary seizure of property and the denial of due process—not for the diffuse and institutionalized taxation that characterizes modern states. Even if contemporary taxation is heavy by historical standards, it is not arbitrary in the way Locke's theory of revolution was designed to address. Modern states operate through legislatures, courts, and constitutional frameworks that, however imperfect, provide mechanisms for contesting taxation and government policy. The existence of these mechanisms means that the conditions for revolution—specifically, the failure of legal remedies—are not met.

This argument has some merit, but I think it ultimately concedes too much to Mohr's framework. Mohr's argument depends upon a narrow interpretation of Locke's criteria for revolution that focuses exclusively on the procedural mechanisms Locke describes. But Locke's theory also contains a deeper normative claim: that government exists to protect natural rights, and that when government becomes destructive of those ends, it loses its legitimacy. The question is not merely whether modern states have failed to provide legal remedies, but whether their policies are consistent with the protection of natural rights in the first place.

Consider progressive taxation. A Lockean might argue that taxation is justified only insofar as it is proportional and consented to, since the right to property is fundamental. Under this interpretation, progressive taxation—which takes a larger percentage from higher earners—violates the Lockean principle of equal protection of property rights. Similarly, regulatory regimes that restrict economic freedom—minimum wage laws, zoning regulations, occupational licensing—might be seen as violations of the natural right to use one's labor as one sees fit.

If these interpretations are correct, then Mohr's claim that modern taxation does not constitute justifiable grounds for rebellion is questionable. Under a robust Lockean interpretation, contemporary taxation and regulation may well violate the criteria for legitimate governance, even if the procedural mechanisms for addressing these violations remain formally available. The fact that individuals can petition their representatives or challenge regulations in court does not mean that the underlying governance is just. Locke himself recognized that the formal existence of legal institutions does not guarantee their justice.

However, I want to resist the further conclusion Mohr draws from this—that modern individualists are therefore left with no legitimate recourse but submission or revolution. The Lockean framework, even on a strict interpretation, does not reduce to a binary choice between passive obedience and violent insurrection. There are intermediate options: civil disobedience, emigration to more libertarian jurisdictions (such as Hong Kong or Singapore, or emerging libertarian enclaves), the construction of alternative institutions within existing states, and the gradual reform of existing institutions through democratic processes. These options may not be fully satisfying from a Lockean perspective, but they are far preferable to the stark choice Mohr presents.

## VI. Independent Objection: The False Dichotomy of Mohr's Thesis

I now turn to my independent objection to Mohr's thesis. Mohr's central claim is that contemporary individualists and libertarians are left with no legitimate recourse other than submission to majority rule or revolution. This is a striking claim, and I think it is ultimately unsustainable, for several reasons.

First, Mohr's argument conflates the practical difficulty of finding legitimate recourse with the absence of legitimate recourse. The fact that exit is difficult in the modern world does not mean that the right of exit is conceptually invalid. The right of exit is a normative claim about the proper limits of political authority, not merely a description of what is physically possible. Even if few individuals can realistically exercise the right of exit, the right itself remains a meaningful constraint on governmental power. It tells us that governments which effectively trap their citizens—through excessive regulation, confiscatory taxation, or denial of emigration rights—are violating a fundamental Lockean principle.

Second, Mohr underestimates the resources Locke's theory provides for challenging modern governance without resorting to revolution. Locke's theory is not merely a theory of revolution; it is a theory of limited government grounded in the protection of natural rights. This theory provides a framework for evaluating the legitimacy of specific policies, institutions, and legal arrangements. Under this framework, individualists and libertarians can argue that particular policies violate natural rights, can organize politically to change those policies, and can build alternative institutions that embody Lockean principles. None of this requires revolution, and all of it is consistent with the Lockean framework.

Third, Mohr's argument assumes a particular and contestable interpretation of Locke's theory of tacit consent. If tacit consent is understood as requiring a genuine opportunity to exit, then Mohr is correct that modern conditions undermine it. But if tacit consent is understood differently—as requiring that the political system provide meaningful avenues for participation, contestation, and reform—then modern states may still satisfy the Lockean standard, albeit imperfectly. The Lockean framework is more flexible than Mohr's critique suggests.

Fourth, and most importantly, Mohr's conclusion that individualists are left with submission or revolution ignores the rich tradition of Lockean incrementalism. Locke himself was not a revolutionary in the radical sense; he was a constitutionalist who believed in the gradual reform of political institutions through legal and political means. The Glorious Revolution, which Locke supported, was in many respects a constitutional settlement rather than a violent overthrow. Locke's theory is fundamentally compatible with the idea of working within existing political institutions to move them closer to the Lockean ideal of limited government. Modern libertarians and individualists can pursue this strategy: advocating for lower taxes, deregulation, school choice, constitutional amendments, and the creation of libertarian zones within existing states.

Fifth, Mohr's argument overlooks the possibility that the very existence of a robust Lockean intellectual tradition serves a legitimating function even in the absence of immediate practical success. The fact that individuals can articulate coherent philosophical objections to modern governance—objections grounded in natural rights, property theory, and the social contract—has political and cultural significance that goes beyond the immediate feasibility of revolution or exit. These objections shape public discourse, inform legal arguments, and provide moral resources for political movements. They constitute a form of legitimate resistance that Mohr's framework does not adequately recognize.

## VII. A Deeper Engagement: The Normative Structure of Locke's Theory

To further strengthen my objection, I want to engage more deeply with the normative structure of Locke's theory and show how it can be adapted to contemporary circumstances without abandoning its core commitments.

Locke's theory rests on a fundamental normative claim: that individuals possess natural rights that precede and are prior to political society. These rights are not granted by government; they are inherent in human nature as rational and morally autonomous agents. The purpose of government is to protect these pre-existing rights, and government derives its legitimacy from its success in doing so. When government fails in this purpose—when it systematically violates the rights it was established to protect—it loses its legitimacy, and the people have the right to resist.

This normative structure is remarkably robust and does not depend upon the empirical conditions Locke himself took for granted. The existence of unoccupied land or the small scale of political communities are contingent features of Locke's historical context, not necessary components of his normative theory. The right of exit and the doctrine of tacit consent are applications of the deeper principle that political authority requires consent, but they are not the principle itself. The principle—that legitimate governance requires the consent of the governed—can be applied in contexts where exit is impossible by identifying alternative forms of consent: explicit consent through democratic participation, structural consent through constitutional design, and ongoing consent through the provision of just governance.

This suggests that Mohr's critique, while powerful as an empirical observation about the limitations of Locke's original framework, does not succeed as a philosophical refutation of Lockean principles. The Lockean framework can be refined and adapted to modern conditions while retaining its core commitments. The result would be a contemporary Lockean theory that acknowledges the challenges of scale, density, and institutional complexity while still providing resources for evaluating and challenging modern governance.

## VIII. Addressing the Practical Dilemma

Mohr's conclusion—that modern individualists face a stark choice between submission and revolution—deserves further scrutiny. Even if we grant Mohr's premises, the conclusion does not follow as strongly as he suggests. The choice between submission and revolution is a practical dilemma, not a philosophical necessity. Philosophy can illuminate the moral dimensions of this dilemma without resolving it in Mohr's preferred direction.

Moreover, I would argue that Mohr's framing of the dilemma is itself problematic. By presenting individualists with only two options—submission or revolution—he forecloses a range of intermediate positions that are both morally defensible and practically viable. Civil disobedience, for instance, occupies a middle ground between submission and revolution. It involves the deliberate violation of unjust laws as a form of moral protest, without resorting to violence or the wholesale rejection of political authority. Thoreau's famous essay "Civil Disobedience," which draws explicitly on Lockean principles, exemplifies this approach. The individualist who refuses to pay taxes that fund unjust wars or programs violates the law openly, accepts the consequences, and thereby affirms both the importance of law and the limits of legitimate government authority.

Similarly, the strategy of building alternative institutions—communities, cooperatives, mutual aid networks, and parallel legal systems—offers a way for individualists to live according to their principles without either submitting to or violently overthrowing existing government. These strategies do not resolve the Lockean dilemma, but they complicate it in ways that Mohr's binary framing does not capture.

## IX. Conclusion

Tyson Mohr's argument that Locke's political theory fails to support contemporary individualists and libertarians raises important and legitimate questions about the applicability of seventeenth-century political theory to modern circumstances. The absence of unoccupied land, the scale of modern states, and the complexity of contemporary governance all pose challenges to the Lockean framework that Mohr rightly identifies. The doctrine of tacit consent, in particular, becomes deeply strained when exit is no longer a realistic option for most people.

However, I have argued that Mohr's critique, while powerful, is ultimately insufficient to support his conclusion that modern individualists are left with no legitimate recourse other than submission or revolution. The core normative commitments of Locke's theory—the protection of natural rights, the requirement of consent, and the limited purposes of government—do not depend upon the empirical conditions of Locke's historical context. These commitments can be adapted and applied to modern circumstances in ways that provide resources for challenging contemporary governance without resorting to either passive submission or violent insurrection.

The Lockean framework offers a rich vocabulary for evaluating the legitimacy of modern policies, for organizing political resistance, and for imagining alternative institutional arrangements. It does not provide easy answers to the challenges Mohr identifies, but it does not leave individualists without legitimate recourse either. The task of contemporary Lockean political philosophy is not to abandon the framework Mohr critiques, but to refine it—to acknowledge its historical limitations while recovering its enduring normative insights. In doing so, we can honor Locke's original vision of a political order grounded in consent, limited government, and the protection of individual rights, even in a world Locke could never have imagined.

The real lesson of Mohr's argument is not that Locke's theory is irrelevant, but that it is incomplete. It needs to be supplemented with contemporary reflections on scale, technology, and institutional complexity. But this is not a refutation of Locke; it is an extension of the Lockean tradition, and it is precisely the kind of work that keeps political philosophy alive and relevant across centuries of social and political transformation.
