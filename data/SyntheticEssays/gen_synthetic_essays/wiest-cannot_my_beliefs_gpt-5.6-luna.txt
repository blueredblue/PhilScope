# Practical Judgment, Desire, and the Sources of Motivation

A central dispute in the philosophy of action concerns the relation between reasons and motivation. When an agent acts for a reason, what makes that reason capable of moving the agent to act? Is motivation ultimately grounded in some antecedent desire, so that practical reasoning merely determines how an independently desired end may be achieved? Or can an agent’s practical judgment itself generate motivation, even when no prior desire supplies the motivational force?

Thomas Nagel and Michael Smith represent two influential answers to this question. Nagel defends a broadly rationalist conception of motivation. On his view, an agent’s recognition of what there is reason to do can itself motivate action. Practical reasoning is not merely an instrumental device for satisfying desires that have arisen independently of reason. It can disclose reasons that possess motivational force in their own right. Smith, by contrast, develops a Humean account according to which motivation necessarily involves a desire or some other appropriately directed conative state. Beliefs and judgments can tell an agent what would promote a desired end, but they cannot motivate by themselves. In Smith’s account, practical reasoning is therefore dependent upon an unmotivated desire that gives the agent something to care about.

The disagreement is especially vivid in cases of means-end reasoning, such as Nagel’s example involving soda pop and a dime. The case appears at first to support the Humean view: a person wants soda, believes that a dime will purchase it, and is thereby motivated to use the dime to obtain the soda. Yet Nagel uses the structure of the example to suggest that motivation can be transmitted from an end to the means through rational recognition, rather than being generated by a separate desire for the means. The question is whether this transmission merely transfers motivation supplied by an antecedent desire, as Smith maintains, or whether rational judgment can itself establish a motivational connection between end and means.

The best resolution is not a simple victory for either side. Smith is right that beliefs alone do not normally motivate and that action requires some conative orientation toward a goal. But his conclusion is too strong if it says that all motivation must originate in desires that are independent of practical reason. Nagel is right that practical judgment can alter, generate, and organize motivation in a way that cannot be reduced to the satisfaction of preexisting desires. Nevertheless, rational judgment does not motivate in an entirely disembodied manner. It motivates by presenting an end, requirement, or reason as something to be done, thereby producing or reshaping a goal-directed orientation in the agent. Thus, practical judgment can be motivationally fundamental without being a bare belief detached from any intentional orientation.

## I. The Humean model: desire as the source of motivation

The Humean theory of motivation begins with a familiar thought: beliefs represent the world as being a certain way, but desires aim at making the world a certain way. A belief, by itself, is inert with respect to action. If I believe that there is food in the kitchen, that belief does not imply that I will go to the kitchen. I might be indifferent to food, asleep, incapable of moving, or committed to remaining where I am. The belief provides information, but information alone does not supply a reason for action in the motivational sense.

A desire, by contrast, supplies direction. If I want food and believe that food is in the kitchen, the combination of desire and belief can explain why I go there. The desire gives me an end; the belief connects a possible action with that end. This is the classic Humean picture:

1. The agent desires an end.
2. The agent believes that a certain action is a means to that end.
3. The desire and belief together motivate the action.

Michael Smith’s account gives this picture a sophisticated form. A motivating reason is not simply a fact that counts in favor of an action, nor is it merely a proposition believed by the agent. It is a psychological state that explains the agent’s action in light of the agent’s motivational outlook. In the standard case, the relevant psychological state is a desire, while the relevant belief identifies the action that would satisfy it.

Suppose that I desire to drink soda and believe that I can obtain soda by inserting a dime into a vending machine. My desire for soda does not itself tell me how to obtain it. The belief that a dime will purchase soda does not itself make me care about inserting the dime. The action is explained by their combination. The desire provides motivational force, while the belief supplies direction.

The Humean can therefore distinguish between normative and motivating reasons. A normative reason is a fact that counts in favor of an action. For example, the fact that inserting the dime will produce soda may be a reason for me to insert it. But a normative reason becomes a motivating reason for me only if I possess the appropriate desires and beliefs. If I do not want soda, the vending-machine fact may remain normatively relevant in some abstract sense, but it will not explain my action. What turns the reason into a motivating reason is its relation to my desires.

Smith’s position is not that people act irrationally whenever desires are involved. Rather, he argues that rationality operates within a motivational structure whose basic elements are not themselves supplied by reason. Practical reasoning can assess whether the agent’s beliefs are coherent, whether the means are appropriate to the ends, and whether the agent’s desires are consistent. But the ultimate ends—the things for which the agent acts—must be supplied by desire or some analogous conative state. If I desire soda, practical reasoning can tell me whether a dime is an effective means. It cannot, by itself, make soda matter to me.

This account captures an important feature of human action. There is a difference between recognizing that something would be good or reasonable and being moved to do it. A person might judge that exercise is beneficial while remaining entirely unmotivated to exercise. Someone might believe that donating to famine relief would be morally admirable but feel no inclination to donate. If judgment alone were sufficient for motivation, such cases would be difficult to explain. The Humean account explains them easily: the agent has the relevant belief or judgment but lacks the appropriate desire.

## II. Nagel’s rationalist challenge

Nagel’s rationalism challenges the idea that desires are the ultimate source of motivation. He does not deny that desires frequently motivate or that means-end reasoning often involves desires. His deeper claim is that the connection between practical judgment and action cannot be fully explained by an antecedent desire. There are cases in which an agent recognizes a reason for action and is thereby moved, even though the agent did not previously possess a desire directed toward that action or end.

For Nagel, practical reason is not merely a faculty for calculating the best means of satisfying contingent preferences. Rational reflection can reveal that an action is called for. The recognition of a reason can itself alter the motivational situation. If I recognize that I have promised to do something, that recognition may motivate me to do it even if I have no antecedent desire to keep promises. If I see that another person is in serious danger, recognition of the person’s need may move me to help, even if helping was not part of my prior motivational set. If I understand that a certain action is necessary to achieve a rationally adopted goal, that understanding can move me toward the action.

The rationalist therefore rejects the idea that practical reason is motivationally parasitic upon desire. The agent does not always begin with a desire and then use reason to find an appropriate means. Sometimes reason determines what the agent ought to want, what the agent has reason to pursue, or what the agent must do given a commitment or goal. Practical judgment has a constructive role in motivation.

This does not mean that every judgment motivates. A person can make a detached or merely theoretical judgment that something is good, right, or required. Nagel’s claim is instead about practical judgment: judgment made from the standpoint of deliberation, in which the agent considers what to do. A practical judgment is not simply the belief that an action has some property. It is an assessment of the action as a possible object of choice. When the agent judges that a certain action is called for, that judgment can directly engage the will.

The issue, then, is whether practical judgment contains motivational force in its own right or whether it requires a desire to become action-guiding. The soda-pop example helps expose the disagreement.

## III. The soda pop and dime thought experiment

Consider an agent who wants a soda and sees a vending machine. The agent has a dime and knows, or comes to know, that inserting the dime will produce a soda. The ordinary explanation is straightforward. The agent desires the soda, believes that inserting the dime is a means to obtaining it, and inserts the dime.

At first sight, the case seems decisively Humean. The desire for soda supplies the motivation, and the belief about the dime supplies the means. If the agent did not want soda, the belief that the machine accepts dimes would not move the agent. If the agent wanted soda but did not know how the machine worked, the desire would not result in the particular action. The action is explained by desire-belief conjunction.

Nagel’s interest lies in the rational relation connecting the end and the means. Once the agent has adopted the end of obtaining soda, the recognition that inserting the dime is necessary or sufficient for obtaining it gives the agent a reason to insert the dime. The practical significance of the dime is not independent of the end. Its significance derives from its rational relation to the goal. Yet the agent need not have a separate, antecedent desire to insert the dime. The agent does not normally desire inserting a small metal object into a machine for its own sake. The action is motivated because it is recognized as a means to an end.

This raises a question about how motivation is transmitted. The Humean says that the motivation originates in the desire for soda and is transferred, through belief, to the means. The agent wants soda; the agent believes that inserting the dime will produce soda; therefore the agent is motivated to insert the dime. The means has no independent motivational force. It borrows its force from the end.

Nagel can accept much of this description while drawing a different conclusion. He can argue that the agent’s practical judgment—that inserting the dime is the way to obtain the desired or rationally adopted end—constitutes a new reason for action. The judgment does not merely reveal a causal fact. It represents the action as something to be done in light of the end. The agent’s reasoning moves from “I am going to obtain soda” or “I have reason to obtain soda” to “I should insert the dime.” The conclusion is practical rather than theoretical.

The key mechanism is therefore not a separate desire for each means. If an agent needed an antecedent desire for the dime, another desire for the act of approaching the machine, another desire for pressing the button, and so forth, action would generate an implausible regress. What explains the motivational relevance of the means is its rational connection to the end. Means-end reasoning allows the motivational significance of an end to be extended to actions that are not independently desired.

The Humean has a response. The absence of a separate desire for the means does not undermine Humeanism, because the theory does not require a separate desire for every bodily movement. A general desire for soda can motivate a structured sequence of actions once combined with appropriate beliefs. The agent’s desire for soda is motivationally sufficient to explain the desire-like pull of the means. Means-end reasoning is not an independent source of motivation; it is the process by which an existing desire is intelligently directed.

This response is powerful. It shows that the soda case does not, by itself, establish that judgment can motivate without desire. The fact that the agent does not desire the dime as an end does not show that no desire grounds the action. The desire for soda may be enough.

Still, Nagel’s point survives in a modified form. The example shows that motivation is not simply a matter of isolated desires attached to isolated actions. It involves rational organization. The agent is moved to perform the means because the action is understood under a description supplied by practical reasoning: inserting the dime is not merely a hand movement; it is obtaining the soda. The practical identity of the action depends on its place in a rational plan. Even if desire supplies the initial motivational energy, practical judgment determines how that energy is normatively and intentionally structured.

## IV. Direction of fit and the nature of intentional states

The debate is often framed using the notion of direction of fit. Beliefs have a world-to-mind direction of fit. A belief is supposed to conform to the world. If I believe that the vending machine accepts dimes, my belief is correct if the machine in fact accepts dimes. When the world does not match the belief, rational pressure ordinarily falls on me to revise the belief.

Desires, intentions, and other conative states are commonly said to have a mind-to-world direction of fit. A desire for soda represents soda as something to be obtained, and the world is to be changed so that it fits the desire. If I desire soda, I am not merely representing the soda’s existence; I am oriented toward bringing about a state of affairs in which I have soda.

The wording of the present issue requires some care. Goal-directed intentional states ordinarily have a mind-to-world direction of fit, whereas beliefs and judgments, insofar as they are representational, have a world-to-mind direction of fit. A practical judgment may have a world-to-mind aspect because it aims to represent what there is reason to do, but it also has an action-guiding or directive role that distinguishes it from ordinary theoretical belief. The question is whether goal-directed action requires a conative state with mind-to-world direction of fit—such as a desire, intention, commitment, or goal—or whether a world-to-mind judgment can itself explain action.

Smith argues that a purely world-to-mind state cannot motivate. If I merely believe that I have reason to help someone, the belief represents a normative fact but does not create an orientation toward changing the world. A desire or intention is required to supply that orientation. Nagel challenges the assumption that all practical judgments are merely beliefs. A practical judgment is not a detached representation of a reason; it is an appraisal made from the standpoint of deliberation. It can include an incipient commitment to act. When I judge that I ought to help, I do not merely represent an obligation; I place myself under it.

This suggests that the dispute may partly depend on how practical judgment is characterized. If practical judgment is defined as an ordinary belief about what is right or required, then Smith’s objection is compelling: beliefs are not generally motivational. If practical judgment is a distinct kind of intentional state that presents an action as to be done, then it may be motivational without being a desire in the ordinary sense.

However, simply relabeling a practical judgment as a “desire” would not resolve the issue. Nagel’s point is substantive: motivation can arise from rational recognition, not merely from an antecedent preference. The relevant state may have an action-guiding direction of fit without being a desire for an independently specified object. Its motivational force derives from the agent’s recognition of a reason, requirement, or rational relation.

One way to express the distinction is this. A desire says, in effect, “I want the world to be like this.” A practical judgment says, “Given what is the case, what I am committed to, or what reason requires, this is what I am to do.” The second can motivate because it is not simply a passive representation. It is a judgment about the agent’s own practical standpoint. It identifies an action as incumbent, appropriate, or necessary. Its action-guiding force may generate a new goal-directed state rather than presuppose one.

## V. Can reasons transmit motivation without an underlying desire?

The idea of transmission is crucial. In instrumental reasoning, the motivational influence of an end seems to pass to the means. If I want to make coffee and believe that turning on the kettle is necessary, I become motivated to turn on the kettle. But what exactly passes from the end to the means?

The Humean answer is that the desire remains the sole source of motivation. The means is motivating only under a description that connects it with the desired end. The belief supplies the connection, but the belief contributes no motivational force of its own. The action is motivated by the desire as mediated through the belief.

Nagel’s answer is that reasons can have relational motivational force. An action need not be independently desirable in order to be a reason for action. Its status as a means can make it something the agent has reason to do. The practical reason for inserting the dime is not merely “I desire soda.” It is “inserting the dime is necessary for obtaining the soda.” Once that relation is understood, the reason for the end can rationally support the means.

Does this require an underlying desire? In many ordinary cases, some goal-directed orientation is clearly present. The soda case includes a desire for soda. The agent’s practical reasoning is therefore not occurring in a motivational vacuum. Nagel’s thought experiment cannot prove that no desire is involved in all means-end action.

But the more important question is whether the desire is conceptually prior and motivationally independent. Consider an agent who accepts a moral principle, recognizes another person’s claim, or understands that a promise generates an obligation. The agent may have no antecedent desire to perform the required action. Indeed, the agent may positively dislike doing it. Nevertheless, the recognition of the reason can generate motivation. The agent may come to desire, intend, or feel committed to performing the action because the practical judgment has changed the agent’s motivational orientation.

If Smith insists that the agent must already possess a desire to do what is right, keep promises, or help others, his theory risks making these apparent sources of motivation explanatory fictions. It can say that the agent has a general desire to be moral, or a desire to avoid guilt, or a desire to maintain self-respect. Sometimes that will be true. But such explanations can become ad hoc when they are introduced merely to preserve the theory. The agent may act against self-interest, contrary to inclination, and without concern for social approval. The most natural explanation may be that the agent recognizes a reason and is moved by that recognition.

The Humean can respond that “desire” need not mean a narrow appetite or explicit preference. It may include commitments, cares, ideals, policies, values, and standing concerns. If so, the Humean theory becomes more plausible empirically. Most human agents possess broad motivational dispositions that explain why rational judgments matter to them. A person who is moved by justice may have a standing desire to act justly, even if no specific desire preceded the particular judgment.

But this expanded notion of desire creates a theoretical cost. If every stable motivational orientation is classified as a desire, the theory may become difficult to test. The claim that motivation requires desire becomes true by stipulation. More importantly, it no longer captures the contrast between reason and desire that originally motivated Humeanism. If a rational commitment can be counted as a desire, then saying that motivation requires desire does not show that motivation originates independently of reason. The commitment may itself have been generated through rational reflection.

The strongest Humean position therefore requires desires to be motivationally prior to practical judgment. That stronger claim is vulnerable. Human agents often deliberate about what to care about, what to pursue, and what commitments to adopt. Deliberation can reshape the motivational outlook rather than merely calculate within it. A person can recognize the force of a reason and thereby acquire an intention or commitment that did not previously exist. Practical judgment can be motivationally productive.

## VI. The problem of unmotivated desires

Smith’s Humean account requires some motivational starting point. If every desire had to be produced by a prior desire through reasoning, motivation would face an infinite regress. At some point, there must be desires that are not motivated by other desires. These are “unmotivated desires” in the relevant sense: not desires that appear causally from nowhere, but desires whose motivational force is not explained by a further desire.

This requirement is not necessarily a defect. Every theory may need some basic psychological element. Rationalism also needs to explain why a rational judgment is capable of moving an agent. It cannot simply repeat that the judgment is rationally compelling without explaining how rational force enters psychology. The Humean says that basic desires are the primitive source; the rationalist says that practical recognition can be primitive or irreducibly motivating.

The question is whether restricting motivation to unmotivated desires adequately captures human action. It does capture some important facts. Human beings have appetites, inclinations, affections, and concerns that are not the products of conscious reasoning. Hunger, thirst, curiosity, affection, and fear can motivate directly. In these cases, Humean explanations are entirely appropriate. The desire for soda can explain why an agent attends to the machine, acquires the dime, and inserts it. Practical reasoning organizes the action, but the basic motivational impetus may come from thirst or preference.

Yet human action also includes activities in which agents criticize, resist, or transform their desires. People fast despite hunger, study despite boredom, fulfill unpleasant obligations, expose themselves to danger for strangers, and adopt long-term projects that require sacrificing immediate satisfaction. These actions are not necessarily explained by a prior desire that remains untouched by reflection. Often the agent acts because a judgment changes the agent’s understanding of what matters or what is required.

The Humean may describe such cases in terms of a hierarchy of desires: a desire for health overcomes a desire for food; a desire for integrity overcomes a desire for comfort; a desire for long-term success overcomes a desire for immediate pleasure. This is a useful model in some cases, especially when the agent already has a stable concern for health, integrity, or achievement. But it cannot explain how the higher-order desire arose or why the agent treats it as authoritative. If the agent’s judgment that integrity matters is itself motivated only by another desire, the regress reappears. If it is not, then practical judgment has generated a motivational orientation independently of a prior desire.

The rationalist need not deny that basic desires exist. The claim is rather that not all motivational force is reducible to desire. Reasons can be motivationally fundamental, and practical judgment can bring an agent under their influence. The agent’s recognition of a reason may produce an intention, commitment, or desire-like state. This does not mean that reason mechanically forces action. Agents can be irrational, akratic, distracted, or emotionally resistant. But the fact that a rational judgment can fail to motivate perfectly does not show that it lacks motivational force. A desire can also fail to produce action because of competing desires or practical obstacles.

## VII. Practical judgment alone: what could that mean?

The phrase “practical judgment alone” can be misleading. If it means that an abstract belief, with no connection to agency, intention, concern, or deliberation, causes action, then the rationalist position is implausible. Merely believing that one has a reason does not guarantee motivation. People can make detached moral judgments without acting, just as they can recognize the benefits of exercise without exercising.

But if “practical judgment alone” means that no preexisting, independently motivating desire is required, the claim is more defensible. A practical judgment can be a judgment that settles what the agent is to do. It can constitute a commitment or generate an intention. In that sense, it is not psychologically inert. It transforms the agent’s practical perspective.

Consider the difference between these two thoughts:

- “Helping the person would be morally good.”
- “I ought to help this person now.”

The first may be theoretical or observational. The second is practical and self-involving. It places the agent under a demand. If the second judgment is sincere and made under conditions of rational reflection, it may motivate directly. It need not be accompanied by an antecedent desire to help. The agent’s recognition of the reason is itself a form of practical engagement.

The rationalist should therefore distinguish practical judgment from normative belief. A normative belief represents a proposition about reasons. A practical judgment determines what to do in light of reasons. The latter has a special relation to action because it is made as a conclusion of deliberation. Its content is not simply that an action is good but that this is what the agent should now undertake.

This distinction also clarifies the role of intention. Perhaps the judgment itself does not motivate in isolation; perhaps it motivates by generating an intention. But that does not restore the strong Humean theory. The intention may be a rationally produced state rather than a desire that existed prior to judgment. The causal sequence would then be:

1. The agent recognizes a practical reason.
2. The agent makes a practical judgment about what to do.
3. The judgment generates an intention or commitment.
4. The intention motivates action.

The Humean could call the intention a desire. Yet if the intention is generated by the judgment and not by a prior desire, the substantive rationalist point remains. The source of the new motivational orientation is practical reasoning.

## VIII. A qualified resolution

The tension between Nagel and Smith can be resolved by distinguishing three claims.

First, it is false that beliefs and judgments are always motivationally inert. Practical judgments can motivate when they are made from the agent’s deliberative standpoint and concern what the agent is to do. They can generate commitments, intentions, and goal-directed orientations. Rational recognition can therefore be a source of motivation.

Second, it is also false that every recognition of a reason automatically motivates. A detached judgment may fail to engage the agent. Motivation normally requires some psychological state that is directed toward action. In ordinary cases, this state may be a desire; in others, it may be an intention, commitment, concern, or practical judgment itself.

Third, the relation between ends and means does not require a separate desire for each means. Nagel’s soda and dime example correctly shows that the motivational significance of a means derives from its rational relation to an end. The agent need not desire the dime or the bodily movement of inserting it as an end. The action is motivated under the description of being a means to obtaining soda. Smith is right that, in the basic example, the desire for soda helps explain why the means matters. But he is wrong if he treats this as proving that desire is the only possible source of the motivational force of the relation.

Reasons can transmit motivational influence across the end-means relation. Sometimes this transmission is best understood as the rational direction of an antecedent desire. At other times, the practical recognition of the relation can itself generate a commitment to the means. What is essential is not an independently existing desire for every object of action, but the agent’s practical apprehension of what the action is for and why it is called for.

The language of direction of fit supports this conclusion. Action generally requires some world-directed orientation, not merely a passive belief. But that orientation need not always be a preexisting desire. Practical judgment can produce an intention or commitment with the relevant action-guiding direction. The crucial distinction is not between desire and no desire in a narrow psychological sense. It is between a purely theoretical representation and an engaged practical state that presents an action as to be done.

## Conclusion

The debate between Nagel and Smith concerns the deepest source of action. Smith’s Humean account rightly emphasizes that information alone does not move agents. Beliefs identify facts and means, but desires, intentions, or comparable conative states are needed for action. The soda-pop and dime case illustrates this well: the agent’s desire for soda, combined with the belief that a dime will obtain it, explains why the agent inserts the dime.

Nevertheless, the case also reveals a limitation of a desire-centered theory. The agent need not desire the means independently. The means becomes motivating through practical reasoning, which represents it as necessary or appropriate for the end. This rational relation does more than causally connect two events. It gives the means its practical identity and motivational significance.

Nagel is therefore correct that practical judgment can have genuine motivational force. Agents do not merely reason instrumentally within a fixed set of desires. They can deliberate about what to pursue, what to regard as obligatory, and what commitments to adopt. Recognition of a reason can generate an intention or goal-directed orientation even when no antecedent desire explains it. This is especially apparent in moral action, promise-keeping, self-discipline, and the adoption of long-term projects.

Still, “practical judgment alone” should not be understood as a detached belief causing action. Motivation requires an engaged practical state, whether desire, intention, commitment, or a judgment that directly settles what the agent is to do. The rationalist does not need to deny this psychological requirement. The important point is that such a state can be produced by rational reflection rather than presupposed as an unmotivated desire.

Thus, restricting motivation to unmotivated desires does not adequately capture the full range of human action. It explains appetite-driven and straightforwardly instrumental behavior, but it struggles to explain how agents revise their ends, acquire commitments, and act against inclination in response to reasons. Practical judgment can successfully motivate agents—not invariably, mechanically, or in the absence of all practical orientation, but as a genuine source of new motivation. Reasons do not merely borrow their force from preexisting desires. Through practical reasoning, they can transmit motivational influence from ends to means and can sometimes create the very commitments by which agents come to act.
