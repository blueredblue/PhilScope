# The Witness and the Word: Testimony as Philosophical Structure in Ricoeur, LDS Theology, and the Trial of Abinadi

## I. Introduction: The Paradox of Bearing Witness

Testimony occupies a peculiar philosophical position: it is at once the most intimate act of self-disclosure and the most public declaration of truth before others. To testify is to stand at the intersection of the personal and the universal, the subjective and the objective, the temporal and the eternal. The word itself derives from the Latin *testis*, meaning witness, and etymologically evokes the figure of a third party standing beside—*testis* shares its root with the Latin for "third"—who provides an account of events witnessed. Yet testimony is never merely reportage. It carries within it the weight of commitment, the risk of vulnerability, and the demand that one's very being be rendered credible through the act of speaking.

Paul Ricoeur devoted considerable philosophical attention to the structures of testimony, analyzing it through both semantic and juridical lenses. His work reveals testimony to be a complex speech act that simultaneously performs multiple functions: it asserts truth claims, it constructs the identity of the witness, it establishes relations between the speaker and the audience, and it participates in broader narratives of justice and meaning. When we bring Ricoeur's analyses into dialogue with the theological framework of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS), where bearing testimony constitutes a central religious practice and epistemological category, remarkable tensions and resolutions emerge. The LDS understanding of testimony—rooted in the personal witness of the Holy Ghost, yet necessarily embedded in narrative content about Christ, scripture, and divine history—provides a rich case study for examining how testimony operates as both confession and narration, as both personal witness and cosmic declaration.

This essay explores these structures by examining the prophetic trial of Abinadi, a figure from the Book of Mormon whose trial before King Noah constitutes one of the most philosophically rich examples of testimony in religious literature. Abinadi stands simultaneously as an accused prophet before a worldly court and as an emissary of divine judgment, his words functioning as both confession and narrative testimony. Through this analysis, I will demonstrate that bearing testimony constitutes active participation in what Ricoeur might call the "cosmic" dimension of discourse—the ongoing struggle between Christ and the world, between authentic witness and the forces that suppress truth.

## II. Ricoeur's Semantic Analysis: Testimony as Speech Act

Ricoeur's semantic analysis of testimony begins with the recognition that testimony is not merely information transfer but a particular kind of speech act with distinctive illocutionary force. In his work on narrative theory and the philosophy of language, Ricoeur distinguishes between different modes of discourse and their modes of reference. Testimony belongs to the category of what he calls "emplotment"—the structuring of events into meaningful narrative configurations that make them intelligible as actions rather than mere occurrences.

The semantic structure of testimony involves what Ricoeur terms the "double orientation" of the witness: the witness orients toward the event witnessed and toward the audience to whom the testimony is given. This dual orientation creates a distinctive temporal structure in which the past event, the present speaking, and the future consequences of the testimony are braided together. The testimony does not merely report what happened; it brings the past into the present in a way that transforms both the witness and the hearer.

Crucially, Ricoeur recognizes that testimony involves a specific kind of commitment that goes beyond assertion. When one testifies, one stakes one's credibility, one's identity, and one's relationship to the truth. The semantic content of testimony is inseparable from the existential commitment it expresses. This is why testimony occupies a privileged position in juridical contexts—it is not merely that the witness claims to know something, but that the witness commits to the truth of that knowledge with the risk of their own reputation and integrity.

Ricoeur further analyzes testimony through what he calls the "hermeneutic arc"—the movement from the testimony itself to its interpretation, and from interpretation to the broader context of meaning-making. Testimony is never self-interpreting; it requires a community of interpretation that can evaluate its claims, assess the credibility of the witness, and situate the testimony within larger narratives of meaning. This hermeneutic dependency reveals that testimony is fundamentally relational: it exists only in the space between speaker and hearer, between event and interpretation, between individual witness and communal understanding.

## III. Ricoeur's Juridical Analysis: The Witness as Function

Ricoeur's juridical analysis of testimony examines the institutional and procedural dimensions of witness-bearing. In legal contexts, the role of the witness is precisely defined: the witness is called to account, compelled to speak, and evaluated according to criteria of competence, credibility, and completeness. The juridical framework establishes rules about who may testify, what may be testified, and how testimony is to be received and evaluated.

Ricoeur's insight is that the juridical analysis of testimony reveals something essential about the structure of all testimony, not merely legal testimony. The witness stands in a particular relation to truth and to community. The witness is not the author of the events they recount but rather a mediator between events and their significance. This mediation role is structurally analogous to what Ricoeur calls "second naivety"—the post-critical mode of engagement with narrative and testimony that acknowledges its constructed character while still entering into its truth claims with full commitment.

The juridical dimension also reveals the performative nature of testimony. The act of bearing witness is itself a form of judgment—the judgment of the witness about the truth of events, and simultaneously the judgment of the community about the worth of the witness's account. Ricoeur notes that in juridical proceedings, the witness is both subject and agent: they are called to account (subject) but they also render account (agent). This dual role—being judged while judging—creates a structure of mutual accountability that is fundamental to the ethics of testimony.

Ricoeur further explores what he calls the "aporia of testimony"—the apparent contradiction between the subjective certainty of the witness and the objective standards by which testimony is evaluated. The witness knows something with subjective certainty, but the community must evaluate that certainty through objective criteria. This aporia is not merely a problem to be solved but a constitutive feature of testimony itself. The tension between subjective certainty and objective evaluation is what gives testimony its philosophical richness and its ethical significance.

## IV. LDS Testimony: The Holy Ghost as Witness

Within the theological framework of LDS thought, testimony occupies a central and distinctive position. The LDS understanding of testimony is rooted in the concept of the Holy Ghost as the ultimate witness of truth. In LDS theology, the Holy Ghost is understood as a personage of spirit who communicates truth to the human mind through feelings of peace, joy, and spiritual confirmation. When a person receives a "testimony" of the gospel, they are understood to have received a personal witness from the Holy Ghost that certain propositions about God, Christ, and the plan of salvation are true.

This conception of testimony has several distinctive features. First, it is fundamentally experiential: the testimony is not merely intellectual assent to propositions but a felt certainty that comes through spiritual experience. Second, it is personal and individual: each person must obtain their own testimony; it cannot be transferred or inherited. Third, it is relational: the testimony is received through covenants, ordinances, and ongoing spiritual practice, situating the individual within a community of faith.

However, LDS testimony also has a necessary narrative dimension. The testimony of the Holy Ghost is never given in isolation from content; it always testifies of specific things—of the divinity of Christ, of the reality of modern revelation, of the truthfulness of the Book of Mormon, of the restoration of the gospel through Joseph Smith. The personal witness of the Holy Ghost is always a witness *of* something, and that something is constituted by narrative content drawn from scripture, prophetic teachings, and personal spiritual history.

This creates a philosophical tension that mirrors the aporia Ricoeur identifies in testimony generally: how does the subjective certainty of spiritual experience relate to the objective narrative content it affirms? Is the testimony valid because of its subjective certainty, or because of the truth of its content? The LDS tradition answers this question by maintaining that the Holy Ghost witnesses of truth precisely because truth is objective and the Holy Ghost is a spirit of truth. The subjective certainty and the objective content are not in competition but in harmony—the Holy Ghost illuminates the mind and heart to recognize what is already true.

## V. The Trial of Abinadi: Testimony as Prophetic Performance

The story of Abinadi, as recorded in the Book of Mormon (Mosiah 11-17), provides a paradigmatic case for examining the structures of testimony. Abinadi is a prophet sent by God to warn King Noah and his people of their wickedness and impending destruction. He enters the royal court and delivers a message of repentance, is imprisoned, and is brought before the king for trial. The trial itself becomes the occasion for Abinadi's most powerful testimony—the testimony of Christ, which he delivers in what becomes one of the most theologically significant passages in LDS scripture.

The quasi-judicial structure of Abinadi's trial reveals several dimensions of testimony that illuminate the philosophical structures at work. First, Abinadi is called to account: the king demands that he answer for his words and actions. The trial establishes Abinadi as a witness who must justify his testimony. Second, Abinadi's testimony is both confession and narration: he confesses the sins of the people and narrates the coming judgments of God. Third, Abinadi stands simultaneously as an accused person before a worldly court and as an emissary of divine judgment—the trial becomes a collision between two jurisdictions, the temporal authority of King Noah and the eschatological authority of God.

Abinadi's testimony of Christ at the trial is particularly significant because it reveals the cosmic dimension of bearing witness. When Abinadi speaks of Christ's atoning sacrifice, he is not merely reporting historical events or expressing personal piety; he is participating in what might be called the "testimony of Christ"—the ongoing cosmic witness that Christ gives to the Father and that the Spirit witnesses to the world. Abinadi becomes, in this moment, an instrument of that cosmic testimony. His words are not his own but are given to him by the Holy Ghost, and in delivering them, he participates in the divine witness to truth.

The trial structure also reveals the danger inherent in testimony. Abinadi's testimony condemns the king and the people, and his willingness to bear that testimony leads to his death. The testimony that confronts power is a dangerous testimony, and Abinadi's willingness to die for his witness reveals the depth of his commitment to truth. This echoes the LDS understanding that bearing testimony often involves sacrifice and that the most powerful testimonies are those delivered at personal cost.

## VI. The Dual Tension: Confession and Narration

The tension between testimony-confession and testimony-narration is central to understanding how testimony functions in both Ricoeur's philosophy and LDS theology. Testimony-confession refers to testimony as the acknowledgment of truth, the bearing witness to what one knows to be true, often involving an element of personal commitment and vulnerability. Testimony-narration refers to testimony as the telling of a story, the recounting of events and their meaning within a larger narrative framework.

In Ricoeur's framework, this dual tension reflects the relationship between the performative and the narrative dimensions of testimony. The performative dimension—the act of witnessing—is an assertion of truth that commits the speaker to what they say. The narrative dimension—the telling of the story—provides the content and context that gives the testimony its meaning and its intelligibility. Testimony without narration is mere assertion; narration without testimony is mere storytelling. True testimony requires both.

In the trial of Abinadi, this tension is vividly displayed. Abinadi's testimony-confession is evident in his declaration of the sins of the people and his acknowledgment of the truth he has been commanded to speak. His testimony-narration is evident in the story he tells—the story of Christ's coming, Christ's atoning sacrifice, and the conditions of salvation. The confession gives the narration its urgency and moral force; the narration gives the confession its content and specificity. Neither alone would constitute complete testimony.

This dual structure has important implications for understanding how testimony works in religious contexts generally and in LDS contexts specifically. A testimony that is merely confession—"I know these things are true"—without narrative content is incomplete and ultimately unintelligible to others. A testimony that is merely narration—"here is the story"—without the confession of personal witness lacks the existential commitment that gives testimony its power. The most effective testimony integrates both dimensions: it tells a story and it stakes a claim upon the speaker's own credibility and commitment.

## VII. The Quasi-Judicial Trial: Witness as Accused and Emissary

The mechanism of the quasi-judicial trial in which the witness stands simultaneously as accused and emissary represents one of the most philosophically rich structures in the analysis of testimony. In Abinadi's trial, this structure is clearly present: Abinadi is accused before King Noah's court, yet his testimony functions as an accusation against the king and his people from the authority of God. The witness is simultaneously in the dock and on the bench.

Ricoeur's juridical analysis helps illuminate this structure. In legal proceedings, the witness occupies a liminal position—they are neither fully party to the proceedings nor fully outside them. They are called to account but they also hold authority over the truth. This liminality is intensified in prophetic trials like Abinadi's, where the witness not only testifies to truth but also pronounces judgment upon those who reject it. The witness becomes, in effect, a judge, and the trial becomes a judgment not merely of the witness but of all who are present.

The quasi-judicial structure reveals something important about the nature of testimony as a performative act. When one testifies, one is not merely stating facts but is actively participating in a judgment—about the truth, about the credibility of claims, and about the moral standing of those involved. The testimony of Abinadi judges King Noah and his priests not merely by revealing their sins but by demonstrating, through Abinadi's willingness to suffer and die for truth, the moral bankruptcy of those who would silence truth.

This structure has a profound eschatological dimension. The trial of Abinadi is not merely a historical event but a rehearsal of the final judgment—the world's judgment upon itself as it rejects the testimony of God's servants. Abinadi, as an emissary of eschatological judgment, stands for all prophets who have been rejected by the world, and his trial prefigures the ultimate judgment in which all testimony will be weighed and all witnesses will be vindicated or condemned according to their faithfulness.

In LDS theology, this eschatological dimension is particularly significant. The LDS understanding of the plan of salvation includes a final judgment in which all will be judged according to their works and the testimony they have received and borne. The prophetic trial of Abinadi thus functions as a microcosm of the final judgment—a preview of the cosmic courtroom in which Christ himself will be the judge and in which all testimony, all confession, and all narration will be evaluated according to their relationship to truth.

## VIII. Resolving the Contradiction: Personal Witness and Historic Narrative

The apparent contradiction between personal testimony of the Holy Ghost and the necessity of historic narrative content is one of the most philosophically significant tensions in the theology of testimony. On one hand, the personal witness of the Holy Ghost is immediate, subjective, and experiential—it is something one "knows" in the deepest part of oneself. On the other hand, testimony always has content—it testifies of specific things, and those things are constituted by narrative claims about history, scripture, and divine action.

Ricoeur's hermeneutic framework provides resources for resolving this tension. Ricoeur distinguishes between the "world of the text" and the "world of the reader" and argues that interpretation involves a dynamic interplay between these two worlds. The text (or testimony) opens up a world of meaning that the reader must enter, and the reader brings their own world of experience and understanding to the encounter. This dynamic is not a defect of testimony but its constitutive feature: testimony creates meaning through the encounter between the testimony's content and the hearer's understanding.

Applied to the LDS understanding of testimony, this framework suggests that the personal witness of the Holy Ghost and the historic narrative content of testimony are not in opposition but in dynamic relationship. The Holy Ghost witnesses of the truth of the narrative content not by making the narrative literally true in the mind of the believer but by opening the believer to the meaning and reality that the narrative conveys. The personal witness and the narrative content are integrated through what Ricoeur calls "appropriation"—the process by which the hearer makes the testimony their own, integrating its content into their own understanding and commitment.

This resolution has important implications for how testimony is understood and practiced. It suggests that testimony is not valid merely because it is subjectively certain, nor merely because it corresponds to historical facts, but because it participates in what Ricoeur calls the "world of the text"—the world of meaning that testimony opens up and that the hearer enters through interpretation. The Holy Ghost's witness is precisely the illumination that enables this entry into the world of testimony, making the narrative content not merely believed but personally appropriated and lived.

Furthermore, this resolution suggests that the tension between personal witness and narrative content is not a problem to be eliminated but a creative tension that gives testimony its philosophical richness. The personal witness without narrative content would be formless certainty; the narrative content without personal witness would be empty tradition. Together, they constitute testimony as a complete act: the personal witness provides the existential commitment, and the narrative content provides the intellectual and imaginative framework through which that commitment is expressed and communicated.

## IX. Testimony as Participation in the Cosmic Struggle

The final dimension of this analysis is the recognition that bearing testimony constitutes active participation in what might be called the cosmic struggle between Christ and the world. This struggle is not merely a future eschatological event but an ongoing reality in which every act of testimony participates. When one bears testimony, one is not merely making a claim about truth but is actively siding with Christ against the forces of the world that suppress truth and persecute witnesses.

Ricoeur's concept of "the hermeneutic claim" provides a framework for understanding this participation. Every testimony makes a claim not merely about the truth of its content but about the nature of reality, the character of truth, and the relationship between the speaker and the hearer. This claim is simultaneously a claim about what is true and a claim about what is good—a claim that participates in the ongoing cosmic drama in which Christ bears witness to the Father and the Spirit bears witness of Christ, while the world bears false witness and suppresses the truth.

In the trial of Abinadi, this cosmic struggle is enacted with particular clarity. Abinadi's testimony is not merely a declaration about King Noah's sins but a participation in Christ's own testimony to the world. When Abinadi speaks of Christ's atoning sacrifice, he is not merely reporting information but is actively participating in the testimony that Christ gives to the world through prophets and apostles. His words are Christ's words, and in speaking them, Abinadi becomes a participant in the cosmic witness that Christ continually gives.

The LDS understanding of testimony as participation in the cosmic struggle is reflected in the practice of bearing testimony in church meetings, in missionary work, and in personal spiritual development. When a member of the Church bears testimony, they are not merely sharing their personal experience but are participating in what the LDS tradition calls "the testimony of Jesus"—the ongoing witness that Christ gives of himself through his servants and through the Holy Ghost. This participation is not optional or peripheral but constitutive: to bear testimony is to be an active participant in the cosmic struggle, to take sides with Christ against the world, and to contribute to the unfolding of the divine plan.

This understanding also illuminates the ethical dimension of testimony. If bearing testimony constitutes participation in the cosmic struggle, then testimony is not merely a communicative act but an ethical act—it involves commitment, risk, and responsibility. The witness who testifies takes a stand, not merely about facts but about allegiance. The testimony of Abinadi cost him his life because it was a testimony of allegiance to Christ against the powers of the world, and every testimony that is genuinely rooted in the witness of the Holy Ghost participates in that same cost.

## X. Conclusion: The Witness and the Word

The philosophical structures of testimony, as illuminated through Ricoeur's semantic and juridical analyses and through the LDS understanding of religious witness, reveal testimony to be far more than the communication of information. Testimony is a mode of being—a way of standing in relation to truth, to community, and to the cosmic drama in which Christ and the world contend for the souls of humanity.

The dual tension between testimony-confession and testimony-narration is not a problem to be resolved but a constitutive feature of testimony itself. Confession provides the existential commitment that gives testimony its force; narration provides the content and context that give testimony its intelligibility. Together, they constitute testimony as a complete speech act that simultaneously discloses the witness, communicates truth, and participates in the ongoing drama of meaning-making.

The quasi-judicial trial structure, as exemplified in Abinadi's trial, reveals the liminal position of the witness—simultaneously accused and accusing, judged and judging, a participant in both temporal and eschatological proceedings. This structure illuminates the paradox of testimony: the witness who stands before the world to speak truth is simultaneously standing before God, and their testimony is evaluated not merely by human standards but by the ultimate standard of divine truth.

The resolution of the apparent contradiction between personal witness and historic narrative content lies in recognizing that the personal witness of the Holy Ghost and the narrative content of testimony are integrated through the hermeneutic process of appropriation. The Holy Ghost illuminates the meaning of the narrative, making it not merely believed but personally known and lived. This integration preserves both the subjective certainty of personal witness and the objective content of historic narrative, avoiding the extremes of subjective mysticism and empty historicism.

Finally, bearing testimony constitutes active participation in the cosmic struggle between Christ and the world. Every testimony, whether spoken in a church meeting or whispered in private devotion, is a participation in the ongoing witness that Christ gives of himself through the Spirit. The witness who bears testimony does not merely report truth but enacts it—making themselves vulnerable, staking their credibility, and aligning themselves with the divine cause against the forces of darkness. In this sense, testimony is not merely what we do with words but what we are with our lives: a standing witness, a living confession, a continuing narration of the cosmic drama in which Christ is both the witness and the truth.

The philosophical structures of testimony, thus understood, reveal that bearing witness is among the most profound human acts—one that engages the full range of human capacities: language, commitment, narrative imagination, ethical courage, and spiritual sensitivity. To testify is to stand at the intersection of all these capacities, and in that standing, to participate in the great work of making truth known in a world that too often suppresses it. The witness and the word are one, and in their unity, they constitute the testimony that Christ himself has given and continues to give to the world through those who are willing to stand and speak.
