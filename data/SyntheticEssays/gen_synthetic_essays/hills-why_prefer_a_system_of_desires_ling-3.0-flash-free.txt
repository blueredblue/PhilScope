**The Architecture of the Will: Coherence, Systematization, and the Nature of Rational Deliberation**

The question of what it means to be a rational agent has long been the cornerstone of philosophical inquiry into action, motivation, and normative assessment. At the heart of this inquiry lies a fundamental tension: must an agent’s desires be systematically justifiable, or is it sufficient for rational deliberation that those desires merely avoid internal contradiction? The debate between the internalist tradition, exemplified by Michael Smith’s account of fully rational agents, and the subjective motivational framework of Bernard Williams, offers a profound lens through which to evaluate the necessity of systematic justifiability. This essay argues that making desires systematically justifiable is not a necessary component of rational deliberation. A rational agent requires only the weaker sense of coherence—the avoidance of practical conflicts—rather than the stronger sense of subsuming specific desires under general ones. Furthermore, the analogy between theoretical reasoning and practical systematization ultimately fails to justify the reduction of independent underived preferences into a unified desiderative profile. Finally, the rejection of systematic justification implies that internal reasons do not require non-relativistic grounding, as their validity is securely anchored in the agent's subjective motivational set.

To evaluate the necessity of systematic justifiability, one must first understand the nature of rational deliberation as described by Michael Smith. In *The Moral Problem*, Smith articulates an internalist, Humean account of rationality. For Smith, an agent is fully rational if and only if their beliefs and desires are mutually consistent and their instrumental reasoning is flawless. That is, a fully rational agent must not have any false beliefs about the means to their ends, nor must they have any conflicting ends that would prevent them from pursuing what they most want. Smith’s fully rational agent is an idealized Humean: desires are primitive, motivational forces, and rationality consists in the efficient and consistent pursuit of those desires given the agent’s beliefs. 

Crucially, Smith’s internalism dictates that an agent’s desires are only reasons for action if they are causally connected to the agent’s motivation. This means that an underived preference—a desire that is not traceable to some more fundamental desire or principle—can still function as a reason for the agent, provided it genuinely motivates them. Smith does not demand that every desire be justified by a more general principle; he demands only that the agent’s practical reasoning be coherent. If an agent has an underived preference for, say, a particular shade of blue, and this preference motivates them to buy a blue shirt, Smith would consider this practically rational so long as the agent believes the blue shirt will satisfy their preference and they do not simultaneously desire not to buy it. Thus, within Smith’s framework, systematic justifiability—the requirement that specific desires be subsumed under general ones—is not a necessary component of rational deliberation. The agent need only ensure that their desires do not contradict one another in ways that paralyze action or lead to self-defeating means-end calculations.

Bernard Williams’s account of the subjective motivational set (SMS) provides a complementary and deeply influential framework for understanding the limits of rational systematization. Williams argues that an agent’s reasons for action are intrinsically tied to their SMS, which is the set of desires, projects, and commitments that constitute the agent’s practical identity and motivate their deliberation. For Williams, an internal reason is something that is connected to the agent’s SMS in a specific way: it must be a consideration that can motivate the agent given their existing set of desires and projects. Conversely, external reasons—considerations that would motivate a different kind of agent or an idealized agent, but not *this* particular agent—are not genuine reasons for them.

Williams’s framework inherently privileges the agent’s particularity. Because the SMS is subjective and historically contingent, it resists the kind of systematic unification that the stronger sense of coherence demands. The stronger sense of coherence requires that specific desires be derived from or subsumed under general principles or a master desire. For instance, it would require that the agent’s specific desire to eat chocolate be justified by a general principle such as "maximize pleasure" or "pursue sensory enjoyment." Williams vehemently rejects this demand, arguing that it alienates the agent from their own agency. When we force an agent to subsume their particular desires under general ones, we transform their motivational life from a set of lived projects into a mere instantiation of abstract rules. Williams contends that such systematization is not a requirement of rationality, but rather a distortion of it. Rational deliberation, for Williams, only requires that the agent’s desires do not engage in practical conflicts—where the agent is simultaneously compelled to pursue X and not-X, or where the means to an end contradict the end itself. This is the weaker sense of coherence: the avoidance of internal practical inconsistency.

The distinction between the stronger and weaker senses of coherence is pivotal to understanding why systematic justifiability is unnecessary for rational deliberation. The weaker sense of coherence is strictly procedural: it demands that an agent’s practical reasoning does not contain explicit contradictions. If an agent wants to go to the party but also wants to stay home to study for an exam, and they believe they cannot do both, they face a practical conflict. Rational deliberation requires them to resolve this conflict—perhaps by prioritizing studying over the party, or by finding a way to do both. The weaker sense is satisfied once the conflict is resolved and the agent has a coherent plan of action.

The stronger sense of coherence, by contrast, is architectural. It demands that the agent’s entire desiderative profile be unified under a set of general principles or a master desire. This is the view often associated with Kantian or utilitarian theories of rationality, where specific inclinations must be subordinated to a universal maxim or the greatest good. The stronger sense requires not just the resolution of conflicts, but the *derivation* of all desires from a common foundation. However, there is no compelling reason to believe that rational deliberation must demand this architectural unity. Human beings are not calculating machines optimized for a single utility function; they are complex agents with heterogeneous, often conflicting, projects and attachments. Demanding that these be systematically unified is not a constraint on rationality, but an external imposition on the agent’s psychological reality. Rational deliberation is a tool for navigating the world and achieving one’s goals; it does not require the architect of the self to first dismantle the self’s particularity and rebuild it as a unified edifice of general principles. The weaker sense of coherence—ensuring that one’s means and ends align and that one does not actively sabotage one’s own projects—is entirely sufficient for rational deliberation.

To further solidify this argument, we must examine the analogy between theoretical reasoning and practical systematization. It is a common intuition in philosophy that rationality demands a kind of systematic coherence in our beliefs. In theoretical reasoning, we expect our beliefs to be consistent and unified; we seek explanatory theories that reduce diverse phenomena to a few fundamental laws. A belief set that contains random, unconnected, and unprincipled propositions is often considered epistemically defective because it fails to provide a unified picture of reality. This has led some to argue that practical reasoning must mirror theoretical reasoning: just as we systematize our beliefs into a coherent worldview, we must systematize our desires into a unified desiderative profile. Under this view, independent underived preferences are akin to epistemically unjustified beliefs—they are random, unprincipled, and indicative of irrationality.

However, this analogy ultimately breaks down upon closer inspection. Theoretical reasoning is fundamentally about representation: beliefs aim to accurately depict the world. The unity of theoretical reason is a unity of truth; a unified belief system is better because it explains more and predicts more accurately. Practical reasoning, on the other hand, is fundamentally about motivation and action. Desires do not aim to represent the world; they aim to move the agent through it. The unity of practical reason is a unity of agency, not a unity of truth. 

When we demand that practical systematization mirror theoretical systematization, we commit a category mistake. Reducing independent underived preferences into a unified desiderative profile would mean forcing the agent’s motivational life into a single, overarching explanatory framework. But the value of a theoretical system lies in its explanatory power, not in its aesthetic unity. In practical life, the value of a desire lies in its motivational force, not in its derivability from a general principle. An underived preference for a particular hobby, a specific food, or a certain person may not fit neatly into a general theory of the good life, but it is no less real or rational as a motivator. To demand that such preferences be justified under a general principle is to misunderstand the nature of desire itself. Rationality in the practical domain does not mandate the reduction of independent underived preferences; it mandates only that those preferences, when acted upon, do not contradict the agent’s other commitments or lead to practical impasses. The analogy between theoretical and practical reasoning fails because the functions of belief and desire are fundamentally different, and thus the standards of coherence appropriate for each are distinct.

This conclusion—that systematic justifiability is unnecessary and that the weaker sense of coherence is sufficient—has profound implications for the nature of internal reasons and the question of relativism. If rational deliberation does not require the subsumption of specific desires under general principles, then the validity of an agent’s reasons does not depend on their connection to a universal or non-relativistic standard. Instead, the validity of internal reasons is tethered entirely to the agent’s subjective motivational set. 

The question then arises: does this tether make internal reasons relativistic? In philosophy, "relativism" often carries a pejorative connotation, suggesting that reasons are merely a function of arbitrary, contingent psychological facts, devoid of any objective grounding. If internal reasons are simply whatever the agent happens to desire, one might worry that they are nothing more than expressions of subjective whim, and thus relativistic in a trivial or uninteresting sense. However, the rejection of systematic justification does not entail that internal reasons must be non-relativistic in the sense of being universally objective. Rather, it clarifies that internal reasons are non-relativistic *relative to the agent* while remaining relativistic *relative to different agents*. 

An internal reason is non-relativistic in the sense that it is not merely an external imposition; it is grounded in the agent’s own psychological reality and can genuinely motivate them. When Williams argues that a consideration is an internal reason only if it connects to the agent’s SMS, he is not saying that the reason is arbitrary or that "anything goes." The SMS is a structured, historically developed set of desires and projects; it imposes its own constraints on what can count as a reason. For example, if an agent’s SMS includes a deep commitment to artistic integrity, then the consideration that a certain action would compromise that integrity is an internal reason for them not to perform it. This reason is not relativistic in the sense of being capricious; it is firmly anchored in the agent’s evaluative framework. 

Systematic justification, conversely, would require internal reasons to be non-relativistic in a different, more demanding sense: they would have to be derivable from principles that are independent of the agent’s particular SMS and applicable to all rational agents. But as we have seen, this demand is neither necessary for rationality nor consistent with the nature of human motivation. The requirement of systematic justification would effectively collapse the distinction between internal and external reasons by forcing all internal reasons to be justified by a universal standard, thereby erasing the agent’s particularity. By abandoning the demand for systematic justification, we preserve the distinctness of internal reasons. Internal reasons are relativistic because they are relative to the agent’s SMS, but they are not relativistic in the sense of being groundless or arbitrary. Their objectivity is internal to the agent’s life and projects.

In conclusion, the requirement that desires be made systematically justifiable is not a necessary component of rational deliberation. Michael Smith’s account of the fully rational agent demonstrates that rationality requires only the consistency of beliefs and desires and the flawless execution of instrumental means-end reasoning, not the architectural unification of all desires under general principles. Bernard Williams’s subjective motivational set further reinforces this point by showing that an agent’s reasons are intrinsically tied to their particular projects and desires, and that forcing these desires into a unified, systematic profile constitutes an alienation of the self rather than an achievement of rationality. The distinction between the weaker and stronger senses of coherence is thus decisive: the weaker sense—avoiding practical conflicts—is entirely sufficient for rational deliberation, while the stronger sense—subsuming specific desires under general ones—is an unnecessary and distorting demand. 

The analogy between theoretical and practical reasoning ultimately fails to justify the reduction of independent underived preferences into a unified desiderative profile, precisely because theoretical reasoning aims at truth and explanatory unity, whereas practical reasoning aims at motivation and action. By rejecting systematic justification, we do not fall into a void of arbitrariness; rather, we affirm that internal reasons are non-relativistic relative to the agent’s own subjective motivational set. They are grounded in the agent’s lived reality and possess their own internal structure and constraints. Thus, rational deliberation remains a deeply personal, particular, and coherent process—one that requires only that our means serve our ends, and that our ends do not mutually destroy one another, leaving the rich, irreducible plurality of human desire intact.
