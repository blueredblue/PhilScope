# Practice, Disclosure, and the Problem of Transcendence: Rorty, Brandom, and Heidegger

Richard Rorty’s pragmatism is animated by a decisive ambition: to dissolve the philosophical demand for a standpoint outside history, language, and social practice. On his view, traditional ontology has too often functioned as the search for a vocabulary that would describe reality as it is independently of all human purposes. The pragmatist instead asks what vocabularies enable us to do, what practices they sustain, what forms of solidarity they make possible, and whether they contribute to human flourishing. “Reality,” in this setting, is not denied but deprived of the role of an ahistorical tribunal standing over every human description. Philosophy becomes cultural politics: the comparative invention and criticism of ways of speaking and acting.

This replacement seems to promise a successful avoidance of metaphysical transcendence. If there is no vocabulary privileged by nature, no final description of Being, and no standpoint outside all possible practices, then philosophy need not posit a transcendent source of authority. Yet the success of this strategy depends upon what “social practice” is taken to explain. Does it explain only the norms by which claims are justified? Or does it also explain the existence, intelligibility, and givenness of entities? Robert Brandom’s reading of Heidegger’s categories in *Being and Time*—especially *Zuhandensein*, *Vorhandensein*, and *Dasein*—provides a useful test. Brandom shows how much of Heidegger’s analysis can be reconstructed in terms of socially instituted norms, practical capacities, and inferential commitments. But Heidegger’s categories also point toward a dimension of disclosure that cannot easily be reduced to explicit or implicit social agreement.

The issue becomes sharper when one considers a tension within Rorty’s own pragmatism. Rorty rejects any “background of all possible practices,” since such a background would amount to a transcendent framework against which actual practices could be judged. Yet his pragmatism often appeals, following William James, to human happiness, satisfaction, or flourishing as a general end of social and intellectual activity. If happiness is merely one contingent preference among others, it cannot serve as a standard for criticizing practices that produce misery. If it is more than a contingent preference, then Rorty appears to rely on a structural norm that exceeds the particular practices he otherwise regards as the only sources of normativity.

Thomas Sheehan’s interpretation of Heidegger’s *Ereignis* and the *Kehre* helps clarify the alternatives. Heidegger’s later thought does not simply reinstate a metaphysical realm beyond social practice. Nor does it reduce the givenness of entities to communal construction. Rather, it describes an event or appropriation through which human beings and Being belong together in a clearing of intelligibility. This “between” may be called a *tertium quid*, but only if that expression is carefully understood. It is not a third entity or transcendent substance. It is a structural dimension of disclosure that precedes the opposition between independently existing objects and socially imposed meanings. Heidegger therefore suggests that Rorty successfully rejects classical metaphysical transcendence while failing to eliminate a weaker, ontological form of transcendence: the irreducible givenness that makes social practices possible without being produced by them.

## I. Rorty’s Anti-ontological Pragmatism

Rorty’s central target is not the ordinary belief that things exist independently of our minds. His target is the philosophical project of describing that independence in a vocabulary whose authority would not itself depend upon human purposes, historical circumstances, or linguistic practices. This project typically assumes that behind changing descriptions there is a final structure of reality to which thought ought to conform. Truth then becomes correspondence to that structure, and philosophy becomes the discipline charged with discovering or representing it.

Rorty rejects this picture in favor of a pragmatist account of language and inquiry. Vocabularies are tools rather than mirrors. They are developed within practices and evaluated by reference to what they permit human beings to accomplish. A vocabulary may be more useful, more inclusive, more predictive, more expressive, or more conducive to solidarity than another, but it is not “closer to reality” because it captures the intrinsic essence of things from nowhere. The standards of evaluation are themselves historically situated.

This position is not straightforward relativism. Rorty does not claim that every belief is as good as every other belief, nor that one may choose one’s beliefs arbitrarily. Practices contain standards of justification, and participants in those practices can offer reasons, criticize one another, and revise their commitments. What he rejects is the idea that there is a single standard of validity that applies independently of every possible vocabulary and practice. Objectivity, in the strongest philosophical sense, is replaced by what Rorty calls solidarity: agreement and mutual accountability within an expanding community of inquiry.

The political dimension of this argument is essential. Rorty’s goal is not merely to undermine metaphysics but to redirect philosophy toward the reduction of cruelty, the enlargement of sympathy, and the creation of democratic forms of life. Philosophers should not seek the final vocabulary of the human condition; they should participate in the imaginative redescription of social possibilities. Literature, politics, and historical memory are therefore as important as traditional epistemology. A culture becomes more rational not by approximating a timeless order but by becoming more capable of self-correction and more inclusive in its practices of justification.

This program does avoid a familiar version of metaphysical transcendence. No God, Platonic Form, noumenal world, or absolute vocabulary is required. Truth is not a relation to a reality articulated independently of all human concerns. Yet the question is whether Rorty’s social-pragmatic vocabulary can account for everything that ontology was intended to account for. If ontology asks not only “What is ultimately real?” but also “How is anything intelligible as an entity at all?” then Rorty’s answer may appear incomplete. The difficulty is not that he lacks a theory of objects. It is that he tends to treat the object’s significance as exhausted by the roles it plays in human practices. Heidegger’s categories expose the pressure point.

## II. Brandom’s Reconstruction of Heidegger

Brandom reads Heidegger as a precursor to a pragmatist and inferentialist account of intelligibility. The basic insight is that entities do not first appear as neutral objects possessing intrinsic properties and only later acquire practical significance. They ordinarily show up within contexts of use. A hammer is initially encountered as something for hammering, a door as something to be opened, a road as something to be traveled, and a tool as belonging to a network of purposes and dependencies. The world is not first a collection of objects to which human beings subsequently assign functions. It is encountered through practical involvement.

This is the significance of *Zuhandensein*, or readiness-to-hand. The ready-to-hand is not merely an object that happens to be useful. It is an entity whose intelligibility consists in its place within an equipmental context. A hammer is intelligible through its relation to nails, boards, construction, and the activity of building. Its being is disclosed in a practical nexus. In ordinary absorbed activity, the hammer is not thematized as an object with properties. It recedes into use.

Brandom reconstructs this insight in terms of implicit norms and practical know-how. To use equipment is to be able to distinguish appropriate from inappropriate performances, successful from unsuccessful applications, and relevant from irrelevant circumstances. Practical competence involves norms even when the agent cannot state them propositionally. The user knows how the equipment is to be handled, what it is for, and how it fits into a broader task. Such norms are not private mental representations but socially intelligible abilities. They belong to practices in which performances can be assessed.

The ready-to-hand therefore supports a pragmatist reversal of the traditional epistemological order. Knowledge is not fundamentally the contemplation of objects by detached subjects. Explicit cognition emerges from more basic involvement in a world. Theoretical description is a derivative mode of comportment, made possible by practical familiarity with entities and their relations.

*Vorhandensein*, or presence-at-hand, names a different mode in which entities can appear. When equipment breaks, becomes obstructive, or is deliberately examined, it can cease to recede into its practical function and become thematically present. The hammer may be considered as an object with a certain weight, shape, or material composition. Theoretical inquiry similarly treats entities as objects bearing properties that can be described, measured, and explained.

Brandom’s interpretation emphasizes that presence-at-hand is not an absolutely superior or more fundamental disclosure of what things really are. It is a transformation of practical intelligibility. An entity can be encountered as present-at-hand because it has already been situated within a practical world. Theoretical objectivity is thus not the elimination of practical involvement but its reorganization into a distinct form of norm-governed activity.

This reconstruction is congenial to Rorty. Both thinkers reject the image of an essentially detached mind confronting a world of bare objects. Both insist that intelligibility is inseparable from ways of dealing with things. Both challenge the notion that theoretical description supplies a more metaphysically authoritative picture than practical engagement. The present-at-hand does not reveal the intrinsic essence of reality behind appearances; it represents one socially and historically organized way of making entities available for inquiry.

Brandom’s account of *Dasein* extends this pragmatist reconstruction. Dasein is not simply the human animal considered biologically. It is the being for whom its own being is an issue, the being that inhabits a space of reasons and can take up normative commitments. Dasein is capable of understanding entities as equipment, objects, persons, obligations, and possibilities. It is the site at which intelligibility is enacted.

For Brandom, this can be described through the structure of normative statuses and normative attitudes. A community establishes or maintains commitments by recognizing individuals as responsible for certain claims and actions. To be a believer, promise-maker, authority, or accountable agent is to occupy a socially intelligible normative status. The community’s practices of attributing, acknowledging, challenging, and revising commitments constitute a space in which reasons can function.

This account should not be confused with the claim that whatever a community happens to approve is thereby true. Brandom’s social pragmatism is not simple conventionalism. Social practices include practices of criticism, correction, and distinction between better and worse performances. A community can misattribute authority or misunderstand its own norms. Moreover, the material world constrains what can be successfully done. A hammer that is too fragile to drive a nail will frustrate a practice, regardless of communal endorsement.

Nevertheless, the authority of norms, on Brandom’s account, depends upon their role in practices of recognition and assessment. The force of a reason is not a mysterious metaphysical power existing outside all linguistic and social activity. It is manifested in what agents are committed to, what they are entitled to claim, and what follows inferentially from their assertions. In this respect Brandom appears to support the reduction of epistemic authority to social practice.

Yet one must distinguish three claims:

1. Epistemic authority is socially articulated.
2. Ontological intelligibility is practically disclosed.
3. The existence or givenness of entities is socially produced.

The first two claims follow plausibly from Brandom’s reading of Heidegger. The third does not. A social practice may determine whether a hammer is recognized as a tool, how it is classified, and what uses are assigned to it. But it does not follow that the hammer’s resistance, materiality, or capacity to interrupt action is created by the practice. Nor does it follow that the world disclosed through practice is exhausted by the norms through which it is interpreted.

Brandom’s philosophical vocabulary tends to emphasize the first-person and third-person dimensions of normative practice: what agents do, what they are committed to, and how others keep score. Heidegger’s analysis, however, also includes a dimension that is neither a norm explicitly instituted by a community nor an object independently present to a detached observer. The world is the opening in which entities can matter at all. It is not merely a system of social rules. This is where a stronger Heideggerian critique of Rorty begins.

## III. Does Social Practice Exhaust Ontological Authority?

The proposition that all epistemic and ontological authority reduces to social practice can mean several different things. In its weakest form, it means that authority is never exercised outside a concrete historical community. No claim is justified without standards of assessment, and standards of assessment are socially transmitted and contestable. This proposition is plausible and is consistent with much of Heidegger, Brandom, and Rorty.

In a stronger form, the proposition says that what counts as an entity, what properties it has, and what constraints it imposes are wholly constituted by social practice. This view is more problematic. It risks confusing the conditions under which something is intelligible with the production of the thing itself. The hammer’s being-as-equipment depends upon a practical context, but the hammer is not simply a projection of that context. Indeed, equipment can fail. It can resist, break, or exceed its assigned use. Such failures are not merely additional social interpretations; they are events in which the limits of a practice are disclosed.

Heidegger’s analysis of readiness-to-hand makes this point indirectly. Equipment is normally inconspicuous because it functions smoothly. Its breakdown makes its presence obtrusive. The tool’s resistance reveals something about the world that the practice did not simply posit. The practice is dependent upon a material and worldly field that allows it to succeed or fail. A socially instituted norm can say that a tool is for a particular task, but it cannot guarantee that the tool will perform that task.

The same argument applies to epistemic authority. Social practices determine who is regarded as an expert, what counts as evidence, and which inferential transitions are licensed. But inquiry is not arbitrary because things can answer back. Experiments fail, predictions are disconfirmed, bodies become ill, structures collapse, and political projects produce consequences that their advocates did not intend. These events must be interpreted through practices, but they are not thereby created by interpretation.

A Rortyan might reply that “answering back” is simply another way of describing the role that events play within our practices. There is no need to posit a metaphysical reality behind the vocabulary of resistance. The fact that a bridge collapses matters because of human purposes and concepts. The bridge’s “resistance” is not a self-interpreting message from reality; it is a description within a practice.

This response is powerful against naïve realism. The event of collapse is never encountered without concepts, purposes, and linguistic resources. But it does not establish that the event is nothing more than those resources. There is a difference between saying that something can be encountered only within a practice and saying that it is produced by that practice. The former is a claim about access and intelligibility; the latter is a claim about constitution. Rorty often moves too quickly from the inescapability of vocabulary to the sufficiency of vocabulary.

Heidegger’s distinction between *Zuhandensein* and *Vorhandensein* makes the problem especially visible. The ready-to-hand is constituted by practical relations, but the present-at-hand is not simply an alternative social convention. It is the disclosure of an entity as independently determinable, resistant, or available for theoretical investigation. Heidegger does not claim that presence-at-hand gives access to entities “in themselves” in the classical metaphysical sense. Still, he refuses to reduce it to a freely chosen redescription. The mode of disclosure is historical and situated, but what is disclosed is not thereby invented.

Dasein further complicates the social reduction. Dasein is essentially being-in-the-world and being-with-others, so no account of Dasein can neglect sociality. The norms of everyday existence are governed by *das Man*, the anonymous “they” that determines what one does, says, and values. Yet Dasein is not identical with the public norms of the they. Anxiety, conscience, resoluteness, and being-toward-death disclose possibilities that cannot be reduced to conformist social recognition. Even if one interprets these existential structures non-metaphysically, they indicate that Dasein is not merely a node in a system of socially administered statuses.

The social is therefore necessary for human intelligibility but not exhaustive of it. The world is public, but it is not simply a consensus. It includes inherited practices, material environments, bodily capacities, historical situations, and possibilities that may be appropriated or transformed. Social practice is one dimension of world-disclosure, not the absolute source of every dimension.

## IV. Rorty, James, and the Norm of Happiness

Rorty’s rejection of a background of all possible practices is one of the strongest features of his anti-foundationalism. Any appeal to such a background would reintroduce the very standpoint he seeks to abandon. To judge practices from outside all practices would require a vocabulary not itself subject to historical contingency. Rorty therefore treats standards as internal to forms of life and sees philosophical criticism as a redescription from one contingent standpoint to another.

The difficulty is that Rorty’s pragmatism is not normatively empty. His favored political vocabulary includes liberal democracy, anti-authoritarianism, reduction of cruelty, openness to conversation, and expansion of solidarity. These are not presented merely as arbitrary preferences. They are offered as reasons for preferring one form of life to another. Rorty’s appeal to James intensifies the problem. Pragmatism is associated with the idea that beliefs and practices should be evaluated by their consequences for human life, including their contribution to happiness, satisfaction, or flourishing.

If human happiness is only a locally preferred goal, Rorty can maintain consistency. A society that values domination, sacrifice, or ascetic suffering may reject happiness, and there would be no practice-independent reason to correct it. But then Rorty’s liberalism loses its critical force. He could say only that he prefers a less cruel and more democratic society, not that such a society is better for human beings in a more general sense.

If, by contrast, happiness names a general standard for social action, then it begins to function as a structural end. It need not be a metaphysical object or a transcendent Form. James’s pragmatism is not hedonism in a simple sense, and “happiness” can include meaningful activity, successful adjustment, freedom, growth, and the satisfaction of purposes. Nevertheless, a standard that evaluates practices by their contribution to human flourishing is not reducible to the mere fact that a practice recognizes it. It is a norm by which existing practices can be criticized.

The tension can be formulated in three ways. First, Rorty needs a distinction between genuine and defective forms of human life. Second, he needs an account of why solidarity should be enlarged rather than restricted to a privileged group. Third, he needs a reason to prefer conversation and democratic experimentation to coercion when coercion might produce stability or even the satisfaction of some desires.

Rorty might answer that these commitments emerge from the contingencies of modern Western history. Liberal democracy has no timeless justification; it is simply the best vocabulary that our historical experience has so far produced. Its justification is retrospective and practical rather than metaphysical. It has enabled more people to pursue their projects and reduced cruelty more effectively than its competitors.

This answer is coherent, but it does not entirely eliminate transcendence. It shifts transcendence from a metaphysical realm to a regulative orientation. The future community, the enlargement of solidarity, and the reduction of cruelty function as ideals that exceed any existing practice. They are not standards outside history, but they are standards by which historical practices are judged. Rorty can describe them as contingent projects, but their critical role resembles that of a quasi-transcendental norm.

The same point applies to happiness. Human happiness need not be an essence possessed by all persons or an objective value written into the cosmos. It can be an open-ended horizon generated through historical experimentation. But precisely as a horizon, it is not identical with any current practice. It organizes criticism without being fully realized in the practices it evaluates. Rorty therefore avoids metaphysical transcendence only by transforming it into political futurity.

This transformation may be philosophically legitimate. There is no contradiction in saying that norms are historically generated yet capable of criticizing their historical origins. But the account must acknowledge that the social is not self-sufficient in a static sense. Social practices contain aspirations that point beyond their present arrangements. The authority of those aspirations cannot be explained solely by their current social acceptance.

## V. Sheehan, *Ereignis*, and the Meaning of the *Kehre*

Thomas Sheehan’s interpretation of Heidegger’s later thought is especially relevant because it prevents us from treating *Ereignis* as a mystical substitute for the old metaphysical God or as a transcendent super-entity called Being. Sheehan emphasizes that Heidegger’s question concerns the meaning or intelligibility of Being rather than a supreme being standing over other beings. The decisive issue is how entities can show up as meaningful at all.

On this interpretation, *Ereignis* should not primarily be translated as an event occurring at a particular time. It is better understood as appropriation or belonging-together. Human beings are appropriated into a clearing in which entities can be encountered, while Being is disclosed only through the openness of human existence. The relationship is reciprocal: there is no disclosure of Being without human openness, but human openness is not an arbitrary construction. It is itself given, situated, and appropriated.

The *Kehre*, or turn, does not represent a rejection of the concerns of *Being and Time* so much as a shift in emphasis. Early Heidegger begins from the analysis of Dasein as the site where the question of Being can arise. Later Heidegger places greater emphasis on the giving or granting of the clearing itself. The focus moves from the structures of human existence to the event through which both human existence and the intelligibility of entities are brought into their relation.

Sheehan’s reading helps avoid a common misunderstanding. If *Ereignis* were simply a hidden metaphysical cause behind the world, Heidegger would have returned to the ontology he had criticized. But if *Ereignis* means the originary opening of intelligibility, then it is not a thing that exists alongside other things. It is the event of there being a meaningful “there” in which things can appear. The expression “there is” becomes crucial: entities are given within a clearing, but the giving is not necessarily attributable to a personal giver or a transcendent substance.

This account challenges Rorty at a precise point. Rorty is right that no entity is encountered outside language, history, or practice. But *Ereignis* names the condition that makes such encounter possible without being equivalent to any particular language, history, or practice. The clearing is not a background of all possible practices in the sense of a completed super-vocabulary. It is not a catalogue of every possible norm. Yet it is more than the collection of actual practices. It is the open dimension in which practices can arise, change, and encounter what they did not anticipate.

The clearing also explains why social construction cannot be the whole story. Social practices disclose entities in particular ways, but disclosure is not identical with fabrication. A practice inherits a world it did not create from nothing. It encounters possibilities, resistances, and historical conditions that can be transformed but not simply willed away. *Ereignis* gives a name to this prior belonging between human intelligibility and the world’s capacity to matter.

Does this require a *tertium quid*? The answer depends on what the phrase means. If it means a third realm between society and reality, or an intermediary entity that transmits metaphysical meanings to human beings, then no. Heidegger’s later thought does not require such a realm. *Ereignis* is not an object, foundation, or substance. It cannot function as an explanatory item added to the inventory of beings.

If *tertium quid* means a third dimension irreducible to the alternatives of social construction and independent objectivity, then yes. An adequate account of givenness needs something like it. The relation among entity, practice, and disclosure cannot be captured if we insist that an entity is either wholly independent of human intelligibility or wholly constituted by social norms. There is a middle dimension in which entities become available, significant, and resistant. It is not a compromise between realism and constructivism but a more primordial structure within which that opposition arises.

## VI. Can Rorty Incorporate This Middle Dimension?

One might try to absorb Heidegger’s insight into Rorty’s pragmatism. The “clearing” could be redescribed as the totality of linguistic and social practices through which things become intelligible. *Ereignis* would then be a poetic name for the historicity of vocabularies. The advantage of this reading is economy: it avoids introducing obscure ontological language and remains faithful to Rorty’s insistence that there is no access to reality apart from practices.

But the cost is substantial. If the clearing is identified with actual practices, then it cannot explain the emergence of new practices or the breakdown of old ones. A vocabulary can be redescribed only because it encounters problems it cannot adequately handle. A community can revise its norms only because something—an event, experience, resistance, or possibility—disturbs its established patterns. If everything is reduced to practice, then the distinction between practice and what challenges practice disappears.

Rorty could reply that challenges are simply new practices. A scientific anomaly becomes philosophically significant only when a community incorporates it into a revised vocabulary. A political injustice becomes visible only when a new moral vocabulary redescribes it as injustice. This is true as far as it goes. But it does not explain why some redescriptions gain traction and others fail, except by appealing to further consequences within practice. The account risks becoming circular: a practice is successful because it works, and it works because it is successful within a practice.

Heidegger’s language of disclosure avoids this circularity without restoring traditional foundationalism. Entities are not first present in a fully determinate form, waiting to be copied by language. Nor are they empty until social practice gives them meaning. They are given through a historically situated opening in which their significance can be articulated. The opening is itself subject to transformation, but it is not an arbitrary product of decision.

This has consequences for epistemic authority. Social recognition is indispensable to the operation of reasons, but reasons are not exhausted by recognition. A person may be recognized as an authority and yet be wrong. A community may fail to acknowledge a truth that later becomes unavoidable. A practice may exclude voices whose claims eventually transform the criteria of legitimacy. Social authority is therefore reflexive and revisable because it is answerable to something that it cannot fully control.

The “something” should not be imagined as a set of propositions existing outside history. The world does not deliver ready-made judgments. It gives occasions, resistances, possibilities, and consequences that require interpretation. But interpretation is not omnipotent. The world’s givenness is precisely what prevents practice from being a closed system of self-legislation.

## VII. Transcendence Reconsidered

The debate can be clarified by distinguishing three senses of transcendence.

The first is substantial transcendence: a divine being, Platonic order, noumenal reality, or metaphysical foundation existing beyond the world of practices. Rorty successfully rejects this kind of transcendence. Nothing in the analysis of practical intelligibility, social normativity, or historical language requires such an entity.

The second is epistemic transcendence: a final standpoint from which all practices could be evaluated. Rorty also rejects this. There is no neutral vocabulary that compares all vocabularies without itself belonging to a practice. Philosophical criticism is always situated.

The third is structural or ontological transcendence: the fact that entities and possibilities can exceed the practices through which they are disclosed. This does not mean that entities exist in a fully determinate form outside all relations to human beings. It means that practices are finite openings rather than total creations. They disclose and conceal; they enable and restrict; they encounter what they cannot entirely master.

Rorty’s philosophy is most vulnerable with respect to this third sense. He can avoid it by stipulating that “exceeding practice” is merely another description within practice. But this move does not refute the Heideggerian claim; it changes the subject. The issue is not whether we can speak of excess without a vocabulary. Of course we cannot. The issue is whether the excess named by a vocabulary is reducible to the vocabulary’s own social production. Heidegger’s answer is no.

Brandom’s account occupies an intermediate position. It explains how practical and theoretical intelligibility can be reconstructed through norms, commitments, and social recognition. This is a major advance over representationalist epistemology. It also shows why human access to entities is inseparable from practices of assessment. But Brandom’s inferentialism can be supplemented by Heidegger’s account of world-disclosure. Normative statuses are socially instituted, while the field in which entities can become objects of commitment is not simply instituted by those statuses.

The same supplementation helps with Rorty’s appeal to happiness. Human flourishing can be understood not as a fixed essence but as a historically developing horizon. It is articulated in social practices, yet it also provides a standpoint from which practices can be criticized. The horizon is neither a metaphysical standard nor a mere report of current preferences. It is a practical ideal that emerges through the experience of suffering, exclusion, failure, and possibility. Such an ideal has a quasi-transcendent function because it exceeds any settled social order without existing outside history.

The danger is that “human happiness” may conceal unjustified anthropological assumptions. Who counts as human? What forms of life count as happiness? Does happiness mean pleasure, autonomy, recognition, achievement, communal belonging, or something else? James’s pragmatism is valuable precisely because it refuses to reduce life to a single abstract essence. Yet Rorty’s political use of happiness requires more explicit reflection on these questions. Otherwise the term either becomes too thin to guide criticism or too thick to avoid a substantive philosophy of human nature.

Heidegger would likely object that happiness is not the most fundamental measure of disclosure. A society could be satisfied within a restricted and forgetful world. The question is not simply whether people are happy but what possibilities of being are made visible or concealed. Rorty, however, might answer that such ontological language ultimately serves no purpose unless translated into political consequences. This disagreement cannot be settled by choosing between “ontology” and “politics.” It concerns whether political evaluation presupposes an account of the kinds of disclosure that make freedom, suffering, and possibility intelligible.

## Conclusion

Rorty’s pragmatic replacement of ontology with cultural politics successfully avoids metaphysical transcendence in its classical forms. It rejects the idea that philosophy can discover a final vocabulary, represent reality from nowhere, or appeal to a timeless realm that legislates over historical practices. Its emphasis on contingency, solidarity, and democratic redescription is both philosophically defensible and politically fruitful.

Yet Rorty does not fully establish that all epistemic and ontological authority reduces to social practice. Brandom’s reading of Heidegger demonstrates that readiness-to-hand, presence-at-hand, and Dasein can be understood through practical competence, theoretical thematization, and socially articulated norms. This reconstruction shows that objectivity is not detached from practice and that reasons function within communities of recognition and assessment. But it does not entail that entities, resistance, or the conditions of intelligibility are socially produced. The distinction between social articulation and ontological constitution remains decisive.

Rorty’s reliance on Jamesian happiness reveals a parallel difficulty. If happiness is only a contingent preference, it cannot ground the critique of cruelty, exclusion, and domination that animates Rorty’s liberalism. If it is a general standard of flourishing, it functions as more than an accepted norm within particular practices. Rorty can preserve consistency by treating happiness as an open-ended political horizon rather than an eternal value. But this horizon retains a structural transcendence: it judges present practices without standing outside history.

Sheehan’s interpretation of *Ereignis* and the *Kehre* offers the most adequate way to understand what is missing. The givenness of entities does not require a transcendent substance or a realm of metaphysical meanings. It does require an irreducible clearing in which human beings and entities belong together. This clearing is not a background containing all possible practices, nor is it identical with the sum of actual practices. It is the event of intelligibility itself, the condition under which practices can disclose, transform, and be disrupted by entities and possibilities.

Thus an adequate account does require a *tertium quid*, but only in a formal and ontological—not substantial or metaphysical—sense. The third term is neither an independently present world untouched by human understanding nor a socially constructed world exhausted by communal norms. It is the happening of disclosure that mediates between them. Rorty is right to deny that this mediation can be represented as a final foundation. He is less persuasive when he concludes that what cannot serve as a foundation is therefore nothing more than social practice.

The deepest lesson is that transcendence need not mean escape from history. There is a transcendence internal to finitude: the capacity of entities, events, and possibilities to exceed the practices in which they first become intelligible. Cultural politics depends upon this excess, because criticism and transformation would be impossible if practices could only reproduce their own norms. Rorty’s pragmatism remains compelling as an anti-foundational politics of hope, but it becomes philosophically adequate only when supplemented by the Heideggerian insight that the social is not the ultimate source of disclosure. Practices are historical ways of opening a world; they are not the authors of there being a world to open.
