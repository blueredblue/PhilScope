## The Empty Wind: Sartre’s Radicalization of Husserlian Phenomenology and the Non-Substantiality of Consciousness

The history of philosophy is punctuated by attempts to locate the seat of the self. From Descartes’ *res cogitans* to Kant’s transcendental unity of apperception, the subject has often been conceived as a kind of metaphysical anchor—a substance, a container, or a transcendental pole that undergirds experience. At the dawn of the twentieth century, Edmund Husserl sought to rescue philosophy from the twin specters of psychologism and naive realism by grounding it in a rigorous, descriptive science of consciousness. His transcendental phenomenology, articulated most powerfully in *Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology* and *Cartesian Meditations*, proposed a radical new method—the *epoché*—to bracket the natural attitude and reveal the essential structures of subjectivity. Yet within this very project, Husserl retained two seemingly substantialist elements: the *hyle* (the passive sensory "matter" of experience) and the *transcendental ego* (the pure subject of intentional acts). It was precisely against these residual Cartesianisms that Jean-Paul Sartre launched his most devastating critique. In *The Transcendence of the Ego* and *Being and Nothingness*, Sartre argued that Husserl had failed to fully liberate consciousness from the shackles of thinghood. By positing a hyletic stratum and a transcendental ego, Husserl, Sartre claimed, had surreptitiously reintroduced a hidden metaphysical realm, turning consciousness into a "thing" rather than the pure, empty spontaneity it truly is.

The central question that emerges from this confrontation is not merely exegetical but profoundly ontological: Does Sartre’s rejection of the hyle and the transcendental ego successfully establish consciousness as an empty nothing—a *néant*—rather than a substantial entity? Or does Sartre’s reading of Husserl constitute an unfair caricature, a misrepresentation of a nuanced descriptive project that never intended to posit a metaphysical substance? This essay will argue that Sartre’s critique, while founded upon a selective and arguably unfair reading of Husserl’s descriptive methodology, constitutes a genuine and necessary radicalization of the phenomenological project. More specifically, I will defend the Sartrean position that consciousness does not add a mental substance to the world but rather functions as the pre-reflective self-consciousness of an existent’s appearances. To achieve this, I will first reconstruct Husserl’s architecture of consciousness, paying close attention to the roles of the hyle and the transcendental ego. Second, I will delineate Sartre’s critique, focusing on his arguments in *The Transcendence of the Ego* and *Being and Nothingness*. Third, I will analyze the dialectical tension between the two thinkers, assessing whether Sartre’s critique is a misinterpretation or a radicalization. Finally, I will defend the position that consciousness is fundamentally non-substantial, arguing that Sartre’s elimination of the hyle, while problematic in its details, is a necessary consequence of a coherent phenomenological ontology.

### I. Husserl’s Descriptive Architecture: The Hyle and the Transcendental Ego

To understand Sartre’s critique, one must first grasp the precise function of the hyle and the transcendental ego within Husserl’s system. Husserl’s project, as articulated in *Ideas*, is not to construct a metaphysical theory of reality but to describe the essential structures of consciousness as they are given in pure experience. The method of the *epoché*—the phenomenological reduction—brackets the "natural attitude," suspending our naive belief in the independent existence of the external world. What remains after this reduction is the sphere of pure consciousness, the field of intentional acts. For Husserl, consciousness is fundamentally *intentional*: it is always consciousness *of* something. This intentional correlation is captured in the distinction between the *noesis* (the act of thinking, perceiving, judging) and the *noema* (the object as intended, the perceived-as-such, the judged-as-such). The noema is not the real object in the world but the object as it appears within the act of consciousness.

Within this noetic-noematic correlation, Husserl identifies a crucial stratum that he calls the *hyle* (from the Greek for "matter"). In *Ideas* (§85), Husserl describes hyletic data as the "sensory" or "material" components of experience—the raw colors, tones, tactile sensations, and so forth that are "animated" or "ensouled" by noetic acts. For example, when I perceive a red apple, there is a hyletic datum of "redness" that is not yet the apple itself but the sensory stuff that, through noetic apprehension, becomes the red apple. The hyle is thus the passive, given content of sensation that provides the "matter" for intentional acts. It is important to note that Husserl does not conceive of the hyle as a physical thing or a psychic substance. Rather, it is a descriptive element within the transcendental field—a stratum of pure experience that is given prior to any objectification. The hyle is not "in" consciousness as a pebble is in a box; it is a structural component of the act itself, the "stuff" that gets shaped by the noesis.

Similarly, Husserl posits the *transcendental ego* as the necessary pole of identity that unifies the stream of conscious experiences. In *Cartesian Meditations*, Husserl argues that the ego is not an empirical self, nor a psychological entity, but the pure, transcendental subject that remains after the reduction. It is the "I" that performs the acts of consciousness, the source of all intentionality. Crucially, Husserl insists that the transcendental ego is not a "thing" or a substance in the Cartesian sense. It is not an object among objects; rather, it is the *subject* of all experience, the formal condition of possibility for the unity of consciousness. The ego is the "identical pole" that persists through the flux of noetic acts, the "center" from which all intentional rays emanate. Without this ego, Husserl argues, the stream of consciousness would be a chaotic flux of unrelated acts, lacking the unity required for synthetic experience.

It is here that we encounter the central tension that Sartre will exploit. On the one hand, Husserl explicitly rejects the Cartesian notion of a *res cogitans*—a thinking substance. He insists that the ego is not a "little man in the head" nor a transcendental object. On the other hand, by positing a hyletic stratum and a transcendental ego as essential structures of consciousness, Husserl seems to reintroduce a degree of substantiality. The hyle, as a passive "given," suggests that consciousness has a receptive, material component—an "inside" that receives sensory data. The transcendental ego, as a unifying pole, suggests that consciousness has a center, a self-identical subject that underlies its acts. Sartre will argue that these two elements are not merely descriptive conveniences but metaphysical residues that betray the radical implications of Husserl’s own method.

### II. Sartre’s Critique: The Transcendence of the Ego and the Rejection of the Hyle

Sartre’s critique unfolds in two primary texts: the early essay *The Transcendence of the Ego* (1937) and the magnum opus *Being and Nothingness* (1943). In the former, Sartre directly attacks the notion of the transcendental ego. He begins by noting that Husserl’s own method, the *epoché*, is supposed to bracket all transcendent entities—everything that is not immanent to consciousness. Yet, Sartre argues, Husserl violates this principle by positing the transcendental ego as a constituent of consciousness. For Sartre, the ego is not a transcendental condition of consciousness but a *transcendent object*—an object that appears *to* consciousness, just like a chair or a table. The ego is not the subject of consciousness; it is an object *for* consciousness.

Sartre’s argument hinges on a distinction between *pre-reflective* and *reflective* consciousness. When I am absorbed in reading a book, I am not explicitly aware of a "self" that is reading. My consciousness is entirely directed toward the book, its characters, its plot. This is pre-reflective consciousness—pure, non-positional awareness of an object. It is only when I reflect upon my reading that an "I" appears: "I am reading." This "I," Sartre argues, is not a pre-existing subject that was always there; rather, it is a *product* of reflection. The reflective act constitutes the ego as an object, unifying my past and present acts into a single, transcendent pole. The ego is thus a "virtual" object, a synthesis of my acts that I project onto the flux of consciousness. It is not the source of consciousness but a consequence of it.

Sartre famously illustrates this with the example of counting cigarettes. When I am counting cigarettes, I am pre-reflectively aware of the act of counting—I know that I am counting—but I am not aware of an "I" that counts. The "I" only appears if I reflect on the counting: "I am counting." The pre-reflective consciousness is a pure spontaneity, a "nothing" that is entirely absorbed in its object. The ego, therefore, is not a necessary condition of consciousness but a contingent, transcendent object that arises in reflection. To posit a transcendental ego as a constituent of consciousness is, for Sartre, to reify a reflective abstraction. It is to mistake the object of reflection for the subject that reflects.

In *Being and Nothingness*, Sartre extends this critique to the hyle. He argues that Husserl’s postulation of hyletic data is a "residue" of substantialist metaphysics. The hyle, as passive sensory "matter," introduces a moment of *passivity* and *opacity* into consciousness. If consciousness contains hyletic data, then it has an "inside"—a stuff that it receives and processes. This would make consciousness a *thing*, an *en-soi* (in-itself), rather than a *pour-soi* (for-itself). For Sartre, consciousness is fundamentally *spontaneity*—it is pure activity, pure intentionality, a "wind" that blows through the world. It has no "content" in the sense of a passive given. The world is not "given" to consciousness as raw sensory data; rather, consciousness *reveals* the world through its intentional acts. The redness of the apple is not a hyletic datum inside consciousness; it is a quality of the apple *as it appears* to consciousness. The "sensory" is not a stratum of consciousness but a mode of the world’s appearing.

Sartre’s rejection of the hyle is rooted in his ontology of consciousness as *nothingness* (*néant*). In *Being and Nothingness*, Sartre defines consciousness as a being that is not what it is and is what it is not. Unlike the *en-soi*, which is dense, opaque, and self-identical (a rock is a rock), the *pour-soi* is characterized by a fundamental lack of identity. It is a "nihilating" power that introduces negation into the world. Consciousness is not a substance but a *process*—the perpetual act of transcending itself toward the world. It is "empty" in the sense that it has no positive content of its own; it is entirely defined by its intentional relation to what it is not (the world). If consciousness contained hyletic data, it would have a positive, substantial being—it would be a thing. But for Sartre, consciousness is precisely the *negation* of thinghood. It is the "hole in being" through which the world appears.

### III. Dialectical Tension: Misinterpretation or Genuine Radicalization?

The question of whether Sartre’s critique is a fair reading of Husserl or a genuine radicalization is complex. On the one hand, it is easy to accuse Sartre of straw-manning Husserl. Husserl explicitly states that the hyle is not a "psychic content" but a descriptive stratum of the intentional act. He also insists that the transcendental ego is not a substance but a formal pole of identity. Husserl’s project is *descriptive*, not *metaphysical*. He is not claiming that consciousness is a thing with parts; he is describing the essential structures of experience as they appear. The hyle and the ego are not "hidden metaphysical realms" but phenomenological data—elements that are given in reflection. To accuse Husserl of reintroducing a Cartesian substance is to ignore his explicit anti-substantialist intentions.

However, this defense misses the deeper point of Sartre’s critique. Sartre is not simply accusing Husserl of bad metaphysics; he is arguing that Husserl’s *descriptive* categories have *ontological* implications that undermine the very possibility of a pure, spontaneous consciousness. The hyle, even if described as a "stratum" rather than a "thing," still introduces a moment of *passivity* into consciousness. It suggests that consciousness is not purely active but is also *receptive*—that it receives something from outside itself. This passivity, Sartre argues, is incompatible with the absolute spontaneity of the for-itself. If consciousness is receptive, then it is not fully self-determining; it is conditioned by something other than itself. This would make consciousness a *finite* being, a being that is limited by its given content. But for Sartre, consciousness is *infinite* in its spontaneity—it is a "nothing" that is entirely self-creating.

Similarly, the transcendental ego, even if described as a "formal pole," introduces a moment of *identity* into consciousness. It suggests that consciousness has a self-identical core, a "subject" that underlies its acts. But for Sartre, consciousness is characterized by *non-identity*. It is never self-identical; it is always in the process of becoming, always transcending itself toward the future. The ego, as a pole of identity, would freeze this flux, turning consciousness into a static substance. Sartre’s point is not that Husserl *intended* to reintroduce a Cartesian substance, but that the *logic* of his descriptions leads in that direction. By positing a hyle and an ego, Husserl inadvertently reintroduced the very "hidden metaphysical realms" that his method was supposed to eliminate.

In this sense, Sartre’s critique is a *genuine radicalization*. He takes Husserl’s own principle—"back to the things themselves"—and pushes it to its logical conclusion. If consciousness is truly a pure intentionality, then it cannot have a hyletic "inside" or a transcendental "subject." It must be *nothing*—a pure act of revealing the world. Sartre is not misreading Husserl; he is drawing out the ontological implications of Husserl’s own insights. Husserl remained a transcendental idealist, committed to the existence of a transcendental subjectivity. Sartre, by contrast, is an existentialist who insists that consciousness is not a "subject" at all but a "nothing" that exists in the world. This is not a rejection of Husserl’s phenomenology but its radicalization—a move from transcendental idealism to existential phenomenology.

Yet, we must also acknowledge the unfairness in Sartre’s reading. Sartre tends to conflate Husserl’s *descriptive* categories with *ontological* claims. When Husserl speaks of the hyle, he is describing how experience is structured; he is not making a claim about the being of consciousness. Sartre, however, reads the hyle as a positive, substantial element—a "stuff" that consciousness contains. This is a misreading of Husserl’s intent. Similarly, when Husserl speaks of the transcendental ego, he is describing the formal unity of consciousness; he is not positing a "little man in the head." Sartre’s critique often attacks a caricature of Husserl rather than the nuanced position Husserl actually held. Nevertheless, this unfairness is productive. By pushing Husserl’s descriptions to their ontological limits, Sartre reveals a genuine tension within phenomenology: the tension between the descriptive project of mapping consciousness and the ontological question of what consciousness *is*. Sartre forces us to confront this question directly, and his answer—that consciousness is a nothing—is a radical departure from both Cartesianism and Husserlian idealism.

### IV. Defending the Position: Consciousness as Pre-Reflective Self-Consciousness

Having established the dialectical tension, I now turn to the central philosophical question: Does consciousness add a mental substance to the world, or does it merely function as the pre-reflective self-consciousness of an existent’s appearances? I will defend the latter position, arguing that Sartre’s ontology of consciousness as nothingness is both coherent and philosophically superior to Husserl’s retention of the hyle and the ego.
