The thesis that conclusive empirical beliefs are empirically indefeasible represents a bold and counterintuitive claim within epistemology. At first glance, it seems to champion the most robust form of empirical knowledge—the kind that leaves no room for doubt, the bedrock of our sensory engagement with the world. However, to evaluate this thesis is to confront a fundamental question about the nature of sensory experience: how do the distinct sensory modalities generate distinct kinds of experience and information types that ground a hierarchy of empirical defeasibility? If sensory modalities produce fundamentally different informational structures, then the degree to which a belief derived from them can be defeated is not uniform. Rather, a graded hierarchy of empirical vulnerability emerges, one that ultimately undermines the claim of empirical indefeasibility. 

To explore this, we must examine the phenomenological and epistemological architecture of sensory experience. Empirical defeasibility can be divided into two primary categories: overriding defeaters and undermining defeaters. An overriding defeater is a competing empirical belief that, when held, makes the original belief rationally unacceptable (e.g., seeing a stick in water and believing it is bent, only to override this with the belief that light refracts). An undermining defeater is a piece of evidence that attacks the reliability of the very mechanism that produced the original belief (e.g., learning that one is intoxicated, thereby undermining the reliability of visual perception). The thesis of empirical indefeasibility suggests that for a belief to be conclusive, it must be immune to both. Yet, by analyzing the distinctions drawn by philosopher Bryce Gessell regarding flash and spherical vision, the resistance of compound perceptually justified beliefs to testimonial challenges, and the nature of tactile confirmation, we can see that no empirical belief reaches the summit of absolute indefeasibility.

**The Hierarchy of Experience Kinds and Information Types**

Before evaluating the thesis, we must understand how sensory modalities generate distinct experience kinds and information types. Vision, for instance, does not merely deliver a stream of data; it constructs a specific type of experiential information characterized by spatial extension, visual angle, and luminosity. Touch, conversely, generates an information type characterized by pressure, temperature, and material resistivity. These are not just different data sets; they are different *kinds* of experience. The visual experience of redness is qualitatively distinct from the tactile experience of roughness. Because these modalities process information through entirely different neural architectures and phenomenal structures, they are susceptible to different types of empirical error. 

This diversity of experience kinds naturally grounds a hierarchy of empirical defeasibility. Some sensory information types are inherently more stable and resistant to empirical undermining than others. A belief formed through a modality that is highly resistant to localized illusion will occupy a lower position on the hierarchy of defeasibility (i.e., it is harder to defeat) than a belief formed through a modality prone to illusion. The thesis of empirical indefeasibility posits that at the absolute bottom of this hierarchy, where the most conclusive empirical beliefs reside, defeaters vanish entirely. But does the structure of our sensory modalities actually permit such a floor? 

**Gessell’s Distinction: Flash Vision and Spherical Vision**

Bryce Gessell’s distinction between flash vision and spherical vision provides a crucial lens through which to examine the relationship between sensory modalities and the hierarchy of empirical defeasibility. Flash vision, as Gessell describes it, is the focal, high-acuity, object-oriented mode of perception. It is the vision we employ when we direct our gaze at a specific object, reading a sign or identifying a face. It is narrow, concentrated, and highly susceptible to illusion and manipulation. Spherical vision, by contrast, is the ambient, whole-field awareness that surrounds the focal point. It is the diffuse perception of the room, the sky, and the spatial orientation of the body. Spherical vision provides the background context within which flash vision operates; it is less prone to the specific, localized illusions that plague focal attention, but it is also less precise in its informational content.

When we map Gessell’s distinction onto the hierarchy of empirical defeasibility, we see that flash vision and spherical vision generate beliefs with vastly different levels of vulnerability. A belief derived from flash vision—such as "that object is a straight stick"—is highly susceptible to overriding empirical defeaters. The bending of a stick in water is a classic example: the flash vision delivers the information that the stick is bent, and this belief is easily overridden by the overriding defeater of the known law of refraction. However, spherical vision provides a different kind of information. The ambient sense of spatial orientation—the spherical vision of the room—is far more resistant to overriding defeaters. It is incredibly difficult to override the spherical vision that you are standing in a room with a floor and a ceiling, even when flash vision is deceived by a mirage. 

Yet, does the resistance of spherical vision to *overriding* defeaters make it empirically indefeasible? The answer is no, because spherical vision remains vulnerable to *undermining* defeaters. If one were to enter a state of global hallucination or neurological distortion, the spherical vision itself—the ambient spatial awareness—would be compromised. The experience kind of spherical vision, while robust against localized empirical challenges, is still generated by a fallible sensory modality. Therefore, even the most "conclusive" beliefs derived from spherical vision remain within the realm of empirical defeasibility. The hierarchy of defeasibility is not a ladder that leads to a roof of indefeasibility; it is a gradient of resistance, and Gessell’s distinction illustrates that different experience kinds merely occupy different rungs of that gradient.

**Compound Perceptually Justified Beliefs and Testimonial Defeaters**

The thesis of empirical indefeasibility might find more traction when applied to compound perceptually justified beliefs. A simple perceptual belief, such as "I see a red patch," is easily defeated by an optical illusion or a change in lighting. However, when perceptual beliefs are compounded across multiple modalities and integrated into a unified perceptual experience, they acquire a structural resilience that makes them highly resistant to empirical defeaters. Consider the belief "This is a wooden table." This compound belief integrates the visual experience of brown, rectangular geometry; the tactile experience of smooth, resistant solidity; the auditory feedback of a solid thump when knocked; and the olfactory experience of wood. 

Because compound perceptually justified beliefs are constituted by the convergence of multiple, distinct experience kinds and information types, they resist empirical defeaters in a powerful way. To override such a belief, one would need a competing empirical belief that accounts for all of these converging modalities simultaneously. An optical illusion cannot account for the tactile resistance; a hallucination of a smell cannot account for the auditory feedback. The sheer informational density of a compound perceptually justified belief makes it highly resistant to overriding defeaters. It occupies a very low rung on the hierarchy of empirical defeasibility, requiring immense empirical evidence to displace.

However, resistance to overriding defeaters does not equate to indefeasibility, particularly when we consider the role of testimonial challenges. Testimonial evidence is a form of empirical evidence—it is information received from others that purports to describe the world. When a testimonial challenge is introduced, it acts as an empirical defeater. The question is whether it can override or undermine compound perceptually justified beliefs. 

Testimonial challenges typically function as undermining defeaters rather than overriding ones. If a trusted scientist tells you that the wooden table is actually a sophisticated quantum hologram, they are not providing an alternative perceptual experience that overrides your senses; rather, they are undermining the reliability of your perceptual interpretation by providing a competing empirical framework. Compound perceptually justified beliefs resist this kind of undermining defeater precisely because testimonial evidence lacks the multi-modal corroboration of direct perception. The hologram theory does not give you the tactile resistance of wood; it merely offers a theoretical alternative. The compound belief resists the testimonial challenge because the testimonial evidence cannot replicate the rich, integrated information types of the compound percept. 

Nevertheless, this resistance is not absolute. If the testimonial challenge is backed by its own empirical evidence—such as the scientist producing a device that measures the table as a probability field rather than solid matter—the testimonial challenge ceases to be a mere assertion and becomes an empirical defeater that attacks the reliability of the perceptual modalities themselves. In such a case, the compound perceptually justified belief is genuinely undermined. The belief that "this is a wooden table" is defeated not because the table has changed, but because the empirical evidence now supports an alternative interpretation of the sensory data. Thus, even the most robust compound beliefs are vulnerable to empirical defeat when the testimonial challenge is sufficiently grounded in independent empirical evidence.

**Tactile Confirmation and the Guarantee of Material Existence**

Perhaps the strongest candidate for a conclusive empirical belief is the belief in material existence grounded in tactile confirmation. When we touch a wall, we feel its impenetrability, its solidity, its resistance to our hand. Common sense and Cartesian intuition suggest that tactile confirmation provides an unassailable guarantee of material existence. Touch is direct contact; it is the sense of the real. Unlike vision, which is mediated by light and distance, touch involves actual physical interaction. It seems that tactile confirmation must provide an empirical belief that is empirically indefeasible, as it is the very bedrock of our belief in a material world.

However, a critical assessment reveals that tactile confirmation fails to provide an unassailable guarantee of material existence. The guarantee of tactile confirmation is phenomenological, not ontological. When you feel the solidity of a wall, your tactile experience kind is one of resistivity. This experience kind is generated by the compression of mechanoreceptors in the skin. The question is not whether the experience of resistivity is real—you genuinely do feel resistance—but whether the *material existence* of the external object that caused the resistance is guaranteed by the experience. 

Consider the possibility of a brain in a vat. In this scenario, the neuroscientists stimulate the somatosensory cortex of the brain, generating the exact same tactile experience kind as touching a solid wall. The experience of resistivity is identical; the information type of tactile confirmation is identical. Yet, there is no material wall. The tactile confirmation has been entirely simulated. If the experience kind and information type of tactile confirmation can be replicated in a simulated environment, then tactile confirmation does not guarantee material existence. It only guarantees the presence of a specific tactile experience. 

Furthermore, tactile confirmation is vulnerable to undermining defeaters that operate at the neurological level. Conditions such as phantom limb syndrome, tactile hallucinations induced by drugs, or somatic delusions demonstrate that the tactile modality can generate vivid, compelling experiences of material contact where no material contact exists. These are empirical defeaters that undermine the reliability of the tactile modality itself. If the tactile modality can be deceived by internal neurological states, then beliefs grounded in tactile confirmation are not empirically indefeasible. They are merely highly resistant to empirical defeat under normal circumstances. 

The hierarchy of empirical defeasibility places tactile confirmation at a very low level of vulnerability—lower than vision, because touch requires actual physical interaction and is less prone to the abstract illusions that plague vision. But being at the bottom of the hierarchy of defeasibility is not the same as being outside the hierarchy altogether. The thesis that conclusive empirical beliefs are empirically indefeasible requires a categorical break from the hierarchy, a point at which empirical evidence can no longer touch the belief. Tactile confirmation does not provide this break. It only provides the most robust empirical foundation we possess, a foundation that remains, in principle, open to empirical challenge.

**Conclusion**

The thesis that conclusive empirical beliefs are empirically indefeasible collapses under the weight of a properly understood relationship between sensory modalities, experience kinds, and the hierarchy of empirical defeasibility. The distinct sensory modalities—vision, touch, hearing—generate distinct experience kinds and information types that are differentially susceptible to empirical error. Bryce Gessell’s distinction between flash and spherical vision illustrates that even within a single modality, experience kinds vary in their resistance to overriding and undermining defeaters, but none reach the status of indefeasibility. Compound perceptually justified beliefs, while highly resistant to testimonial and overriding challenges due to their multi-modal informational density, remain vulnerable to empirical defeaters that provide alternative empirical frameworks capable of undermining the reliability of perception. Finally, tactile confirmation, despite being the most phenomenologically immediate and seemingly unassailable form of empirical evidence, fails to provide an unassailable guarantee of material existence because the experience kind of tactile resistivity can be simulated or neurologically generated without corresponding material reality. 

Ultimately, the hierarchy of empirical defeasibility is not a ladder leading to a roof of certainty; it is a gradient of resilience. Conclusive empirical beliefs are those that occupy the deepest, most resistant stratum of this gradient, beliefs that require immense countervailing empirical evidence to dislodge. But they are not empirically indefeasible. The very fact that they are empirical—grounded in fallible sensory modalities—means that they remain within the domain of empirical possibility and empirical defeat. The most we can hope for from our senses is not the certainty of indefeasibility, but the profound, practical resilience of a belief that has survived the relentless testing of our diverse sensory faculties.
