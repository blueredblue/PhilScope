## The Atheological Ground: Evaluating Andersen’s Critique of Heidegger’s Ontological Corrective for Theology

The relationship between philosophy and theology has always been a contested borderland, marked by mutual suspicion, occasional synthesis, and frequent territorial disputes. In the twentieth century, Martin Heidegger’s work—particularly his existential analytic in *Being and Time* and his subsequent lecture “Phenomenology and Theology”—reconfigured this borderland with unprecedented radicalism. Heidegger’s central claim is deceptively simple yet profoundly disruptive: theology, as a positive or ontic science, must ground its conceptual understanding in a pre-theological ontology. This ontology, which he identifies with fundamental philosophy, remains methodologically atheistic—it brackets any reference to God to analyze the pure structures of Dasein’s existence. Philosophy, therefore, provides an ontological corrective to the ontic science of theology, clarifying the vague and unexamined concepts that theology naively employs.

This claim has generated sustained philosophical and theological resistance. Nathan Andersen, a contemporary philosopher of religion, offers a particularly incisive critique of Heidegger’s position. Andersen argues that Heidegger’s attempt to ground theological concepts in an atheistic ontology fundamentally misconstrues the nature of theological understanding. For Andersen, theology begins and ends in faith; it is not merely an ontic region to be examined from the outside but a mode of existence constituted by divine revelation. Consequently, theology cannot utilize Heidegger’s analysis of Dasein and its essential “Being-guilty” without losing its necessary relation to faithful existence. The tension is stark: either theology accepts Heidegger’s ontological framework and thereby reduces its concepts to anthropological structures, or it rejects that framework and forfeits the philosophical clarity Heidegger promises.

This essay will evaluate Andersen’s critique by addressing the central question: can an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis of Dasein as thrown projection account for theological phenomena such as man’s guilt before God and the creative existence of the Word? I will argue that Andersen’s critique is fundamentally sound. Heidegger’s existential analytic, while illuminating certain formal structures of human existence, cannot account for the material content of theological concepts without either reducing them to mere expressions of Dasein’s finitude or distorting their essential revelatory character. The “Being-guilty” of Heidegger’s analysis is an existential-ontological nullity, not a relational rupture before a personal God. The “Word” of theology is a creative event that interrupts Dasein’s projections, not a possibility projected by Dasein itself. Therefore, theology cannot achieve conceptual understanding *through* Heidegger’s philosophy in the way he demands while remaining within faith. The resolution lies not in synthesis but in a critical dialectic: theology may borrow formal structures from philosophy as heuristic tools, but it must ground its understanding in revelation, not in an atheistic ontology.

### I. Heidegger’s Project: The Ontic/Ontological Distinction and Methodological Atheism

To evaluate Andersen’s critique, one must first understand the precise nature of Heidegger’s claim. In *Being and Time*, Heidegger undertakes a fundamental ontology—an inquiry into the meaning of Being as such. This inquiry proceeds through an existential analytic of Dasein, the being for whom Being is an issue. Dasein is characterized by two fundamental existential structures: thrownness (*Geworfenheit*) and projection (*Entwurf*). Dasein finds itself already thrown into a world it did not choose, and it projects itself upon possibilities for its own being. This thrown projection constitutes Dasein’s existence as care (*Sorge*), a temporal unity of past, present, and future.

Crucially, Heidegger distinguishes between the ontic and the ontological. The ontic concerns beings in their specific, regional determinations—the facts of a particular science or domain. The ontological concerns the Being of those beings, the a priori conditions that make ontic beings intelligible. Sciences are ontic disciplines: they investigate particular regions of beings (physics investigates nature, biology investigates life, theology investigates faith). But these ontic sciences lack a secure foundation because they do not examine the ontological structures that underlie their subject matter. Philosophy, as fundamental ontology, provides this foundation by clarifying the existential structures of Dasein that make ontic phenomena possible.

In his 1927 lecture “Phenomenology and Theology,” Heidegger applies this framework to theology. Theology, he argues, is a positive science—an ontic discipline that studies a specific region of beings: the believer and the community of faith. Its subject matter is faith itself, understood as a mode of existence. However, theology’s concepts are vague and unexamined. It speaks of sin, grace, guilt, and redemption without having clarified the existential-ontological structures that underlie these concepts. Philosophy’s task is to provide this clarification. By analyzing Dasein’s existential structures, philosophy can show theology what it is actually talking about when it speaks of human existence before God.

Heidegger is explicit that this philosophical clarification is methodologically atheistic. He writes: “Philosophy remains atheism.” This does not mean that philosophy denies God’s existence or engages in anti-religious polemic. Rather, it means that philosophy brackets any reference to God in order to analyze the pure structures of Dasein. Philosophy does not ask whether God exists; it asks what it means for a being to exist at all. The analysis of Dasein’s thrownness, projection, guilt, and temporality is conducted without recourse to any transcendent reality. It is an analysis of human finitude as such.

This methodological atheism is not incidental to Heidegger’s project; it is essential. To analyze Dasein’s structures, one must first strip away all ontic determinations, including religious ones. The believer and the atheist share the same existential structure of thrownness and projection. The difference lies in how they interpret this structure. Theology, as an ontic science, interprets Dasein’s structures in light of faith. But to do so, it must first understand those structures *as such*. This understanding is provided by philosophy, which remains atheistic because it refuses to import any ontic content into the ontological analysis.

Heidegger’s claim, therefore, is that theology needs philosophy as a corrective. Without philosophical clarification, theology’s concepts remain confused and ungrounded. With it, theology can achieve conceptual precision and rigor. The corrective is ontological: philosophy reveals the existential structures that underlie theological concepts, thereby grounding them in the fundamental nature of human existence.

### II. Andersen’s Critique: The Incommensurability of Guilt and the Word

Nathan Andersen’s critique targets the core of Heidegger’s claim. Andersen argues that Heidegger’s ontological corrective is not merely a clarification of vague concepts; it is a fundamental transformation that distorts the essential character of theological phenomena. Andersen focuses on two paradigmatic concepts: “Being-guilty” (*Schuldigsein*) and the “Word” (*Logos*). In both cases, he argues, Heidegger’s ontological analysis is incommensurable with the theological meaning.

Consider first Heidegger’s analysis of guilt. In *Being and Time* (§58), Heidegger defines guilt ontologically, not morally. Dasein is guilty in the sense that it is “the ground of a nullity.” Dasein is thrown into existence; it is a ground that it did not itself posit. It must take over its thrownness and project itself upon possibilities, but it can never fully master its own being. This structural nullity—the fact that Dasein is not the origin of its own existence and yet must assume responsibility for it—is what Heidegger calls “Being-guilty.” This guilt has nothing to do with moral transgression, legal violation, or a breach of divine law. It is a purely existential-ontological structure, a feature of Dasein’s finitude.

Andersen points out that the theological concept of guilt is radically different. In Christian theology, guilt *coram Deo* (before God) is a relational concept. It involves a personal address from a holy God, a violation of a covenant, and a need for atonement. Guilt is not merely a structural feature of human finitude; it is a specific state of brokenness in a relationship. The believer is guilty because they have sinned against a personal God who has revealed themselves in history. This guilt is forensic—it involves judgment—and it is relational—it involves an other.

Heidegger’s existential guilt cannot account for this theological meaning. The ontological concept is neutral regarding any personal Other. It describes Dasein’s thrownness, not a rupture with a specific being. To say “I am guilty before God” is not merely to say “I am thrown.” It is to say that I have broken a relationship with a being who addresses me, who calls me to account, and who offers forgiveness. Heidegger’s analysis abstracts from this relational context entirely. It reduces guilt to a structural feature of Dasein’s existence, stripping it of its personal, forensic, and redemptive dimensions.

Andersen argues that applying Heidegger’s existential guilt to the theological concept is a category mistake. It is akin to explaining the concept of “betrayal” by analyzing the structure of human sociality. While sociality may be a necessary condition for betrayal, it cannot account for the specific meaning of betrayal—the violation of a trust between particular persons. Similarly, while thrownness may be a necessary condition for guilt before God, it cannot account for the specific meaning of that guilt—the rupture of a covenant with a personal God. Heidegger’s ontological analysis provides a formal structure, but it cannot provide the material content of theological guilt.

Consider second the “creative existence of the Word.” In Christian theology, the Word (*Logos*) is not merely a human projection. It is God’s self-communication, an event that creates ex nihilo. The prologue of John’s Gospel declares: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. All things were made through him.” The Word is not a possibility that Dasein projects; it is an address that interrupts Dasein’s projections. It comes from beyond the horizon of Dasein’s understanding, breaking into the world as a creative and redemptive event.

Heidegger’s Dasein, by contrast, is a projector of possibilities. It constitutes a world of meaning through its projects. Dasein does not receive meaning from beyond; it projects meaning onto its thrownness. The Word, if it were to be understood ontologically, would have to be conceived as a projection of Dasein’s own possibilities. But this is precisely what the theological concept denies. The Word is not something Dasein projects; it is something that addresses Dasein. It is not a possibility Dasein chooses; it is an event that constitutes Dasein anew.

Andersen’s critique is that an ontology of thrown projection cannot account for an event that breaks into the horizon of intelligibility from the outside. The Word is not a possibility that Dasein discovers within its own existential structure; it is a revelation that comes from God. To reduce the Word to a projection of Dasein is to deny its revelatory character. It turns revelation into self-discovery, grace into self-actualization. The ontological corrective, in this case, is not a clarification but a distortion. It transforms the Word from a divine address into a human project, thereby evacuating it of its theological meaning.

### III. The Faithful Existence: The Impossibility of Neutral Grounding

The counter-argument to Heidegger’s position—and the foundation of Andersen’s critique—is that theology begins and ends in faith. Faith is not merely an ontic attitude that can be analyzed from the outside; it is a mode of existence constituted by the revelation of God. The believer does not first understand Dasein’s existential structures and then add a religious interpretation. Rather, the believer’s entire existence is transformed by the encounter with God. Faith is not a predicate of an otherwise neutral Dasein; it is a new way of being in the world.

This has profound implications for the question of grounding. If theology begins and ends in faith, then its concepts cannot be grounded in a pre-theological ontology. The believer’s understanding of guilt is not derived from a neutral analysis of Dasein’s thrownness; it is derived from the encounter with God’s grace. The believer’s understanding of the Word is not derived from an analysis of Dasein’s projection; it is derived from the event of revelation. To ground these concepts in a philosophy that brackets God is to impose an alien framework on them.

Andersen argues that Heidegger’s claim to be “pre-theological” is false. His existential analytic is not neutral; it is a specific philosophical anthropology that presupposes a certain view of human finitude. The concept of “thrown projection” is an atheistic interpretation of human existence. It posits that human beings are fundamentally alone, projecting meaning onto a meaningless thrownness. There is no transcendent source of meaning, no divine address, no grace. The only meaning is that which Dasein projects for itself.

To use this framework to “correct” theology is to impose this atheistic interpretation onto the believer’s experience. The believer experiences guilt not as a structural nullity but as a personal offense against a loving God. The believer experiences the Word not as a projection but as a gift. An outside, neutral analysis necessarily distorts these experiences because it abstracts from their essential relational and revelatory character.

This is not to say that philosophy has no role in theology. Andersen acknowledges that philosophy can help clarify the formal structures of human existence—temporality, historicity, care, anxiety. These structures are present in the believer and the non-believer alike. But they are not the *ground* of theological concepts. They are the *conditions* under which faith is possible. The believer is a thrown projection, but faith is not a projection; it is a response to a divine address. The believer is guilty in the existential sense, but guilt before God is not merely this existential nullity; it is a specific rupture in a relationship.

The theological counter-argument, therefore, is that theology must begin and end in faith. It cannot ground its concepts in a philosophy that brackets God, because the concepts themselves are constituted by the revelation of God. To understand what it means to be guilty before God, one must be in a relationship with God. To understand what it means to hear the Word, one must be addressed by the Word. This understanding is not available to a neutral observer; it is available only to the believer.

### IV. Can a Non-Faithful Ontology Account for Theological Phenomena?

The central question of this essay is whether an explicitly non-faithful ontological analysis of Dasein as thrown projection can account for theological phenomena such as man’s guilt before God and the creative existence of the Word. Based on the preceding analysis, the answer must be no—if “account for” means providing the *ground* or *source* of these phenomena.

Heidegger’s ontology can illuminate the *formal* structures of human existence. It can show that the believer, like the non-believer, is a thrown projection, a being-toward-death, a being-in-the-world. It can describe the anxiety that precedes the encounter with grace, the temporality that underlies eschatological hope, the historicity that shapes the community of faith. These formal structures are important; they help theology understand the human condition that is addressed by the Word.

But the *material* content of theological phenomena is irreducible. Guilt before God is not just existential anxiety; it is a specific relational rupture. The creative Word is not just a projection of possibilities; it is a divine act. To make these fit the ontological grid, one must either (a) reduce them to anthropology or (b) leave them as unaccountable excesses that the ontology cannot explain.

The first option—reduction to anthropology—is exemplified by Rudolf Bultmann’s demythologizing project. Bultmann, deeply influenced by Heidegger, sought to interpret the New Testament in terms of existential possibility. The Word, he argued, is not a supernatural event but an address that calls the hearer to authentic existence. Faith is not assent to doctrine but a new self-understanding. This approach, however, has been widely criticized for reducing theology to anthropology. The Word becomes a projection of Dasein’s own possibilities; grace becomes self-actualization.
