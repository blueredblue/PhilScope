# Empirical Conclusiveness and the Hierarchy of Defeasibility

The thesis that *conclusive empirical beliefs are empirically indefeasible* appears at first to express a straightforward point: if a belief is conclusively justified by experience, then no further empirical evidence can rationally overturn it. Yet the thesis is more complicated than this formulation suggests. It depends on what counts as a conclusive empirical belief, what kind of evidence sensory experience supplies, and what it means for a belief to be defeated. A perceptual belief may be conclusive relative to a particular experiential situation without being immune to every possible challenge. Equally, a belief may be highly resistant to ordinary empirical revision while remaining vulnerable to philosophical doubt, conceptual reinterpretation, or evidence concerning the reliability of the conditions under which it was formed.

A satisfactory account must therefore distinguish different kinds of sensory experience and different kinds of information conveyed by them. Vision, touch, hearing, proprioception, and the other modalities do not merely provide more or less evidence of the same kind. They present objects under different modes of access. Vision tends to disclose spatial arrangement and publicly observable structure; touch supplies contact, resistance, texture, and bodily engagement; hearing discloses events and temporal patterns; proprioception informs us about our own bodily position and movement. These differences may ground a hierarchy of empirical defeasibility. A belief based on a fleeting visual impression may be more vulnerable than one based on sustained, multimodal, practically integrated perception. But even the most robust multimodal belief may not be absolutely indefeasible if “material existence” is understood as existence independent of all possible experience.

Bryce Gessell’s distinction between *flash vision* and *spherical vision* offers a useful way to articulate this hierarchy. Flash vision presents a rapid, relatively isolated visual intake: an object appears in a moment, often without extensive perceptual context or opportunities for active exploration. Spherical vision, by contrast, represents a more encompassing and interactive mode of seeing, in which the perceiver is situated within a stable field of objects and relations. The distinction is not simply between weak and strong visual evidence. It concerns two forms of perceptual information. Flash vision tells us that something visually appears in a particular way at a particular moment. Spherical vision places the apparent object within an organized world of perspectives, spatial relations, continuities, and possible actions. The latter therefore seems better positioned to resist defeaters. Nevertheless, its superiority is a matter of degree and structure, not an automatic guarantee of metaphysical certainty.

The central argument of this essay is that conclusive empirical beliefs can be empirically indefeasible only in a restricted sense. They may be indefeasible with respect to certain classes of empirical defeaters because the evidence constituting the belief already incorporates, neutralizes, or outruns those defeaters. Compound perceptually justified beliefs are especially resistant to testimonial challenges and similar forms of empirical attack when they are grounded in mutually reinforcing sensory contents and practical engagement. But this resistance does not amount to an unassailable guarantee that the perceived object exists as a mind-independent material thing. Tactile confirmation deepens and stabilizes perceptual justification, yet it remains subject to error, illusion, simulation, hallucination, and uncertainty about the interpretation of bodily experience.

## I. Conclusive empirical belief and the problem of defeasibility

A belief is ordinarily called *defeasible* when its justification can be undermined or overridden by further evidence. If I believe that the road is clear because I look down it and see no cars, my belief may be defeated by a reliable report that a vehicle is approaching from a hidden side road. The original visual evidence need not become unreal; it simply ceases to justify the belief once new information changes its significance. Defeaters can be divided into at least two broad kinds. An *undercutting defeater* attacks the connection between evidence and belief: poor lighting, intoxication, or knowledge of a deceptive mirror may show that my visual experience is unreliable. An *overriding defeater* supplies evidence for the negation of what I believe: a trustworthy witness may tell me that a car is present even though I do not see it.

The thesis of empirical indefeasibility states, roughly, that some beliefs are so conclusively grounded in empirical evidence that no empirical information can defeat them. This thesis should not be confused with the claim that such beliefs are infallible. Infallibility concerns whether a belief can be false. Indefeasibility concerns whether it can rationally lose its justification in light of further evidence. A belief could be false but indefeasible for the believer because no available evidence could reveal the error. Conversely, a true belief could be defeasible if the evidence supporting it is weak or if a credible contrary report emerges.

The phrase “conclusive empirical belief” can also be interpreted in at least two ways. On a narrow interpretation, a belief is conclusive when its perceptual grounds entail its truth. On a broader epistemic interpretation, a belief is conclusive when the total experiential situation makes rejection irrational, even though the evidence does not entail the belief in a strict logical sense. The narrow interpretation seems too demanding for ordinary perception. Seeing a cup does not logically entail that a cup exists, since the experience could occur in a dream or hallucination. The broader interpretation better captures the force of everyday perception: in normal conditions, a vivid, stable, and practically accessible cup is not merely probable but rationally undeniable.

Still, if the broader interpretation is adopted, “indefeasible” must be relativized. A belief may be indefeasible relative to ordinary empirical challenges but not relative to every skeptical hypothesis. A testimony that contradicts a momentary visual impression may be a relevant empirical defeater. But the abstract possibility that one is in a computer simulation does not operate in the same way. It is not ordinary empirical evidence against the particular belief; it is a global hypothesis concerning the reliability of all experience. Treating every skeptical possibility as an empirical defeater would make empirical knowledge impossible by definition.

The most plausible version of the thesis is therefore conditional:

> A conclusive empirical belief is empirically indefeasible relative to defeaters that are compatible with, and genuinely directed at, the experiential conditions constituting the belief.

This formulation preserves the intuition that some perceptual beliefs are extraordinarily secure while avoiding the implausible conclusion that no additional experience could ever matter. It also directs attention to the structure of the experience itself. A belief based on a single glance is not supported in the same way as a belief based on continuous perception, multiple perspectives, bodily interaction, and independent sensory confirmation.

## II. Sensory modalities and distinct information types

The hierarchy of empirical defeasibility arises partly because sensory modalities generate distinct kinds of experience. It is tempting to speak of the senses as if they were interchangeable channels, each reporting the same external facts with varying degrees of accuracy. But perception is more differentiated than that. Each modality makes certain features salient and makes certain errors possible.

Vision characteristically provides information about visible shape, color, distance, motion, orientation, and spatial arrangement. It allows the perceiver to survey a scene without physically contacting its objects. This distance is one of vision’s greatest strengths and vulnerabilities. Vision can disclose inaccessible or dangerous objects, but it can also be deceived by lighting, perspective, camouflage, mirrors, images, and optical illusion. Visual information is often rich in spatial structure but comparatively indirect with respect to solidity, weight, temperature, and resistance.

Touch provides a different informational profile. It conveys pressure, texture, temperature, vibration, shape under contact, and resistance to bodily movement. Touch is not merely vision at close range. To touch an object is to enter into a causal and bodily relation with it. When the hand presses against a table, the experience includes not just a surface appearance but a felt resistance that constrains movement. This makes touch especially significant for beliefs about materiality. Yet touch also has characteristic vulnerabilities: numbness, referred sensation, phantom limbs, tactile hallucination, prosthetic mediation, and technologically generated haptic feedback.

Hearing primarily presents temporally unfolding events: speech, music, impacts, approaching vehicles, and environmental continuity. It can provide evidence about hidden or distant causes, but it is highly dependent on source interpretation. A sound may be real while its perceived source is mistaken. Proprioception and kinesthesia disclose the body’s position and movement, often without conscious attention. They help integrate vision and touch into an embodied perspective. Smell and taste provide chemical information that can identify substances or danger, but they are particularly sensitive to adaptation, expectation, and contextual influence.

These modalities generate different *information types*. We may distinguish at least four:

1. **Appearance information**, concerning how something presents from a particular perspective.
2. **Structural information**, concerning stable relations among parts, objects, and spatial locations.
3. **Causal-interactive information**, concerning resistance, impact, affordance, and the consequences of action.
4. **Intersubjective information**, concerning what is available to multiple observers and can be independently checked.

No single modality automatically supplies all four kinds. A flash of vision may provide appearance information but little causal or intersubjective information. Touch can provide causal-interactive information but not necessarily independent information, since the tactile experience may be generated by a device or mediated by an abnormal bodily state. A compound perceptual experience, by contrast, may integrate several types at once. The more types of information a belief incorporates, the more difficult it becomes for a single defeater to undermine it.

This is the basis for a hierarchy of empirical defeasibility. At the lower end are beliefs based on isolated, momentary, or modality-specific appearances. They are vulnerable to defeaters that reveal ambiguity in the perceptual presentation. At a higher level are beliefs supported by continuous perception, stable spatial organization, active exploration, and cross-modal agreement. At the highest ordinary level are beliefs embedded in a shared practical environment, confirmed by repeated interactions and by the independent experiences of other people. These beliefs may be empirically indefeasible in the ordinary sense, even though they are not logically or metaphysically invulnerable.

## III. Flash vision and spherical vision

Gessell’s distinction between flash vision and spherical vision clarifies the difference between isolated visual presentation and integrated visual inhabitation. *Flash vision* can be understood as the experience of a visual object appearing in a momentary burst. One sees a shape, color, or configuration, but the experience is temporally thin. Its informational content is largely perspectival: this is how something looks from here, now. Flash vision is useful and often accurate, but it leaves open many questions. Is the apparent object stable? Does it occupy the location it seems to occupy? Does it persist when attention shifts? Can it be approached, touched, or seen from another angle? Is it an object in the environment or an image, reflection, projection, or hallucination?

The epistemic weakness of flash vision does not arise because visual appearances are inherently unreliable. Rather, it arises because the experience contains relatively little information with which to distinguish competing explanations. Consider seeing what appears to be a person standing at the end of a dark corridor. A momentary visual presentation may justify the belief that someone is there. But a subsequent discovery that the figure was a coat on a stand, a life-sized photograph, or a reflection would be an effective empirical defeater. The original experience was genuine as an experience, yet it did not contain enough structure to exclude the alternatives.

*Spherical vision* represents a richer mode of visual awareness. The metaphor of a sphere suggests not merely a wider field but an enveloping organization of perception. The perceiver experiences objects as located within a surrounding world that can be explored from multiple perspectives. The object is not just a visual patch; it has a front and back, a near and far side, relations to other objects, and a place within an environment. Spherical vision therefore incorporates possibilities of movement and re-identification. One can walk around the object, observe the continuity of its appearance, see how it blocks and reveals other objects, and locate it within a stable spatial system.

The difference between flash and spherical vision is epistemically important because spherical vision supports object permanence and perspective-transcendence. An object seen only from one angle may be an image or deceptive surface. An object that remains visually coherent as one moves around it, that changes appearance according to predictable perspectival rules, and that interacts with surrounding objects provides stronger evidence for its independent existence. Spherical vision also supplies what may be called *counterfactual information*: if I moved to the left, I would see one side; if I looked behind the screen, the object would remain hidden; if another person occupied the opposite position, they could see a different aspect. Such possibilities are not all actual experiences, but the current perceptual field presents the object as available for this kind of exploration.

Nevertheless, spherical vision should not be treated as a magical escape from perceptual fallibility. A sophisticated virtual-reality environment could produce stable, navigable, and richly structured visual experience. A dream can contain apparent spatial depth and continuity. A stage set can support multiple perspectives while remaining deceptive about the objects it represents. The distinction therefore concerns the *degree and organization of evidence*, not an infallible criterion of reality.

Spherical vision also depends upon the active body. It is not purely visual in the narrow sense. Eye movements, head movements, locomotion, and expectations help generate the experience of a stable world. In that respect, the contrast between flash and spherical vision anticipates the importance of multimodal perception. The richer visual experience is already connected to proprioception, vestibular information, and practical possibilities of action. Its epistemic strength derives from integration rather than from vision alone.

## IV. Compound perceptual beliefs and resistance to testimony

A compound perceptually justified belief is a belief supported by several perceptual contents that converge on one object or state of affairs. For example:

- There is a red cup on the table.
- The cup has a particular shape and size.
- It remains in the same location as I move.
- My hand encounters resistance when I reach for it.
- I hear the sound of the cup being placed down.
- Another person can also see and touch it.

The belief is compound because its justification is not exhausted by a single experience. It incorporates visual appearance, spatial continuity, tactile resistance, auditory event-information, bodily action, and perhaps intersubjective confirmation. Such a belief is not merely more probable than a simple visual belief. It is justified by a network of mutually supporting informational relations.

This structure makes compound beliefs resistant to testimonial defeaters. Suppose I see, approach, and touch a cup on a table, while another person tells me, “There is no cup there.” The testimonial challenge may matter if the witness is close, attentive, and normally reliable. But the testimony must overcome a substantial body of perceptual evidence. If I can pick up the cup, feel its weight, see its underside, hear it strike the table, and observe the witness’s hand pass around it, the report is not simply an alternative piece of evidence of equal standing. It conflicts with a coordinated perceptual situation.

The resistance can be explained in two ways. First, the compound belief has greater evidential density. A testimony that undermines one perceptual appearance may not undermine the other components. If the witness says the cup is blue while I see it as red, the testimony may defeat the color component without defeating the belief that a cup exists. If the witness says there is no cup, the challenge must explain away not only the visual presentation but also the tactile resistance, bodily manipulation, auditory effects, and the object’s relation to the table.

Second, compound beliefs possess a kind of *defeater absorption*. A defeater that would normally undermine a perceptual belief can be neutralized by other perceptual evidence. Suppose I am told that the room contains a highly realistic projection. This testimony undercuts my initial visual belief. Yet if I touch the apparent object and feel its surface, lift it, and observe its shadow change as I move it, the original concern may be absorbed by the broader perceptual pattern. The testimony may still prompt investigation, but it no longer rationally compels rejection.

The idea of absorption should not be overstated. A testimonial challenge can be powerful when it concerns the reliability of the entire perceptual system or the conditions common to all the experiences. If a trusted technician tells me that the entire room is a virtual-reality simulation, and provides independent evidence about the equipment, then the testimony is not merely contradicting one visual appearance. It attacks the common source of the visual, tactile, and auditory experiences. A compound belief is most resistant when its components are at least partially independent. If all sensory outputs are generated by one deceptive system, their agreement may be evidentially redundant rather than genuinely corroborative.

This point reveals an important distinction between *modal diversity* and *modal multiplication*. Five experiences generated by the same source may not provide five independent reasons. Seeing an object through five cameras does not establish its material existence if all cameras display the same manipulated feed. Likewise, a virtual environment can coordinate visual, auditory, and haptic signals. What matters is not simply the number of experiences but the diversity of their causal and informational routes.

Testimony itself is not epistemically inferior by nature. In many circumstances testimony is the best source of empirical knowledge. I may rationally believe that a distant city exists, that a laboratory discovered a particle, or that a historical event occurred because reliable people tell me so. The point is instead that testimony has a distinctive defeasibility profile. Testimonial evidence is mediated through another person’s observation, memory, interpretation, and communication. Direct, compound perception may outrank testimony concerning a presently accessible object because it supplies immediate and manipulable information. But testimony may outrank perception when the perceptual conditions are poor or when the witness has access to information unavailable to me.

Thus, a perceptually justified belief resists testimony not absolutely but proportionately to the richness and independence of its perceptual grounds. The stronger belief is not “I saw it, therefore no one can rationally deny it.” It is “the total perceptual situation is so integrated and independently accessible that an unsupported contrary report does not defeat it.”

## V. Undermining defeaters and the unity of perceptual experience

Compound perception is also resistant to *undermining* defeaters, though again not immune to them. An undermining defeater does not assert that the perceived object is absent; it challenges the reliability of the perceptual route. For instance, if I learn that the room contains an optical illusion, my visual experience becomes less trustworthy. If I discover that I have taken a hallucinogenic substance, my visual and tactile judgments are placed under suspicion. If I learn that the object is a highly advanced hologram, the apparent materiality of the object is undermined even if its visual presence remains undeniable.

The effectiveness of an undermining defeater depends on the level at which it operates. A defeater directed at one sensory modality may leave the others intact. Knowledge that the lighting is deceptive may undermine color judgments while leaving tactile judgments about shape and resistance unaffected. Knowledge that one’s hand is numb may undermine tactile judgments while leaving visual judgments intact. By contrast, evidence that one is dreaming, hallucinating, or immersed in a comprehensive simulation threatens the reliability of the entire compound experience.

This suggests that perceptual justification has a hierarchical architecture. At the first level are modality-specific contents: “something red appears there,” “pressure is felt here,” “a sound occurs.” At the second level are integrated object beliefs: “there is a red cup located there.” At the third level are world-involving beliefs: “the cup is a material object in a shared external environment.” Defeaters may attack one level without attacking the others. An image can be visually real as an image while not being a cup. A tactile stimulus can be genuinely felt while not being caused by a material object of the relevant kind. A stable object belief can survive the correction of a color judgment.

The unity of the senses is therefore epistemically valuable, but it is not automatic. Perception actively constructs object identity across time and modality. The perceiver treats the thing seen as the thing touched, the thing heard, and the thing encountered in action. This synthesis is generally reliable because the world supplies regular correlations among sensory channels. Yet the synthesis can fail. A person may see a prosthetic object but feel no corresponding resistance; a virtual reality system may create coordinated signals; a ventriloquist performance may separate auditory source from visual source. The possibility of such failures does not eliminate perceptual knowledge. It shows that perceptual knowledge is grounded in patterns of successful integration rather than in an isolated “given.”

## VI. Does tactile confirmation guarantee material existence?

Tactile confirmation often appears to possess a special epistemic authority. Vision can be deceived by images, mirrors, and appearances, but touch seems to disclose the object’s material presence directly. If I reach out and feel resistance, grasp the thing, move it, and experience its texture, surely I have an unassailable guarantee that a material object exists. This intuition is powerful because touch involves causal interaction. The object does not merely appear; it obstructs and responds to my bodily activity.

Yet tactile confirmation cannot provide an absolute guarantee of material existence. Several distinctions explain why.

First, touch guarantees, at most, that a tactile experience is occurring. It does not by itself guarantee the external cause or the interpretation of that experience. Phantom limbs demonstrate that a person can feel touch, pressure, or pain in a limb that is absent. Neurological stimulation can produce tactile sensations without ordinary external contact. Dreams and hallucinations can include bodily experiences that seem tactile. These cases show that felt resistance or contact is not logically sufficient for the existence of the perceived material object.

Second, tactile experience can be technologically generated. A haptic device may produce pressure, vibration, and resistance. In a virtual environment, the user may feel the boundary of an object that has no corresponding material object in the surrounding room. One could object that the haptic device itself is material. That is true, but it does not establish the existence of the virtual object represented by the experience. The experience confirms some causal interaction with matter, not necessarily the existence of the object as experienced.

Third, touch is interpretively structured. Feeling resistance does not identify the nature of what resists. A surface may be a table, a wall, a rigid projection system, or part of one’s own body. Tactile information gives access to pressure, texture, temperature, and force; the inference to “a mind-independent material object of this particular kind exists” involves integration with other sensory and conceptual information.

Fourth, tactile confirmation is not independent of the body and its conditions. The hand’s receptors, nerves, brain, and bodily expectations contribute to the experience. If the entire system were malfunctioning or manipulated, tactile evidence could be systematically misleading. The skeptical possibility is not that every tactile experience is usually false, but that tactile experience lacks the sort of self-authenticating status needed for an unassailable guarantee.

Still, refusing an absolute guarantee should not diminish the epistemic importance of touch. Tactile experience is often more resistant than vision to particular forms of defeater. A visual illusion may be corrected by touch. A suspected reflection or photograph can be distinguished from an object by grasping and moving it. Touch supplies information about resistance and manipulability that vision alone does not. In ordinary conditions, touching an object is an exceptionally strong confirmation of its presence. It may justify belief to a degree that is practically conclusive and empirically indefeasible against ordinary challenges.

The proper conclusion is therefore neither that touch guarantees material existence nor that touch is epistemically insignificant. Tactile confirmation provides *causal evidence* of extraordinary strength, but causal evidence is not identical to metaphysical certainty. It establishes that the perceiver is being affected in a structured way; it does not completely settle what the affecting source is or whether the perceived object exists independently in the exact manner represented.

## VII. Against an unrestricted thesis of empirical indefeasibility

The thesis that conclusive empirical beliefs are empirically indefeasible is attractive because it captures the experience of perceptual certainty. When one is looking at, handling, and moving around an object, a contrary assertion can seem absurd. But an unrestricted thesis generates counterexamples.

Imagine a person in an impeccably realistic simulation. The system provides flash-like and spherical visual experiences, tactile resistance, auditory feedback, and apparent interaction with other observers. The person forms the compound belief that a physical book lies on a table. From within the simulation, the belief may be fully justified by all available empirical evidence. Yet if one later exits the simulation, the belief may be revealed as false, at least if it concerned a book in the external room. This case shows that even maximally integrated perception can be defeated by new evidence about the common causal source of experience.

One might respond that the later evidence is not an empirical defeater of the original belief but a metaphysical revelation about the framework of experience. However, the simulation may be empirically discovered through equipment readings, bodily observation, or testimony from technicians. If so, the defeater is not purely philosophical. It is evidence concerning the conditions under which the original experience occurred. The original belief was rational, perhaps conclusive relative to the previous evidence, but not globally indefeasible.

A second counterexample involves expert testimony. Suppose I see what appears to be a rare medical instrument in a museum. I touch it and examine it from all sides. A curator tells me that the object is an exact replica and that the original is elsewhere. My perceptual belief that “there is a physical object here” survives, but my belief that “this is the original instrument” is defeated. The case demonstrates that compound perception may be conclusive for some components of a belief but not others. The more specific the proposition, the more kinds of defeaters can become relevant.

A third case concerns anomalous but coordinated perception. Several people may see and touch what appears to be a solid object, but later learn that they were all exposed to the same drug or were interacting with a shared virtual display. Inter-subjective agreement does not automatically produce indefeasibility when the observers share a common source of error. Independence is crucial.

These cases do not show that conclusive empirical beliefs are weak. They show that conclusiveness is indexed to a proposition and an evidential context. “There is a resistant surface before me” may remain justified even if “there is a wooden table in the room” is defeated. “A tactile experience is occurring” may be indefeasible in a first-person sense, while “a mind-independent object causes it” remains fallible. The thesis survives only if it respects these distinctions.

## VIII. A revised hierarchy of empirical defeasibility

We can now formulate a more precise hierarchy.

At the lowest level is **momentary appearance**. Flash vision, isolated sounds, or unconfirmed impressions support relatively thin propositions. They are vulnerable to ordinary overriding defeaters and to undercutting information about perceptual conditions.

The next level is **temporally stable perception**. When an appearance persists and remains coherent over time, the belief gains resistance. Persistence reduces the likelihood of momentary distortion, though it does not eliminate deception.

A third level is **spatially integrated or spherical perception**. Multiple perspectives, object continuity, occlusion relations, and navigable surroundings support beliefs about stable objects rather than transient appearances.

A fourth level is **active sensorimotor confirmation**. The perceiver acts on the object and receives predictable consequences. Touch, movement, manipulation, and resistance provide causal-interactive information that can defeat appearance-based alternatives.

A fifth level is **cross-modal convergence**. Vision, touch, hearing, proprioception, and perhaps smell jointly support one interpretation. The convergence is strongest when the modalities are causally and informationally independent enough that a single error source cannot explain them all.

A sixth level is **intersubjective and practical integration**. Other observers can interact with the object, actions involving it have publicly observable consequences, and the object remains embedded in a shared environment. At this level, the belief is normally beyond rational empirical challenge unless the defeater concerns a common source affecting the entire community or environment.

The highest level is not absolute metaphysical certainty but **ordinary empirical indefeasibility**. A belief at this level is not rationally overturned by an isolated testimony, a fleeting contrary appearance, or a single anomalous sensation. To defeat it, one needs evidence that addresses the integrated structure itself: evidence of systematic deception, pervasive malfunction, simulation, or a comprehensive alternative explanation.

This hierarchy should not be treated as a simple ladder in which later levels always supersede earlier ones. Some visual evidence is better than some tactile evidence; some testimony is more reliable than some perception. Nor is multimodal agreement necessarily additive in a mechanical way. The hierarchy concerns the organization, independence, and explanatory reach of evidence. Its key principle is that a belief becomes more resistant as it is supported by more varied informational relations that must be jointly explained away.

## IX. The philosophical significance of empirical indefeasibility

The debate has broader implications for the relation between perception and knowledge. If empirical beliefs are always defeasible, then the concept of perceptual certainty may seem empty. But if some perceptual beliefs are entirely indefeasible, skepticism seems too easily dismissed. The better position occupies a middle ground.

Perceptual justification is neither an inference from sensations nor an infallible presentation of reality. It is a form of embodied access to a structured environment. Experiences have epistemic force because they place the perceiver in reliable relations to objects and events. The force of a single experience depends on background conditions, while the force of a compound experience derives from its integration across time, space, action, and social confirmation.

Gessell’s flash/spherical distinction helps explain why the phenomenology of certainty varies. Flash vision presents an object as a possible occupant of a scene. Spherical vision presents an object as a stable node in a world of possible perspectives and actions. The transition from flash to sphere is a transition from appearance to organized presence. It is also a transition from evidence that something looks a certain way to evidence that something exists as a persisting object with causal and spatial properties.

But organized presence remains a mode of presentation, not a deductive proof. The world may be represented with extraordinary coherence while the representation is technologically or psychologically generated. The fact that we cannot rationally doubt an object in ordinary practical circumstances does not entail that the object’s material existence is logically guaranteed. Empirical indefeasibility should therefore be understood pragmatically and contextually. It marks the point at which further empirical challenge would require abandoning the normal framework of perception rather than merely revising one belief within it.

## Conclusion

The thesis that conclusive empirical beliefs are empirically indefeasible is defensible only after the terms “conclusive” and “indefeasible” are carefully qualified. Sensory modalities generate distinct experience kinds and information types. Vision presents appearance and spatial organization; touch presents contact, resistance, and texture; hearing presents temporal events; proprioception integrates the body with its environment. These modalities can combine into compound perceptual beliefs whose evidential strength exceeds that of any isolated sensation.

Bryce Gessell’s distinction between flash vision and spherical vision captures a fundamental difference within visual experience. Flash vision is temporally and perspectivally thin, leaving many competing explanations open. Spherical vision situates objects within a stable, navigable, and relational field, thereby supporting stronger beliefs about persistence and externality. Its epistemic superiority lies not in infallibility but in the richer structure of information it provides.

Compound perceptually justified beliefs resist testimonial and other empirical defeaters because they are supported by multiple, mutually reinforcing channels of information. A contrary report must explain away not merely an appearance but a coordinated pattern of visual, tactile, auditory, bodily, and intersubjective evidence. Yet the resistance depends on the relative independence of those channels. A common source of deception—a simulation, hallucination, or systemic malfunction—can undermine the whole compound.

Tactile confirmation is especially powerful because it involves causal interaction and felt resistance. It can correct purely visual mistakes and provide practical evidence of material presence. Nevertheless, touch does not offer an unassailable guarantee of a mind-independent material object. Tactile experience can be hallucinated, technologically produced, neurologically distorted, or misinterpreted. It confirms that a structured bodily experience occurs and normally indicates an external cause, but it does not logically settle the metaphysical nature of that cause.

The strongest conclusion is therefore a qualified one: conclusive empirical beliefs can be empirically indefeasible relative to ordinary, local, and modality-specific defeaters when they are grounded in integrated perceptual experience. They are not indefeasible relative to every possible empirical discovery about the conditions of perception, nor do they provide an absolute guarantee of material existence. Empirical certainty is best understood as the maximal stability of belief within an embodied, shared, and practically successful world—not as immunity from every conceivable form of revision.
