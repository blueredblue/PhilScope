# Mental Health between Physiology and Anomalous Monism

Christopher Boorse’s physiological model of health and illness appears, at first sight, to stand in direct opposition to Donald Davidson’s principle of the anomalism of the mental. Boorse’s account treats health as a matter of normal biological functioning, and disease as a failure of the organism’s statistically typical contribution to survival and reproduction. If mental disorders are diseases, then they must ultimately be grounded in physiological dysfunctions. Such an account seems to require laws connecting mental diseases with their biological causes and effects. Davidson, by contrast, argues that there are no strict psychological laws and no strict psychophysical laws. Mental events are causally related to physical events, but mental descriptions are “anomalous”: they do not participate in a closed system of exceptionless laws. The apparent conflict becomes sharper when Davidson’s holism of the mental is considered. Beliefs, desires, intentions, and other mental states are individuated by their relations to one another and by standards of rationality. Consequently, the mental cannot be mapped neatly onto isolated physiological states or organized into strict laws resembling those of physics or physiology.

This conflict, however, depends on how Boorse’s physiological model is interpreted. If the model requires mental-health science to formulate strict laws at the mental level, Davidson’s anomalism presents a serious challenge. If, instead, Boorse’s central claim is that disease is a biological dysfunction, then his model can remain compatible with Davidson’s position. The relevant laws may be physiological rather than psychological, while mental-health explanations retain a distinctive intentional and normative vocabulary. Robert Klee’s ceteris paribus objection to anomalous monism helps clarify this issue. Klee’s challenge is that Davidson’s contrast between strict scientific laws and looser intentional generalizations may be too sharp. Scientific explanations themselves frequently depend on background conditions and idealizations. If scientific and intentional explanations share the same kinds of ceteris paribus conditions, then Davidson cannot simply infer that intentional explanations lack causal or scientific legitimacy.

The most plausible conclusion is therefore qualified. A fully autonomous science of mental health cannot be autonomous in the sense of being metaphysically independent of physiology or governed by strict psychological laws. But it can remain autonomous in an explanatory, taxonomic, and methodological sense. It need not achieve type-to-type physical reduction in order to be scientifically legitimate. Boorse’s model can survive Davidson’s anomalism if it is understood as a theory of biological dysfunction rather than as a demand for strict laws stated in mental vocabulary. The resulting science of mental health would be physically grounded but explanatorily pluralistic.

## I. Boorse’s Physiological Model

Boorse’s account is designed as a naturalistic and value-free theory of health. Rather than defining health in terms of happiness, social adjustment, subjective wellbeing, or ideal human flourishing, he defines it through biological functioning. A healthy organism is one whose parts and processes perform their statistically typical functions for members of the relevant reference class. A disease is an internal state that interferes with one or more normal functional abilities. Health and disease, on this account, are principally matters of biology rather than evaluations of the person as a whole.

The model has several important components. First, it distinguishes between the normal and the merely average. A trait may be statistically common without being a biological function, and a trait may be biologically functional without being possessed by every member of a population. Second, it identifies disease with internal dysfunction rather than with any condition that is undesirable or socially disapproved. Third, it explains disease through the organization and causal operation of biological systems. A disease is not simply a label for suffering; it is a malfunction of a mechanism that normally performs a certain contribution to the organism’s survival or reproduction.

This framework creates an immediate difficulty for mental disorders. Many mental conditions are initially identified through behavior, subjective report, or interpersonal judgment. Depression, schizophrenia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and personality disorders are not ordinarily diagnosed by observing a single defective organ in the way that pneumonia may be diagnosed through infection of the lungs. Their identification often depends on the person’s beliefs, emotions, reasons, patterns of action, and relation to social norms. These features appear to belong to the mental and intentional domain, not to physiology narrowly understood.

Boorse’s response is not necessarily that these phenomena are unreal or explanatorily irrelevant. Rather, if a mental condition counts as a disease in the strict medical sense, it must be underwritten by an underlying biological dysfunction. A psychiatric syndrome may be described psychologically, but its status as a disease ultimately depends on a disturbance in the functioning of the nervous system or some related biological mechanism. The physiological model thus promises to distinguish genuine disorder from mere eccentricity, unhappiness, unconventionality, or social nonconformity.

The model therefore appears to impose a causal requirement. To classify a mental condition as a disease is to say more than that it is unpleasant or irrational. It is to say that something in the organism is functioning abnormally and that this abnormality explains the characteristic manifestations of the disorder. A physiological account requires causal mechanisms: disruptions of neural circuits, abnormal regulation, inherited vulnerabilities, altered neurotransmitter systems, impaired cognitive processing, or other biological processes. It might also require generalizations linking such dysfunctions to characteristic symptoms.

The key question is what kind of generalizations Boorse needs. Does he require strict laws of the form:

> Whenever an organism is in physiological state P, it is in mental state M?

Or does he require only biological explanations of the form:

> Under appropriate physiological conditions, mechanism P tends to produce a pattern of behavior, experience, and cognitive functioning characteristic of disorder D?

The first formulation would place Boorse in direct conflict with Davidson. The second is more compatible with Davidson because it locates the governing laws at the physiological level while treating the mental description as a higher-level characterization of a complex pattern.

## II. Davidson’s Anomalous Monism

Davidson’s anomalous monism combines three claims. First, mental events causally interact with physical events. A desire can cause an action, a perception can cause a belief, and a belief can cause another belief or bodily movement. Second, causal relations fall under laws. If one event causes another, there must be some law that subsumes the events under appropriate descriptions. Third, there are no strict laws connecting the mental with the physical, and no strict laws governing the mental as such.

The apparent inconsistency among these claims is resolved by Davidson’s distinction between events and descriptions. Events are concrete particulars, and a single event may be described physically, psychologically, intentionally, or in several ways at once. A particular decision, for example, may be identical with a neural event while also being describable as the formation of an intention. As an event, it can enter into causal relations governed by physical laws. But as an intention, it may not fall under any strict psychological law. Davidson therefore defends a form of token identity without type identity: each particular mental event is a physical event, but mental kinds are not necessarily identical with physical kinds.

This position is “monist” because there are not two kinds of events, mental and physical, existing in separate ontological realms. It is “anomalous” because the mental vocabulary does not form a law-governed system comparable to physics. The mental is causally efficacious, but its causal efficacy is not captured by strict laws stated purely in mental terms.

Davidson’s argument depends heavily on the nature of mental concepts. Mental predicates are not merely descriptive labels for inner physical states. They are embedded in a rational and normative framework. To attribute a belief or desire to an agent is to interpret the agent as occupying a position in a network of beliefs, desires, intentions, perceptions, and actions. The content of one belief depends partly on its inferential relations to many other beliefs. A belief that it is raining has a different content depending on what the person believes about weather, evidence, testimony, umbrellas, and the reliability of perception.

This is Davidson’s holism of the mental. Mental states are not individuated atomistically. They acquire their identity from their place in a broad psychological pattern. The same neural state could, in principle, have different intentional significance in different cognitive systems. Conversely, different neural configurations might realize the same belief or intention in different organisms or contexts. Mental properties are thus resistant to simple one-to-one correspondence with physical properties.

The principle of rationality is central to this holism. Davidson’s theory of interpretation requires the interpreter to maximize rationality, coherence, and agreement with reality when attributing beliefs and desires. This does not mean that people are perfectly rational or that irrational behavior does not exist. It means that interpretation must begin by treating the agent as broadly rational enough for his or her attitudes to form an intelligible pattern. Apparent irrationality is understood against a background of rationality, or else reinterpreted as a mistake in attribution.

This principle has a constitutive, not merely empirical, role. Rationality is not simply a regularity that happens to be found in human behavior. It helps determine what counts as having a belief, desire, or intention in the first place. A belief is not individuated solely by a physical state; it belongs to a system in which the agent can be interpreted as responding to reasons. Hence the mental cannot be isolated from the totality of the agent’s other attitudes and from the interpretive standards that make those attitudes intelligible.

The result is that strict psychological laws are unavailable. A strict law would have to connect a finite set of psychological predicates under all relevant conditions. But because the content and identity of psychological states depend on an open-ended network of other mental states, it is difficult to specify a complete set of antecedent conditions. The background conditions are not simply additional measurable variables. They include the agent’s total belief-desire structure, relevant standards of rationality, context, history, and relation to the world. The required conditions are therefore potentially indefinite.

## III. The Tension with Boorse’s Requirement for Causal Laws

Davidson’s position challenges Boorse at two levels. The first concerns the status of mental disease categories. The second concerns the kind of causal explanation required in psychiatry.

Suppose depression is a disease because it results from a dysfunction in a biological system. Boorse needs some account of what makes that dysfunction a dysfunction and how it produces the symptoms associated with depression. The physiological part of the explanation may be perfectly compatible with strict laws. A neural circuit may operate abnormally; a regulatory system may fail; a mechanism may cease to perform its normal function. In this respect, Boorse can rely on laws of neurophysiology without needing laws about depression as an intentional or experiential category.

However, many psychiatric conditions are not exhausted by physiology. Depression includes feelings of hopelessness, changes in self-conception, altered motivation, and patterns of reasoning. Schizophrenia includes delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thought, and disturbances in the person’s interpretation of the world. These features are partly defined by their content and relations to other beliefs. The question is whether a physiological model can explain them without psychological laws.

A strict reductionist might hope to identify depression with a neural type, schizophrenia with another neural type, and so forth. This would produce a type-to-type reduction: mental disease kind M would be identical with physical disease kind P. Davidson’s anomalous monism makes such reductions doubtful. Since mental states are holistically individuated and multiply realizable, there may be no single physical type corresponding to a given mental type. Even if every particular mental event is physical, a mental category may be realized by different physical mechanisms in different people.

This creates a dilemma for Boorse if his theory requires that each mental disease correspond to a fixed physiological dysfunction. Either the mental disease is reduced to a physical type, in which case its psychological content risks disappearing from the explanation, or it remains a psychological category, in which case strict laws may be unavailable.

The dilemma is especially serious if causal explanation is understood narrowly. A strict law must state a necessary and exceptionless connection between types. But mental disorders are typically sensitive to history, learning, social environment, comorbidity, interpretation, and the agent’s broader cognitive structure. Even when a physiological mechanism is stable, its psychological manifestation may vary. The same biological disturbance may produce anxiety in one person, compulsive behavior in another, and no clinically significant symptoms in a third. Conversely, similar intentional patterns may arise from different biological pathways.

Davidson would argue that this variability is not merely a temporary defect of psychiatric science. It reflects a structural feature of the mental. Because the mental is holistic and governed by rational interpretation, no finite set of psychological variables will provide a complete law-like account. The instability of psychiatric categories may therefore be partly conceptual, not merely empirical.

Yet Davidson’s position does not by itself refute Boorse. It shows that a mental-health science cannot expect the same kind of laws as fundamental physics. It does not show that physiological dysfunctions lack causal regularities, nor that mental descriptions cannot support useful generalizations. Boorse can distinguish three claims:

1. A mental disorder has a physical basis.
2. The physical basis is a biological dysfunction.
3. There are strict psychological laws linking mental states to one another or to physical states.

The first two are not obviously threatened by anomalous monism. The third is. Boorse’s model needs the first two for its naturalistic account of disease, but it need not require the third unless he treats psychiatry as a self-contained psychological science.

This distinction also reveals an ambiguity in the phrase “mental health.” If it means a person’s overall wellbeing, meaning, emotional stability, or rational agency, then a physiological model may be too narrow. If it means the absence of biologically grounded dysfunctions that manifest in mental life, then Boorse’s account is more defensible. Davidson’s anomalism primarily affects the way these manifestations are explained, not necessarily whether they can be caused by biological dysfunction.

## IV. Holism, Rationality, and the Possibility of Mental Disease

The holism of the mental has a further consequence. Mental illness is often characterized by departures from rationality: delusions that resist evidence, compulsive actions that conflict with the agent’s considered judgments, or patterns of thought that appear self-defeating. But Davidson’s principle of charity seems to require maximizing rationality when interpreting the agent. Does this make mental illness impossible or render psychological diagnosis conceptually unstable?

It does not. Davidson’s principle is best understood as a condition on interpretation, not as a denial of irrationality. An interpreter must attribute enough coherence to the agent to identify beliefs and desires at all. Once a belief system has been mapped, one can describe particular beliefs as false, unjustified, inconsistent, or irrational. A delusion can be identified as a belief within a broader system, even though it is poorly supported by evidence. What is impossible is to identify a belief independently of all relations that make it recognizable as a belief.

This matters for mental disease because the category of disorder may involve a contrast between the agent’s mental organization and a norm of rationality, but the norm cannot be applied to an isolated state. A delusion is not simply a neural state with the property “irrational.” It is a belief that occupies a certain role in an interconnected system and fails to respond appropriately to evidence or reasons. The disorder is partly constituted by this systemic failure.

Boorse’s physiological model can accommodate this only if it treats the mental characteristics of disease as manifestations or functional consequences of biological dysfunctions, rather than as independently law-governed states. A physiological explanation might show that certain neural processes impair the capacity to revise beliefs in response to evidence. The psychological description then explains what this impairment means in the agent’s cognitive life. The two explanations are not competitors. They operate at different levels.

Nevertheless, an important tension remains. The biological notion of dysfunction is itself functional. To say that a mechanism has failed is to say that it is not performing the function characteristic of that system. This is relatively straightforward for organs and physiological processes. It becomes more complicated when the relevant function is cognitive or intentional. Is a failure to abandon a delusion a failure of a biological mechanism, a failure of rationality, or both? Boorse can answer that it is a biological dysfunction when the failure is caused by an abnormal mechanism, but Davidson reminds us that the psychological description cannot be replaced by a purely mechanistic one without loss of meaning.

Mental disorders thus occupy a border zone. Their physical realization may be governed by biological regularities, while their identification and interpretation depend on holistic relations among beliefs, desires, reasons, and actions. This is not a contradiction, but it prevents any simple reduction of psychiatric explanation to physiology.

## V. Klee’s Ceteris Paribus Objection

Robert Klee’s objection to anomalous monism targets Davidson’s treatment of ceteris paribus generalizations. A ceteris paribus law says that something happens under the condition that other relevant things are equal or remain normal. Psychological generalizations often have this form. For example:

> If an agent desires X and believes that doing A is the best available means to X, then, other things being equal, the agent will intend or attempt to do A.

Such a principle is not exceptionless. The agent may be distracted, physically restrained, confused, subject to another stronger desire, or affected by an unrecognized mental disturbance. Davidson treats these generalizations as too loose to be strict laws. They may summarize patterns, but they do not provide the strict nomological basis required for causal relations.

Klee’s objection is that the contrast between psychological ceteris paribus clauses and scientific laws may be overstated. Scientific explanations also depend on background conditions. The law that unsupported objects fall toward the earth assumes a gravitational field, absence of countervailing forces, an appropriate scale, and many other conditions. Chemical and biological laws likewise depend on idealized environments and auxiliary hypotheses. In actual scientific practice, laws are often applied only when a complex set of background conditions is satisfied.

If this is right, the fact that psychological generalizations are ceteris paribus does not by itself distinguish them from respectable scientific laws. A law need not be exceptionless in the naïve sense that it applies regardless of context. It may instead operate within a specified domain, with its ceteris paribus clause encoding conditions that are understood by the relevant science. Klee’s point is not necessarily that psychological laws are strict in Davidson’s sense, but that Davidson has not shown why their defeasibility makes them uniquely non-scientific.

The objection can be formulated more sharply. Suppose a person’s desire and belief cause an action. Davidson says that the causal relation must be subsumed under a strict law, but not under a psychological law. Klee can ask whether the physical law that ultimately subsumes the event also depends on background conditions. If it does, then strictness alone cannot explain the asymmetry between physical and psychological explanations. And if the background conditions are held fixed, why could the psychological generalization not also be regarded as law-like?

This issue raises the question whether scientific and intentional explanations share identical background conditions. In one sense, they do. The intentional explanation and the physiological explanation concern the same event and the same world. If an action is caused by a person’s desire and belief, then all the physical conditions present during the action are also present when the action is intentionally explained. There is no ontologically separate set of “mental circumstances” floating free of physical circumstances. The token event has one causal history, which can be described at multiple levels.

In another sense, however, the background conditions are not identical in explanatory function. A physiological explanation treats variables such as neural activation, hormonal states, and sensory input as components of a mechanism. It seeks to isolate, measure, and manipulate them. An intentional explanation treats beliefs and desires as reasons within a rational system. Its background conditions include the agent’s other attitudes, standards of coherence, practical identity, history, and interpretation of the situation. These are not simply additional physical variables waiting to be listed. They are conditions that determine the content and relevance of the intentional descriptions themselves.

Therefore, Klee is right to insist that scientific explanations are not free from background assumptions. Davidson cannot establish the anomalism of the mental merely by observing that psychological generalizations have exceptions or require normal conditions. But Klee’s point does not demonstrate that intentional and physiological explanations have identical background conditions in the relevant sense. They share the same physical reality, but they organize that reality through different explanatory frameworks.

The difference can be illustrated by a simple case. A person raises an umbrella because she believes it is raining and wants to remain dry. A physical explanation might specify visual input, neural processing, motor activation, and muscular contraction. Its background conditions include the integrity of the relevant mechanisms and the absence of external interference. The intentional explanation cites the belief and desire as reasons for the action. Its background conditions include the belief’s place in the person’s wider cognitive system and the practical assumption that she is acting normally rather than pretending, sleepwalking, or being coerced.

These explanations are about the same event, but they do not have the same standards of adequacy. The physiological explanation seeks causal mechanism; the intentional explanation seeks rational intelligibility. A complete physical specification might in principle account for every movement involved, but it would not thereby preserve the explanation that the action was done because the agent wanted to stay dry. Conversely, the intentional explanation does not specify the neural pathway by which the action occurred.

Klee’s objection nevertheless has an important consequence: ceteris paribus generalizations should not be dismissed as scientifically worthless. Mental-health science routinely uses defeasible generalizations. Certain cognitive impairments tend to produce characteristic symptoms; certain therapeutic interventions tend to alter certain patterns; certain combinations of beliefs and affect tend to generate maladaptive behavior. These generalizations may be reliable enough for prediction and intervention even if they are not strict laws.

The strongest version of Davidson’s position therefore risks an overly narrow conception of science. If “law” means only an exceptionless statement formulated in a vocabulary that excludes all contextual qualifications, then much of biology, medicine, and psychology may fail to qualify as genuinely law-governed. But scientific legitimacy does not necessarily require such laws. Mechanistic explanation, statistical regularity, causal modeling, and stable functional organization may suffice.

## VI. Can Mental-Health Science Be Autonomous?

The final question is whether an autonomous science of mental health remains possible without type-to-type physical reductions. The answer depends on what “autonomous” means.

If autonomy means ontological independence, the answer is no. Davidson’s anomalous monism denies that mental events form a second substance or a domain causally insulated from the physical. Every particular mental event is also a physical event. Mental health cannot therefore be independent of the organism’s physiology. A theory that treated psychological disorders as causally sealed off from the brain and body would be incompatible with both Davidson and Boorse.

If autonomy means the existence of strict laws applying only to mental kinds, the answer is also doubtful. Davidson’s holism and the constitutive role of rationality make it unlikely that there are strict type-to-type laws connecting beliefs, desires, intentions, and actions. The same is true of strict psychophysical laws connecting mental kinds to physical kinds. Mental categories are too context-sensitive and multiply realizable for a simple one-to-one mapping.

But there is a third form of autonomy: explanatory or methodological autonomy. A science is explanatorily autonomous when it identifies patterns, kinds, and causal relations that cannot be replaced without remainder by the vocabulary of a more fundamental science. This does not require that its entities be metaphysically independent or that its laws be strict in the manner of fundamental physics. Economics, ecology, evolutionary biology, and much of medicine appear to possess this kind of autonomy. Their objects are physically realized, but their explanations use concepts such as function, adaptation, organism, niche, cost, risk, and regulation that are not reducible in any straightforward way to microphysical descriptions.

Mental-health science can be autonomous in this sense. It can classify disorders by patterns of cognition, affect, behavior, and social functioning. It can formulate causal models that connect symptoms, developmental histories, biological vulnerabilities, and environmental conditions. It can explain why a patient’s beliefs and actions form a self-defeating pattern and how therapy or medication may alter that pattern. None of this requires a type-to-type reduction.

Boorse’s model can support such autonomy if it is understood as a theory of the biological grounding of disorder rather than as a demand that all clinically relevant descriptions be translated into physiology. A biological dysfunction may be multiply realized and may manifest differently depending on the person’s cognitive and social context. The disease is still a real dysfunction, but its clinical expression is mediated by the holistic organization of the person.

This approach also helps separate disease from mere difference. The fact that mental states are interpreted through norms of rationality does not mean that every deviation from conventional rationality is a disease. A biological model supplies one criterion: there must be a dysfunction in the organism’s normal functioning. The intentional level supplies another kind of understanding: it explains how the dysfunction affects the agent’s reasoning, experience, and action. The physiological and intentional levels constrain one another without collapsing into one another.

A possible objection is that if mental-health laws are merely ceteris paribus generalizations, they may be too weak for medical practice. Diagnosis and treatment require prediction and intervention. If mental states have no strict laws, how can clinicians know that a treatment will work?

The answer is that clinical science rarely depends on strict universal laws. It relies on probabilistic and mechanistic regularities. A medication may increase the probability of symptom reduction for a relevant population; a psychotherapeutic intervention may alter a cognitive mechanism under certain conditions; a treatment may work by changing both physiological processes and patterns of attention, belief, and behavior. These regularities can be causally informative without being exceptionless. Their limitations are not evidence that they are unscientific; they reflect the complexity of biological and intentional systems.

Indeed, Davidson’s anomalism may explain why psychiatric prediction is difficult without implying that it is impossible. Since mental states are embedded in holistic systems, prediction requires sensitivity to the individual’s history, reasons, environment, and broader patterns of response. The absence of strict psychological laws encourages a form of clinical explanation that is individualized and context-sensitive. This may be a limitation from the standpoint of prediction, but it can also be a strength from the standpoint of understanding.

The resulting science would be neither a Cartesian science of immaterial minds nor a simple branch of neurophysiology. It would be a layered science. At one level, it would investigate biological mechanisms and functional failures. At another, it would interpret the effects of those failures in terms of experience, agency, rationality, and social interaction. Causal explanation would run through both levels, but the laws and concepts appropriate to one would not be identical with those appropriate to the other.

## VII. Final Assessment

Boorse’s physiological model cannot succeed if it requires a strict, self-contained system of psychological laws governing mental disease. Davidson’s anomalism and holism make such a system implausible. Mental states are individuated by their place within a rational network, and their contents cannot be fixed independently of an agent’s wider pattern of attitudes and behavior. The principle of maximizing rationality ensures that intentional states are interpretive and normative in a way that resists atomistic classification. Consequently, no simple type-to-type reduction from mental diseases to physiological states is likely to capture the full meaning of psychiatric disorders.

Klee’s ceteris paribus objection shows, however, that Davidson cannot establish this conclusion merely by contrasting strict laws with exception-prone psychological generalizations. Scientific laws themselves operate against background conditions and often include idealizations. Intentional and scientific explanations concern the same physical events and share the same underlying reality. Their background conditions are therefore not wholly separate. Nevertheless, they differ in explanatory role: physiological explanations identify mechanisms, while intentional explanations locate actions and mental states within a rational and holistic system. The conditions required for these explanations overlap physically but are not identical conceptually or methodologically.

The best resolution is a distinction between biological grounding and explanatory reduction. Boorse is justified in insisting that disease involves dysfunction in the organism’s normal biological functioning. This requirement protects mental-health theory from collapsing into mere social deviance or subjective dissatisfaction. But it does not entail that every mental disease must correspond to a single physical type or that every clinical generalization must be a strict psychological law. Davidson’s anomalous monism permits token mental events to be physical while denying that mental kinds reduce neatly to physical kinds.

An autonomous science of mental health therefore remains viable, but only in a modest and pluralistic sense. It cannot be autonomous as a causally independent realm or as a science governed by strict laws entirely detached from physiology. It can be autonomous as an explanatory discipline with its own concepts, classifications, forms of evidence, and ceteris paribus causal generalizations. Its theories may describe biologically realized patterns at the levels of agency, cognition, affect, and social functioning. Such theories will be constrained by physiology without being replaceable by physiology.

The philosophical lesson is not that mental health is either purely biological or purely interpretive. Rather, mental disorder is a case in which biological dysfunction and intentional organization intersect. Boorse explains why disorder should count as disease; Davidson explains why the mental manifestations of disease cannot be captured by strict laws or simple reductions; and Klee reminds us that the absence of strict laws does not automatically eliminate scientific status. A successful philosophy of mental health must therefore accept physical dependence without demanding explanatory identity. It must recognize causal mechanisms without treating all causation as reducible to strict laws. Under those conditions, Boorse’s physiological model can survive the challenge of anomalous monism, but only by giving up the hope that mental health science will become a purely physiological or strictly nomological science.
